## RECORD 002

## THE CLAIMANT

The dragon does not answer.

I wait.

Then I wait longer.

The first ten seconds feel deliberate.

The next twenty feel rude.

After that I stop pretending I know what we're doing.

The dragon's eye stays on me.

“Not this time,” it says.

“Yes.”

“Say it again.”

“I just did.”

“I heard you.”

“Then we're making progress.”

“No.”

I rub the back of my neck.

The cave is warm enough that sweat has started drying under my collar. My pack is still on because taking it off feels too much like admitting I plan to stay.

The dragon notices that too.

Of course it does.

“What does the sentence mean?”

I look at it.

“It means I wasn't there this time.”

“That is the sentence with different words.”

“Correct.”

“You are avoiding the question.”

“Also correct.”

The Dragon settles its chin more comfortably against the stone.

That bothers me more than if it had stood up.

“I have time.”

“I noticed.”

“You are attempting to use that as a criticism.”

“Yes.”

“It does not function.”

“Worth trying.”

The eye narrows.

Not angry.

I think.

“You said you watched the Line fail.”

“Yes.”

“It has not failed during your life.”

“Yes.”

“You possess knowledge of structures that have not been taught during your life.”

“Yes.”

“You reached me by a route you claim no one taught you.”

“Yes.”

“And your explanation is that these things occurred at another time.”

“More or less.”

“Less.”

I sigh.

“Fine.”

The word leaves me, and something about the Dragon changes again.

It is not moving closer.

It has stopped waiting for me to become useful.

Apparently I already am.

“I remember another life.”

The cave is quiet.

I expect a reaction.

I get one eventually.

“How much?”

That is not the question I expected.

“How much what?”

“Of the life.”

“Most of it.”

“Most.”

“I was there for the whole thing.”

“That was not my question.”

I look at the Dragon.

It looks back.

“Enough,” I say.

“No.”

“You like that word.”

“It remains useful.”

I shift my pack higher.

“Forty years.”

The eye does not blink.

“Approximately?”

I stare at it.

The Dragon's mouth moves.

Not a smile.

Close enough that I don't appreciate it.

“Fuck you.”

“You introduced the uncertainty.”

“I remember about forty years.”

“Beginning when?”

“Nineteen.”

“And ending?”

“Fifty-nine.”

The Dragon's gaze moves over my face.

Then my hands.

Then the left knee I keep pretending isn't bothering me.

“You are fifty-nine now.”

“Approximately.”

This time it definitely smiles.

“I'm going to stop saying that.”

“No.”

“Good talk.”

“You claim to remember living from nineteen to fifty-nine twice.”

“I claim I remember the first one.”

“That distinction matters.”

“Sure.”

“And the second?”

I spread my hands.

“Still working on it.”

The Dragon goes quiet.

I can hear water somewhere deeper in the mountain. Not dripping. Running.

Slowly.

I hadn't noticed it before.

Maybe it just started.

Maybe I have spent too much of the last year around failing infrastructure and now every sound turns into something expensive.

“You believe you returned to nineteen.”

“Yes.”

“In the same world.”

“Mostly.”

The eye sharpens.

I close mine for a second.

“Don't.”

“Do not what?”

“Mostly.”

“You used the word.”

“I know.”

“Then explain it.”

“The world looked the same.”

“Looked.”

“Yes.”

“The same people?”

“The ones I checked.”

“The same places?”

“Mostly.”

I open my eyes.

The Dragon waits.

I hate patient people.

They make silence feel like work.

“The same parents,” I say. “Same town. Same house. Same scar on my mother's hand. Same crack in the kitchen wall. Same neighbor who stole our ladder and swore it had always been his.”

“Was it?”

“The ladder?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“You are certain.”

“He painted over my father's name.”

“That does increase confidence.”

I stare.

The Dragon does not smile.

Maybe that was a joke.

I decide not to reward it.

“Same king,” I continue. “Same roads. Same stupid licensing office. Same people I knew at nineteen. Mostly the same history.”

“Mostly.”

“There were differences.”

“How soon did you notice?”

I stop.

The Dragon notices that too.

“Not immediately.”

“How long?”

“Years.”

“That is not immediately.”

“No.”

“You lived with foreknowledge of forty years and did not identify a changed world for years.”

“When you say it like that, it sounds bad.”

“I said it accurately.”

“I was busy.”

“With what?”

“Being nineteen again.”

For the first time, the Dragon looks confused.

Not much.

Enough.

I laugh.

“You really don't get that part.”

“Explain it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because if you can't understand why having a nineteen-year-old back after living in a fifty-nine-year-old one might be distracting, I'm not sure forty minutes on the subject is going to help.”

“Forty minutes would be acceptable.”

“Of course it would.”

The Dragon waits.

I don't give it the satisfaction.

Eventually it asks, “What did you do in the first life?”

There it is.

A question with an answer I know how to give.

“I was a Ranker.”

“You are a Ranker.”

“A better one.”

“How much better?”

I hesitate.

Not because the answer is secret.

Because saying it out loud still does something stupid to me.

“S-class.”

The Dragon does nothing.

I had not expected applause.

Still.

“That's supposed to mean something.”

“To humans.”

“Yes.”

“What?”

I think about it.

“Mostly that people stopped sending me places with supervision.”

“That seems unwise.”

“It was.”

“What could you do?”

“A lot.”

“That is not a measure.”

“Neither is S-class, apparently.”

“No.”

I almost smile.

“Fine. I could cross an active pressure break without a shield team.”

The Dragon lifts its head slightly.

“I could hold a body reinforcement for most of a day if I wasn't doing anything stupid with it.”

“Were you?”

“Usually.”

“I assumed.”

“I could cut through a plated crawler at the joint.”

“With what?”

“Sword.”

“What kind?”

“Steel.”

The Dragon waits.

“Good steel.”

It keeps waiting.

“Expensive steel.”

“That is still steel.”

“Yeah.”

Something low moves in the Dragon's throat.

It takes me a second to realize it might be amusement.

“I killed a redback alone.”

That stops it.

Not dramatically.

The eye focuses.

“Where?”

“North of Vey.”

“There are no redbacks north of Vey.”

“There was one.”

“No.”

“I killed it.”

“What did it have beneath the third plate?”

I frown.

“What?”

“The third dorsal plate. What was beneath it?”

“Meat.”

The Dragon's eye closes.

“Very useful.”

“It was forty years ago.”

“So you have said.”

“Twice.”

“Three times.”

“Are you counting?”

“Yes.”

I take a breath.

“Black tissue. Stringy. It smelled like burnt hair when I opened it.”

The eye opens.

“Not black.”

“Looked black.”

“Purple.”

“It was full of blood.”

“Redback blood does not make it darker.”

“Well, mine did.”

The Dragon studies me.

I can feel myself getting defensive about the color of something I killed in another life four decades ago.

This is not how I planned the negotiation.

“Continue.”

“There was a hard membrane behind the plate. Thin. It folded instead of tearing.”

The Dragon's head rises another few inches.

“And?”

“I thought it was armor.”

“It was not.”

“I figured that out when it inflated.”

“How?”

“It tried to throw me off.”

The Dragon is completely still now.

I point at it.

“There. That.”

“What?”

“You know what I'm talking about.”

“I know what you are describing.”

“Same thing.”

“No.”

“Close enough.”

“No.”

I let my hand fall.

“Did I describe it wrong?”

“You described it from the wrong side.”

I blink.

“What does that mean?”

“You believed the membrane protected the animal.”

“It was under armor.”

“It regulated pressure.”

I think back.

The redback bucking under me.

My sword jammed halfway into a seam.

Heat.

That awful burned-hair smell.

Something beneath the plate swelling hard enough to lift me.

Then collapsing when I cut it.

I had thought I'd found a weak point.

Maybe I had.

Just not for the reason I thought.

“Huh.”

The Dragon watches my face.

“You remember the event.”

“Yeah.”

“You did not understand it.”

“Apparently.”

“That is useful.”

“For the Line?”

“No.”

“Of course not.”

“For you.”

I stare at it.

“There it is again.”

“What?”

“You keep making this about me.”

“It is about you.”

“Two million people.”

“Two point three.”

I hate it.

The Dragon continues before I can answer.

“A false memory can contain detail. A story told often enough can become intimate. A prediction can resemble recollection after the event. You may be lying. You may be ill. You may carry another person's memories and mistake possession for identity. You may have experienced something I do not yet have a useful category for.”

“That last one sounds promising.”

“It is the least informative.”

“Still.”

“But your error interests me.”

“My error.”

“You remembered the redback accurately enough to describe tissue most humans never see. You remembered the movement. The smell. The membrane. You were wrong about what it did.”

“People are wrong.”

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

“That is why I am still listening.”

That lands harder than I expect.

The Dragon lowers its head again.

Not all the way.

It has found a height it likes for this conversation.

“So you believe I remember it.”

“I believe you possess memories consistent with having been there.”

“That's the same thing with worse manners.”

“No.”

“Right.”

“I do not yet believe your explanation for the memories.”

“Also right.”

“You are less offended by that.”

“I've had forty years to get used to sounding insane.”

“Forty.”

“Don't.”

The Dragon's eye shifts to my pack.

“Food for three days.”

“About.”

“You said the return journey requires eight.”

“I can hunt.”

“With that knee?”

“Yes.”

It waits.

“Poorly.”

“Then your planning is inconsistent.”

“My planning got me here.”

“It may not get you back.”

I look toward the passage.

“Are you threatening me?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I am observing that you prepared to reach me more carefully than you prepared to leave.”

I look back.

The Dragon says nothing else.

It does not need to.

That one gets too close to something I have not agreed to discuss.

“The Line,” I say.

“No.”

“We've established I'm interesting.”

“Yes.”

“Congratulations. Help me.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I do not know what you are.”

“I'm Greg.”

“That is a name on a badge.”

I laugh despite myself.

The Dragon waits it out.

Then it asks, “In the first life, when you were S-class, did you know about the lower channels?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“Contract.”

“With whom?”

“Crown.”

“Which crown?”

“Ours.”

“Your current kingdom did not control the western sink forty years ago.”

I stop.

It watches me.

I go back through the memory.

A table.

Maps.

A seal.

Blue wax.

No.

Green.

Maybe.

A man with a beard I never liked.

Someone saying western allotment.

Someone else saying provisional authority.

“I don't remember.”

“You answered quickly.”

“I was wrong quickly.”

“That is better.”

“Who controlled it?”

“I don't know.”

“Who hired you?”

“I don't remember his name.”

“Where were you?”

“Below the ninth station.”

“That station did not have a lower entrance.”

“It did.”

“When?”

“When I was there.”

The Dragon studies me.

I feel the old irritation coming up now.

Not fear.

Not even embarrassment.

The specific anger of knowing something happened and being asked to prove it by details that mattered less than surviving it.

“There was a stair behind the spill chamber,” I say. “Not a real stair. Cut stone. Narrow. Thirty, maybe thirty-five steps. The last six were wet all the time. There was a split in the left wall halfway down that whistled when the upper valves cycled.”

The Dragon does not move.

“The door at the bottom wasn't a door. It looked like one because someone had put hinges on it. The hinges did nothing.”

One nostril opens slightly.

“There were marks under the lintel. Three sets. The middle one had been chiseled out.”

“What did the first say?”

“I couldn't read it then.”

“Then?”

“Later.”

“What did it say?”

I tell it.

The words are old.

Older than the kingdom.

I learned them badly and late, from a woman who charged too much and enjoyed correcting my vowels.

The Dragon listens until I'm done.

Then it says nothing.

Not for a long time.

This silence is different too.

I am starting to learn them.

“Wrong?” I ask.

“One word.”

“Which?”

It tells me.

I repeat it.

The Dragon corrects me.

I repeat it again.

“No.”

“That's what I said.”

“It is not.”

“Close.”

“No.”

I try a third time.

The Dragon finally leaves it alone.

“Does that prove it?”

“No.”

“Does anything?”

“Perhaps.”

“What?”

“I do not know yet.”

That answer surprises me more than anything else it has said.

The Dragon sees that.

“You expected me to know.”

“Yeah.”

“I do not.”

I look down at my hands.

Old hands.

Second set.

Same scars in some places.

Different in others.

The Dragon asks, “Why did you not become S-class again?”

There it is.

Simple.

Clean.

Meaner than it sounds.

“I don't know.”

“That is false.”

I look up.

“You really like that one too.”

“You know some of the answer.”

“Some.”

“Tell me.”

“No.”

The Dragon's eye narrows.

I point west again.

Still rock.

Still stupid.

“The Line.”

“The Line will remain in danger while you answer.”

“People won't.”

“They will also remain in danger while you argue.”

I open my mouth.

Close it.

The Dragon waits.

It is very good at this.

“I came here to make a trade.”

“Yes.”

“I have things you want to know.”

“Yes.”

“You have something I need.”

“Perhaps.”

“So trade.”

“What do you believe I need from you?”

“The information.”

“No.”

“My memories.”

“Closer.”

I don't like that.

The Dragon shifts its foreclaw.

Stone complains.

“I want to understand the discrepancy.”

“The what?”

“You.”

“Could've just said me.”

“I did. You objected.”

“Fair.”

“You claim a life in which you became substantially more capable than you are now. You claim knowledge from that life. You also claim the world repeated closely enough that the knowledge remained useful, but not exactly enough that your second life followed the first.”

“Yes.”

“You have spent forty years with that discrepancy.”

“Yes.”

“And instead of bringing it to anyone who might examine it, you used it.”

“Yes.”

The Dragon is quiet.

“What?”

“That was not criticism.”

“Sounded like it.”

“It was description.”

“Still.”

“What did you want when you were nineteen?”

I know the answer before it finishes.

“To do it better.”

“What does better mean?”

“Faster. Fewer mistakes.”

“Stronger?”

“Yes.”

“Richer?”

“I wasn't poor the first time.”

“That was not my question.”

“Yes.”

“Safer?”

I think about that.

“For people I cared about.”

The Dragon notices the pause.

I wish it wouldn't.

“And were you?”

“No.”

The word comes out too quickly.

The Dragon lets it sit there.

I don't take it back.

Eventually it asks, “What happened?”

I look at the passage again.

The way out.

Eight days, give or take.

Three days of food.

Bad knee.

No horse.

Two point three million people who do not know I am in here arguing about a life that never happened.

“Long story.”

The Dragon settles deeper against the stone.

Its eye remains open.

“Good.”
