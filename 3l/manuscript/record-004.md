## RECORD 004

## THE RETURNED

I woke up nineteen years old with both hands closed around a blanket I had not seen in forty years.

For a while I did not move.

That was not restraint.

My body had forgotten the habit of waking carefully.

No bad knee.

No shoulder catching under my weight.

No fingers that needed a second try before they closed.

No little inventory of damage before deciding whether standing was worth it.

I lay there staring at a ceiling with a water stain shaped like a boot.

The boot had been there when I was a boy.

It was still there when I left home the first time.

My father had promised to fix it for six straight years.

I had forgotten the stain before I forgot him.

Then I heard him snore through the wall.

That was when I sat up too fast.

The room moved.

I caught myself on one hand and nearly laughed.

Not because anything was funny.

Because my wrist took the weight.

Just took it.

No pain. No warning. No old break reminding me how clever I had been at twenty-eight.

I pressed my palm against the mattress harder than necessary.

Young skin.

Calluses, but not the right ones.

A thin scar across the thumb from a fishhook when I was twelve.

Nothing from the gate.

Nothing from the crawler.

Nothing from the winter outside Hollis when I had lost feeling in two fingers and lied about it until the healer threatened to cut them off.

I opened and closed my hand.

Fast.

Again.

The fingers obeyed.

Someone knocked on the wall.

“Stop doing whatever that is.”

My sister.

I froze.

She was seventeen again.

Alive.

Annoyed.

Apparently awake.

I looked at the wall.

“You awake?” she called.

“No.”

A pause.

“Then shut up quieter.”

I put both hands over my face.

That sounded exactly like her.

Not memory exactly.

Memory gets cleaned when you use it too often. It keeps the line and loses the breath before it. The little scrape in the throat. The way she said quieter like I had personally offended the concept.

This had all of it.

I sat there until she kicked the wall once and went away.

Then I got out of bed.

My feet hit cold boards.

Both knees bent.

Nothing hurt.

That almost put me back down.

I had been nineteen once already. I remembered being strong for nineteen. Too strong, according to me at nineteen. I had also been fifty-nine, which gave me a more useful comparison.

The body in that room was healthy.

It was not mine.

That was the first thought that felt dangerous.

Not because the face in the little mirror was wrong.

It was right.

Too right.

Black hair I had not yet started losing. Jaw without the old break. Nose before the second one. Eyes without the red veins that showed up after three bad years of sleeping in armor.

I knew the face.

I had just stopped thinking of it as me.

I touched my cheek.

The boy in the mirror did the same.

“Shit.”

From the kitchen, my mother yelled, “Language.”

I sat down on the bed.

My mother had been dead thirty-one years.

The sentence arrived neatly.

Too neatly.

I hated it.

There are facts that become smooth because you have carried them too long.

Then someone says your name from the next room and the fact gets edges again.

“Greg?”

“Yeah.”

“You sick?”

“No.”

“You sound sick.”

“I'm fine.”

“You want eggs?”

I stared at the wall.

“Yeah.”

“Then get dressed.”

I dressed.

Badly.

The shirt was too small by the standards I remembered and exactly right by the standards of the body wearing it. My boots were under the chair. I knew one lace had been knotted around a break because I had refused to buy another pair until payday.

It was there.

The knot.

Same place.

I stood holding the boot for too long.

Then I smelled eggs.

That got me moving.

The kitchen was smaller than I remembered.

Everything was.

My father sat at the table with a cup in one hand and a folded work sheet in the other. Same heavy shoulders. Same gray already coming into his beard. Same missing nail on his left little finger.

My mother stood at the stove.

There was a white line across the back of her hand from where a jar had broken when I was eight.

Same scar.

I checked before I could stop myself.

She noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You staring at my hand?”

“No.”

“You are.”

My father looked over the work sheet.

“He's nineteen. Maybe hands are interesting now.”

“Fuck you,” I said.

My mother pointed the spoon at me.

“Greg.”

“Sorry.”

My father lowered the sheet.

For one second I saw the old man he never got to become.

Then it was gone.

He was forty-three and trying not to smile.

I sat down.

My sister came in wearing one boot and carrying the other.

“You look weird.”

“Thank you.”

“No. Like actually.”

“I slept bad.”

“You were punching your bed.”

“I was testing something.”

She looked at my mother.

“See?”

My mother put eggs in front of me.

I ate too fast.

That became the first thing I understood about being nineteen again.

Hunger was different.

At fifty-nine I could miss breakfast and complain about it later.

At nineteen my body treated food like an emergency contract.

I finished the eggs, took bread, took more bread, and caught my mother watching me.

“What?”

“You said you weren't sick.”

“I'm hungry.”

“That part appears healthy.”

My father folded the sheet.

“You still going to Guild Hall?”

I stopped chewing.

There it was.

The date arrived before I could ask for it.

Guild Hall.

First registry.

The old route.

I looked toward the window.

Morning rain on the glass. Cart wheels outside. Somebody yelling about onions.

I knew this day.

Not every minute.

Not what shirt my father wore or whether it had rained.

But I knew the decision attached to it.

At nineteen, first time, I had gone to Guild Hall and registered for field work.

Three weeks later I took the ridge contract because it paid nearly double local rate.

Six months later I could reinforce my legs without vomiting.

Four years later I crossed the western cuts with people who taught me things no hall instructor ever had.

Twenty-one years later I wore an S on my badge and had enough money to pretend the first contract had been a good decision.

I looked at my father.

“Yeah.”

He nodded once.

“Don't take the first thing they offer.”

I almost laughed.

He had said that the first time too.

I had taken the second.

“Good advice.”

He looked at me strangely.

“You feeling all right?”

“Everyone keeps asking me that.”

“Because you're acting like somebody hit you in the head.”

“Maybe somebody did.”

My sister took my last piece of bread.

I let her.

That scared her more than anything else.

I left twenty minutes later.

The street outside was exactly where I remembered it.

Which felt unreasonable.

There should have been a sign.

A crack in the sky.

A bell nobody else could hear.

Something.

Instead there was mud.

A delivery cart had one wheel in a rut. A woman in a blue coat argued with the driver. Two boys ran past carrying a hoop between them. The baker across the way had already put yesterday's rolls in the cheap basket.

Normal morning.

I stood in it like an idiot.

Then a pressure bell sounded from somewhere east.

One low note.

Pause.

Two higher ones.

Line maintenance.

Routine.

Nobody else stopped.

I did.

The sound hit something in memory.

Not a scene.

A thousand scenes.

Roads closed during pressure work. Temporary barriers. Contractors complaining about lost hours. Stations humming under stone. The Line so ordinary I had spent most of my first life treating it like weather.

At fifty-nine, people were going to die because we had treated it like weather.

At nineteen, someone on this street swore because the maintenance bell meant his cart would be late.

I laughed.

The woman in the blue coat looked at me.

I kept walking.

Guild Hall was three districts over.

My body remembered the route badly.

My mind remembered it well.

That distinction became important before I reached the second hill.

I took the stairs two at a time because I remembered being able to.

Halfway up, my lungs informed me we had different records.

By the top I was breathing hard.

I leaned against the wall and stared at my hands.

“Fine.”

I knew how to fix that.

That was the dangerous part.

I knew reinforcement better than most instructors who had ever taught me. I knew breathing sequences. Load paths. How to move pressure through the hips without wasting it in the spine. How to hold a sheath under the skin and let impact disperse instead of stopping it.

The knowledge was there.

My body was nineteen.

I smiled.

Then I did something stupid.

Not very stupid.

I had standards by then.

I set my feet in the alley beside the stairwell, drew one slow breath, and opened the first reinforcement pattern I had learned properly at twenty-six.

The pattern answered.

For half a second.

Pressure moved through my chest.

My shoulders locked.

My right calf cramped so hard I hit the wall.

Then the whole thing collapsed and I threw up beside a rain barrel.

A woman passing the alley stopped.

“You all right?”

I wiped my mouth.

“Excellent.”

She looked at the wall I was using to stay upright.

“Sure.”

“Thank you.”

She left.

I stayed there until my calf stopped trying to become a fist.

That was the second thing I understood about being nineteen again.

Knowledge did not weigh anything.

Capability did.

The old pattern was still mine.

The lungs weren't.

The tendons weren't.

The pressure tolerance wasn't.

The body had not spent forty years becoming the thing my memory expected to inhabit.

I laughed again.

Then I threw up one more time.

Less impressive.

By the time I reached Guild Hall, I had mud on one boot and a sour taste in my mouth.

The building looked exactly as terrible as I remembered.

Long stone front. Narrow windows. Three lines outside before the doors even opened.

Field registry on the left.

Trade certification in the middle.

Contract labor on the right.

At nineteen the first time, I had looked at those doors and felt like my life was about to begin.

At nineteen the second time, I knew where each one went.

Not perfectly.

Enough.

I knew which instructors were worth listening to.

Which contracts paid late.

Which roads washed out every spring.

Which exam questions were traps.

Which habits took me years to unlearn.

Which people I had loved.

Which people I had buried.

Which mistakes had cost months.

Which had cost blood.

I stood across the street with my young heart beating too fast and forty years of memory sitting behind it.

I could do this better.

That was what I believed.

Not differently.

Better.

I crossed the street when the doors opened.
