## RECORD 012

## THE LEDGER

Nessa and I combined our money by accident.

Then we spent two years pretending we had not.

That was easier than discussing it.

Most things were.

Rent came due every fourth Monday.

I paid it because the apartment had been mine first.

Nessa bought food because she hated the market decisions I made when left unsupervised.

I bought lamp oil.

She bought soap.

I paid for coal in winter.

She paid the healer when I burned my hands because apparently refusing treatment did not count as saving money.

We both bought tea.

Only one of us bought tea worth drinking.

We disagreed about which one.

If one person was short, the other covered it.

If something broke, whoever noticed first usually paid.

If neither of us noticed, the thing remained broken until it developed enough personality for Nessa to defend it.

This system worked beautifully until it didn't.

Most systems do.

The problem was my mother's roof.

The same roof had leaked when I was twenty.

At twenty-eight it stopped leaking.

This was not an improvement.

A winter storm pulled six slate panels off the north side and put one through the shed roof.

My father described the damage as manageable.

My mother described my father as a liar.

She sent my sister to East Four with a note because apparently that made the lie official.

I was in the lower gallery when Dera handed it to me.

“Family?”

“Yes.”

“Dead?”

“No.”

“Then finish the valve.”

I liked Dera because she had priorities.

I hated Dera because they were sometimes correct.

I finished the valve.

Then I read the note properly.

Roof.

North side.

Water in the back room.

Father fell off ladder but says fine.

Mother says not fine.

Bring money if you have it.

Do not bring your father another ladder.

That last line was underlined twice.

My sister knew us well.

I went home after shift, took the tin from behind the flour jar, counted what was in it, and discovered adulthood had become irritatingly numerical.

Thirty-two silver.

Four copper.

Not poor.

Not enough to feel rich.

Enough that the roof could become my problem without asking permission.

I took twenty-six.

That left six silver and four copper.

I remember the amount because three days later the stove cracked.

Not the apartment stove.

The plate.

A thick iron heat plate that sat over the bakery flue and let us cook without paying for a separate winter fire most nights.

It had come with the rooms.

So had the table.

The table had survived us.

The plate had less character.

Nessa put a pot down.

There was a sharp sound.

The iron split from the vent hole to the edge.

She stared at it.

I stared at it.

Heat pushed through the crack hard enough to redden the pot base.

Nessa lifted the pot off.

“That's bad.”

“Yes.”

“You say that like you're pleased.”

“I am pleased when a problem is obvious.”

“Why?”

“Less arguing.”

She looked at me.

“We are absolutely going to argue.”

She was right.

The replacement plate cost eleven silver.

Twelve if we wanted the heavier casting.

Nessa wanted the heavier casting.

I wanted the cheaper one because I had six silver and four copper.

Neither of us knew this about the other yet.

The ironmonger brought both samples to the counter.

Nessa tapped the thicker plate with one knuckle.

“This one.”

“Why?”

“Because the cheap one flexes.”

“It is an iron plate.”

“I can see the edge move.”

“You cannot.”

She pressed down.

The edge moved.

Very slightly.

I hated when evidence took her side.

“Twelve,” the ironmonger said.

“Eleven and six,” Nessa said.

“Twelve.”

“Eleven and eight.”

The man looked at me.

I shrugged.

He looked back at her.

“Eleven and ten.”

“Done.”

She held out her hand.

Then looked at me.

“You paying or am I?”

That should have been a simple question.

Instead I said, “Can you?”

Nessa's hand stopped.

Not dramatic.

Just stopped.

The ironmonger suddenly became very interested in a box of hinges.

“Yes,” she said.

She paid.

We carried the plate home between us because it was too heavy for dignity and not heavy enough to justify delivery.

Neither of us spoke for three streets.

Then Nessa said, “How much do you have?”

“Enough.”

“That is not a number.”

“Six silver.”

She stopped.

The plate nearly pulled my shoulder out because I kept walking.

“Greg.”

“What?”

“You had more than six silver last month.”

“Yes.”

“How much more?”

“Some.”

“That is also not a number.”

“Twenty-six.”

Her eyebrows went up.

“You spent twenty-six silver in a month?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I spent it in a day.”

The eyebrows went higher.

There are expressions you should not collect from people you love.

I had several.

“On what?”

“My mother's roof.”

The eyebrows came down.

Not all the way.

“Is she all right?”

“Yes.”

“Your father?”

“Fell off a ladder.”

“Of course he did.”

“He's fine.”

“According to him?”

“Yes.”

“Then he may be dead.”

“Probably not.”

Nessa adjusted her grip on the plate.

We walked another half street.

Then she said, “Why didn't you tell me?”

“I just did.”

“Before you spent it.”

I looked at her.

“Why would I?”

That was the wrong answer.

I knew it as soon as it left my mouth.

Nessa knew it earlier.

She stopped again.

This time I stopped too.

The iron plate hung between us.

People walked around us.

A delivery boy muttered something rude because we were blocking half the pavement.

Nessa ignored him.

“Why would you.”

“It was my money.”

“Yes.”

“My mother needed a roof.”

“Yes.”

“So I paid for it.”

“Yes.”

I waited.

Nessa waited longer.

This technique had become unfair since the conversation about my first life.

She knew I was capable of forty years of bad habits now.

I no longer had youth as a defense.

“What?” I asked.

“Our stove broke.”

“The plate.”

“Our plate broke.”

“You paid for it.”

“Yes.”

“I'll pay you back.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because it is our fucking stove plate.”

A woman passing us looked over.

Nessa stared until she looked away.

Then she turned back to me.

“You paid the rent this month.”

“Yes.”

“I bought food.”

“Yes.”

“I paid for the coal delivery.”

“You did?”

There it was.

Nessa's face changed.

I had found the important wrong answer.

“You didn't know.”

“I thought I paid it.”

“You paid the last one.”

“Oh.”

“And I paid the healer after your shoulder locked.”

“I didn't need the healer.”

“You could not put on a shirt.”

“I eventually would have figured something out.”

“You tried to use a broom handle.”

“That almost worked.”

“It did not almost work.”

“It moved.”

“You screamed.”

“Quietly.”

Nessa closed her eyes.

The iron plate remained between us.

It was becoming symbolic in a way I resented.

“We are taking this home,” she said.

“Good.”

“And then we are writing down every coin that leaves that apartment.”

“That seems excessive.”

“You spent twenty-six silver without noticing I paid for winter coal.”

“I noticed the coal.”

“You did not notice paying for it.”

“That is different.”

“It is worse.”

We took the plate home.

Then Nessa found a ledger.

Of course she already owned one.

Nessa owned four blank ledgers because she believed blank paper was a form of preparedness.

I had once asked why anybody needed four.

She had said emergencies.

Apparently I was the emergency.

She put one on the table.

The table leaned toward the window.

The ledger slid half an inch.

Nessa looked at me.

“Don't.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You thought level.”

“I did not.”

“Reduced mysterious bullshit.”

“That rule has expanded beyond reason.”

“Sit.”

I sat.

She opened to the first page.

“What do you make?”

I told her.

She wrote it down.

“What do you make?” I asked.

She told me.

I blinked.

“That much?”

Nessa looked offended.

“Yes, Greg. They pay me for working.”

“I know.”

“You look surprised.”

“I thought the pressure desk rate was lower.”

“It is.”

“Then how?”

“Night differential. Transfer qualification. Two inspection supplements.”

I stared.

“When did you get transfer qualification?”

“Last spring.”

“You didn't tell me.”

“I did.”

“No.”

“Yes. You were fixing your father's mill housing.”

I searched the memory.

There had been grease.

A split bearing.

My father swearing at a pulley.

Nessa at the door saying something about a test.

I had kissed her forehead and said congratulations without putting down the wrench.

“Oh.”

“Remarkable.”

“I remember now.”

“Do you.”

“Yes.”

“What did I score?”

I looked at the ledger.

Nessa waited.

“High.”

She made a small sound through her nose.

“How high?”

“Very.”

“Asshole.”

“Ninety-one?”

“Eighty-eight.”

“Close.”

“It is not close when you are pretending to remember.”

“I was not pretending. I remembered the feeling.”

“What feeling?”

“Proud.”

That stopped her.

I had not meant to say it beautifully.

Good.

I was more reliable by accident.

Nessa looked down at the page.

Then wrote her wage beneath mine.

The numbers were closer than I thought.

Some weeks she made more.

That surprised me enough that I had the sense not to say so.

My Ranker supplement paid two extra silver when East Four logged me as active hazard coverage.

Halden's review work sometimes paid another one.

Nessa's transfer nights paid better than both if the station ran late.

Money had been moving around our apartment for years while I held an outdated picture of it in my head.

That was becoming a theme.

“Rent,” Nessa said.

I gave her the number.

“Food?”

“Depends.”

“Average.”

“I don't know.”

“I do.”

Of course she did.

She wrote it down.

Coal.

Lamp oil.

Soap.

Laundry.

The healer.

Guild dues.

My Ranker badge renewal.

Nessa's station certification fees.

Boots.

Tools.

Tea.

We argued about whether tea belonged under food.

Nessa said no because I bought terrible tea and the household should not be held responsible.

I moved it back under food when she went to the wash basin.

She moved it out again when she returned.

Then she wrote FAMILY.

I did not like the size of the word.

“How much do you give your parents?”

“Depends.”

“Greg.”

“Two silver most weeks.”

Nessa looked up.

“Most?”

“Sometimes three.”

“Since when?”

“Since I started making enough.”

“Eight years?”

“Not at first.”

“How long?”

“Six.”

She wrote it down.

No judgment.

That made me defensive anyway.

“They need it.”

“I didn't say they didn't.”

“My father still works.”

“I know.”

“My mother keeps the house.”

“I know.”

“They never asked for most of it.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you making that face?”

“What face?”

“The money face.”

“I don't have a money face.”

“You do now.”

She turned the ledger toward me.

“I'm not angry that you help them.”

“It feels like you're angry.”

“I'm angry that you think helping them has nothing to do with the rest of your life.”

I looked at the page.

My wages.

Her wages.

Rent.

Food.

Coal.

My parents.

Everything sat beside everything else with the indecency of arithmetic.

Money did not care which obligations felt noble.

It just left.

“My mother is not your expense,” I said.

Nessa leaned back.

“No.”

I relaxed.

“She is my family.”

I looked up.

That was not where I expected the sentence to go.

Nessa tapped the page.

“She feeds me every third Sunday.”

“Every second.”

“Your sister steals one.”

“True.”

“Your mother keeps a blanket for me because she thinks I am always cold.”

“You are always cold.”

“You sleep with the window open.”

“You steal the blanket.”

“Because I am cold.”

“This supports my argument.”

Nessa ignored that.

“Your father spent six hours fixing my work chest after the hinge tore out.”

“He likes fixing things.”

“He insulted the hinge for an hour.”

“He likes that too.”

“Your nephew calls me Aunt Ness even though nobody taught him to.”

“My sister taught him.”

“She denies it.”

“She lies.”

Nessa tapped FAMILY again.

“Your mother needing a roof affects me because if you have no money after paying for it, I buy the stove plate.”

“I said I'd pay you back.”

“That is exactly the stupid part.”

I frowned.

“Why?”

“Because you're treating the apartment like two people renting half a life from each other.”

That shut me up.

Nessa looked slightly surprised too.

Maybe she had not known the sentence until it came out.

Good sentences do that sometimes.

Bad ones too.

I had more experience with the second kind.

She looked down again.

“I'm not saying every coin is ours.”

“Good.”

“My money is still mine.”

“Also good.”

“Your money is yours.”

“Yes.”

“If I want to buy a stupid coat, I will.”

“You own three stupid coats.”

“I own one stupid coat and two excellent coats you dislike because they have color.”

“One is orange.”

“It is rust.”

“It can be both.”

“Not the point.”

She drew three lines down the page.

One column.

Then another.

Then a third.

“House,” she said.

She wrote HOUSE over the first.

“Nessa.”

Second column.

“Greg.”

Third.

I looked at the page.

“That is your solution?”

“No. It is paper.”

“That sounded like Dera.”

“Good.”

“She would be offended.”

“Also good.”

Nessa pointed at HOUSE.

“Rent. Food. Coal. Plates when you break them.”

“I did not break it.”

“Things we both use.”

She pointed at NESSA.

“My certification. My clothes. My sister when she inevitably asks me for money again.”

“You have a sister?”

Nessa stared at me.

I regretted everything.

“Of course I know you have a sister.”

“What is her name?”

“Lena.”

“Good recovery.”

“Thank you.”

“You have met her eleven times.”

“She talks fast.”

“She hates you.”

“She does not.”

“She calls you the station ghost.”

“That sounds affectionate.”

“It is not.”

Nessa pointed at GREG.

“Guild fees. Ranker equipment. Your terrible boots. Whatever weapon you pretend you don't want next.”

“I don't need another weapon.”

“You touched the short sword in the south market six times.”

“It had good balance.”

“Greg.”

“I didn't buy it.”

“Because you spent the money on a roof.”

“That proves responsibility.”

“That proves arithmetic.”

She took the pencil back.

Then looked at FAMILY.

There was no family column anymore.

She had not copied it.

“Where does my mother's roof go?” I asked.

Nessa thought.

“Not house.”

“Good.”

“Not Greg either.”

I looked at her.

“Why?”

“Because if your father falls off another ladder, I am going to their house whether you invite me or not.”

“That is not an accounting category.”

“It is now.”

She wrote FAMILY across the bottom of all three columns.

The word crossed every line.

I hated how much I liked that.

“It looks bad,” I said.

“You look bad.”

“The lines are wrong.”

“The lines are honest.”

That annoyed me more.

We spent two hours on the ledger.

Not because we had much money.

Because money carries memory.

Every number required a story.

The three silver I gave my mother the week my father's mill stopped.

The four Nessa sent Lena after Lena's youngest got fever.

The healer bill Nessa paid and never mentioned.

The boots I replaced for her because the sole split and she claimed one dry foot was enough.

The winter coat she bought me because my old one had become more patch than coat.

The seven silver I thought I had saved but had actually loaned my sister when the baby came early.

The six silver Nessa kept in a jar marked FLOUR even though neither of us had bought flour in months.

“What is this?” I asked.

“Emergency.”

“Why flour?”

“No thief steals flour.”

“That is not true.”

“A competent thief.”

I counted it.

Six silver.

Two copper.

“You have more emergency money than I do.”

“We have more emergency money than you do.”

I looked at her.

Nessa looked back.

There it was.

Small word.

We.

She had used it before.

So had I.

Hundreds of times.

We need coal.

We are late.

We should eat.

We have no soap.

We hate that neighbor.

That last one had been mostly me.

But money made the word heavier.

Not romantic.

If anything, the opposite.

Romance rarely smells like lamp oil and wet roof slate.

The six silver in the flour jar meant that if I broke an arm, Nessa still ate.

It meant if Lena's child got sick, we had a discussion instead of a disaster.

It meant if my mother's roof opened to the sky, the stove plate still got replaced.

I had spent one life earning enough money that I stopped thinking about small amounts.

By the time I reached S-class, contracts paid numbers that would have sounded obscene to the man sitting at that table.

I bought equipment because it was good.

I paid travel fees without reading them.

I rented rooms for months and slept in them twice.

Money became distance.

It removed small problems before they reached me.

I had thought that meant I understood money.

I did not.

I understood earning.

Different skill.

Nessa understood the distance between twelve silver and six.

She understood which week could absorb boots and which week could not.

She knew food cost more before storms because market carts arrived late.

She knew winter coal was cheaper if ordered with the bakery downstairs.

She knew the healer would accept two payments if you asked before treatment and three if you asked while bleeding enough to embarrass him.

I knew how to kill a redback alone.

She knew the laundress overcharged us for blankets.

Only one skill was useful that night.

That was becoming another theme too.

We did not merge everything.

Nessa refused.

I suggested putting both wages into the house column and taking personal money back out as needed.

She looked at me like I had proposed eating the ledger.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I am not asking permission to buy my own boots.”

“You wouldn't.”

“I know. That is why we are not building a system where either of us could start.”

I thought about that.

She was right.

Annoying woman.

Instead we set a house amount.

Enough for rent, food, coal, oil, and the kind of repairs that always appeared two days after payday.

We each put in the same base amount.

When my Ranker supplement hit, I put in part of the extra.

When Nessa worked transfer nights, she did the same.

Not half.

Not all.

Enough that neither person could accidentally become the other's employer.

That was how Nessa phrased it.

I laughed.

Then stopped when I realized she was serious.

“People get strange when one person pays for everything,” she said.

“People get strange without money too.”

“Yes. We are preparing for both.”

We made another rule.

Large family expenses got spoken aloud before the coin left.

Not permission.

Information.

I argued with that distinction for twenty minutes.

Nessa let me.

Then she asked what I would have done if she had emptied her savings to help Lena and told me only after our rent came due.

I stopped arguing.

I hated examples that worked.

The next week my father tried to repay six silver from the roof.

He put the coins in my hand after dinner like he was passing me contraband.

“Don't tell your mother.”

“She knows.”

“She does not.”

My mother called from the other room.

“I know.”

My father closed his eyes.

Nessa laughed into her tea.

I pushed the coins back.

My father pushed them toward me again.

This could have continued until one of us died.

First life, probably me.

Second life, my mother ended it.

“Take three,” she said.

My father looked offended.

I looked offended too.

Nessa said, “Good.”

Everyone looked at her.

She put down her cup.

“The roof was twenty-six. You can pay six if you want, but then Greg will sneak six back into the flour jar or buy you something equally stupid.”

I stared at her.

My mother said, “What flour jar?”

“Nothing,” I said.

Nessa continued.

“Three means you contributed. He helped. Nobody has to win.”

My father considered that.

Then handed me three silver.

I took it.

I hated the compromise because it worked immediately.

On the walk home I said, “You told them about the flour jar.”

“I did not.”

“You nearly did.”

“Reduced mysterious bullshit.”

“That is not what that means.”

“It means whatever I need now.”

She was smiling.

I put the three silver into the FAMILY line when we got home.

Nessa moved one to HOUSE because she said we had eaten dinner there and accounting should recognize hospitality.

I moved it back.

She moved it again.

The coin stayed in HOUSE because I went to bed first.

That was how our financial system matured.

Not elegantly.

Repeatedly.

The ledger lasted four years.

Not that page.

That page became illegible by winter.

Nessa wrote too small when annoyed.

I wrote too hard and tore the paper near the binding.

We started another.

Then another.

The categories changed.

Coal went up.

Rent went up.

Our wages did too.

Nessa's faster for a while after she took senior transfer qualification.

Mine jumped when Halden started paying station-review rates instead of ordinary hazard supplement.

My mother needed medicine one winter.

My father pretended not to.

Lena borrowed money twice and repaid it once.

My sister stopped asking for help with the baby and started asking for help with school fees instead.

The baby had become a child while I was not paying attention.

That happened constantly.

Children were inconsiderate about chronology.

A line item called SHOES appeared with suspicious frequency after that.

Nessa's cracked molar finally broke.

She paid the healer from NESSA.

I moved half the cost into HOUSE.

She moved it back.

I moved it again.

She asked whether I intended to chew with her teeth.

I said occasionally.

She threw the pencil at me.

The cost remained NESSA.

The healer bill for my shoulder stayed GREG for the same reason, though I argued that she used my shoulder when she slept.

That earned another pencil.

We lost many pencils in those years.

The ledger did not make us better with money.

It made hiding from money harder.

Different achievement.

There were still weeks we overspent.

Still weeks Nessa bought something stupid.

Still weeks I bought something more expensive and defended it as equipment.

Once I spent nine silver on a field coat because the stitching was triple-backed and the collar sealed properly in rain.

Nessa called it a cape for a maintenance worker.

I wore it for eleven years.

She stopped complaining after six.

Technically I won.

She would disagree.

The important part was not the numbers.

Ithar would have hated that sentence.

He liked numbers because numbers told on people.

So fine.

The important part was also the numbers.

Twelve silver for the plate.

Twenty-six for the roof.

Two most weeks to my parents.

Six in the flour jar.

Three returned by my father.

Numbers made the shape visible.

Before the ledger, I thought commitment was a feeling you eventually named.

Maybe it is sometimes.

I was bad at that part.

What I knew was this.

There was a winter when Nessa earned more than I did for three months and I did not feel threatened.

There was a spring when my father could not work full days and money moved toward my parents without either of us pretending it came from nowhere.

There was a year when Nessa paid for a qualification that took her away from East Four two days each month and the house did not collapse because I learned to buy food that was not smoked meat and onions.

Mostly.

There was a month when I missed two station reviews because my mother needed help getting my father to a healer.

Nessa covered the extra rent without calling it a loan.

The next month I covered coal without calling it repayment.

We still knew the numbers.

That was the difference.

Nothing disappeared just because we stopped keeping score against each other.

The ledger stayed honest even when we became generous.

Years later, when I had more money again, I kept one.

Not the same book.

Not even the same system.

But I wrote things down.

People owed.

People paid.

Food.

Repairs.

Travel.

Medicine.

School fees.

Equipment.

Money sent home.

Money given away.

Money I would never see again and did not need to.

A life becomes expensive in very ordinary ways.

That is not a complaint.

It is evidence.

I remember one page better than the rest.

Not because the amount was large.

Because of the handwriting.

Nessa had written FAMILY across the bottom of three columns.

The word crossed every line.

I had complained the lines were wrong.

They were.

We kept them that way.
