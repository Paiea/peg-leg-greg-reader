## RECORD 021

## THE BROTHER

My sister moved across the river because I told her not to.

That is not exactly why.

It is the version she preferred.

Lena had been working at the printer since I came home nineteen again.

Not the same job.

Jobs grow around people if the people stay long enough.

At first she trimmed paper.

Then set type.

Then corrected other people's setting because apparently literacy becomes management if enough errors happen nearby.

By thirty-one, I was doing district inspection and pretending private repair work was not becoming a second job.

Lena was supervising the evening floor at South Print.

She had Eli.

A husband named Oren who repaired clocks badly enough to stay employed and well enough to stay married.

A rented house three streets from my parents.

And, according to me, a perfectly good arrangement.

That should have warned everyone.

She told us about the offer at dinner.

My father's dinner.

He still cooked once a week because retirement had not damaged his confidence with carrots.

The stew was orange.

Nessa looked into the pot and said, “You know other vegetables exist.”

My father said, “Unproven.”

Lena waited until bowls were full.

Smart.

People argue worse when hungry.

“I got offered North River.”

My mother looked up first.

“North River what?”

“Floor manager.”

My father stopped chewing.

Oren smiled into his bowl.

That meant he already knew.

Traitor.

I said, “No.”

Everyone looked at me.

Lena blinked.

Then smiled.

Not kindly.

“I wasn't asking you.”

“I know.”

That was worse.

North River Print was larger than South.

Much larger.

Government contracts.

Guild manuals.

School books.

Election sheets when the city bothered pretending the ballots were interesting.

Higher pay.

Regular day schedule after six months.

Better pension contribution.

And across the river.

Not another province.

Not even another city.

Forty minutes by tram when the bridge line behaved.

An hour when it did not.

A different school for Eli if they moved near the shop.

Less time near my parents.

That was the part I heard.

Lena heard the rest.

“It's nearly four silver more,” she said.

“After transport?”

“If we move.”

“So move.”

“I am.”

There it was.

I put down my spoon.

My mother did not.

Interesting.

“You already decided.”

“Yes.”

“Then why are we discussing it?”

Lena's face changed.

Nessa kicked me under the table.

Hard.

I looked at her.

She kept eating.

Coward.

Lena said, “Because I live in a family, Greg.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

That was rude.

Also fair.

My father cleared his throat.

“How far?”

“Three stops past the bridge.”

“You'll bring Eli Sundays?” my mother asked.

“Yes.”

“Every Sunday?”

“Most.”

My mother nodded.

That was it.

No objection.

No speech about distance.

No reminder that her knees hurt and my father's balance had become an unreliable employee.

I waited for someone else to say the obvious thing.

Nobody did.

So I did.

“You help here.”

Lena looked at me.

“Yes.”

“With appointments.”

“Yes.”

“Medicine.”

“Yes.”

“Eli comes after school sometimes.”

“Yes.”

“Moving makes all of that harder.”

“Yes.”

She kept agreeing.

I hated when people did that.

It removed the easy argument.

“So?” I asked.

“So it gets harder.”

“That is your answer?”

“No. My answer is I still want the job.”

I laughed once.

Wrong sound.

Lena heard it.

“You think that's stupid.”

“I think you're adding a problem to solve a problem.”

“What problem am I solving?”

“Money.”

Her expression went flat.

Oren stopped pretending the stew mattered.

Nessa kicked me again.

I moved my leg out of range.

Lena said, “You think my job is money.”

“That is generally why people have jobs.”

“Is that why you inspect old stations?”

“Yes.”

Everyone stared at me.

“Mostly.”

Nessa made a sound into her cup.

I ignored her.

Lena leaned back.

“I want the floor.”

“What?”

“The floor. Forty people. Two presses. Scheduling. Training. I want it.”

I had not expected that sentence.

That bothered me more than it should have.

“You supervise now.”

“Twelve people.”

“Still supervision.”

“You were C-class and turned down the west contract because you had already been S-class once.”

The table went quiet.

My parents did not know the truth.

Not the whole truth.

Lena knew only pieces.

Apparently Nessa had shared enough of my western-contract philosophy to weaponize it socially.

I looked at her.

She shook her head.

Not me.

Lena had heard me say it myself years earlier.

Right.

People listen when you are being dramatic at dinner.

She continued.

“You had somewhere higher to compare yourself to. I don't.”

That landed.

Harder than I wanted.

I looked down at the orange stew.

My first life had given me a bad habit.

Not foreknowledge.

Scale.

I knew what continental work looked like.

S-class.

Kings.

Guild halls changing schedules because I arrived.

That made ordinary ambition look small if I was not careful.

Four silver.

Forty workers.

A print floor.

Three tram stops.

I had almost turned my sister's next life into transportation math because I could not feel the size of it from inside my own history.

That was one problem.

There was another.

In the first life, Lena had stayed close.

Not always the same house.

Close.

I remembered letters.

Her first child.

A second baby who did not exist in this history.

A winter when Oren's shop failed and they moved back near my parents.

A later year when she managed a small printer, not North River.

I remembered her complaining about ink shortages.

I remembered missing too much of it.

The memories formed a shape.

Home.

Lena nearby.

Parents aging.

That shape felt safe because it had already happened.

That was not the same as good.

I knew that.

Apparently knowing and using knowledge remained separate careers.

“North River closed in my first life,” I said.

Nessa looked at me sharply.

Lena frowned.

“What?”

Too late.

There are sentences you cannot put back in your mouth.

That was one.

“When?” Lena asked.

I should have stopped.

I did not.

“Not soon. Years.”

“How many?”

“I don't remember exactly.”

“Why?”

“Contract loss. Maybe. Government moved school printing. There was a strike too.”

I could see the memory while I spoke.

First-life memory.

A notice in a Guild hall because document supply had been disrupted.

I had not cared which printer lost the contract.

North River was just a name attached to delayed maps.

Now my sister wanted to move her life there.

Lena stared at me.

Nessa said, “Greg.”

Quiet.

Warning.

I knew.

I kept going anyway.

“If you stay at South, you already have seniority. North River could be gone before Eli finishes school.”

Oren spoke for the first time.

“Could.”

“Yes.”

“You know?”

“No.”

“Then why are you talking like that?”

Good question.

I hated him for asking it.

Lena's face had changed again.

Not anger now.

Something worse.

Doubt.

I had put it there.

Not evidence from this life.

A memory from mine.

First mine.

The distinction mattered.

Nessa put down her spoon.

“What do you know now?” she asked.

I looked at her.

She meant current evidence.

Our rule had been about choices touching her.

Apparently she had expanded jurisdiction.

Again.

“North River has city contracts.”

“Current?”

“Yes.”

“Solvent?”

“I don't know.”

“Staffing?”

“I don't know.”

“Management?”

“I don't know.”

Lena said, “I do.”

We looked at her.

She had interviewed twice.

Walked the floor.

Talked to three supervisors.

Read the contract board.

Asked about the last four years of layoffs.

North River had lost one government contract and replaced it with school work.

The day schedule was real.

The pension was real.

The move allowance was small but real.

They had been profitable three years.

Not rich.

Stable.

Current life.

Current evidence.

My memory had none of that.

I had a closure years from now under conditions I did not understand in a history already broken at North Vey.

I looked at my sister.

“You did your homework.”

She stared at me.

“Yes, Greg.”

“I didn't mean that badly.”

“You usually don't.”

That hurt because it was accurate.

My mother finally spoke.

“I want you closer.”

Lena's eyes went to her.

There.

The actual argument.

Not North River's future.

Not my memories.

A mother whose knees hurt wanted her daughter nearby.

Lena reached across the table.

My mother took her hand.

“I know,” Lena said.

“I still want you to go.”

That surprised me.

My mother looked at me.

“Children leaving is not betrayal.”

I knew that sentence was for me.

I did not appreciate it.

My father said, “You came back.”

Everybody looked at him.

He shrugged.

“You left first. Then came back.”

First life, I had left.

Second life, I had stayed.

My sister had lived with both consequences without knowing there were two versions.

I rubbed my face.

“Fine.”

Lena's eyebrows went up.

“Fine?”

“That sounded like permission.”

“It did.”

“I withdraw it.”

“Good.”

“You should go.”

She watched me.

Then smiled a little.

“Still sounds like permission.”

“Fuck.”

Nessa laughed.

My father added more carrots to his bowl.

Conversation moved on.

Not completely.

Nothing moves on completely.

Lena and Oren moved six weeks later.

North River district.

Two rooms over a cooper's shop for the first year.

Eli hated the new school for twelve days and then developed a best friend named Jori who taught him how to spit between tram rails.

Apparently adaptation had standards.

My mother cried after they left.

Not before.

She made Lena take three blankets she did not need and a pot she did not want.

My father gave Oren a box of tools and explained each one twice.

I carried furniture.

That was my role.

Useful.

Simple.

At the new apartment, Lena pointed at a press schedule pinned beside the kitchen door.

“You want to inspect that too?”

“No.”

“Sure?”

“Yes.”

“North River might close.”

I deserved that.

“I was an asshole.”

She stopped.

I had not planned to say it there.

Good apologies rarely arrive under ideal conditions.

There was a chair in my hands.

Oren was arguing with the cooper downstairs about staircase width.

Eli was under the table even though the table was not assembled.

Lena looked tired.

Happy too.

Both can happen.

“I used something I remember as if it gave me authority over your choice,” I said.

She leaned against the wall.

“You do that a lot?”

“Less than I used to.”

“That is not comforting.”

“I know.”

“Do you remember me making bad choices?”

“Yes.”

“Good ones?”

“Yes.”

“Did you stop either?”

I thought about the first life.

Mostly letters.

Visits.

Advice given from cities far away.

Money sent when asked.

Advice sent when not.

“No.”

“Then maybe keep that tradition.”

I laughed.

“Fair.”

She took the chair from me.

“Also I'm older than you.”

“No, you're not.”

“In this life?”

“No.”

“In your head?”

I hesitated.

Lena smiled.

“There. That's the stupid part.”

She carried the chair inside.

I stood in the hall.

The stupid part.

I had forty extra years in my head.

That did not make me forty years older than my sister.

It made me a man with more memories.

Some useful.

Some dangerous.

None of them promoted me to father.

North River Print did not close that year.

Or the next.

I stopped checking after a while.

Not because I stopped caring.

Because checking had become another way to wait for my memory to be right.

Lena became floor manager.

Then good at it.

Those were different events.

The first happened when they gave her the key.

The second took years.

She trained people.

Fired one.

Hired three.

Lost a school contract and gained a medical-printing contract I had never heard of in the first life.

Eli learned the tram route.

My parents learned to plan appointments on Lena's days off instead of assuming she lived three streets away.

I learned to take my mother to the healer without treating the schedule change as evidence that Lena had made the wrong choice.

Mostly.

Once, when the bridge tram failed and Lena missed dinner, I said nothing.

Nessa noticed.

“You want credit?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

Cruel woman.

Years later I remembered North River's first-life closure more clearly.

It had not failed because the company was badly run.

The city had consolidated printing after a budget crisis.

The strike came afterward.

I had reversed the order.

Memory had preserved the shape and lost the mechanism.

Again.

By then the second-life city had different contracts, different officials, different workers, and a print floor run in part by my sister.

The old future had no jurisdiction there.

Neither did I.

I was her brother.

That turned out to be enough work.
