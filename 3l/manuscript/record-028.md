## RECORD 028

## THE BIDDER

I lost my first large contract to a man who owned twelve ladders.

That was not the only reason.

It was the one I complained about.

The contract covered winter maintenance for six public buildings and two market branches.

Not stations.

Building-side systems.

Heat exchangers.

Pressure regulators.

Emergency shutoffs.

Scheduled inspections before winter load.

Call response if something failed after.

Ordinary work multiplied until it became money large enough to frighten me.

The notice went up at Guild Hall in spring.

Lorn found it.

He brought the sheet to our new room and put it on Nessa's desk.

She looked at him.

“Wrong desk.”

“They are touching.”

“That does not make his paper mine.”

“Household precedent says otherwise.”

I looked up.

“What?”

Lorn pointed at the contract.

“Bid.”

I read it.

City School Three.

Public bath east.

Two market halls.

Three administrative buildings.

Eight sites total.

Five-month service term.

Routine inspection plus emergency coverage.

Fixed base fee.

Materials separate above threshold.

Response within two bells for critical heat loss.

I read the total estimate twice.

Then a third time because large numbers develop additional digits when observed.

“We can do that.”

Nessa said, “Can you?”

“Yes.”

Lorn said, “Yes.”

Nessa looked between us.

“That was fast.”

“We know the systems,” I said.

“You know eight buildings?”

“I know four.”

“Half.”

“Lorn knows the bath.”

“I know the bathhouse owner owes money to everyone.”

“That counts.”

Nessa took the notice.

“What is the call requirement?”

“Two bells.”

“Every day?”

“Critical failures.”

“That was not what I asked.”

I read the small line.

Service coverage continuous during contract term.

There it was.

“We rotate.”

“With whom?”

“Me. Lorn. Rell.”

“Rell agreed?”

“No.”

“Excellent crew.”

“She will.”

“How do you know?”

“Current evidence.”

Nessa stared.

I sighed.

“I will ask.”

The bid packet was fourteen pages.

I hated it before page three.

Guild registration.

Worker list.

Trade authority.

Rank coverage.

Emergency response plan.

Material rates.

Wage schedule.

Surety reserve.

That one stopped me.

“What is a surety reserve?”

Lorn looked at me.

“I don't know.”

Good.

Shared incompetence.

We went to Guild Hall.

Clerk Aven was not at the front desk.

Thank God.

The younger clerk explained.

For public maintenance contracts above fifty silver, bidder had to prove they could finish or fail safely.

Cash reserve, bonded sponsor, or Guild surety.

Enough to cover one month of wages plus emergency replacement labor if the contractor disappeared, died, became insolvent, or decided winter was spiritually optional.

“How much?” I asked.

He calculated.

Forty-eight silver minimum.

I laughed.

The clerk did not.

“That is not funny.”

“I know.”

“It is your workers' wages.”

“I know.”

“Then why laugh?”

“Alternative to crying.”

We had savings.

Household savings.

Private-work float.

Tool replacement.

Family reserve.

Not forty-eight silver we could freeze for five months without becoming stupid.

“Guild surety?”

“Premium.”

“How much?”

He gave the number.

Also not funny.

“Who pays that?”

“Contractors who do not have reserve.”

“Then how do they get reserve?”

“By completing contracts.”

I stared.

The clerk shrugged.

“Yes.”

Civilization had discovered circles before I did.

There were other requirements.

Three documented comparable jobs.

We had dozens of small calls.

Not comparable scale.

Two qualified response workers available at all times.

Hard but possible.

Written substitute coverage if both lead workers were unavailable.

We had none.

Parts account with approved supplier.

Mina's cousin sold fittings from a cart.

Apparently that did not count.

Liability assessment.

Our Rope Street complaint appeared there.

Resolved.

Still appeared.

I disliked records when they remembered me.

Lorn read the packet at our table that night.

“Still bid.”

“We cannot meet surety.”

“Guild surety.”

“Costs nine silver.”

“Contract pays more.”

“Only if we win and nothing goes wrong.”

“That is business.”

Nessa looked up from her district schedule.

“No. That is gambling with invoices.”

“Same family.”

She came over.

We made numbers.

Because of course.

Base fee looked large.

Then wages.

Rell, if she joined.

A second occasional fitter.

Travel.

Guild surety premium.

Call availability.

Unpaid time inspecting before quote.

Tool wear.

Administrative filing.

Emergency cart hire.

Material float before city reimbursement.

By the bottom of the page, the impressive number had become ordinary.

Still profitable.

If we estimated correctly.

If emergency calls stayed normal.

If clients paid on time.

If nobody got hurt.

If winter behaved.

Nessa tapped the page.

“You can bid.”

I looked at her.

“You just spent an hour making it smaller.”

“Yes.”

“Why bid?”

“Because now you know the price.”

That annoyed me.

Good advice often does.

Rell agreed.

Not immediately.

She wanted guaranteed minimum pay during the contract even if call volume was low.

Lorn objected.

“Why pay if we don't need her?”

Rell looked at him.

“Because if you reserve my winter, I cannot take other work.”

Lorn stopped.

Good.

I liked workers who improved meetings by refusing bad assumptions.

We added standby pay.

Our price went up.

Then Rell asked who covered if my shoulder flared.

“I do.”

“That is not an answer.”

Nessa smiled from across the room.

Everyone had learned my weak points.

We arranged reciprocal call coverage with another small contractor named Sera Pellin.

Not free.

Her rate went into the bid.

Price up again.

By submission day we were no longer cheap.

That felt like failure.

Lorn said it felt like adulthood.

Neither of us knew.

The Guild opened bids publicly.

Not the full details.

Names and totals.

Five bidders.

We were second cheapest.

The lowest bidder was Harven Works.

Established firm.

Twenty-three employees.

Two carts.

Parts yard.

Twelve ladders.

I counted.

They had brought a equipment list.

“Who needs twelve ladders?” I whispered.

Lorn said, “People with eight buildings.”

Traitor.

Harven's price was only three silver below ours.

I became hopeful.

Mistake.

The technical review gave us good marks.

Best emergency stop plan.

Good field experience.

Strong local references.

Harven beat us on coverage depth.

Parts inventory.

Substitute staffing.

Financial reserve.

Documented public-contract history.

And yes, ladders.

Equipment availability, technically.

Same insult.

The award went to Harven.

I read the decision twice.

Lorn read it once.

“Fuck.”

“Yes.”

“We were good.”

“Yes.”

“Then why them?”

I pointed at the sheet.

He read the scoring again.

“Because they own everything.”

“No.”

I hated the answer while saying it.

“Because if their lead gets sick, the contract still exists.”

Lorn looked at me.

That was it.

Our entire operation still concentrated too much risk in people.

Me.

Him.

Rell.

Harven had redundancy.

Not better workers necessarily.

More ways for work to continue after one worker became unavailable.

Record Twenty-Two returned rudely.

Shoulder.

Desk.

Other people rearranging around me.

A public client did not want to rearrange.

Reasonable.

I still hated them.

Harven started the contract in autumn.

I waited for them to fail.

This was petty.

I admit it.

One market regulator stuck in November.

Harven replaced it in one bell.

School heat exchanger cracked.

They had a spare plate in their yard.

Public bath lost feed on a Sunday.

A crew arrived while their lead inspector was apparently two towns away.

Infuriating competence.

Then one of their ladders broke.

Nobody was hurt.

I enjoyed that too much.

Nessa said so.

“They have eleven now.”

“Still excessive.”

“Let it go.”

“No.”

We did not grow into Harven Works overnight.

That would have been the wrong lesson.

I did not want twenty-three employees because a scoring sheet said larger was safer.

Larger creates different failures.

I had seen enough institutions to know that.

But we changed three things.

First, reciprocal coverage became permanent with Sera instead of something arranged only for bids.

Second, we opened a supplier account with an actual parts yard and kept a small standard inventory instead of buying every fitting after the problem existed.

Third, I started documenting comparable jobs properly.

Scope.

Hours.

Workers.

Outcome.

Client reference.

Not because memory failed.

Because nobody awarding a contract could inspect the contents of my head.

They should not have to.

A year later we bid a smaller public package.

Three buildings.

No continuous winter coverage.

Surety twelve silver.

We won.

That is not the important part.

The important part is the first loss remained fair.

No corrupt official.

No idiot procurement clerk.

No institution too stupid to recognize Greg's brilliance.

Harven Works was more legible as a promise.

They had people.

Parts.

Records.

Money held against failure.

Ladders.

Too many ladders.

I was technically good enough for the work.

The contract required more than technical goodness.

That offended me until I started paying other people.

Then it became obvious.
