## RECORD 065

## THE CORRESPONDENT

Halden left the district and became more annoying by mail.

Distance improved neither of us.

He took a central review post in the capital three years after Market Seven.

Not promotion exactly.

Different authority.

Less field work.

More access to records from places normal district engineers never saw.

Dera said this made him dangerous.

Halden agreed.

Then left anyway.

His first letter arrived at the shop two months later.

Eight pages.

No greeting beyond GREG.

Professional warmth.

Page one described a pressure anomaly in a northern station I had never visited.

Page two included a copied sketch.

Page three asked whether the mark beside an old sleeve matched anything in our archive.

Pages four through seven were measurements.

Page eight said:

DO NOT SAY INTERESTING. ANSWER THE QUESTION.

I wrote back:

INTERESTING.

Then answered.

The mark family matched Market Seven loosely.

Observed behavior did not.

No conclusion.

I attached North Cut's failed hypothesis.

Halden's reply came three weeks later.

RUDE.

Then:

USEFUL.

That became our correspondence.

Not friendship letters.

We did not discuss weather unless weather changed load.

We did not ask about health unless injury altered work.

Occasionally one of us remembered manners.

Nessa found the letters ridiculous.

“You've known him twenty years.”

“Not twenty.”

“Close.”

“You write like hostile accountants.”

“We are efficient.”

“You sent him one sentence last month.”

“It answered.”

The sentence had been:

NO, THAT MARK IS NOT THE SAME.

Important clarity.

Halden's central position gave him a view I did not have.

District reports from north, coast, interior.

Procurement summaries.

Failure investigations.

Capital engineering studies.

Most were ordinary.

Good.

We needed ordinary denominator.

If every legacy structure in our archive came from a problem site, we would eventually conclude legacy structures caused problems because we only looked when things went wrong.

Halden started sending examples discovered during unrelated construction where old sleeves existed and nothing strange happened.

Useful.

Annoying.

Negative evidence again.

One coastal station contained nearly identical black-stone geometry to Market Seven.

No measurable load response.

Another had hooked marks beside a dead sleeve cut decades earlier with no reported consequence.

That challenged our safe-action rules.

Not completely.

Maybe dead.

Maybe local system already disconnected.

But enough that DO NOT CUT became too broad.

We revised:

DO NOT CUT ACTIVE OR UNCHARACTERIZED LEGACY CONNECTIONS WITHOUT RELATION TEST.

Less dramatic.

More useful.

Halden sent a note:

YOU ARE BECOMING BORING.

I replied:

PROMOTION.

His letters also carried institutional behavior.

That became as interesting as the Line.

One region measured pressure drift in absolute units.

Another used percentage from local baseline.

One called every old sleeve conduit.

Another called them drains.

One district recorded operator notes.

Most did not.

Some archives preserved pre-reform station books.

Others had thrown them away during standardization because new forms made old books look redundant.

No villain.

Just cleaner shelves.

Halden hated that.

“Modernization destroys evidence politely,” he wrote.

Good sentence.

I kept it.

We developed a request template for central review.

Not mandatory.

Suggested.

When a district found legacy infrastructure, send:

Location.

Elevation.

Current station function.

Physical form.

Marks.

Observed relation to load.

Whether tested.

Whether modified.

Operator observations.

Source record quality.

Photographs/sketches.

Unknowns explicitly listed.

The last line caused complaints.

People dislike writing unknown because it feels like failure.

Halden made it required for studies he controlled.

Good use of authority.

Responses came slowly.

Not a flood.

Twelve the first year.

Twenty-eight second.

Enough to make maps ugly.

Patterns appeared.

Then disappeared with more data.

Some legacy marks clustered by construction era rather than geography.

Some sleeve geometries crossed modern district boundaries.

Some black-stone blocks sat near old river systems.

Others did not.

One theory connected them to pre-kingdom urban centers.

Pellin liked it.

Then a rural site ruined it.

Good.

No answer yet.

The most important letter Halden sent that decade contained no old-Line discovery.

It contained a procurement table.

Central engineering had standardized replacement valve contracts across six regions.

Cheaper overall.

Fewer part types.

Easier training.

Reasonable.

Halden circled one column.

LEAD TIME: SPECIALIZED LEGACY-ADAPTER FITTINGS.

Before standardization: eight to twelve days locally fabricated.

After: twenty-one to thirty-five days through approved supplier.

Cheaper unit price.

Slower emergency replacement.

He wrote:

THIS IS NOT NECESSARILY BAD. TELL ME WHAT I AM MISSING.

Good question.

I asked Hema.

She became angry before finishing the page.

“They're not buying adapter blanks locally anymore.”

“Yes.”

“So local smiths stop stocking bronze mix.”

“Probably.”

“Then when central supplier fails, nobody remembers how to make the damn things quickly.”

There it was.

Second-order capacity.

Not price.

Not part.

Skill and material availability decaying because demand consolidated elsewhere.

I wrote Halden:

UNIT SAVINGS REAL. RESPONSE COST NOT IN TABLE. LOCAL FABRICATION CAPACITY WILL ATROPHY IF UNUSED. MAY BE ACCEPTABLE. SHOULD BE NAMED.

He replied:

YES.

Then three pages about contracting options to retain emergency local capacity.

That was Halden.

Survival in Record Seven had not turned him into my plot delivery system.

He had a whole career I mostly encountered as paper.

Committees.

Budgets.

Arguments I never heard.

People he trusted whom I never met.

Mistakes I only saw after correction.

Good.

He changed things without me.

I changed things without him.

Then letters connected selected pieces.

Years later people would describe us as collaborators on legacy-Line review.

True enough.

It makes the relationship sound more organized than it was.

Mostly he sent me problems.

I sent him doubt.

He sent more records.

I sent field context.

Pellin sent translations.

Tova sent measurements.

Osa sent insults disguised as technical notes.

Nessa occasionally wrote in the margin when a supposed engineering anomaly obviously followed a scheduling event.

Knowledge developed correspondence style.

Messy.

Distributed.

Nobody held the whole thing.

That became one of its strengths.

Halden's final line in one letter stayed with me:

IF WE EVER UNDERSTAND THIS COMPLETELY, CHECK WHETHER WE HAVE STOPPED LOOKING.

I accused him of becoming philosophical.

He denied it.

Reasonable.

Engineers have reputations to protect.
