The written record

I told Nessa the truth three nights after Bren left.

That is the generous version.

The less generous version is that I ran out of smaller lies.

We were at the table.

The good one.

One leg still had folded leather under it, although by then the leather had compressed enough that the table leaned slightly toward the window.

Nessa said this was character.

I said it was bad repair.

She said I was welcome to fix it when I learned not to ruin things by making them level.

We had been having that argument for almost a year.

That night there was stew between us.

Not my father's.

Nessa's.

Less carrot.

More salt.

Better, although I never told my father that.

She ate half a bowl before speaking.

“So.”

I knew which so it was.

There are many kinds.

That one had been waiting three days.

I kept eating.

Nessa watched me take another bite.

“You said you could explain some of it at home.”

“I remember.”

“Good. I was worried your mysterious old records had eaten that too.”

I put the spoon down.

She waited.

That was the problem with living with someone patient enough to know when silence was work and when it was cowardice.

Nessa could tell the difference.

Usually before I could.

“Bren was my friend,” I said.

“I got that.”

“No. He wasn't.”

Her forehead tightened.

“Useful beginning.”

“He was my friend before.”

“Before what?”

I looked at the table.

There was a burn mark near my right hand from a pan I had set down without a board two winters earlier.

Nessa had sworn about it for six months.

I remember the mark because I stared at it instead of her.

“Before this life.”

Nothing moved.

Not Nessa.

Not me.

Even the bakery below us had gone quiet between batches.

Then she said, “All right.”

That was not the response I expected.

I looked up.

She had the expression she used when somebody at work gave her a pressure reading that could not possibly be right.

Not disbelief exactly.

Preparation to find the stupid part.

“What does that mean?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

“Greg.”

“No. I mean I actually don't know.”

She sat back.

I tried again.

“I remember being nineteen once before.”

Her face did not change.

“I remember being twenty. Thirty. Forty. Fifty.”

Still nothing.

“I remember getting to fifty-nine.”

That did it.

Her eyes moved over my face like she was checking whether I had become fifty-nine while she was eating.

“Then what?”

“I woke up nineteen.”

“Where?”

“My parents' house.”

“The same house?”

“Yes.”

“You were nineteen.”

“Yes.”

“And you remembered forty years that hadn't happened.”

“Yes.”

She looked down at her stew.

Then at me.

“You understand that sounds insane.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“I've had time with it.”

“How much?”

“Eight years this time.”

She closed her eyes.

Not long.

Just enough to hurt me.

“This time.”

“Yeah.”

She stood up.

I thought she was leaving.

Instead she took both bowls to the counter, came back, and sat down again.

That was worse somehow.

If she had shouted, I could have answered shouting.

If she had laughed, I could have gotten angry.

Nessa had a talent for refusing the useful version of a problem.

“Start smaller,” she said.

“What?”

“You're asking me to fit forty years into my head because Bren walked into a station. Start with Bren.”

So I did.

Not all of it.

Not Gray Pass.

Not Ilya.

Not the healer's room.

Not his death.

Those belonged to Bren even if this Bren had never lived them.

I did not know how that worked morally.

I still don't.

I gave Nessa the part that belonged to the explanation.

First life, I took the ridge contract.

I met Bren there.

We worked together.

Kept working together.

Became friends.

Brothers, eventually, though neither of us would have used the word while sober.

I told her about the crushed ankle at North Vey.

The scar that should have been over his eye.

The rank he should not have reached yet.

Then I told her about Halden.

The bypass.

The three men who died the first time and did not die the second.

The central review.

North Vey.

The rupture that did not happen.

Nessa listened without interrupting for almost five minutes.

That may still be her personal record.

When I finished, she said, “You're saying Halden is alive because you remembered he died.”

“Partly.”

“And because he's alive, he joined a review.”

“Yes.”

“And because of that review, eighty-six people didn't die.”

“As far as I know.”

“And Bren didn't get hurt.”

“Yes.”

“But still became A-class.”

“Yes.”

She rubbed both hands over her face.

“That's annoying.”

I blinked.

“That is your problem with it?”

“No. But it is annoying.”

“Why?”

“Because if you're lying, you built the lie out of things I already know happened.”

“I know.”

“And if you're not lying, your proof keeps destroying itself.”

I stared at her.

Nessa pointed at me.

“Don't make that face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you're surprised I understand the problem.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were.”

“A little.”

“Asshole.”

There were the familiar landmarks.

I relaxed enough to breathe.

That was a mistake.

Nessa saw it.

“I'm not done.”

“Of course not.”

“East Four.”

My stomach tightened.

There it was.

“What about it?”

“The transfer.”

She leaned forward.

“You knew.”

“I remembered an accident.”

“You knew I was on the crew before I told you?”

“No.”

“But you knew someone died.”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

“Fourteen.”

Her face changed.

She knew that number.

I had said it once in the healer's ward and pretended later that I had been guessing.

Apparently lies do not disappear just because nobody pulls on them immediately.

Nessa was very good at storing loose thread.

She asked the next question quietly.

“Was I one of them?”

I wanted to lie.

That surprised me.

I had finally begun telling the truth and immediately found a part I wanted to remove.

“Yes.”

Nessa stopped moving.

The room did not.

Somebody below dropped a tray.

A cart passed in the street.

Water ticked inside one of the heating pipes.

Ordinary things continued with offensive confidence.

Nessa looked at her hands.

“In your first life.”

“Yes.”

“I died at East Four.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“I don't know.”

She looked up sharply.

“I thought you remembered.”

“I remember your name on a board. I remember the road closure. I remember what people said afterward. Transfer fault. Maintenance. Foundation shift. I wasn't there.”

“You didn't know me.”

“No.”

That landed harder than the death.

I watched it happen.

“When we met?” she asked.

“I didn't know.”

“You didn't recognize my name?”

“No. Vale was a name on a board I'd seen years before. I remembered because of the peaches.”

“The bad peach.”

“Yes.”

She stared at me.

“I hate that peach.”

“That seems fair.”

“You should have let me throw it at someone first.”

“You threw it off the roof.”

“Waste.”

Then the humor left.

Nessa's eyes narrowed.

“So the first five years.”

“What?”

“You didn't know I was supposed to die.”

“No.”

“You weren't staying near me because you knew.”

“No.”

“You didn't move in with me because you knew.”

“No.”

“I moved in with you.”

“Yes.”

“Because my landlord was a bastard.”

“Yes.”

“And you had a room full of tools.”

“Yes.”

“They did live better than most people.”

“They were organized.”

“They had blankets.”

“Oil cloth.”

“Blankets for tools.”

I almost smiled.

Nessa did not.

“Did you know this was going to happen?”

I knew what she meant.

Us.

The apartment.

The table.

Eight years of becoming obvious slowly.

“No.”

“Not even a little?”

“I never knew you.”

The answer came out too hard.

I fixed it.

“First life, I mean. You were a name I saw once. There was no us.”

Nessa looked away.

She was quiet long enough that I began preparing several bad sentences.

Then she nodded.

“Good.”

I had not expected that either.

“Good?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I was starting to wonder whether I've spent eight years walking through something you already knew.”

“No.”

She looked back at me.

“Say that again.”

“You haven't.”

“The work?”

“Changed.”

“Us?”

“New.”

“My life?”

I hesitated.

Nessa's eyes sharpened.

“Greg.”

“New enough that I don't know what's next.”

“That was not a clean answer.”

“It is the cleanest one I have.”

She considered it.

Then nodded once.

That was the first moment the conversation became usable.

Not because she believed me.

She didn't.

Not the whole thing.

I could see that.

I could also see her reorganizing eight years of me around a possibility she had not possessed an hour earlier.

Skills I could not explain.

Failures I feared before there was evidence.

The way I sometimes used past tense for things that had not happened.

The phrase first time escaping my mouth and making me angry at myself.

Bren.

The transfer.

The western contract.

S-class again.

She caught that one next.

“You said you were S-class.”

I winced.

“At the table. When the red contract came.”

“I said maybe I wanted S-class again.”

“Yes. Again.”

She pointed at me.

“I let that go because I thought you were being dramatic.”

“I am occasionally dramatic.”

“You complain when soup is too hot.”

“That is temperature, not drama.”

“You were S-class?”

“Yes.”

“How S-class?”

“There is only one kind.”

“Don't become technical now.”

I sighed.

“One of seven for part of it.”

Nessa laughed.

Not because it was funny.

The sound came out once and died.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“I don't know what you want from me.”

“You clean drains.”

“So do you.”

“I have never claimed continental supremacy while cleaning one.”

“I didn't claim supremacy.”

“One of seven.”

“Approximately.”

She stood again.

This time she paced.

Two steps to the counter.

Turn.

Three to the window.

Turn.

That apartment was too small for revelations.

Most apartments are.

She stopped near the wash line where one of her old red hair cords was tied around a cracked wooden peg.

“What happened to it?”

“What?”

“Your first life.”

I understood.

“I don't know.”

“You remember forty years but not how it ended?”

“I remember being fifty-nine. I remember the life. I remember going to sleep. I remember waking up nineteen.”

“That simple?”

“No.”

She waited.

I searched for a better answer.

“There isn't a clean seam. That's the problem. I don't remember a god. I don't remember a spell. I don't remember choosing it. I don't remember somebody explaining anything.”

“You don't know if you died.”

“No.”

“You don't know why you came back.”

“No.”

“You don't know if came back is even what happened.”

I looked at her.

“No.”

Nessa folded her arms.

“So why call it another life?”

“Because I lived it.”

That answer I did not have to search for.

She heard the difference.

I think that mattered.

She came back to the table.

“Can you prove any of this?”

“Not the whole claim.”

“Any of it?”

“Pieces.”

“Such as?”

“Halden. The bypass. North Vey. Things I knew before I should have known them.”

“You could be very good at guessing.”

“Yes.”

“You could be lying about when you knew.”

“Yes.”

“You could be insane.”

“Yes.”

“You agree too easily.”

“I've considered the options.”

She tapped one finger against the table.

“What about the old chamber?”

“I knew something could be under us because I had seen older Line work later in the first life. I did not know East Four had a chamber.”

“You almost killed us because you guessed wrong.”

“Yes.”

“And then found it.”

“Yes.”

She tapped again.

“So memory isn't proof.”

“No.”

“And memory isn't always right.”

“The memory can be right and my explanation can still be wrong.”

That stopped her finger.

I kept going because I finally knew what I was trying to say.

“I remembered the East Four disaster correctly. People died. Your name was on the board. I thought I understood enough to stop it. I didn't. I dropped the wrong feed and nearly made it worse.”

Nessa's mouth tightened.

“I remember.”

“Knowing the outcome wasn't knowing the mechanism.”

“And now the outcome changes anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Because you touched it.”

“Sometimes.”

“Because Halden touched it.”

“Yes.”

“Because everyone keeps living after you interfere.”

I looked at her.

There it was.

The part I had been taking too personally.

“Yes.”

Nessa leaned back.

“Well.”

I waited.

She looked at the window.

At the table.

At me.

“I don't believe you came back from another life.”

That hurt less than I expected.

Maybe because she said it plainly.

“Okay.”

“I don't mean I think you're lying.”

I said nothing.

“I believe you remember it.”

The sentence landed carefully.

She was not giving me more than she had.

That was Nessa.

“I believe you remember being fifty-nine. I believe Bren scared the shit out of you because you remembered loving a man who looked at you like a stranger. I believe you've known things you shouldn't know. I've watched that part happen.”

She pointed toward East Four, somewhere beyond the wall.

“I don't know why.”

“Neither do I.”

“Good. Then don't hand me a mystery and demand I call it the same thing you call it.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were about to.”

“I was not.”

“You had a face.”

Apparently my face had spent eight years betraying me.

I would have preferred a less observant partner.

Probably not for long.

Nessa looked down at the burn mark in the table.

Then she said, “I'm angry with you.”

“I know.”

“No, you don't.”

“Fair.”

“Eight years.”

I did not answer.

“You let me live with a man who had forty years in his head and acted like the weird part was that he knew how to reseat housings.”

“That wasn't the only weird part.”

“Not helping.”

“Sorry.”

“You knew I died.”

“Five years in.”

“Still not helping.”

“Sorry.”

“You used old records every time I got close.”

“Yes.”

“You let me think you were hiding Guild work.”

“I was also hiding Guild work.”

She stared at me.

“Remarkable.”

“I contain layers.”

“Mostly shit.”

“That too.”

The corner of her mouth moved.

Not forgiveness.

Better than nothing.

Then she became serious again.

“I need a rule.”

That was not what I expected.

“A rule.”

“Yes.”

“For what?”

“For living with this.”

She gestured at my head.

“All of that.”

I waited.

“If some memory changes a choice that touches me, you tell me.”

My first instinct was no.

Not because the rule was unreasonable.

Because secrets become habits, and habits defend themselves.

Nessa saw the hesitation.

“Not every nightmare. Not every person you remember. I'm not asking you to empty forty years onto the floor.”

“That would take a while.”

“Greg.”

“Sorry.”

“If you think I'm in danger because of something you remember, I get to know why.”

I thought about the roof.

The peaches.

Change your shift.

Old records.

“Yes.”

“If you make a decision about our life because you're comparing it to the other one, I get to know that too.”

That one was harder.

Nessa noticed.

“Especially that one.”

I looked at the table.

The red contract had sat there.

So had a hundred smaller choices neither of us had named.

“Okay.”

“No mysterious bullshit?”

“Reduced mysterious bullshit.”

She considered.

“I'll accept reduced.”

“Generous.”

“I can revise the agreement.”

“Reduced is good.”

She nodded.

Then, after a moment, “Does anyone else know?”

“No.”

“Your parents?”

“No.”

“Dera?”

“No.”

“Halden?”

“No.”

“Bren?”

“No.”

She absorbed that.

I did not tell her about Ithar.

There was no Ithar yet.

Not in this life.

Only a name I had carried across years from a conversation that had not happened here.

That secret belonged to a different door.

Nessa looked almost annoyed by being first.

“Why me?”

There are moments a person should answer beautifully.

I have failed most of them.

“Because you wouldn't let it go.”

Her eyes narrowed.

I added, “And because I wanted you to know.”

“Better.”

“It was both.”

“I know.”

She stood and took the bowls to the wash basin again.

This time she actually washed them.

I sat at the table listening to water run.

My body felt strange.

Light was too generous a word.

Exposed was closer.

For eight years I had been the only person in the second life who knew why some days felt repeated and others felt impossible.

Now there were two people in the apartment who knew what I meant when I said before.

Not the same thing as being believed.

Better, maybe.

Nessa set the bowls upside down to dry.

Then she looked at me over her shoulder.

“One more question.”

“Okay.”

“Was your table better in the first life?”

I stared at her.

“What?”

“You were apparently one of seven terrifying men on the continent. I want to know if continental supremacy bought a level table.”

“I don't remember.”

She smiled.

“Then this one wins.”

“That is not how evidence works.”

“Your whole story is about how evidence works.”

“Poorly, apparently.”

She came back and put both hands on the table.

It rocked toward the window.

Once.

Small movement.

Familiar.

“Don't fix it,” she said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“That is a lie.”

“Yes.”

“Reduced mysterious bullshit.”

“Does table repair count?”

“It does now.”

That was the first rule Nessa made for my impossible memory.

The second was about the table.

I obeyed one of them.
