The written record

The first person legally allowed to make decisions about Nessa's body was Dera.

That bothered me more than the accident.

Not at first.

At first the accident did a perfectly good job of bothering me by itself.

It happened in the ninth year at East Four.

Nessa had taken senior transfer qualification the spring before and had started covering two stations besides ours when their regular pressure hands were short.

That was how she ended up at South Six on a Thursday I was supposed to have off.

I did not have it off.

Halden had learned the dangerous lesson that I answered messages.

So I was three districts away looking at a wall somebody had labeled NONSTRUCTURAL with enough confidence to make me suspicious.

The wall was structural.

Obviously.

I was arguing about that when a Guild runner found me.

“Greg Vale?”

“No.”

He looked at the slate.

“Gregory?”

“Unfortunately.”

“East Four Ranker?”

“Yes.”

“Message from South Six.”

The room changed before he finished.

That is memory talking.

Maybe it did not.

Maybe I changed.

Same result.

I took the slate.

TRANSFER INCIDENT.

THREE INJURED.

NESSA VALE TO DISTRICT HEALER.

CONSCIOUS AT REMOVAL.

That last line had been added by somebody who understood what the other three would do to a person.

I appreciated them without knowing who they were.

“Was she walking?”

The runner blinked.

“I don't know.”

“Talking?”

“I don't know.”

“Bleeding?”

“I don't know.”

He looked increasingly guilty for not having witnessed an accident that happened before he received the message.

I stopped asking him questions he could not answer.

That was growth.

Slow growth.

I left Halden with the structural wall.

He called after me, “I'll handle this.”

“You were already handling it.”

“Badly, apparently.”

“Yes.”

“Go.”

I went.

South Six was not East Four.

That sounds obvious until you have worked one station for nine years and discover your body expects every station to put stairs, doors, shutoffs, and bad smells in the same places.

South Six's healer room was one level above street instead of below it.

I went down first.

Lost thirty seconds.

Swore.

Went back up.

A clerk behind the healer counter looked at me.

“Nessa Vale.”

“Relation?”

“Greg.”

“That is a name.”

“I know.”

“Relation?”

“We live together.”

The clerk waited.

Apparently that was not one of the approved words.

“Household,” I said.

“Registered?”

“No.”

“Kin?”

“No.”

“Guild voice?”

“No.”

That was the first time I heard the term.

I hated it immediately.

“Can I see her?”

“She's being treated.”

“What happened?”

“I can tell kin, foreman, or registered voice.”

“I am the station Ranker at East Four.”

“Congratulations.”

The clerk did not sound impressed.

I was not used to that helping my mood.

“It was a Line incident.”

“At South Six.”

“Yes.”

“You are East Four.”

“Yes.”

“Then you are not her foreman.”

“No.”

“Kin?”

“No.”

“Registered voice?”

“No.”

I leaned both hands on the counter.

The clerk looked at them.

“Please don't make me call the hall guard.”

“I am the hall guard in three districts.”

“No, you are a C-class Ranker standing in my intake room.”

That was irritatingly precise.

I took my hands off the counter.

“What is a registered voice?”

The clerk's expression softened by half a degree.

“Someone a Guild worker names to speak if they can't. Treatment choices. Release of tools. Wages if they're laid up. Who gets told first. That sort of thing.”

“Why don't I know about this?”

“Do you read the back of your renewal slate?”

“No.”

“That's probably why.”

I hated competent clerks.

They made anger inefficient.

A door opened behind her.

Dera came through.

Her left sleeve had blood on it.

Not hers.

I knew that immediately because Dera would have been annoyed enough to mention if it was hers.

“What happened?”

“Transfer bar kicked.”

“Nessa?”

“Shoulder. Head. Maybe ribs.”

“Conscious?”

“Not now.”

Everything inside me went still.

Dera noticed.

“She's breathing.”

I nodded.

“What are they doing?”

“Setting the shoulder first. Healer wants to put her under deeper because she keeps fighting them when she wakes.”

“Then do it.”

Dera looked at me.

I knew before she spoke.

That made it worse.

“I did.”

The words landed wrong.

“You did.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I'm her acting foreman on the incident slate and she doesn't have a Guild voice registered.”

There it was.

Dera.

Not me.

Dera had worked beside Nessa for nine years.

She was good.

She cared about her.

She was exactly the person I would have trusted if I had not been there.

I was there.

That changed nothing.

“You made the treatment decision.”

“Yes.”

“Was there risk?”

“There is always risk.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No. It is the answer you get until the healer comes out.”

I looked toward the closed door.

Dera's voice softened.

“She'd have chosen the same.”

“I know.”

That was not the problem.

Dera saw that too.

“Ah.”

“Don't ah me.”

“You aren't registered.”

“I gathered.”

“You two ever talk about anything before it becomes a problem?”

“We have a ledger.”

Dera stared.

I realized how weak that sounded.

“Very romantic.”

“Fuck you.”

“There he is.”

We waited.

Waiting beside a healer's door is one of the few jobs where experience makes you worse.

First-life Greg had done it too many times.

Field teams.

Bren.

People I loved.

People I barely knew but had carried far enough that their blood dried on my coat.

You learn the noises.

Which ones mean urgency.

Which ones mean somebody dropped metal.

Which silence is normal.

Which silence is not.

My body knew all of them.

South Six's hallway did not.

Different doors.

Different floor.

Different life.

I stood anyway.

The healer came out after twenty minutes.

Young man.

Maybe thirty.

He looked tired and irritated.

Good combination.

People who are pleased with themselves in healer rooms make me nervous.

“She'll be fine.”

Dera exhaled.

I did not realize she had been holding it too.

“Concussion. Shoulder dislocation. Two cracked ribs. No internal bleed we can find.”

“Can I see her?”

He looked at the clerk.

The clerk looked at me.

“Household visitor is fine now,” she said.

Now.

I disliked the word.

Nessa was asleep when I went in.

Her right shoulder was wrapped against her body.

There was dried blood in her hair near the temple.

I sat in the chair beside the bed.

Then I stood.

Then sat again.

The chair was bad.

That mattered because I needed something to hate that was not the idea that a clerk had asked me for the correct word and I had not possessed it.

Nessa woke an hour later.

Her eyes opened halfway.

She looked at me.

Then at the room.

Then back at me.

“You look terrible.”

“Thank you.”

“Did I die?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She closed her eyes.

I waited.

They opened again.

“What happened?”

“Transfer bar kicked.”

“I know that part.”

“You hit the rail.”

“I know that part too.”

“Then why are you asking me?”

“To see if you know anything useful.”

“Dera authorized sedation.”

Nessa went still.

Not fear.

Confusion.

“Dera?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“You don't have a Guild voice registered.”

She stared at me.

“You weren't allowed?”

“No.”

That woke her better than the healer had.

“What?”

“Apparently living together for years does not create institutional authority by smell.”

“Did you try telling them you own the table?”

“I considered it.”

She smiled.

Then winced because smiling moved something that hurt.

I waited until the pain passed.

“They were right,” she said.

I looked at her.

“That is not the reaction I wanted.”

“They don't know us.”

“They know Dera?”

“They know her role.”

I hated that she understood it immediately.

“So you're fine with Dera deciding whether they put you under?”

“Yes.”

I stopped.

Nessa watched me.

Then added, “This time.”

There it was.

I sat back.

“Would you have chosen it?”

“Yes.”

“Then what's the problem?”

Nessa's eyes narrowed.

“You tell me.”

“I asked first.”

“You're angry enough that your left hand has been opening and closing for five minutes.”

I looked down.

It had.

Old habit.

Not old body.

“I'm not angry at Dera.”

“I know.”

“Or the clerk.”

“I know.”

“I don't like that you were unconscious and I was a visitor.”

Nessa was quiet.

That was it.

Not ownership.

Not control.

Not even the treatment.

Visitor.

A person could share rent, food, bed, years, parents, medicine, bad tea, broken furniture, and the invisible accumulated logistics of a life.

Then one bar kicked sideways and the institution asked for a word.

I did not have one.

Nessa looked at the ceiling.

“Who is yours?”

“What?”

“Guild voice.”

“I don't have one.”

“Of course you don't.”

“I didn't know it existed.”

“You also don't read renewal slates.”

“The important part is on the front.”

“The back apparently controls whether I can stop a healer from sawing something off you.”

“They don't saw things off that casually.”

“Greg.”

“Fine.”

She looked at me again.

“If you were unconscious today, who decides?”

“Dera if I was working.”

“And if you weren't?”

“My mother, probably.”

“Probably.”

“I don't know.”

Nessa closed her eyes.

“Wonderful.”

“You like my mother.”

“I do.”

“Then what's the problem?”

She opened her eyes.

“I live with you.”

That was all.

Simple sentence.

Annoying weight.

I understood immediately.

I had spent the last hour furious because somebody else could speak for her.

I had never checked who could speak for me.

Nessa watched the understanding arrive.

“Yeah,” she said.

“Fine.”

“That easy?”

“No. I am choosing not to argue because I would lose.”

“Growth.”

“Do not enjoy it.”

She slept again.

The next morning Dera brought two renewal slates and a pencil.

I looked at her.

“You carry those around?”

“No. I sent Lorn to Guild Hall.”

“Why?”

“Because listening to you two discover paperwork one emergency at a time will kill me.”

Nessa laughed.

Then held her ribs and swore.

Dera put the slates on the blanket.

The back was exactly where the clerk said.

WORKER VOICE.

One name.

One Guild registry number if the person had one.

If not, home district and witness mark.

The language around it was old.

Older than the current forms.

Not romantic.

Not legal marriage.

Not inheritance.

A work institution built by people who got crushed, burned, poisoned, buried, or knocked unconscious often enough to decide someone should be able to answer when they could not.

I respected it more once I understood that.

Nessa took the pencil.

She wrote my name.

GREGORY.

I stared.

“You know I hate that.”

“Registry name.”

“You enjoyed it.”

“Both can be true.”

She wrote my Guild number from memory.

I did not know hers from memory.

That felt like an accusation even though she had not made one.

“What's yours?”

She told me.

I wrote NESSA VALE.

Then her number.

My handwriting was worse than hers.

That also felt unfair.

Dera signed as witness.

“Congratulations,” she said.

“For what?”

“You have completed the back of a form.”

“Fuck you.”

“Deeply moving.”

Nessa handed her slate over.

Dera took mine too.

Then she paused.

“There is another line.”

I looked.

SEND FIRST NOTICE TO.

Mine still listed my parents' house.

Nessa's listed a woman named Mara Vale.

I pointed.

“Who's Mara?”

“My aunt.”

“I thought your aunt was Sela.”

“She is.”

“Then who is Mara?”

“My other aunt.”

“You have another aunt?”

Nessa looked at me with genuine offense.

“We have lived together for years.”

“Yes.”

“And you do not know I have two aunts.”

“You have a lot of family.”

“So do you.”

“I know mine.”

“Remarkable achievement.”

Dera cleared her throat.

We looked back at the forms.

Nessa scratched Mara out.

Then stopped.

She did not write me immediately.

That mattered.

This was not a ceremony.

She thought.

Mara had raised her for part of her childhood.

I learned that sentence right there in the healer room.

Not earlier.

Right there.

Nessa told me because the blank line asked.

“She took me when my mother was sick,” she said.

“For how long?”

“Almost three years.”

I had known Nessa for nine years.

Loved her for some unclear fraction of that.

Lived with her for several.

There were still entire rooms in her life I had never entered.

That should have been obvious too.

Life One had made me arrogant about memory.

Not just future memory.

People memory.

I remembered Bren so completely that I sometimes confused knowing one person deeply with understanding how knowledge of a person worked.

Nessa was not a story I had already finished.

She was sitting in front of me with a cracked rib and an aunt I had never heard of.

“You should keep her,” I said.

Nessa looked at me.

“You sure?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because she matters.”

“So do you.”

“I know.”

That came out smug.

She threw the pencil at me with her good arm.

It missed by a lot.

Concussion.

I retrieved it.

Then I crossed my parents' house off my first-notice line.

Nessa watched.

I wrote her name.

She looked at the slate.

“You don't have to do that because I did the other one.”

“I know.”

“Your mother will be offended.”

“She'll be offended if the Guild runner reaches you first?”

“Yes.”

“Probably.”

“Your father?”

“He'll pretend not to be.”

“Worse.”

“Yes.”

I left it anyway.

Not because Nessa had become more important than my parents.

People hate ranking love because ranking love is usually stupid.

She was the person who knew whether I slept.

Whether my shoulder had been bad that week.

What healer I trusted.

Which old injuries mattered.

Whether the face I made meant pain or stubbornness.

She knew where my boots were.

There are institutions built on grand words.

East Four taught me to respect the ones built on practical ones.

Who should we send for first?

Nessa.

That was the answer.

Dera took the forms away.

Two days later Nessa came home with her arm strapped tight and orders not to lift anything heavier than a kettle.

She immediately tried to move the table.

I caught the good side.

“What are you doing?”

“It is leaning more.”

“You told me not to fix it.”

“I am revising the agreement.”

“You cannot revise the table agreement while concussed.”

“You are my Guild voice now.”

“That gives me authority when you cannot speak.”

“I can barely speak.”

“You have been speaking continuously.”

“Then listen. Lift.”

I lifted.

She pushed new leather under the short leg with one foot.

The table settled.

Not level.

Closer.

She looked at it.

“Good.”

I rocked it once.

Still moved.

“Bad.”

“Character.”

“You nearly died.”

“I cracked two ribs.”

“You were unconscious.”

“I got better.”

I wanted to argue.

Instead I looked at the table.

Then at her.

There are commitments you announce.

There are commitments a clerk discovers because somebody gets hurt on a Thursday.

We had not named ours yet.

The Guild had.

Worker voice.

Ugly phrase.

Useful one.

Years later, when the choices became worse than sedation and cracked ribs, that little line on the back of a renewal slate would seem almost childish.

At twenty-eight, it was enough.

Someone had asked who could speak when I could not.

For the first time in either life, I had an answer.
