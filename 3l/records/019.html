The written record

The fairest schedule I ever wrote lasted eleven minutes.

That was generous.

It was probably dead by minute four.

East Two finally had to come apart.

Not the whole station.

The east market branch.

The old bronze structures beneath it had moved enough that even I stopped pretending restricted operation was a long-term plan.

Tova did not say I told you so.

That was almost worse.

District engineering designed a temporary bypass that could carry about sixty percent of normal winter demand while crews opened the foundation and rebuilt the branch supports.

Sixty percent is an excellent number until one hundred percent of people want heat.

Halden called me into a planning room with Tova, Pell, two market representatives, and a city service clerk.

Nessa was not there.

That mattered later.

The clerk rolled out a district map.

Forty-three buildings.

Same branch.

Same problem.

“We rotate,” Pell said.

“Obviously,” I said.

Tova looked at me.

“Nothing is obvious before the schedule exists.”

“Fine. We probably rotate.”

“Better.”

The temporary bypass could hold the clinic continuously if we reduced every other connection.

School kitchen needed morning service.

Market stalls wanted midday wash heat.

Apartment blocks wanted mornings and evenings.

Bakeries wanted before dawn.

Public bath wanted all day because public baths always want all day.

We started with equal hours.

It felt clean.

Six service groups.

Four hours each.

Staggered peaks.

Every ordinary building received the same total daily access.

The clinic sat outside the rotation because healers had better arguments than shopkeepers.

The school kitchen got a protected morning window and lost evening service to balance it.

I looked at the finished page.

“Fair.”

Tova said nothing.

Halden said nothing.

The market representative, a butcher named Orr, said, “No.”

I looked at him.

“What?”

“My cold room gets heat from the exchanger.”

“Cold room gets heat.”

“Yes.”

“That sentence is upsetting.”

“Absorption unit.”

“I know how it works.”

“Then stop looking upset.”

“If you take my midday window, I lose the cool cycle when deliveries arrive.”

“You have evening.”

“I do not receive meat in the evening.”

The other representative, a woman from the laundry guild, pointed at Group Four.

“You put washhouses off between second and sixth bell.”

“Yes.”

“That is when we work.”

“You have six to ten.”

“That is when people collect.”

“You can change hours for nine days.”

She stared at me.

There are sentences that reveal you have never run a washhouse.

Apparently I had found one.

The schedule died.

Minute eleven at the latest.

We tried again.

Use-based windows.

Bakeries early.

Markets midday.

Apartments morning and evening.

Laundry daytime.

Bath reduced.

Clinic continuous.

School morning.

That looked better.

Then Nessa came home.

I showed her the draft over dinner.

She read the first page.

Then the second.

Then went back to the first.

“Who did you ask?”

“Market representatives.”

“Who?”

I told her.

“Apartment tenants?”

“Building owners have the notice.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

I looked at the schedule.

“The apartment windows are morning and evening.”

“Which morning?”

“First to fourth bell.”

“And evening?”

“Seventh to tenth.”

“Night crews sleep when?”

I stopped.

Nessa worked transfers.

Of course.

East Four had night crews.

So did bakeries.

Markets.

Tram.

Healers.

People who cleaned all of those places after everyone else left.

“Our fair schedule freezes people who sleep during the day,” I said.

“Not freezes.”

“Gets cold.”

“Yes.”

“That is less dramatic.”

“Try accuracy.”

I hated that phrase because Tova used it too.

Nessa kept reading.

“What about hot water?”

“Same feed.”

“So the apartment evening window is when everyone cooks, washes, and heats rooms.”

“Yes.”

“Pressure will drop inside the group.”

“The bypass model accounts for simultaneous use.”

“From what?”

“Building demand records.”

Nessa looked at me.

I knew that look.

“What?”

“How old?”

I checked.

Last full survey.

Nine years.

Some updates.

Not all.

A few buildings had changed use.

One apartment block had added twelve rooms in the attic.

A printer occupied what used to be a storage floor.

Mina's bakery had expanded the back ovens.

The bathhouse had added a laundry room.

Reality again.

Refusing four kinds of ink.

“We need current draw,” I said.

Nessa took a bite of stew.

“Yes.”

“You could have said that first.”

“You were proud of your schedule.”

“That was cruel.”

“It was eleven minutes?”

“Approximately.”

She smiled.

The next day we stopped scheduling and started asking.

That was slower.

Halden hated it.

Not philosophically.

The repair start was twelve days away.

“We cannot interview forty-three buildings,” he said.

“Why not?”

“Twelve days.”

“Tova and I can split.”

“You have inspections.”

“Move them.”

“Private contracts.”

“Move them.”

“East Four?”

“I barely have shifts.”

He looked at me.

“You understand you are proving that time is also an allocated resource.”

“Yes.”

“Good. I wanted you to hear it.”

Asshole.

We did not interview forty-three buildings.

Halden was right.

We grouped them.

Residential.

Food.

Health.

Wash.

Retail.

Workshops.

Then we asked a few from each group and checked current meters where records looked old.

That was how I met people who had technically been on my map for years.

Mina, properly this time.

She needed strong service from third to sixth bell.

Not first.

Dough proofed before the ovens peaked.

I had lived upstairs and still guessed wrong.

Senn represented three apartment buildings and arrived with the exact occupancy count because the hearing had apparently turned him into a document enthusiast.

“Forty-eight households,” he said.

“You told district forty-one last year.”

“Three attic rooms became six. Two units were divided.”

“Legal?”

Senn looked at me.

“Relevant?”

Tova said, “To load, yes.”

He sighed.

“Mostly.”

That became someone else's paperwork.

The clinic needed less than we thought.

Continuous service, yes.

But only a protected baseline.

Their coal boiler could carry wash water during two daily Line reductions.

The healer in charge volunteered the cuts because she had read the same district notice as everyone else.

“We are not made of glass,” she said.

The public bath could close two days completely and reduce the rest.

The owner hated that.

His head washer did not.

“We need to descale the north pools anyway.”

The owner turned toward him.

“We what?”

The meeting became internal.

We left.

The school kitchen needed morning heat but could cook one meal on stored thermal stones if necessary.

That reduced their protected window.

The butcher needed midday cooling.

The fish market needed wash water after closing, not during sales.

The laundry guild needed staggered days more than daily hours because half-heated vats were worse than closed vats.

The tailor from my second private job needed almost nothing.

“I can work cold,” she said.

“Comfortable?”

“No.”

“Then why give it up?”

“Because I would rather be cold than have damp cloth from the laundry next door freeze overnight.”

People understood interdependence better when it had a face.

The apartment buildings were harder.

Everyone wanted roughly the same things.

Warm rooms.

Hot water.

Cooking.

Different times.

We held one evening meeting in the public bath's dry hall.

Too many people came.

Of course.

People will ignore infrastructure until you announce less of it.

Then everyone becomes an engineer.

One man wanted continuous service because he paid taxes.

A woman beside him asked when he had last paid them.

That argument had history.

Not ours.

A night tram conductor said daytime heat mattered because he slept from third to eighth bell.

A mother with two small children wanted afternoon because baths before evening kept bedtime survivable.

An elderly man wanted morning because his joints were worst then.

A fish porter wanted hot water after ninth bell because that was when he came home smelling like fish.

Nobody's reason was silly.

That was the problem.

We could not give everyone the hour that made their life easiest.

A young woman from Senn's rear building stood near the wall holding a baby.

She had not spoken.

I asked, “What window do you need?”

She looked surprised.

Then suspicious.

“Same as anyone.”

“That is not helping.”

A few people laughed.

She adjusted the child.

“Afternoon.”

“Why?”

“Room is north side.”

That was all.

I knew Senn's buildings.

Rope Street.

Narrow rear rooms.

Little sun.

Damp after rain.

Afternoon outage would hit those rooms harder than south-facing rooms in the same group.

Our schedule did not know which direction windows faced.

It barely knew windows existed.

I wrote NORTH REAR beside the building number.

The woman saw.

“You're changing it?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Then why write it?”

“So I don't get to say I didn't know.”

That quieted her.

It quieted me too.

There are records that solve problems.

There are records that prevent innocence.

Both matter.

The final schedule was not fair.

I stopped using the word.

It was defensible.

That was different.

Clinic baseline continuous with two voluntary reductions.

School protected mornings.

Bakeries strong pre-dawn windows.

Food markets split between delivery cooling and closing wash.

Laundries received full-power alternating days.

Public bath closed two days and ran reduced the others.

Workshops grouped by actual process need instead of trade name.

Apartments received two heat windows daily, but the timing rotated by building exposure and resident work patterns.

North-facing blocks got longer afternoon service during the coldest forecast days.

Night-worker-heavy buildings got one daytime window every other day.

That made somebody else lose one.

Every improvement moved discomfort.

There was no schedule where discomfort disappeared.

Tova read the final version.

“You still like allocation?”

“I never liked allocation.”

“You liked the first schedule.”

“For eleven minutes.”

“Nine.”

“You were counting?”

“Yes.”

Of course she was.

The repair started.

The bypass held.

Mostly.

Day two, a bakery exceeded its window because a batch was late.

That delayed pressure recovery to two apartment blocks.

Day three, the bathhouse owner opened one pool without authorization.

Pell shut it again.

There was shouting.

Day four, the clinic declined one scheduled reduction because an emergency surgery ran long.

Nobody argued.

Day five, a tram transformer fault elsewhere forced district load changes and ruined half our beautiful schedule.

We rewrote the afternoon by hand.

Nessa came down after shift and helped because transfer scheduling was literally her job and we had been idiots not to ask her earlier.

She did in forty minutes what took us two hours the first day.

Tova watched her work.

“Can we hire you?”

Nessa did not look up.

“No.”

“Why?”

“I already have a job.”

Greg's wife would have been an easy role for the story to give her.

She was not my wife then.

Maybe that sentence is doing too much work.

The important part is she was not an attachment to my inspection career.

She was a senior pressure hand who understood live scheduling better than I did and had her own station waiting for her the next morning.

She helped because the district needed help.

Then she went home before me.

That mattered.

Day six, the rear rooms on Rope Street stayed warmer than predicted because Senn distributed heavy curtains from an empty property.

I did not know whether that was generosity, self-interest, or fear of complaints.

Probably all three.

Useful anyway.

Day seven, the butcher lost cooling for forty minutes and discarded meat rather than sell it.

He complained while doing the correct thing.

Also useful.

Day eight, one north apartment pipe froze in a wall despite the extended window.

We had not eliminated harm.

Just moved and reduced it.

Day nine, East Two's temporary bypass came down.

New branch support held.

Full service returned by evening.

People cheered at the market when the indicators climbed.

Not for us.

For hot water.

Correct priority.

District engineering published a repair report afterward.

Technical findings.

Costs.

Load performance.

Schedule deviations.

Halden added an appendix called OCCUPANCY EFFECTS.

That was new.

Not because I invented the idea.

Because dozens of people had spent nine days explaining how infrastructure entered their actual lives and somebody finally gave those explanations a place in the report.

The appendix was messy.

Night workers.

Building exposure.

Alternate fuel.

Business timing.

Food spoilage.

Temporary closures.

One frozen pipe.

One tray of cold soup thrown at no one this time.

We included complaints too.

Especially complaints.

Years later I would see much larger allocation tables.

Regional load.

Water.

Food.

Transport.

People with clean maps deciding where shortage should go.

Clean maps are dangerous when they make the mess disappear.

At thirty, I learned that from forty-three buildings.

The Line did not fail during East Two's repair.

We rationed it.

That is different.

Unless you are the room that stays cold.
