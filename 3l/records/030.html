The written record

East Four started coughing after I stopped working there.

Dera called it a cough.

Halden called it cyclic return instability.

Nessa called both descriptions annoying.

I called it impossible before I heard it.

That was efficient stupidity.

The station had been losing return pressure for three seconds at a time.

Not enough to shut anything down.

Enough to trip two alarm bells and make the upper feed regulator hunt for balance.

Every forty minutes.

Approximately.

Not exact.

Nothing useful is exact when you first meet it.

East Four's crew had checked the obvious things.

Regulator seat.

Clean.

Return valve.

Good.

Market branch draw.

Normal.

Buried chamber monitoring.

Stable.

No new foundation movement.

No old bronze pressure response outside known range.

The cough remained.

Dera sent me a note.

COME LISTEN.

That was the whole note.

No title.

No courtesy.

Good to know some institutions remained stable.

I went after district work.

East Four looked the same from the street.

Warehouse pretending importance.

Tram above.

Brick dark from years of weather.

Inside, everything had changed slightly enough to be rude.

Different tool hooks.

New pressure desk lamp.

My old locker still there.

Another worker's gloves on top.

Nessa's red cord still tied to a peg inside hers even though she barely worked station shifts now.

Dera stood near the upper manifold.

“Late.”

“You did not give a time.”

“I expected competence.”

“Then why call me?”

She smiled once.

There she was.

The current station Ranker was a woman named Seli Dorn.

B-class.

Stronger than I was then by a useful margin.

Younger by six years.

She had joined East Four after I moved to district hours and apparently resented the fact that people still referred to certain repairs as Greg's way.

I liked her immediately for that.

“Inspector,” she said.

“Ranker.”

Dera said, “If both of you start measuring titles, I will drown you in the lower intake.”

We stopped.

The cough happened fourteen minutes later.

I heard it before the gauge moved.

A hollow double knock somewhere behind the return manifold.

Then the Line hum dipped.

Not much.

Three seconds.

Regulator wheel twitched.

Pressure returned.

Alarm bell clicked once but did not fully ring.

I frowned.

Seli said, “There.”

“Yes.”

“Useful.”

“Fuck you.”

She smiled.

Dera handed me the log.

Twenty-one events in two days.

Intervals from thirty-three minutes to fifty-two.

Not periodic enough for an automatic control cycle.

Too regular for random demand.

Most happened during daylight.

Two at night.

Temperature unrelated.

Market draw unrelated according to current meters.

Rain unrelated.

The old me would have started with the buried chamber.

The slightly less old me did anyway.

I went down.

Checked the access plate.

Old load marks.

Reference chalk.

Temperature tabs.

Nothing.

Seli followed.

“Thought district said chamber stable.”

“It is.”

“Then why are we here?”

“Because stable things can change.”

“Useful philosophy.”

“Do you want help?”

“Not particularly.”

Good.

We measured.

Listened.

Waited through one cough.

No corresponding movement below.

Not old Line.

Probably.

I hated probability less by then.

We went back up.

A mechanic named Jori Pell was lying under the return assembly.

Not related to Inspector Pell as far as anyone knew.

The city reused names irresponsibly.

Jori was fifty-eight and had worked rotating equipment longer than I had been alive in the second life.

He held a metal rod against the housing with the other end touching his ear.

I had seen mechanics do that in my first life.

I had also seen them get mocked by young Rankers with expensive sensing gear.

The mechanics usually kept working after the sensing gear broke.

“What do you hear?” I asked.

Jori did not move.

“Nothing yet.”

“That is encouraging.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because it means I have to stay here.”

Fair.

We waited.

Maintenance contains a lot of waiting beside objects that refuse to perform when observed.

Half an hour.

No cough.

Seli checked the market board.

Normal.

Dera did paperwork.

I inspected the regulator again because doing nothing offended me.

Jori remained under the housing.

Then the cough came.

Knock.

Knock.

Dip.

Jori sat up so fast he hit his head.

“Fuck.”

“What?”

“Bearing.”

I looked at the return assembly.

“Bearing reads clean.”

“Not that one.”

He pointed upward.

At the tram viaduct.

I stared.

“The tram.”

“Maybe.”

Dera came over.

“Explain.”

Jori held out the listening rod.

“Second knock comes through the housing after the first. Not pressure hammer. Mechanical.”

“From above?”

“Feels like.”

I listened.

Nothing.

Of course.

Seli looked toward the ceiling.

“A tram passes every few minutes.”

“Not every tram,” Jori said.

Dera pulled the street schedule.

East freight line crossed the viaduct twice an hour during day.

Less at night.

Heavy cars.

Different axle spacing.

Intervals roughly matched.

Not exactly because freight ran late.

I looked at the log.

Thirty-three.

Forty-one.

Fifty-two.

Two night events on late freight runs.

There it was.

Maybe.

We went outside for the next freight.

Rain had stopped but the street still smelled wet.

Jori crouched beside the station wall with his rod against a support plate.

I felt ridiculous.

Good sign.

The freight tram came.

Heavy cars over the viaduct.

First axle.

Nothing.

Second.

Nothing.

Third car crossed.

Knock.

Inside the station, alarm clicked.

Dera swore.

Jori smiled.

“Bearing.”

“Which bearing?” I asked.

He pointed to the viaduct support directly above the station return anchor.

Not Line bearing.

Tram bearing.

Two systems sharing masonry.

Modern tram support had been replaced four years earlier.

The new bearing plate sat slightly differently from the old one.

Under heavy freight, the viaduct shifted a fraction.

That load traveled through the brick support.

The station return anchor moved with it.

Not enough to damage the pipe.

Enough to momentarily bind the regulator linkage mounted beside it.

Three seconds.

Release.

Cough.

I stood under the viaduct feeling stupid.

Seli looked delighted.

“Old Line?”

“No.”

“Buried chamber?”

“No.”

“Ancient mystery?”

“Fuck you.”

Jori said, “Could still be ancient.”

I looked at him.

He smiled.

Everyone had become unbearable.

Finding the cause did not fix it.

That took three days.

Tram authority said the bearing was within tolerance.

They were right.

District Line said the return anchor was within tolerance.

Also right.

Two acceptable movements combined into one unacceptable interaction.

I had seen that pattern before.

Not the specific fault.

The institutional version.

No single part failed.

The boundary between them did.

That sentence tried to become profound.

We refused it.

We needed a bracket.

Jori designed the first one.

Simple slotted mount that let the regulator linkage float slightly relative to the masonry movement.

I disliked it.

“Too much play.”

“How much?”

“Enough.”

“That is not a measurement.”

Tova had infected everyone.

We measured.

I was wrong.

The play was small.

Seli wanted a stiffer anchor instead.

Jori said stiffness would transfer tram movement into the return housing.

She disagreed.

They built both test setups on a spare bench.

Jori's worked better.

Seli accepted it without ceremony.

Good Ranker.

We installed the slotted mount during a one-hour off-peak reduction.

Dera made me hold the lamp.

“I can fit the bracket.”

“Jori designed it.”

“I understand brackets.”

“Hold the lamp.”

“I used to work here.”

“Yes.”

“That is my point.”

“No. That is mine.”

I held the lamp.

Jori fitted.

Seli handled pressure isolation.

A younger station hand tightened the final bolts.

Dera watched all of us.

Then the freight tram came.

Heavy cars.

Viaduct moved.

No knock.

Regulator steady.

No cough.

Everyone looked at the gauge.

Nothing happened.

Excellent.

We waited for the next freight.

Still nothing.

The fault was gone.

Or hidden.

Maintenance teaches humility by making success indistinguishable from a machine simply choosing not to embarrass you yet.

East Four logged the modification.

TRAM-INDUCED REGULATOR BINDING.

FLOAT MOUNT INSTALLED.

DESIGN: J. PELL.

FIELD REVIEW: S. DORN / G. [my full name because paperwork hated me].

Dera added:

TOOK THREE DAYS BECAUSE INSPECTOR WENT LOOKING UNDERGROUND.

I crossed it out.

She wrote it again.

Some records resist revision.

I stayed for lunch.

Old habit.

Different crew around the table.

Seli had my old seat.

Not assigned.

Just happened.

I noticed.

Then did not care enough to move her.

Growth.

Jori ate slowly because two fingers on his right hand were stiff with age.

Lorn was not there.

Nessa was at district.

Dera still drank tea that looked like metal wash.

The station hummed.

Healthy.

I knew the sound.

Still.

That mattered.

So did the fact that the people keeping it healthy no longer needed me to be one of them every day.

I had been called because East Four had a strange cough.

I arrived with old infrastructure in my head.

The answer was a tram bearing, a shared wall, and a mechanic with a metal rod.

No rank required.

No ancient knowledge.

No impossible memory.

Just somebody willing to lie under a housing for forty minutes and listen.

I had once believed strength made difficult work smaller.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes the work is not difficult because it is strong.

It is difficult because it is specific.

Specific problems do not care who you used to be.

That is one of their better qualities.
