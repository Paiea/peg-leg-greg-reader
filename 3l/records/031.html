The written record

Rankers can open a pressure door that takes four station hands to move.

This does not mean they know which door to open.

West Three learned that during an overtime dispute.

So did the Rankers.

So did district payroll.

I learned it twice because apparently I require repetition.

The dispute began with night coverage.

West Three had lost two experienced operators in the same month.

One retired.

One moved south because his wife's family owned a house there and rent is stronger than loyalty.

The station filled the gaps with overtime.

Reasonable for a week.

Then three.

Then seven.

People began making mistakes.

Not disasters.

Wrong log entries.

Missed lubrication.

One valve tagged on the wrong side.

A night operator fell asleep during a quiet hour and woke before anything noticed.

That last part became a joke.

It should not have.

The crew asked district for temporary hires and an overtime cap.

District approved one temporary hire.

No cap.

West Three's crew refused voluntary overtime after the next schedule.

District called it a staffing action.

The crew called it sleeping.

Both descriptions were accurate.

The problem was West Three could not run nights on its normal roster without somebody staying extra.

So district sent two Authority Rankers as temporary hazard coverage.

Strong people.

Good field records.

One B-class.

One high C.

Neither had operated West Three.

They arrived with the confidence of men told they were the solution.

That is rarely their fault at first.

The station foreman, Iven, showed them the lower controls.

The B-class man asked, “Where is the automatic transfer?”

Iven said, “There isn't one.”

“Manual?”

“Yes.”

“How often?”

“Depends.”

“On?”

“Everything.”

The Ranker did not enjoy that answer.

By second night, West Three had missed two low-load adjustments and overfed the west return enough to wake a whole apartment line with pipe hammer.

Nobody hurt.

Thirty complaints.

District asked Halden for someone who understood station work and Ranker authority.

Halden sent me.

Coward.

I arrived at a meeting with twelve people and no chairs arranged correctly.

Bad start.

West Three crew on one side.

Authority Rankers on the other.

District payroll clerk in the middle looking like she regretted education.

Iven near the wall.

I took a chair and moved it sideways.

The B-class Ranker looked at me.

“You mediation?”

“No.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Halden hates me.”

That was not useful.

The payroll clerk said, “Gregory has station and district authority.”

“Greg.”

She ignored me.

“His role is technical facilitation.”

I looked at her.

“That means mediation.”

“Only if it works.”

Good clerk.

The crew's position was simple.

Too simple, which meant it was hiding details.

No voluntary overtime until district capped consecutive extended shifts and hired enough temporary coverage to restore two-person nights.

District's position was also simple.

Budget did not support full temporary staffing immediately.

Authority coverage met hazard requirements.

The Rankers' position was the simplest.

They were not operators and did not want to be blamed for operating.

That helped.

The high C-class man said, “I can hold anything in this station shut if it breaks.”

Iven replied, “Can you hear the return bearing go bad?”

“No.”

“Can you balance market draw at third bell?”

“No.”

“Can you clear the grit trap without feeding your hand into it?”

The Ranker looked at the trap.

“No.”

“Then you are not night coverage.”

The B-class man bristled.

“I am Authority-certified hazard support.”

“Yes.”

“Then what do you call me?”

Iven thought.

“Expensive emergency furniture.”

The meeting deteriorated.

I stopped it before furniture became personal.

“Both of you are right.”

Everyone hated that immediately.

Good.

“The Authority requirement is hazard coverage. Rankers satisfy it.”

The B-class man nodded.

“The station requirement is operation. They do not.”

The nod stopped.

I looked at the crew.

“Your overtime refusal is legal under Guild rules after six consecutive extended shifts.”

The payroll clerk said, “Voluntary overtime refusal.”

“Fine.”

“Important distinction.”

“Yes.”

I continued.

“You still have minimum emergency duty under your station contracts.”

The crew representative, a woman named Tess Mor, nodded.

“We know.”

“Then nobody is abandoning the station.”

“No.”

“Good.”

That removed the moral story.

Nobody lazy.

Nobody disloyal.

Nobody replacing workers with heroes.

We had a coverage problem.

Those are easier once people stop defending their character.

I asked for the schedules.

Seven weeks.

Ugly.

One operator had worked eleven extended nights in fourteen days.

Another had done nine.

I looked at the payroll clerk.

“You paid all of this?”

“Yes.”

“Cheaper than temps?”

She hesitated.

There.

“Short term.”

“Seven weeks is short?”

“On the hiring cycle.”

That sentence explained more than she intended.

Temporary hiring required qualification checks.

Station orientation.

Guild processing.

Budget transfer.

Overtime required a supervisor signature.

The system had chosen the action with less paperwork until the human limit became the real cost.

No villain.

Paper is powerful enough without intent.

We built a three-week bridge.

Not solution.

Bridge.

Two experienced operators from nearby stations volunteered for paid temporary rotation.

District covered their home-station replacements.

The Authority Rankers stayed nights as hazard support, but stopped being counted as operators.

West Three crew agreed to limited overtime with a hard cap on consecutive extended shifts.

One retired operator came back for two daytime orientation blocks because he missed telling people they were doing things wrong.

My father would have understood.

District accelerated two hires already in process.

The payroll clerk hated the cost.

Tess hated the remaining overtime.

The Rankers hated the night chairs.

Iven hated everyone.

Good settlement.

The B-class Ranker asked me afterward, “Why keep us?”

“Because Iven is right.”

He looked annoyed.

“You are expensive emergency furniture.”

“Fuck you.”

“If something fails hard, those operators need you.”

That changed him.

Not flattery.

Placement.

He had been sent as replacement labor.

Bad fit.

As emergency force, he mattered.

“Then why didn't district say that?”

“Because staffing tables like categories more than reality.”

He laughed.

“That's your technical opinion?”

“Yes.”

The settlement lasted long enough.

One temporary hire stayed.

One quit after four days because West Three smelled like wet stone and fish waste.

Reasonable.

The returning operator trained a younger hand.

Authority night coverage ended after two weeks instead of three.

Overtime fell.

Not to zero.

Stations eat time.

The crew went back to complaining about ordinary things.

Iven sent me a bottle of bad liquor with a note.

FOR THE FURNITURE.

I gave it to Lorn.

He drank it and blamed me for two days.

The useful part came later.

District changed the staffing sheet.

Not dramatically.

One extra line.

HAZARD COVERAGE.

OPERATING COVERAGE.

Separate boxes.

That was it.

A whole labor dispute reduced to two boxes after enough people had been tired.

Institutions again.

Scars with forms.

I had spent First Life in rooms where Ranker capability solved the visible problem.

Break the door.

Hold the wall.

Kill the thing.

Carry the injured.

Necessary work.

Real work.

But strength is not fungible.

That took me a long time to learn.

A B-class body cannot replace a sixty-year-old operator's ear.

A station hand cannot hold a ruptured gate against full pressure.

A payroll clerk cannot grease a bearing.

A mechanic should not set staffing policy just because he understands fatigue.

Dependence moves both directions.

That was the piece I carried out of West Three.

Not harmony.

The crew still thought district was cheap.

District still thought the crew used leverage aggressively.

The Rankers still hated being called furniture.

Everyone was probably right enough.

Mediation did not require making them agree about each other.

Only about what had to happen next.
