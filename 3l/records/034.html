The written record

Three copper sounded cheap until forty-eight households heard it at once.

Then it became politics.

District engineering needed money.

That sentence is less interesting than dragon fire and more common.

Legacy surveys had found enough old structures under enough stations that monitoring could no longer be paid from whatever account Halden had been quietly abusing.

I did not ask which account.

Plausible deniability is a public resource.

The district proposed a Line maintenance assessment.

Three copper per residential unit each month.

Commercial properties by service class.

Large users more.

Public buildings covered from city allocation.

The money would fund legacy inspections, station records, replacement gauges, reserve fittings, and two additional maintenance positions shared across the district.

Good things.

Boring things.

Things people noticed only when absent.

I supported it immediately.

That should have warned me.

Halden showed me the proposal in his office.

“Three copper.”

“Yes.”

“That's nothing.”

He looked at me.

Not Tova's stare.

Not Nessa's.

His own version.

Tired disappointment.

“Per unit.”

“I read it.”

“Per month.”

“Yes.”

“Passed through landlords.”

“Probably.”

“Market stalls?”

“Commercial schedule.”

I read that page.

Small stalls one copper.

Shops two to six depending on draw.

Heavy workshops more.

Still looked small.

Then Halden said, “Public hearing next week.”

“Why?”

“Because people pay it.”

“Three copper.”

He closed his eyes.

Apparently I had become the problem.

Nessa corrected me at home.

Not kindly.

“Three copper is one breakfast.”

“Exactly.”

“For you.”

“For anyone.”

“No.”

She opened the household ledger.

Weapon.

“Your mother buys medicine.”

“Yes.”

“How much did it increase last winter?”

I knew.

“Two copper a bottle.”

“Did she complain?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because she complains.”

Nessa stared.

I sighed.

“Because it adds up.”

“Three copper rent assessment. Two copper medicine. Coal up four. Bread up one.”

“Mina did not raise bread.”

“She raised rolls.”

“That is different.”

“Greg.”

“I know.”

She turned the ledger around.

Our household could pay three copper without noticing.

That was true now.

Years earlier, not always.

Lena's first apartment could have paid it.

Would have noticed.

My parents could pay it.

Would complain and cut something else on principle.

A widow renting one room?

Different.

A family splitting two rooms after rent rose?

Different.

The fee was small.

Small things accumulate best.

The hearing filled the public bath's dry hall.

Same room where we had argued about heat allocation.

Apparently infrastructure democracy required poor acoustics.

Halden presented first.

Charts.

Station ages.

Failure backlog.

Number of undocumented legacy features found.

Inspection hours.

Expected costs.

No scare language.

Good.

Then people spoke.

Senn was there.

Of course.

He represented his buildings and had brought papers.

Prepared people have pages.

“Three copper per unit becomes twelve silver and change across my rooms annually.”

Halden said, “Correct.”

“You assume I absorb it.”

“No.”

“Then tenants pay.”

“Likely.”

Senn nodded.

No trick.

A woman from Rope Street stood.

North rear building.

I recognized her.

The baby was older now and not with her.

“Rent went up last year.”

Senn looked at the floor.

Interesting.

“Coal went up.”

Someone behind her said, “Everything went up.”

She continued.

“I understand repairs cost money. I also understand every office says their three copper is the necessary three copper.”

That sentence stayed.

A baker complained commercial rates punished businesses for using the Line to produce food people needed.

Mina stood and told him bread did not become public service because he sold it.

He told her to mind her ovens.

She said her ovens were why she understood the bill.

Beautiful.

A bathhouse owner said maintenance should come from general city tax because everyone benefited.

A city clerk explained general tax was already committed.

He asked committed to what.

The clerk listed twelve things.

He objected to seven.

That became another meeting inside the meeting.

A retired station hand spoke.

“Charge by draw.”

Halden explained residential meters were building-level, not unit-level.

“Then meter units.”

“Costs more than the assessment.”

“Then stop pretending three copper is precise.”

Good point.

Tova was there in the back.

I looked at her.

She nodded slightly.

The proposal was not wrong.

It was blunt.

Blunt systems create hidden arguments about who resembles the average person they were designed around.

I had learned that from heat schedules.

Apparently learning once remained optional.

Then Halden called me.

I had agreed to answer technical questions.

Mistake.

A man asked what happened if the assessment failed.

Easy.

“Some surveys slow. Monitoring stays temporary longer. Parts replacement remains reactive.”

“Does the Line fail?”

“No.”

The room shifted.

Bad answer politically.

Correct technically.

“Then why pay?” someone asked.

“Because waiting until failure is more expensive.”

“How much?”

“Depends.”

Groans.

Fair.

I tried again.

“East Two's winter restriction required daily monitoring because we did not understand a moving legacy support. That cost workers every shift. If we had better instrumentation earlier, cheaper.”

“Did it fail?”

“No.”

“East Four chamber?”

“Did not fail.”

“South Three?”

“No.”

I could hear how this sounded.

Pay money to prevent things that had not happened.

Safety has terrible marketing.

Senn asked, “How many failures has this new program prevented?”

“I don't know.”

“Then what are we buying?”

There it was.

Not hostility.

A real question.

I thought about gauges.

Records.

Workers.

Time.

“Margin,” I said.

Senn frowned.

“What?”

“Time between noticing and emergency. Spare parts before failure. Enough staff that somebody is not on an eleventh extended shift. Records that tell the next crew what the last one saw. We are buying room to be wrong before wrong becomes expensive.”

The room quieted slightly.

Good sentence.

Still three copper.

The woman from Rope Street asked, “Why same fee for my room and his?”

She pointed at Senn.

People laughed.

Senn did too, barely.

“Property owners pay commercial share?” I asked Halden.

The clerk beside him answered.

“Common service plus per-unit residential assessment.”

“So both.”

“Yes.”

“Still her unit pays same as a larger one.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Administration.”

There.

The ugliest reasonable word.

Different rates required verified unit size, occupancy, or income.

District had none reliably.

Getting it cost money.

A flat fee was cheap to administer.

Cheap administration transferred imprecision to households.

No corruption.

Just tradeoffs all the way down.

The hearing did not resolve anything.

That was not its job, apparently.

The proposal went back for revision.

Halden was angry afterward.

Not at the public.

At the problem.

“I need twenty-two silver a month.”

“Then charge buildings.”

“That was the proposal.”

“No. Charge building service, not units.”

“Landlords pass it through anyway.”

“Yes.”

“But larger buildings distribute more.”

“Maybe.”

“Commercial mixed-use becomes ugly.”

“Yes.”

He looked at me.

“You have become very helpful.”

“Thank you.”

“That was not praise.”

We spent two weeks making the proposal worse in more sophisticated ways.

Lower residential base.

Higher commercial tiers.

Discount for buildings with verified hardship status through existing city relief rolls.

No new income bureaucracy.

Public housing covered through city allocation.

Small landlords could not itemize more than actual assessment in rent notices.

Senn objected to that last one.

Of course.

Then admitted it was reasonable.

The revised plan raised slightly less money than Halden wanted.

District delayed one equipment purchase.

Kept the two positions.

Tova insisted people mattered before instruments.

I agreed.

Interesting change.

The second hearing was still angry.

Better informed anger.

The assessment eventually passed.

Not unanimously.

Not cleanly.

My parents paid two copper more per month under their building share.

My mother complained.

I explained what it funded.

She said, “I know what it funds. I still pay it.”

That was the lesson.

Understanding a cost does not make it disappear.

I had spent years talking about maintenance as though good standards were morally free.

Inspect more.

Staff safely.

Keep records.

Hold reserves.

Replace before failure.

Yes.

Do those things.

Then answer the next question.

Who pays?

If you cannot answer that, you have not finished the maintenance plan.

You have only written the expensive half.
