The written record

Kett corrected me in front of six people.

I recommend avoiding this if possible.

Not because he was wrong.

That would have been easier.

We were at South Two.

Small station.

Newer than East Four.

Older than anyone working there.

The district had flagged a warm section of foundation beneath the north return.

Nothing dramatic.

No movement.

No visible sleeve.

Just heat where the drawings said solid fill should be.

That phrase again.

SOLID FILL.

Words with a long history of embarrassment.

Kett had provisional legacy-assist qualification by then.

He came as my second.

Also present were the station foreman, two operators, a mason, and Mara Vel from district survey.

Mara's handwriting remained hostile.

Some traditions deserve protection.

The warm section formed a narrow line along the base of the wall.

I recognized the pattern.

First-life memory.

Later western stations had used buried condensate returns behind foundation walls.

Not Line pressure.

Heat recovery.

Old but ordinary.

I had seen dozens.

The warmth here felt the same.

“Condensate return,” I said.

Kett crouched.

Mara asked, “Evidence?”

“Pattern.”

“That is a shape.”

“Temperature gradient.”

“That is better.”

I measured.

Warmest near center.

Fading east and west.

Consistent with an old return channel.

The station foreman looked relieved.

“Safe?”

“Probably.”

Kett looked at me.

I saw it.

“Something?”

He hesitated.

Student hesitation.

Bad sign if teacher caused it.

“Say it.”

“The wall is warmer after load drops.”

“No. It is warm now.”

“Warmer.”

“How do you know?”

“I checked before morning peak.”

I stopped.

“What?”

Kett held up his notebook.

He had arrived early.

Of course.

Temperature marks from first bell.

Then third.

Then after peak dropped.

The wall temperature rose as station load fell.

Condensate return could do that depending on cycle.

Not impossible.

Still.

I said, “Delayed thermal mass.”

Kett nodded.

“Maybe.”

My word.

I hated hearing it from him.

Mara smiled.

Traitor.

“Anything else?” I asked.

Kett pointed at the floor drain.

“Air stopped when load dropped.”

I looked.

Different drain from Market Five.

No obvious connection.

“You checked air?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because Market Five.”

Reasonable.

Annoying.

The pulse had been stronger under high load and stopped after.

Wall warmth rose after.

That sequence did not fit my easy condensate story cleanly.

I knew that.

Did not want to know that.

First-life experience had recognized a pattern and offered relief.

Condensate.

Ordinary.

No mystery.

I liked the answer because it closed the job.

That should have warned me.

Kett asked, “Can we test before we call it?”

The station foreman looked at me.

Not Kett.

Me.

Authority remained mine.

That made the next part matter.

I wanted to say the extra testing was unnecessary.

I felt the sentence form.

Experienced judgment.

Known construction family.

Time cost.

No hazard evidence.

All true enough.

Then I remembered Verren.

Memory.

Now.

Meaning.

I looked at Kett's notebook.

Current evidence disagreed with my closure.

“Test,” I said.

Kett exhaled.

Small.

I noticed.

Good.

Bad.

He had expected a fight.

We ran controlled load changes for two hours.

South Two operators hated us.

Not because dangerous.

Because every change required adjusting service elsewhere and filling out transfer notes.

Nessa's district office sent one slate asking WHY SOUTH TWO KEEPS MOVING.

I replied TRAINING.

She returned:

EXPENSIVE TRAINING.

Fair.

At high load, air pulsed through the drain.

Wall stayed only moderately warm.

Load dropped.

Air stopped.

Three minutes later wall temperature rose.

Then faded.

Repeat.

Same.

Not ordinary condensate behavior.

Mara said, “Pressure cavity venting into a heat sink?”

“Maybe.”

Kett looked at me.

I pointed.

“Do not.”

He smiled.

We opened a small inspection hole above the warm line.

Mason first.

No heroics.

Behind the wall was a flattened bronze channel.

Not the same as East Two sleeve.

Narrower.

Above it, ceramic blocks.

Old thermal material.

The channel carried something only under station load.

When load dropped, flow stopped and stored heat bled into the wall.

Not condensate.

Not exactly pressure support either.

Unknown old system doing an understandable local thing.

Good.

The station foreman asked, “Danger?”

“Not from what we see.”

Kett added, “Monitor before sealing.”

I looked at him.

He looked back.

I said, “Yes.”

Mara wrote it down.

Kett's recommendation.

Not mine translated through him.

His.

That was the important part.

Afterward we ate at a stall near the station.

Kett had the expression of someone trying not to look pleased.

I made him suffer for a while.

Then said, “Good catch.”

He nodded.

Too casual.

Young people are terrible at receiving praise when they want it.

I knew.

I had been young twice.

“Why did you check before peak?” I asked.

“Sera.”

“Of course.”

“She said you always arrive after breakfast and call it baseline.”

I stared.

“That is slander.”

Kett ate.

“Is it false?”

“Not the point.”

He smiled.

The correction went into training.

Not Kett was right about South Two.

Too specific.

We added pre-load baseline where possible.

Then post-load observation.

Not because every old structure behaved like South Two.

Because a single measurement can make a dynamic system look static.

Good lesson.

Kett taught it to the next trainee before I did.

That bothered me for several minutes.

Then less.

Authority running both directions sounds elegant in a report.

In practice it means a twenty-two-year-old says your theory is weak while everyone looks at you.

If you want the first part, you have to survive the second.

First-life Greg had trained people too.

Combat.

Reinforcement.

Survival drills.

Students challenged me rarely because the gap in capability was too obvious.

I mistook that for teaching well.

Second-life Kett had enough room to be right where I was wrong.

That made him more useful.

It also made me better.

Against my wishes.
