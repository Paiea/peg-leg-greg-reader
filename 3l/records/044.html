The written record

The part shortage began with a kiln fire I never saw.

That is how regional problems usually arrive.

Somebody else's bad day becomes your empty shelf.

The kiln belonged to Fenner Ceramic outside South Fen.

They made pressure collars, insulating sleeves, and the little white separator blocks everyone ignored until one cracked.

Then everyone wanted one immediately.

The fire destroyed two kilns and half the winter stock.

No deaths.

Three injured.

Production stopped for months.

District inventory lasted eleven days.

Not because the parts failed every eleven days.

Because once people heard shortage, everyone discovered replacements they had been postponing.

Fear has excellent inventory awareness.

Halden's office received requests from nine stations in two days.

He called me because of the map.

Not the paper itself.

The names around it.

Bellwater.

Hillcross.

South Fen.

Cask.

Small workshops.

Station mechanics.

People I had bothered repeatedly until they became contacts.

“You know who has stock.”

“No.”

“You know who might.”

“Different.”

“Yes.”

“What is my job?”

“Find enough collars to keep critical replacements moving.”

“That sounds like procurement.”

“It is.”

“I do not work procurement.”

“Today you know phone numbers.”

We did not have phones.

First-life word.

Again.

“Addresses.”

“Good.”

I hated when Halden accepted corrections without enjoying them.

The first thing I learned was that inventory records were fiction too.

Not lies.

Lag.

Bellwater showed fourteen ceramic collars.

Had nine.

Three installed since last count.

Two broken in storage after a roof leak.

Hillcross showed four.

Had seven because Corren's cousin had overordered the year before and never corrected the book.

South Fen showed zero.

A dye house had six in private stock because their maintenance lead believed public suppliers moved too slowly.

He was right.

He also did not want to share.

Reasonable.

Those six protected his production.

A part on a shelf is not unused if it is preventing future downtime.

That distinction became the whole job.

I could not simply count parts and move them where need looked biggest.

Every transfer moved risk.

Again.

Everything became allocation eventually.

We built a list.

Not stock.

Surplus above local reserve.

Different number.

Bellwater could release two.

Hillcross three.

Cask one standard and two mismatched sizes that might be machined with adapters.

A private workshop in North Mason had eight old-stock blocks from a discontinued design.

Tova inspected them.

Six usable.

Two cracked.

The dye house would release one collar if district guaranteed replacement priority when Fenner restarted.

Halden disliked promising future stock.

I said, “Then they keep all six.”

He promised.

Negotiation is easier when reality participates.

Then came price.

Hillcross wanted replacement cost only.

Bellwater wanted transport covered.

Cask wanted two new collars later for each one released now because brewery maintenance had been burned by shortages before.

“That is profiteering,” a district clerk said.

Cask's mechanic replied by letter:

THEN DO NOT BUY.

I liked him.

We negotiated one-for-one replacement plus priority freight, not two-for-one.

Private North Mason workshop wanted cash above normal rate because old stock had storage cost.

Fair.

District paid.

The largest argument was not money.

It was priority.

Which jobs got the scarce parts?

East Four had two collars beyond wear limit but still functioning.

River Nine had one cracked collar on an auxiliary boiler.

South Three had planned replacement during next shutdown.

A school plant had a leaking separator that could be patched temporarily.

A brewery wanted replacement before festival season.

Everyone had reasons.

We scored them.

I hated it.

Failure consequence.

Remaining life.

Alternate service.

Population affected.

Repair alternatives.

Availability of substitute size.

Tova added confidence in condition estimate.

Good.

A collar we believed had two months left with poor evidence deserved different treatment from one with measured crack growth.

The brewery lost.

They were furious.

Festival beer remained possible with temporary repair.

They called this cultural harm.

Probably true.

Not enough.

River Nine got first.

Auxiliary heat mattered because winter had already taught us what their boilers were for.

East Four got one, not two.

Sera accepted without complaint.

That made me suspicious.

“Why are you easy?”

“We can rotate the better old collar into the worse position.”

“You already planned that.”

“Yes.”

“Then why request two?”

“Because maybe you said yes.”

Honest.

Beautiful.

South Three delayed replacement.

Kel wrote in the margin:

PART SENT TO PEOPLE WHO APPARENTLY NEED HEAT MORE THAN WE DO.

Then beneath it:

CORRECT.

The school used the patch.

Brewery used their patch and complained publicly.

Nobody died.

Fenner Ceramic restarted one kiln seven weeks later.

Not full production.

Enough that the panic softened.

Then the real value of the shortage became visible.

We had a contact list.

Not just suppliers.

Actual stock holders.

Compatible workshops.

People willing to lend.

People willing only to sell.

People who needed written replacement guarantees.

Transport routes.

Which carts could carry fragile ceramic without turning it into gravel.

Who answered slates quickly.

Who never did but could be reached through a cousin.

Not an inventory system.

A network.

Networks are inventory with opinions.

Lorn started using the list for private jobs.

I stopped him.

“District contacts.”

“Parts are parts.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because Bellwater released stock to district under emergency agreement. We cannot turn around and use that relationship to source a landlord repair cheaper.”

He thought.

Then nodded.

Good.

Trust had scope too.

Nessa understood immediately.

“Different ledger.”

“Yes.”

She looked pleased.

“Do not.”

“I said nothing.”

We made separate contacts for private procurement.

More work.

Necessary.

The shortage ended eventually.

Fenner rebuilt.

Another ceramic shop expanded because shortages create businesses faster than planning does.

Prices stayed slightly higher.

Of course.

The contact list remained.

Halden formalized part of it.

Regional reserve exchange.

Not mandatory pooling.

Just current stock ranges and emergency-release terms.

People hated updating it.

Still did often enough to help.

Years later, when failures became larger than collars, I knew which towns answered because of work like this.

At thirty-five, I did not know that.

I was trying to find eight pieces of ceramic without screwing the brewery more than necessary.

That was enough.

Leverage rarely announces itself while you are building it.

Mostly it looks like knowing who has three spare parts and whether they trust you enough to send one.
