The written record

Juna Marr beat my price by thirty percent and used half as many people.

Naturally, I assumed she was dangerous.

This was becoming a personality problem.

The job was a market-hall regulator replacement.

Three halls.

Same hardware family.

Scheduled summer shutdown.

Nothing exotic.

We had done similar work twice.

I priced it honestly.

That was how I described the number.

Nessa described it as expensive.

Lorn described it as “you added every bad thing that ever happened to any job.”

“Contingency.”

“You have contingency for rain.”

“It can rain.”

“The work is indoors.”

“Transport is not.”

“Contingency for client delay.”

“Clients delay.”

“Contingency for damaged fittings.”

“Fittings arrive damaged.”

“Contingency for paperwork.”

“That one is proven.”

Lorn gave up.

Our bid was forty-one silver.

Juna Marr bid twenty-nine.

Harven Works bid forty-six.

The market board chose Juna.

I had never heard of her.

That bothered me first.

Then I learned she was twenty-eight.

That bothered me more.

Not rational.

Second-life Greg was thirty-five and apparently already capable of becoming an old man professionally.

Juna ran a four-person repair crew based in North Mason.

No Ranker on permanent staff.

She hired hazard coverage only when scope required it.

Small parts stock.

One cart.

Three ladders.

Appropriate number.

I asked around.

People liked her.

Suspicious.

Fast.

More suspicious.

No serious incidents.

Annoying.

The market job finished a day early.

I went to look.

Not because I was bitter.

Quality review is important.

Nessa did not believe me.

Juna was there during commissioning.

Short woman.

Black hair cut close because apparently she did not want machinery collecting it.

She recognized me.

“Greg.”

“Juna.”

“You inspecting?”

“No.”

“Then why are you holding an inspection hammer?”

I looked at my hand.

Habit.

“Decoration.”

She smiled.

I hated her slightly.

The installation was clean.

Better than clean.

She had built the replacement assemblies on benches before shutdown.

Valves.

Fittings.

Mounts.

Measured and tagged by hall.

During outage the crew removed old assemblies and installed prepared sections.

Less fitting in place.

Less time on knees.

Fewer opportunities to discover a missing bolt while service was already off.

Obvious.

I had not done it.

“Prefabrication,” she said.

“I know what it is.”

“You look offended.”

“I am looking.”

“With your face.”

Everyone knew the face.

Security disaster.

I checked the mount spacing.

Good.

Valve access.

Good.

Isolation tags.

Clear.

Then I found one thing.

Support strap on Hall Two used a lighter gauge than district recommendation.

“There.”

Juna came over.

“What?”

“Strap.”

“Yes.”

“Light.”

“Rated.”

“District uses heavier.”

“District spec assumes single-point mount.”

She pointed.

Two straps.

Load shared.

I measured.

She was right.

“Why two?”

“Cheaper stock. Easier replacement. If one loosens, the assembly does not drop.”

I disliked how reasonable this was.

“More fasteners.”

“Yes.”

“More inspection points.”

“Yes.”

“Corrosion.”

“Coated.”

She handed me the material sheet.

I read it.

Of course she had one.

“Fine.”

“That sounded painful.”

“Congratulations.”

She laughed.

Three months later she beat us again.

Apartment balancing job.

We priced eight silver.

She priced five.

This time I knew why.

She used temporary clip gauges instead of station brass gauges for room-level balancing.

Cheap little devices.

Less precise.

Precise enough.

I bought two the next week.

Lorn was unbearable.

“Juna tools.”

“Gauges.”

“Juna gauges.”

“Keep talking and I will put one somewhere useful.”

Then she did something stupid.

Thank God.

Warehouse return replacement.

Old building.

Bad access.

Client wanted no full shutdown.

Juna proposed live bypass under reduced pressure using clamp fittings.

Legal within certain limits.

I reviewed the scope because district hazard authorization needed a Ranker opinion.

I said no.

Not because Juna.

Evidence.

The old return wall thickness was unknown.

Clamp load could deform it.

No reliable isolation if bypass slipped.

She argued.

Hard.

“Pressure is under twenty percent.”

“Wall is under unknown percent.”

“We ultrasonic-tested.”

Not our word.

She had used mechanical thickness tapping and probe gauge.

Still limited.

“Three points.”

“Enough.”

“No.”

Client wanted speed.

Juna wanted the contract.

I understood both.

Still no.

She withdrew the live-bypass plan.

Full shutdown.

During removal they found deep internal corrosion near a point none of her tests reached.

Clamp might have held.

Might not.

Juna sent me a note afterward.

YOU WERE RIGHT. DO NOT ENJOY THIS.

I framed it mentally.

Did not actually frame it.

Nessa would have.

That was when rivalry became useful.

Before Juna, I divided methods too easily.

Mine.

Known.

Safe.

Versus shortcut.

Cheap.

Risky.

Juna ruined the categories.

Her prefabricated assemblies were better.

Her clip gauges were better for some jobs.

Her crew setup reduced labor without reducing safety.

Her live-bypass proposal was too aggressive for the evidence.

Same person.

No ideology.

No moral type.

Method by method.

Job by job.

Current evidence.

Again.

She also changed my prices.

Not downward automatically.

Better.

We started prefabricating repeat assemblies.

Reduced site hours.

Charged less on some work while keeping margin.

Stopped carrying contingencies already controlled by preparation.

Kept the ones that remained real.

We won jobs because we became cheaper.

Lost others because Juna remained cheaper.

Good.

Regional clients benefited from two annoying contractors trying to prove the other inefficient.

Competition is romanticized by people who do not have to read losing bids.

Still works sometimes.

Juna and I never became enemies.

That disappointed several people.

Lorn wanted drama.

Nessa wanted us to share supplier freight to reduce both costs.

Juna agreed immediately.

I accused them of conspiring against market principles.

Nobody cared.

Years later Juna's crew invented two field practices I taught under other people's names.

I invented one she copied without asking.

Good.

That is what methods are for.

The first time she beat me, I thought the low price proved something about her judgment.

What it proved was that I had not built assemblies on a bench.

Humility occasionally arrives itemized.
