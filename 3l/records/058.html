The written record

The largest unpaid invoice we ever carried belonged to the city.

That felt appropriate.

Not because the city was dishonest.

Because the city contained enough honest people to delay money indefinitely.

The job was a municipal bath complex near River Three.

Old pressure exchangers.

New pumps.

Bad integration.

Three weeks of work.

Eight workers across trades.

Esset & Vale led pressure commissioning.

Hema fabricated supports.

Jori rebuilt one pump housing.

Rin logged every test because her handwriting remained suspiciously competent.

The work went well.

That was the problem.

No emergency.

No dispute.

No damaged property.

No reason for anyone to become interested after completion.

We signed final acceptance.

The bath reopened.

Steam stable.

Water hot.

Client representative shook my hand.

Invoice: eighty-four silver.

Largest single receivable we had carried.

Terms: twenty-one days.

Day twenty-one arrived.

No payment.

Normal enough.

Day twenty-eight.

Still none.

I sent a notice.

The city accounts office replied politely.

PAYMENT PENDING FINAL TECHNICAL CERTIFICATION.

I had final technical certification.

I sent a copy.

They replied:

PRESSURE COMMISSIONING CERTIFICATE RECEIVED. AWAITING FACILITY COMPLETION CODE.

“What is facility completion code?” Lorn asked.

“I don't know.”

“Good system.”

We asked the bath manager.

He had never heard of it.

We asked district engineering.

They had.

Of course.

The code came from municipal buildings office after fire, sanitation, structure, and mechanical inspections all closed.

Three were done.

Fire remained open because one emergency door swung inward.

Our pressure invoice waited behind a door hinge.

I admired bureaucracy briefly.

Then payroll came due.

Rin.

Daro, our new part-time helper.

Bookkeeper Minae two mornings a week.

Outside trade invoices too.

Hema had already paid for steel.

Jori had bought pump seals.

Our partnership had enough reserve to pay employees.

Barely enough to pay outside trades and maintain two-month payroll reserve.

We had options.

Delay Hema.

Delay Jori.

Use reserve.

Borrow.

Partner cash.

None ideal.

Lorn said, “Use reserve.”

“Payroll reserve.”

“We are making payroll.”

“It is supposed to protect payroll from future problems.”

“This is a current problem.”

Fair.

I still resisted.

Nessa asked why.

I told her.

She looked at me for a long time.

“You like reserves because they let you imagine future safety.”

“Yes.”

“You are refusing to use one for the exact thing it exists for because then the number will get smaller.”

I hated this conversation.

We used part of the reserve.

Employees paid.

Hema paid.

Jori paid.

Partner draws reduced.

Again.

Then I became a collector.

I had thought collection meant threatening people.

Sometimes.

Mostly it meant finding the person whose missing mark stopped the next person from moving paper.

The fire inspector said the door correction was complete but not reinspected.

The carpenter said he finished it six days earlier.

The bath manager had not requested reinspection because he assumed the carpenter did.

The carpenter assumed buildings office scheduled automatically.

Buildings office did not.

Nobody lied.

Money remained trapped behind assumptions.

I filed the reinspection request myself.

Not our job.

Apparently now it was.

Fire inspector came four days later.

Door passed.

Facility code still did not issue.

Why?

Sanitation certificate had expired during the delay because temporary reopening approval lasted thirty days.

I laughed when Minae told me.

Not healthy laughter.

She looked concerned.

“Do you need water?”

“No.”

“A healer?”

“No.”

“Another career?”

“Maybe.”

Sanitation reinspected next day because the bath manager had finally become motivated.

Then buildings office issued completion.

Then accounts office said payment cycle had closed for the week.

Next cycle seven days.

Day fifty-two, eighty-four silver arrived.

No interest.

No apology.

Just money that had technically always been ours finally becoming useful.

I sat at the shop table and stared at it.

Lorn said, “Rich.”

“No.”

“Temporarily looks rich.”

“Most of this belongs elsewhere.”

Materials reimbursement.

Reserve restoration.

Taxes.

Partner draws we had postponed.

The money passed through us quickly.

That was another lesson.

Revenue is not ownership.

Minae had been saying this for months.

I had ignored her because bookkeeping sentences sound less important before they hurt.

We changed contracts after River Three.

Public jobs over a threshold required staged billing.

Materials deposit.

Midpoint payment.

Final retention only after completion.

Some agencies refused.

We raised price accordingly.

Not punishment.

Financing cost.

Clients disliked that phrase.

It remained true.

Private clients changed too.

Repeat late payer? Deposit increased.

New commercial client? Credit check through Guild references.

Small household job? Usually pay at completion because turning every broken heater into a banking relationship was stupid.

Rules with scale.

Good.

Then one of our regular private clients, a dye house named Reval & Sons, went thirty days late.

Not city.

Family business.

Good history.

No previous problems.

Minae flagged it.

I went there expecting excuses.

Found the owner sitting in a half-empty office surrounded by invoices.

His largest buyer had failed.

He was waiting on money too.

Chain.

Always a chain.

“I can pay half,” he said.

“How soon the rest?”

“Two weeks. Maybe three.”

Old Greg wanted certainty.

Business wanted cash.

Current evidence said Reval had paid us on time eleven times.

Meaning: temporary problem, likely.

Inference: flexible terms preserve relationship and maybe recover full amount.

Memory ledger method sneaking into money.

Fine.

We took half.

Wrote three-week plan.

No new work until balance cleared.

He paid in eighteen days.

Good client remained good.

That mattered too.

Collection could become cruelty if every delay was treated as betrayal.

Or stupidity if every story became credit.

The work was distinguishing.

Again.

Everything became distinguishing eventually.

By the third year of the shop, Minae handled most collections.

She was better.

Not because she was meaner.

Because she did not take payment personally.

She called on day one late.

Not day fourteen after resentment fermented.

She documented promises.

Sent reminders.

Escalated predictably.

Clients liked her more than me.

Unacceptable.

I asked why.

She said, “I don't sound wounded when they owe us money.”

Fair.

We gave her more hours.

Another system leaving my head.

Another piece of work becoming less dependent on my mood.

The city bath remained a client.

Two years later they hired us again.

This time the contract had staged payments.

The procurement clerk objected.

I showed her River Three invoice history.

She read it.

“Fifty-two days?”

“Yes.”

“That's bad.”

“Your office.”

“Different department.”

Exactly.

She approved the schedule.

The second job paid in three stages.

No drama.

Workers paid.

Suppliers paid.

Reserve stayed boring.

That was success.

I had once measured completed work by whether the structure stood afterward.

Still important.

Later I learned a job was not finished until the people who carried its cost had been made whole.

Metal could be perfect.

Pressure stable.

Client happy.

And if wages or parts money were still trapped somewhere in a chain of paper, the work was not actually done yet.
