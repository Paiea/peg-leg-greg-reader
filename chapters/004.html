PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 4

THE EXPERT

The first thing I did the next morning was count my money. The second thing I did was count it again because the number offended me. It did not improve. I sat on the edge of the bed with the coins spread across the blanket in little stacks and tried to remember when silver had become invisible to me. Not metaphorically. Literally. There had been a point in my old life when I stopped counting it. Somebody else handled expenses. Somebody else settled rooms, replaced gear, paid porters, bought supplies, tipped runners, bribed clerks, and quietly moved money around so that Greg could continue being Greg without ever needing to wonder whether dinner cost four copper or six.

That arrangement had seemed efficient. Now it seemed suspiciously like being spoiled. I moved one silver from the left stack to the right. Training. Another. Food. Another. Pell. Another. Debt. I stopped. That was already more categories than coins.

"Excellent," I said.

The room did not object. The shale project had become the most dangerous kind of thing: promising. Failure would have been cleaner. If Pell had looked at the sixth disk and said no, useless, wrong, then the project could die with dignity. Instead we had a twenty-percent improvement, a path toward better tests, and no idea whether the final product would take two weeks or two years.

Long-term.

That was the word. I had been treating it like short-term money because I wanted it to be short-term money. It was not. I stared at the coins again. Pell could keep working if we slowed the pace. Antonius had financing rights for three months. The material was cheap enough that securing some supply later would not require a kingdom. Nothing about the project demanded that I bleed myself dry this week except impatience. Impatience had already earned a line item. I took a piece of paper and wrote:

ARWICK / SHALE. LONG TERM.

Under it:

DO NOT FEED IT ALL MY MONEY.

Then I sat back and looked at the sentence.

"Very sophisticated."

The problem was immediate cash. Not future wealth. Not ownership. Not eventual manufacturing.

Cash. Now.

That distinction was embarrassing because I had spent most of the previous day feeling clever for identifying capital as the first bottleneck, then immediately turned around and spent borrowed capital like I still had forty years of accumulated wealth behind me. Six silver here. Three there. Training fee. Delivery. Testing. Food. I remembered buying boots once for thirty gold because I liked the buckle. I looked down at my current boots. The left one had a crack near the toe.

"Different economy."

Same economy. Different Greg.

That annoyed me more. I opened the five-year plan. It looked ambitious in the morning light. It also looked expensive. Mana conditioning three times a week. Better food because Hessa had specifically said nutrition mattered. Guild access. Equipment. Travel eventually. Teachers. Materials. Information. Every single accelerated year had a price attached to it. That was the thing I had not respected. Old Greg had understood leverage abstractly. Young Greg was discovering leverage had rent. I needed income that did not require years of development, did not depend on precise future memory, and did not consume the capital meant to build magic. What was I good at? I wrote the question down. Then, because I had spent enough time around myself to distrust the first answer, I wrote beneath it:

The question was almost too open. My mind answered by producing careers instead of skills. Warrior. Mage. Investor. Merchant. Teacher. Gambler. Strategist. Each answer immediately generated prerequisites, alternate routes, and reasons I could probably do it better this time. Within a minute I had turned a question about earning dinner into a redesign of my entire life.

"Stop," I told myself. "Money. Today."

ACTUALLY GOOD AT.

That narrowed things. Fighting? I looked at the sword. That answer came easier than it would have at nineteen. I was competent. More competent now because sixty-year-old Greg understood timing, distance, fear, and the hundred ugly little ways fights actually went wrong. But skill in a young body was still attached to a young body's speed, strength, conditioning, and habits. I could probably embarrass another Bronze warrior. That was not the same as being worth money. I wrote:

FIGHTING. BETTER THAN I LOOK. NOT SPECIAL.

Strategy? Yes. Then I paused. Strategy for whom? I had no party. No command. No reputation. No one was paying a nineteen-year-old Bronze adventurer to redesign their campaign plans. I crossed it out. Then wrote:

STRATEGY. USELESS WITHOUT PIECES.

That one hurt. Future knowledge? I stared at it. Yesterday I would have put it first. Today I wrote:

FUTURE KNOWLEDGE. BIG THINGS. BAD INDEX. EXPENSIVE TO VERIFY.

Also not immediate. Investing? I almost laughed. Investing required money. I wrote:

INVESTING. APPARENTLY REQUIRES MONEY.

Teaching? Maybe. Who would listen? Consulting? Worse. People reading? I stopped. That one stayed. I was good at people. Not morally. Technically. Faces. Posture. Breathing. The little pauses before someone lied. The difference between fear and caution. The moment somebody wanted to say yes but had not yet found the excuse. Which insult would make a fighter reckless. Which compliment would make a clerk helpful. Who needed to feel clever. Who needed to feel safe. Who needed to believe I disliked them before they trusted me. I had built half a career on that before I admitted it was a skill.

Support magic had made it easier because magic gave me feedback. A reinforcement spell told you things about tension and fatigue. A barrier told you where someone expected force. Buffs turned people's habits into measurable patterns. But I had been reading people long before magic. Probably before I knew I was doing it. I wrote:

PEOPLE. GOOD.

Then underneath:

TOO GOOD?

That was not useful financially. Unless. Gambling. The thought arrived so cleanly I distrusted it. I leaned back. Cards. Dice. Tables. Not pure games of chance. Not the stupid ones. Anything with betting decisions, bluffing, hidden information, player behavior. I had played constantly in my old life. Not professionally. Socially. Expeditions were boring. Sieges were worse. Give twelve adventurers three weeks in a fortress and eventually somebody invented a drinking game, a card table, or a reason to hate whoever packed the dice. I had been good. Very good. How good? I frowned. That was exactly the kind of memory I had learned to question. I remembered winning. People remember winning.

I remembered other people complaining. Also not evidence. I remembered being banned from a table in Hareth because the owner believed I was cheating. That was better. Had I been cheating? Not mechanically. I had known when the owner was bluffing. He considered that cheating because the distinction cost him money. I smiled.

"Okay."

There was a gambling room near the slaughter lane. Antonius's room. That was bad. Maybe useful. Too visible. If I started winning money under my lender's roof, Antonius would notice exactly how I was paying him. Then again, Antonius already thought I was interesting. More interesting was not necessarily better. I needed a different room. Carrow had several. I knew that because nineteen-year-old Greg had been in most of them. That memory came back with enough detail to be embarrassing. The Broken Pike.

Closed after a stabbing. Still open now? Probably. The Copper Nail. Too small. The White Dog. I stopped. The White Dog. I could see the table arrangement. Or thought I could. I remembered losing a boot there. Why? Bet. Of course. I had bet the boot. Young Greg had apparently been an idiot with continuity. I stood. Then sat down again. Money first. How much could I risk? That question felt wrong. Old Greg would have asked how much he could deploy. Young Greg needed to ask how much he could afford to lose. Different language. Different survival rate. I counted again. I separated food. Training. Debt reserve. Then stared at what remained. It was pathetic. A few silver. Enough that losing it would hurt. Not enough that winning modestly would solve anything. Which meant I was immediately tempted to risk all of it. There he was.

Greg. I took half. Put the rest away. Then took one more coin. Put it back.

Progress.

The White Dog existed. That was the first win. The second was that nobody there knew me. At least nobody important. I entered slowly enough to let the room explain itself. Six tables. Three active. One dice game. Two cards. The air smelled like beer, tallow, wet wool, and the particular anxiety of people pretending money was entertainment. I liked it immediately. That was probably another character flaw. A woman at the nearest table looked up. Did I know her?

Name? No idea. Future importance? Nothing. Good. I scanned the others anyway. Old man with red nose. Young laborer with scarred knuckles. Merchant apprentice trying too hard to look bored. Two women in dock coats. A man in guild leathers with a silver clasp. Silver rank?

Decorative clasp. Why did I know that? Because he kept touching it. Pride object. Not rank. I filed him. The dealer at the card table was missing the tip of one ear. Memory tugged. Nothing followed. I stood there too long.

"Playing?" the dealer asked.

"I was deciding."

"About?"

"Whether you're honest."

The table laughed. The dealer did not.

"Sit down."

I sat. Three-card draw. Simple. Too simple. Good. I bought in small. The first hand I lost. Deliberately? That would have been smarter. I simply had bad cards. Important distinction. The second hand I folded too late because I mistook the merchant apprentice's nervousness for weakness. He had an excellent hand. I paid for the lesson.

Interesting.

I watched him. He was nervous because he had an excellent hand. Not because he was bluffing. Different people leaked different information. Of course they did. I knew that. Knowing the principle was not knowing the man. Third hand. Dock-coat woman tapped one finger when she wanted someone to raise. Fourth. Red-nose exhaled through his nose before lying. Fifth. Guild-leathers touched his fake silver clasp when he wanted to look confident. I lost two. Won one. Folded three. The dealer watched me watching.

"First time?" he asked.

"Today?"

"Here."

"Yes."

"You stare."

"So I've been told."

"Bad habit."

"Depends."

He dealt. I picked up the cards. Mediocre. Not useless. The merchant apprentice looked at his hand and went still. Very still. Good hand. Red-nose scratched his cheek. Nothing. Dock coat checked the pot before checking anyone else. Interested. Fake Silver touched the clasp. There. Weak. I raised. Merchant apprentice called. Dock coat called. Fake Silver hesitated, then called because pride had entered the hand. Red-nose folded. Next card. Merchant apprentice blinked once. Better. Dock coat relaxed. Worse. Fake Silver touched the clasp again. Still weak. I had not improved. I raised anyway. Not because my cards deserved it.

Because two of the players wanted permission to leave and one wanted to prove he could stay. Dock coat folded. Fake Silver called. Of course. Merchant apprentice raised me. There was the problem. Good hand. Very good. I looked at him. He was trying not to breathe. I could beat Fake Silver. Not him. I folded. Fake Silver did not. Merchant apprentice took the pot. Fake Silver swore. I smiled. Not because I won. Because the room had become legible. That was different. I stayed three hours. I did not win every hand. That would have been impossible unless everyone else was stupid, and they were not. But I stopped making expensive mistakes quickly. That was the part I had forgotten about myself.

I learned people fast. Not abstract people. Specific people. Red-nose changed his tell after drinking. Dock coat became reckless when behind. Merchant apprentice played tighter after winning because he was more afraid of losing profit than losing principal. Fake Silver could be pushed by disrespect. I used that once.

"Too rich for you?" he asked when I folded.

"Too boring." He stayed in the next hand three raises longer than he should have.

Useful. Ugly. Effective.

I won. The feeling was familiar. Not the money. The steering. That little shift where another person thought they were making a decision and I knew exactly which pressure had nudged it. Old Greg had loved that. I had called it support when the outcome was heroic. Called it strategy when the outcome was political. Called it negotiation when money moved. Called it flirting when clothes came off. Same thing. Read the person. Find the lever. Apply pressure. I looked at Fake Silver stacking fewer coins than he had arrived with. He was angry. At me? At himself. Better. If he blamed me, he might leave. If he blamed himself, he would stay and try to repair the story.

I disliked how natural that thought felt. I used it anyway. By the end of the third hour, I had nearly tripled the money I brought. Nearly. Not life-changing in my old life. Very fucking life-changing now. I stared at the little stack. Training. Food. Interest. A week. Maybe two. My chest tightened with something dangerously close to offense. This amount? This pathetic little pile? I had once tipped more after a dinner. Now it changed whether I could train next week. I hated that. Not poverty exactly. Scale. My mind was calibrated wrong. I had been spending silver because some part of me still treated it like background noise. Silver was not background noise anymore. Silver was time.

The realization should have made me conservative. Instead it made me want more. Of course it did. I looked around the room. New table. Higher stakes. There was one in the back. I had noticed it when I entered and deliberately ignored it. Four players. Better clothes. More money. One empty chair. No dealer. Private game. I could buy in now. Barely. If I lost, I would walk out with less than I started. If I won. There it was again. I laughed under my breath. The dealer looked at me.

I stood. Not toward the door. Toward the back table. The game was Crown and Knives. That was more complicated. Good. Hidden cards. Shared cards. Betting rounds. Enough structure that observation mattered more. A man with silver rings looked up.

"Seat's expensive."

"How expensive?"

He named the buy-in. I had it. I should leave. That thought arrived cleanly. Food secured. Training secured. Debt safer. Leave. I sat down. Because apparently forty years of surviving my own decisions had taught me many things except how to stop at enough. The first hour went badly. Not disastrously. Badly. These players were better. They lied with intention. They manufactured tells. One woman deliberately touched her necklace when bluffing for three hands, then used the same motion with a real hand to punish anyone who had noticed. I paid for that. A thin man named Osric talked constantly. I assumed distraction. Wrong. He talked more when thinking because silence made him uncomfortable.

The information was in when he stopped. I learned that after losing another hand. My stack shrank. I felt irritation. Then something worse. Excitement.

Good players. Finally.

That thought belonged to old Greg too. The room sharpened. I stopped trying to win money and started trying to understand them. Osric's silences. Necklace woman's false tells layered over real ones. Ring man's habit of staring at the player he wanted to challenge. Fourth player, older woman named Senna, had almost nothing visible. I watched her too hard. She noticed.

"You planning to marry me?" Senna asked.

"Not yet."

"Then stop looking at me like that."

"I'm learning."

"Learn quieter."

The table laughed. I smiled. She did not.

Interesting.

Senna was the best player. That meant something. Not future-important. Present-important. Different category. I watched her hands. Nothing. Breathing. Nothing. Eyes. Minimal. Bet sizing. There. Not a tell. A preference. She hated inefficient bets. When the pot was small, she sized precisely. When somebody distorted the pot with an irrationally large raise, she became more conservative even with good hands. Not fear. Disgust. She disliked bad structure. I could use that. Maybe. I tested it. Lost. Wrong. She had known I was testing. Her eyes flicked to me afterward. Tiny smile. Oh. I liked her. That was dangerous in a completely different way.

"Again," I said.

Senna laughed. By midnight I had recovered the losses and pushed slightly above my first-table winnings. Not enormous. Enough. More importantly, I had learned what I was actually selling. Not gambling. Attention. Fast models of people. The cards merely priced the skill immediately. That was useful. It also had a ceiling. I could make silver here. Maybe gold with larger stakes. But I could not spend every day playing cards if the actual goal was mana, access, and progression. Unless. I stopped. Unless gambling became the bridge. Short-term cash. No verification cost. No future knowledge required. No capital beyond the buy-in. Skill portable now.

That was the first idea all day that survived contact with the present without demanding another expert. I stood. This time toward the door. Senna looked up.

"Running?"

"Stopping."

"Different?"

"For me? Apparently." She glanced at my stack.

"You came in small."

"Yes."

"You leave small." I looked at the coins. Old calibration rose again. Small. She was right. And wrong.

"Not to me," I said.

That answer surprised both of us. Outside, the night air felt colder. I walked home slower than I needed to because my head was busy. The board had changed again. Shale: long-term. Pell: long-term, and increasingly not mine. Antonius: eight-day problem. Mana: expensive, but Hessa had turned six years into months. Immediate income: gambling. Future knowledge: support, not foundation. People-reading: real. Fighting: competent. Strategy: waiting for pieces. Money. I stopped walking. Money was the thing. Not earning it. Valuing it. I still did not value it correctly. That was why I had spent six silver without blinking. Why I had wanted the larger loan.

Why I had sat at the higher-stakes table after already solving the day's problem. My old life had trained me to think in leverage, not scarcity. That had been useful when I had resources. Now it was dangerous. I looked at the coins again under a streetlamp. A few silver. Nothing. Everything. Both thoughts felt true. I hated that. Then another thought arrived. If I could reliably make this much in an evening, how fast could I clear Antonius? Two nights? Three?

Bad question. How fast could I clear Antonius while still funding Hessa? Better. And if I could clear Antonius, did I actually want to? The answer should have been yes. Debt was risk. Interest was waste. Freedom mattered. But Antonius was also access. Capital. Information. A future institution still small enough to talk to. I frowned. There it was again. Something useful turning into something I wanted to keep just because I could see leverage in it. Maybe the right move was not to clear him completely. Maybe I should repay enough to prove myself, then borrow again. Larger. Use gambling as the stable base. Use borrowed money for high-upside projects.

Use personal winnings for living and training. Separate survival capital from speculative capital. That was good. Very good. I smiled. Then remembered the entire reason I was currently walking home with gambling winnings was that my first speculative project had nearly eaten me alive. I stopped smiling.

"Right."

Present Greg needed rules. Old Greg had always preferred judgment. Rules were what you gave people whose judgment you did not trust. Apparently I qualified. At the room, I wrote:

1. FOOD / ROOM / TRAINING MONEY DOES NOT GET INVESTED.

2. BORROWED MONEY GETS A PURPOSE BEFORE I BORROW IT.

3. FUTURE MEMORY IS A LEAD, NOT PROOF.

4. LEAVE THE TABLE WHEN THE DAY IS SOLVED.

I stared at number four. Then added:

5. ACTUALLY LEAVE.

Better. Under that I wrote: WHAT AM I GOOD AT? PEOPLE. LEARNING PEOPLE. THINKING IN SYSTEMS. NOT FIGHTING. NOT INVESTING. NOT REMEMBERING EVERYTHING. MAYBE GAMBLING. I paused. Then crossed out MAYBE.

GAMBLING.

That one had earned its place. I looked at the money again. For the first time, I counted it without resentment. Not enough. But real. A beginning. The debt was still there. Hessa still wanted money. Pell still wanted terms. My magical career still technically consisted of a headache and a tingling palm. But now I had something I could do tomorrow that did not depend on history cooperating. That mattered. I lay down. Then sat up again. One more thought. If I was this good at reading gamblers, what else had I been reading all my life without calling it a skill? Parties. Guild clerks. Merchants. Lovers. Enemies. Audiences. The word audiences sat there longer than the others. I did not know why. Not yet. I wrote it down anyway.
