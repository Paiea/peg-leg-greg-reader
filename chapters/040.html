CHAPTER 40

GOOD ENOUGH TWICE

Dinner was fish stew.

I did not ask what fish.

This was growth.

Or cowardice.

The stew had potatoes, onions, something green, and enough salt to make the water afterward necessary rather than optional. I ate two bowls, went upstairs, checked my hands, checked my ribs, checked my channels, and did not check anything else. No sinkstone notes. No manufacturing list. No Pell. No Pel Marris. I slept.

Morning made that decision look better. My right palm had closed enough that gripping no longer hurt unless I squeezed hard. My left blister had decided to become ugly rather than painful. Ribs quiet. Channels quiet. Mild stiffness in my legs from the spar. Nineteen-year-old recovery remained offensive.

Useful.

I ate.

Then I looked at the regulator.

Pell had asked for measurements.

I had measurements.

That was enough reason to go. I brought the device, my notes, and no questions about sinkstone. This lasted until I reached his door. Pell’s workshop was louder than usual. Hammering from the back. A scraping wheel. Someone swearing about ceramic.

Normal.

I entered.

Pell was not at the regulator bench.

Vessa was. Pell stood near the front with a man holding a brass lamp assembly. The customer was angry. Not theatrically. Expensively.

“It failed twice.”

Pell said, “Yes.”

“You repaired it.”

“Yes.”

“It failed again.”

“Yes.”

“That is not repair.”

“No.”

The man stared.

I stopped just inside.

Vessa saw me.

She put one finger to her lips.

Not because silence was required.

Because this was good.

I stayed.

The customer set the lamp on the counter.

Hard.

“Then what did I pay for?”

“First repair.”

“Which failed.”

“Yes.”

“Then refund it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the first fault was repaired.”

The customer looked ready to become louder.

Pell turned the lamp.

Pointed.

“First fault here.”

Small crack in the fuel collar.

“Replaced.”

Then another point.

“Second fault here.”

A blackened contact near the ignition housing.

“Different.”

“You expect me to believe it broke twice by coincidence?”

“No.”

Pell paused.

“I expect it broke twice because it is bad.”

Vessa coughed into her hand.

The customer looked at her.

She became extremely interested in a file.

Pell continued.

“I can repair this fault.”

“How much?”

“Three copper.”

“I already paid four.”

“Yes.”

“For a bad lamp.”

“For a repair.”

The man looked at the lamp.

Then Pell.

Then me.

I had no role.

Good.

“What would you do?” he asked.

Damn.

Pell looked at me.

Vessa looked at me.

The customer waited.

I could answer. The lamp was probably not worth repeated repair if failures were distributed. Maybe replace. Maybe inspect full assembly first. Maybe the customer wanted reliability more than lowest cost. Maybe Pell knew the lamp better. I said, “Ask him.” The customer frowned.

“I did.”

“No. Ask whether he would repair it if it were his.”

Pell’s face changed.

Not much.

The customer looked at him.

“Well?”

Pell picked up the lamp.

Turned it.

Looked at the base.

“No.”

There.

“Why?”

“Body is thin. Heat warped one side. Collar repair holds. Contact can be replaced. Something else will fail.”

“How long?”

Pell shrugged.

“Could be tomorrow. Could be a year.”

“Then why offer repair?”

“Because you asked for repair.”

The customer stared.

Then laughed once.

Not happily.

“Do you sell lamps?”

“No.”

“Of course not.”

He took the lamp.

“Three copper?”

“For this fault.”

“No.”

He left.

Pell watched the door close.

Then looked at me.

“You cost me three copper.”

“You did that.”

“Yes.”

“Would you have repaired it?”

“Yes.”

“You said you would not if it were yours.”

“Yes.”

I smiled.

“Interesting.”

“No.”

Vessa laughed.

Pell pointed at my bag.

“Measurements?”

“Yes.”

That was the end of the lamp.

Just three copper not earned.

I gave him the notes.

He read them standing.

Vertical.

Tilted.

Horizontal.

Tap test.

Cold water.

No hot.

He read that twice.

“Why no hot?”

“Tired.”

He looked up.

“Good.”

That annoyed me.

“Why good?”

“Because tired measurements are bad.”

“I could have measured accurately.”

“Maybe.”

“I dislike maybe.”

“Yes.”

He set the notes down.

“Horizontal worse.”

“Yes.”

“Expected.”

I stopped.

“You knew?”

“No.”

“That is not what expected means.”

“I expected orientation to matter.”

“Why?”

He took my regulator.

Held it sideways.

“The settling pocket.”

Right.

Gravity. The recess that gave flow somewhere to be wrong also had orientation. Of course.

“How much worse did you expect?”

“Didn’t know.”

“Then my measurement matters.”

“Yes.”

Good.

He read the tap test again.

“Repeat?”

“Three times.”

“Same?”

“Same direction. Not same amount.”

He nodded.

“Good.”

“What does it change?”

“Fixture.”

“Again?”

“Yes.”

“Not regulator?”

“Maybe regulator.”

I leaned toward the bench.

He saw.

“No.”

“I said nothing.”

“You expanded.”

Vessa said, “It’s the eyebrows.”

“Traitor.”

She smiled.

Pell put the regulator in a clamp.

Not the old clamp.

A new one.

Fourth fixture?

No.

Different bench.

He tapped the table.

Flow line fluttered.

Not much.

Then he put a folded cloth beneath the fixture feet.

Tapped again.

Less flutter.

I stared.

“Isolation.”

“Yes.”

“That does not fix use.”

“No.”

“It fixes measurement.”

“Yes.”

Good.

Separate the tool from the test.

I liked that.

“What if the use environment vibrates?”

“Then regulator needs to tolerate it.”

“How much?”

“Depends on use.”

“Mine?”

“You tell me.”

There.

Ownership again.

I had wanted low-flow control for magic work eventually.

But what actual environment?

Bench?

Field?

Body?

Armor?

Moving carriage?

I had not specified.

Future devices had context built into memory.

Present device did not.

“What did I ask you to build?”

Pell looked at me.

“Better low-flow regulator.”

“That is vague.”

“Yes.”

“You built it anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Interesting.”

Clean.

I laughed.

Vessa said, “He also wanted to beat Tere’s old one.”

Pell looked offended.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“Not beat.”

“What?”

“Improve.”

“That is beat with paperwork.”

I liked her.

Pell ignored us.

“What do you want it for?”

I looked at the regulator.

Barrier experiments.

Mana feed?

Not yet.

Could be measuring low-output spell components.

Could be material testing.

Could become something else entirely.

“I don’t know yet.”

Pell nodded.

No disappointment.

“Then test broadly.”

That was permission to be annoying.

Dangerous.

I pulled my notes back.

Then remembered the other question.

Kestrin shale.

I had not brought it.

It had followed me anyway.

“Do you use processed kestrin?”

Pell did not react.

Good.

“Sometimes.”

My attention sharpened.

Vessa saw.

Eyebrows.

Fuck.

“For what?”

“Kiln packing. Cheap isolation blocks. Some fixture beds.”

“Mana work?”

“Sometimes.”

“What treatment?”

“Depends.”

“On?”

“What you want.”

Of course.

“Do you make it?”

“No.”

“Who does?”

Pell shrugged.

“Stone yards. Kiln suppliers. Ward-material shops.”

Same broad answer as Edrin.

Good.

“Consistent batches?”

He looked at me now.

“Consistent how?”

“Mana behavior within a narrow band.”

“How narrow?”

I stopped.

Guild evidence.

Do not imply access.

“Better than cheap material usually deserves.”

Pell considered.

“That is not a number.”

“No.”

“Then I don’t know.”

Fair.

“Is unusually consistent processed shale difficult?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Stone varies.”

“Treatment?”

“Also varies.”

“Heat?”

“Yes.”

“Solution?”

“Sometimes.”

“Pressure?”

“Sometimes.”

“Order?”

“Yes.”

“Cooling?”

“Yes.”

“Grain?”

“Yes.”

I smiled.

He frowned.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You like this.”

“Yes.”

Vessa said, “There he goes.”

I ignored her.

“If you wanted consistent output, where would you spend effort?”

Pell stared at me.

“That is not one question.”

“Choose.”

“Sorting.”

Interesting.

“Before treatment?”

“Yes.”

“Why not control treatment better?”

“Because bad stone stays bad stone.”

Good.

“What do you sort for?”

“Depends.”

I waited.

He did not continue.

“Pell.”

“What?”

“You cannot answer every question with depends.”

“Yes.”

Vessa laughed.

I changed angle.

“Say you want mild mana suppression.”

He looked at me.

“Why?”

“Hypothetical.”

“No.”

“What?”

“That is not hypothetical.”

I went still.

He pointed at my face.

Of course.

“What are you working on?”

“Nothing.”

“Bad answer.”

“I am not working on it.”

“Worse.”

Vessa set down her file.

Now both interested.

I could tell them. Public knowledge: processed kestrin exists, some treated forms retain mana, mild suppression known in trade. Not Guild evidence. No Pel Marris. No Darrowmere.

“I saw processed shale that behaved more consistently than I expected.”

True.

“Where?”

“Not mine to say.”

Pell nodded.

Accepted.

Good.

“What did it do?”

“Suppression. Retention.”

“How much?”

“Not mine to say.”

He looked annoyed.

Now he understood Edrin’s life.

Good.

“Then what do you want?”

“Who can make consistent material.”

Pell thought.

Actually thought.

Not performing expertise.

His eyes went to the shelves.

Then the kiln.

Then Vessa.

She shrugged.

“Stone yards can sort.”

Pell said, “Kiln people can control heat.”

Vessa said, “Ward shops can test after.”

Pell nodded.

“Best consistency might not come from one maker.”

I stopped.

There.

Supply chain.

Not secret master craftsman.

Sort here.

Treat there.

Test elsewhere.

Rebatch.

Ordinary manufacturing.

“How would you buy it?”

Pell asked.

“What?”

“If you needed consistent suppression.”

“I would find whoever makes the best.”

“No.”

I stared.

He continued.

“I would buy too much cheap stock, test it, keep the good.”

That was crude.

Effective.

“Wasteful.”

“Yes.”

“Expensive.”

“Less than making a kiln.”

Right.

“What if volume is large?”

“Then contract sorting.”

“Where?”

“Supplier.”

“Who?”

Pell shrugged.

“I buy from Dorrin Stone when I need shale.”

Name.

Small.

Ordinary.

“Are they good?”

“They deliver.”

“That is not good.”

“It is very good.”

Fair.

“Do they process?”

“Some. Mostly sell.”

“Would they know who does?”

“Yes.”

There.

Lead.

Easy.

Legal.

Public.

My brain began building.

Dorrin Stone.

Ask about sorted kestrin.

Ask who treats for mana suppression.

Do not mention sacks.

Do not mention Guild.

Compare trade terms.

Maybe find Pel Marris indirectly.

No.

Stop.

Pell watched me.

“Do not go now.”

I looked at him.

“Why?”

“Because I need you.”

That changed everything.

“For?”

He pointed at the ceramic-bodied regulator.

The cracked one.

“Still failing.”

I had forgotten.

Good.

“What do you need?”

“Hands.”

I looked at my blisters.

“Bad timing.”

“Eyes, then.”

He set the ceramic regulator on the bench.

Hairline crack around inlet.

Dark staining.

Same as before.

“What changed?”

“Nothing.”

“That is poor progress.”

“Yes.”

“What have you tested?”

He handed me a page.

Pressure.

Heat.

Firing.

Fluid.

Clamp.

Assembly torque.

Three ceramics.

Two glazes.

Same crack location in four of seven trials.

Not random.

“Why ceramic?”

“Customer wants nonmetal body.”

“Why?”

“Reagent compatibility.”

“What reagent?”

“No.”

Fair.

“What do you want me to see?”

“Nothing.”

I looked at him.

“You said you need me.”

“I need another person to watch assembly.”

That was disappointingly sensible.

Vessa said, “I watched.”

“You assemble.”

“I can watch.”

“You correct.”

“That is watching with value.”

Pell looked at me.

“You don’t know enough.”

“Thank you.”

“Exactly.”

Excellent.

He wanted ignorance.

I sat.

Pell assembled the regulator.

Slowly.

Not his normal speed.

He narrated nothing.

Good.

Body.

Collar.

Seal.

Inlet.

Ceramic housing.

He tightened.

Stopped.

Looked at me.

“What?”

“You paused.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Habit.”

“What habit?”

“Nothing.”

“Do it again.”

He disassembled.

Reassembled.

Same point.

Tiny pause before final collar turn.

“Again.”

Third.

Pause.

“There.”

“What?”

“You change grip.”

Pell looked at his hand.

“No.”

“Yes.”

Vessa leaned closer.

Pell assembled again. At final collar turn, his thumb shifted from side pressure to top.

Small.

Why?

Ceramic body wanted stability.

He was unconsciously bracing.

“Why do you brace there?”

“I don’t.”

“You do.”

He tried without.

The body rotated a fraction in the fixture.

Vessa said, “Oh.”

Pell stared.

The fixture held the collar.

Not body.

Like regulator fixture four.

But ceramic had less tolerance for torsion.

Pell’s hand had been compensating.

His hand was part of the fixture.

Not repeatable.

“There,” I said.

Pell looked pleased.

Very pleased.

Dangerously.

“Again.”

We ran it.

Vessa assembled.

Different grip.

Less rotation.

Pell assembled with deliberate body support.

Less.

Not solved.

Better.

He wrote.

Just finding a thing.

I forgot Dorrin Stone for almost an hour.

That might have been the most interesting part. By the time Pell stopped, my stomach had started making decisions.

“Food,” Vessa said.

“I know.”

“That was not for you.”

Pell looked at her.

She pointed at the door.

“Both.”

We left.

Pell did not.

Vessa came back, grabbed his sleeve, and pulled.

He resisted for two steps.

Then went.

I followed because apparently I had been included.

The food stall was around the corner.

Rice.

Roasted vegetables.

Thin slices of pork.

Pell paid for his.

Vessa paid for hers.

I paid for mine.

No one offered.

Good.

We ate standing.

I remembered Dorrin Stone halfway through the pork.

“Dorrin Stone.”

Pell looked annoyed.

“You remembered.”

“Yes.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Why?”

“They close early.”

I looked at the sun.

He was right.

Good.

“Tomorrow.”

Vessa said, “What are you buying?”

“Questions.”

“Expensive.”

“Usually.”

She nodded.

No interest beyond that.

Pell finished eating.

“Bring regulator next time.”

“I brought it today.”

“Next time.”

“For?”

“Vibration.”

“Mechanical?”

“Yes.”

“Mana?”

“No.”

Good.

I could wait.

Again.

Suspicious.

We returned to the workshop.

I collected my regulator and notes.

The ceramic problem remained on the bench.

Pell had work.

Vessa had work.

I had a stone supplier name.

Not a conspiracy.

Not an answer.

A place that sold rocks.

Excellent.

Outside, I considered going to Dorrin Stone anyway.

Closed soon did not mean closed.

Could hurry.

Could arrive before shutter.

Could ask one question.

Could get one name.

Then another.

Old pattern.

One more.

I looked at my hands.

Better.

Not good.

My stomach full.

Body tired in a clean way.

Tomorrow existed.

I went home by the long route.

Not because it was efficient.

Because the weather was good.
