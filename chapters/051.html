PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 51

THE SIGNAL

The blister was worse in the morning.

Not dramatically. It had simply become more interested in existing.

I found it while buttoning my shirt. A tender spot at the base of my thumb, skin lifted and pale at the edge. I pressed it once.

Bad decision.

I stopped pressing it.

The rest of me was better. Ribs quiet. Legs good. Channels quiet. No headache. No strange pressure behind the eyes.

That was enough to make me suspicious of the day.

I ate bread, cheese, and an apple that had been cut yesterday and had therefore lost most of its enthusiasm. Then I looked at the regulator sitting on my table.

I had not brought it home.

Pell had kept it.

That was probably correct.

I had the notes.

The notes were mine.

The device was his workshop problem.

Good.

I put the notebook away.

There was no reason to go to Pell.

No reason to go to Dorrin Stone.

No reason to go to Varo Chemical.

No reason to find Alden.

No reason to look for Sevren.

I had, apparently, developed a morning routine consisting of identifying things I could do and then refusing to do half of them.

Progress.

The Guild had work.

That was different.

I went.

The contract hall was already crowded. Not packed. Crowded in the ordinary way, which was worse because nobody had a dramatic reason for being in each other's way.

A cart driver argued with a clerk about a missing seal.

Two women in road leathers were comparing bruises.

A man asleep on a bench had one boot off and a Guild form across his chest.

Someone had spilled grain near the door.

Nobody was stopping the entire institution to address it.

I liked that.

I went to the board.

Most of the jobs were ordinary.

Fence inspection.

South road escort.

Warehouse unloading.

Two days of guard work for a salt wagon.

A farm contract involving three pigs and a broken pen.

I considered the pigs.

Then decided against them.

A voice behind me said, “Greg?”

I turned.

A Guild runner I did not know stood there holding a folded sheet.

“Yes.”

“You've got a moment?”

“I have several.”

He handed me the paper.

“Field desk.”

I opened it.

REQUEST: GREGORY
BRONZE
AVAILABLE TODAY
ASK FOR TAVIN.

I stared at the name.

Tavin.

No.

Wrong Tavin.

Almost certainly.

The universe did not get to recycle every name just because I had been thinking about memory too much.

“Which Tavin?”

The runner blinked.

“The field lead.”

“Family name?”

“Didn't give one.”

“Where?”

“West gate office.”

“What kind of work?”

“Didn't say.”

I looked at the paper.

This was suspicious.

Not because of Tavin.

Because somebody had requested me.

That was new enough to make the rest of my brain stand up.

The runner was already walking away.

“Wait.”

He stopped.

“Was this a specific request?”

“Yes.”

“From him?”

“That's what the form says.”

“Why me?”

The runner shrugged.

“Ask him.”

Good answer.

I folded the paper.

Then I almost went to the board again.

Pigs.

No.

West gate.

I went.

The field office was a narrow room beside the gatehouse with two desks, a rack of route markers, and a window overlooking the freight road. A woman in a dark road coat stood over a map. She looked up when I entered.

“Greg.”

“Apparently.”

She pointed at the chair.

I sat.

She did not.

“I'm Tavin.”

I waited.

No recognition.

Good.

“Tavin what?”

“Marek Rusk.”

Nothing.

Definitely not the wagon Tavin.

He had been sixteen.

This Tavin was perhaps forty.

I was getting better at names.

“You asked for me.”

“I did.”

“Why?”

She picked up a report.

“West Orchard.”

I recognized the job.

“Alden's escort.”

“Yes.”

“Pessa and Dorn.”

“Yes.”

She tapped the page.

“You wrote that the rear cart was running two inches low on the left wheel before anyone noticed.”

“I noticed.”

“The team leader wrote that you checked the wheel before loading the second cart.”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“It was leaning.”

She looked at me.

I looked back.

That was the entire explanation.

She set the report down.

“I've got a roadwright crew working at the old south freight spur this afternoon. They're replacing a failed drain crossing.”

“How failed?”

“Enough that carts are using one side of the road.”

“How much traffic?”

“More than they should.”

“Why?”

“Because the east spur is still carrying Darrowmere reroutes.”

There it was.

Darrowmere.

Not the mystery.

Traffic.

The road had changed because people had changed routes.

That was the sort of consequence I liked. It did not require a monster.

“What do you need me for?”

Tavin pointed to the map.

“The roadwright owns the repair. The Guild lead owns the contract. The haulers own their carts. The gate clerk controls the queue.”

“And?”

“And all four can do their jobs correctly and still make a mess.”

I smiled.

She noticed.

“That's what the report says you are good at.”

“I wouldn't phrase it that way.”

“I didn't.”

Fair.

She tapped a narrow section of road.

“The repair crew needs a clear lane. Carts need to get through. The drain is unstable enough that heavy loads shouldn't cross while they're resetting the stone. The clerk can hold carts at the gate, but she can't see the repair site.”

“What can I do?”

“Watch the lane.”

I waited.

“If the roadwright calls a stop, enforce it for the waiting carts. If the queue starts moving when the repair crew isn't ready, stop the movement. If the repair crew changes the condition and needs traffic handled differently, tell the clerk.”

“That's the job?”

“That's the job.”

“How much discretion?”

She considered.

“Enough to stop a cart entering the repair lane. Not enough to redesign the road.”

“I wasn't going to redesign the road.”

She looked at me.

“Good.”

I disliked how often that word was becoming associated with me.

“How long?”

“Three hours.”

“Pay?”

“Eight copper.”

I looked at the map again.

Eight copper.

Ordinary Bronze work.

Someone had looked at something I had done and decided I might be useful for another thing.

Not because I was special.

Because a report had survived contact with another person's problem.

That was different.

“I'll do it.”

“Good.”

She handed me a brass route marker.

“You're lane support. Yellow marker means hold. White means clear.”

“Who controls the red?”

“Roadwright.”

“Good.”

She paused.

“You ask a lot of questions.”

“I ask the ones that prevent me from accidentally becoming the roadwright.”

That almost made her smile.

“Go to the south spur. Foreman is Leth. He knows you're coming.”

I stood.

At the door she said, “Greg.”

I turned.

“If the road is unsafe, don't try to be useful about it.”

That was a strange sentence.

I liked it.

“I won't.”

The south freight spur smelled like wet stone, horse, and old water.

The drain crossing had failed in a boring way.

A line of paving had sunk near the center, leaving a shallow trough where runoff collected. A timber barrier marked the repair lane. Three roadwrights were already working beside it.

Leth was a broad man with gray in his beard and a hammer tucked through his belt.

He looked at the brass marker.

Then at me.

“You're the Bronze.”

“Apparently.”

“You know the job?”

“Hold traffic when you tell me.”

“Mostly.”

“What's the other part?”

He pointed with his chin.

“People.”

I looked.

A cart waited fifty yards back.

Another behind it.

Then three more.

The gate clerk was arguing with a driver who wanted to pass before the queue closed.

No one looked incompetent.

That was the problem.

Everyone had a reasonable thing they wanted.

Leth crouched beside the broken stones.

“We're pulling these six. Once they're out, nobody crosses until the base is packed.”

“How long?”

“Could be ten minutes.”

“Could?”

“Could be twenty.”

“Good.”

He looked at me.

“You like maybe?”

“No.”

“Me neither.”

He returned to work.

I took the yellow marker.

The first cart rolled forward.

I raised it.

“Hold.”

The driver leaned down.

“I'm loaded for the north warehouse.”

“Hold.”

“I was told the road was open.”

“It was.”

“Now?”

“Now it isn't.”

He stared at me.

Then at the marker.

Then at the roadwright crew.

He shrugged.

“Fine.”

He stopped.

That was almost disappointingly easy.

The second cart tried a minute later.

I stopped it too.

Then the third.

Then a handcart came through from the opposite side.

That one was allowed.

Small load.

The roadwrights were not working on that lane.

I watched the difference.

Not weight alone.

Position.

Good.

The next half hour was mostly waiting.

That was harder than it sounded.

I had spent years believing that if I could see the system clearly enough, I should intervene.

Now I had a yellow marker and a man with a hammer.

My job was to hold it.

So I held it.

The queue grew.

The gate clerk came down.

“We're backing up.”

“Yes.”

“Can you let the first two through?”

I looked at Leth.

He was kneeling in the trench, packing crushed stone into the base.

He looked up.

“Not yet.”

I raised the yellow marker toward the clerk.

“Not yet.”

She frowned.

“That puts the whole queue into the gate.”

“Yes.”

“Then I need to send them around.”

“Probably.”

She looked at the road.

Then at the queue.

Then back at me.

“How many?”

“All of them if this takes another fifteen minutes.”

She nodded.

No argument.

She walked back toward the gate.

I watched her go.

That was the job.

Not solving.

Translating.

Ten minutes later, a driver tried to cut around the barrier.

I stepped sideways.

“Stop.”

He stopped.

“You said north traffic could pass.”

“Not through here.”

“Where?”

“Gate road.”

“That's backed up.”

“Yes.”

He swore.

I understood.

He still had to stop.

Leth stood.

“Ready.”

I lowered the yellow marker.

“Clear.”

The first cart crossed.

Slow.

The wheel passed over the repaired base.

Nothing happened.

Second cart.

Then third.

The roadwrights watched each axle.

I watched them.

Everyone had a job.

Nobody needed me to be clever.

The last cart crossed.

Leth picked up the yellow marker from my hand.

“Done.”

Three hours had become two hours and twenty minutes.

Eight copper.

No monsters.

No mystery.

No magic.

I walked back toward the city with wet grit stuck to my boots.

At the gate, the clerk called after me.

“Greg.”

I turned.

She held up a form.

“You want the route report?”

“Yes.”

She handed it over.

I read it while walking.

Repair completed.

Traffic held.

No damage.

No injury.

Queue redirected.

One driver complaint.

I smiled.

That was almost embarrassingly good.

Not because I had done anything extraordinary.

Because the page made sense.

The next person could use it.

I folded the report.

Then stopped.

A thought arrived.

I could improve the wording.

I looked at the form.

No.

It was not mine.

I kept walking.

At the first food stall, I bought lunch.

The vendor gave me a choice between stew and fried roots.

“Stew.”

“Good.”

“Why good?”

“Because you're holding your hand funny.”

I looked down.

I was protecting the blister.

“Apparently I'm delicate.”

The vendor laughed.

I ate.

Eight copper richer.

One report completed.

Someone had asked for me.

That last part stayed with me longer than it should have.

Not important.

Not yet.

But useful.

I finished the bowl.

Then went back toward the Guild.

There were more jobs on the board.

There were always more jobs.

I did not need to take one.

I looked anyway.

The pigs were still there.

I considered them again.

“No.”

The clerk at the desk glanced up.

“Talking to yourself?”

“Negotiating.”

“With the pigs?”

“They're unreasonable.”

She looked toward the board.

Then back at me.

“They pay six.”

“I know.”

“Then you're unreasonable.”

“Also possible.”

I left the hall.

My hand had started to throb.

Not badly.

Enough.

At an apothecary I bought a small tin of salve.

Two copper.

The woman behind the counter told me to keep the blister clean.

I almost explained that I knew how skin worked.

Instead I paid.

Outside, I opened the tin, smelled herbs and wax, and put it away.

One less thing to think about.

Or one more.

I walked home.

Tomorrow had no fixed shape yet.

That was fine.

For once, I had done one thing that somebody else had asked me to do.

Then I had stopped.
