CHAPTER 62

THE BACKSTOP

I arrived at Hessa's early.

This was not because I was worried.

It was because I had written MORNING on a calendar and discovered that calendars made me behave like a clerk.

Terrible development.

Hessa was not impressed.

“You're early.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I have a schedule.”

She looked at me for several seconds.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Everyone keeps doing that.”

“Take off your coat.”

I did.

The room smelled faintly of boiled herbs and hot ceramic. A stack of practice tiles sat near the wall. The same ceramic bowl from the other day was gone.

Someone else's lesson.

Someone else's problem.

Good.

Hessa held out her hand.

I gave her mine.

“Road?”

“Two days.”

“Magic?”

“One cast first day. None second.”

“What?”

“Coin Barrier. Directional. One breath. Moved water enough to clear a leaf mat from a culvert outlet.”

“Symptoms?”

“None.”

“After?”

“None.”

“Later?”

“None.”

She pressed along the base of my thumb.

The blister had become ordinary skin with a slightly different color.

“No pain?”

“No.”

“Training soreness?”

“Gone.”

“Road soreness?”

“Feet yesterday. Fine now.”

She checked shoulders, ribs, neck.

Breathing.

Balance.

Eyes.

She made me stand on one foot.

Then the other.

I smiled.

“Stop.”

“What?”

“You are pleased again.”

“I am excellent at feet.”

“Greg.”

I stopped smiling.

Mostly.

She had me crouch, rise, turn, walk heel to toe, step sideways, then repeat with my eyes closed.

Young body.

Good body.

Not perfect.

Mine.

Nothing strange.

Then Barrier.

Coin.

Five breaths.

Clean.

Drop.

No pressure.

Angled.

Four breaths.

Clean.

Five.

Faint pressure.

One.

Drop.

Gone quickly.

Hessa waited.

I waited.

“Again?” I asked.

“No.”

Of course.

“Palm?”

She looked at me.

“Brief.”

I blinked.

“That was easier than expected.”

“Do you want me to change my mind?”

“No.”

Palm-sized Barrier.

I built it against the floor seam.

Not my shoulder.

Not my hand.

External reference.

One breath.

Two.

Pressure behind the eyes.

One.

I dropped it.

Hessa nodded.

“Same.”

“Same as last time?”

“Approximately.”

“So coin work improved more than palm.”

“Maybe.”

“Because channel recovery favors lower load?”

“Maybe.”

“Because geometry stability is returning before throughput?”

“Maybe.”

“You're very helpful.”

“I know.”

She sat.

I sat opposite her.

“Small work is reliable enough for ordinary utility.”

I tried not to react.

Failed.

“Reliable?”

“Enough.”

“That is a terrible word.”

“It is a useful word.”

“How many casts?”

“No.”

“Hessa.”

“No quota. Need first. Clean recovery between. Stop if pressure accumulates.”

“Palm?”

“Still brief and necessary.”

“Sustained?”

“No.”

“Load testing?”

“No.”

“Moving?”

“Small, if you have an external reference and the task needs it.”

“That is new.”

“It is a clarification.”

“Same thing when I like it.”

“No.”

I leaned back.

She watched me.

“What?”

“You're going to work.”

“Eventually.”

“Today?”

“Nothing scheduled.”

Her eyebrows moved.

“I know.”

“Disturbing.”

“Yes.”

“Then eat.”

“I do.”

“Sleep.”

“I did.”

“Do not turn being healthy into an assignment.”

That sounded targeted.

“I wasn't.”

“You were about to.”

Probably.

“When do I come back?”

“Five days.”

I stopped.

“Five?”

“Yes.”

“Not three?”

“No.”

“That is progress.”

“Yes.”

There.

Simple.

No ceremony.

I wrote it on the card she gave me.

Five days.

Morning.

Another future square.

Hessa said, “Greg.”

I looked up.

“You are doing well.”

That was worse than Pessa.

I did not know what face I made.

Hessa did.

“Go eat.”

I left before she could ruin it.

Breakfast was eggs, rice, and something fried enough that its original identity no longer mattered.

Excellent.

I had nothing scheduled afterward.

This lasted twelve minutes.

Pell was back from Maret's.

I knew because I walked past his street without intending to turn and then turned.

Curiosity remained expensive.

The workshop door was open.

Pell sat at the rear bench with the second regulator body in pieces again.

Vessa was not there.

He looked at me.

“No.”

“I haven't asked.”

“You were going to.”

“Did it work?”

“Mostly.”

I stepped inside.

“That is not a mechanical result.”

“It is a customer result.”

Better.

“What happened?”

“Dressed burr. Reassembled. Bench clean. Maret mounted it with their wrench and their hands.”

“Flutter?”

“Once.”

“Hot?”

“Yes.”

“Then?”

“Settled.”

“Output change?”

“Small.”

“Same click?”

“Quieter.”

“So burr mattered.”

“Probably.”

“Not all.”

“No.”

I leaned over the pieces.

The seat now had a faint fresh contact line, more even than before.

Pell pointed.

“Maret wants two more bodies eventually.”

I looked at him.

“Eventually?”

“If this one behaves for a week.”

There.

A week.

Not solved.

Useful enough to continue.

“What changes?”

“Thread finish. Seat check before delivery. Maybe torque mark.”

“Maybe?”

“Customer may ignore it.”

“Then why mark?”

“So when they ignore it, I know they ignored it.”

Excellent.

“Do you need me?”

“No.”

I nodded.

He looked at me.

“What?”

“You took that well.”

“I am growing.”

“Vessa's word.”

“She's not here.”

“She'll know.”

Information disease.

I left.

At the Guild, I was not looking for work.

This was becoming a recurring lie.

The clerk saw me before I reached the board.

“Greg.”

“Yes?”

“Dorn left something.”

That changed the shape of the room.

Not because Dorn was urgent.

Because he had a life and had apparently used part of it to leave something with my name.

The clerk handed me a folded job notice.

WEST RIVER YARD
MILL FRAME TRANSFER
TOMORROW, SECOND BELL
BRONZE SUPPORT
CLEARANCE / STOP CALL
ASK FOR DORN

I read it twice.

“Requested?”

“Dorn put your name as preferred.”

“Preferred.”

“Not required.”

Good.

“What is a mill frame transfer?”

“Ask Dorn.”

“Is he here?”

“No.”

“Who leads?”

She checked.

“Ressa Vale.”

I stopped.

“Vale?”

“No relation listed.”

Good.

Probably.

“What does the job actually need?”

“Clearance support for a heavy frame moving from river barge to yard cart.”

“Rigging?”

“Ressa.”

“Cart?”

“Dorn.”

“Hoist?”

“River yard crew.”

“And me?”

“Clearance and stop call between hoist lane and cart approach.”

There.

Interface.

Again.

“How heavy?”

She told me.

I stared.

Not impossible.

Heavy enough that I had no useful business touching it.

Good.

“Magic expected?”

“No.”

Better.

“Why me?”

“Dorn's note says, ‘He stops early and doesn't grab things.’”

I looked at the clerk.

She looked back.

“That is my recommendation now?”

“Apparently.”

I liked it.

Far too much.

“When do I answer?”

“Before evening.”

“Can I see the yard first?”

She shrugged.

“Public freight yard.”

I took the notice.

Not accepted yet.

This was important.

I went to the west river yard.

Carrow's river edge smelled like wet rope, tar, fish, sawdust, and money.

Barges sat low against stone quays. Cranes and fixed hoists stood over loading lanes. Men and women moved sacks, barrels, timber, crates, and things large enough to deserve their own arguments.

Nothing stopped because I arrived.

Excellent.

I found Dorn near a cart with four broad wheels and a reinforced bed.

He saw me.

“You got it.”

“Yes.”

“You taking it?”

“I haven't decided.”

He nodded.

Good.

No offense.

“What are we moving?”

He pointed.

A barge carried a rectangular iron-and-oak frame strapped flat to its deck.

Mill machinery.

Not the working parts.

Support frame.

Large.

Awkward.

The yard hoist would lift it from the barge, swing it over the quay edge, and lower it onto Dorn's cart.

Simple.

Which meant not simple.

“Why clearance support?” I asked.

Dorn pointed again.

The cart could not sit directly under the hoist because a stone drainage lip and fixed bollard narrowed the lane.

The load would be lifted, moved laterally a short distance on the hoist arm, then lowered while the cart backed beneath it.

Two moving systems.

Hoist.

Cart.

Different operators.

Different sightlines.

There was a blind moment when the load crossed behind the hoist post from the cart driver's view.

Ah.

“Ressa?” I asked.

“Rigging lead. Good.”

“Yard hoist operator?”

“Old Marn. Better than good.”

“Why not one of them call the whole move?”

“Because Ressa watches sling and frame. Marn watches hoist. I watch cart.”

“And nobody sees the interface continuously.”

Dorn nodded.

“That.”

Reasonable.

Not because Greg was special.

Because four people each needed to own one thing.

“Who owns final stop?”

“Anyone.”

“Final go?”

“Ressa.”

Good.

“What do I own?”

“Clearance between load and cart. If you can't see, stop. If cart isn't where you want, stop. If load swings outside chalk lane, stop.”

“Do I direct hoist?”

“No.”

“Cart?”

“You can tell me stop. You can tell me inches if I ask. I decide steering.”

“Rigging?”

“No.”

“Load stabilization?”

“No.”

“Magic?”

“No.”

Clean.

I looked at the yard.

Chalk marks already traced the planned cart approach.

Ressa had apparently been here.

I walked the route.

Not because I owned it.

Because if I accepted, I needed sightlines.

At the first position I could see barge, load, hoist post, and cart nose.

At the second I could see cart bed and load underside but not the outer sling.

Ressa would see sling.

Good.

At the third, near the drainage lip, I could see the final lowering clearance.

I stepped there.

A yard worker rolled a barrel past me.

Normal traffic.

Tomorrow the lane would be closed during the lift.

“Who closes yard lane?”

“Yard crew.”

“Before lift?”

“Yes.”

“Bollard removable?”

“No.”

“Drainage lip fixed?”

“Yes.”

“Cart loaded suspension height known?”

Dorn looked at me.

“Empty height is. Loaded drop estimated.”

“How much?”

He told me.

“Estimate from this frame?”

“From weight and springs.”

“Any reason estimate is bad?”

“No.”

I crouched beside the rear suspension.

Dorn did not stop me.

Leaf springs.

Iron.

Used.

Maintained.

One side had a newer retaining strap.

I pointed.

“Recent?”

“Two months.”

“Why?”

“Old cracked.”

“Other side same age?”

“No.”

I looked at him.

“Does that change loaded drop?”

“Maybe a little.”

“How much?”

“Not enough to matter for bed clearance.”

He sounded certain.

He knew carts.

I accepted that.

“What could matter?”

Dorn pointed to the yard surface.

“Wheel position.”

Stone paving near the hoist.

One patch had been replaced with timber plates over a drainage channel.

“Rated?”

“Yard says yes.”

“For loaded cart?”

“Yes.”

“When checked?”

“Today.”

“By?”

“Yard crew.”

Good.

I kept walking.

Nothing hidden announced itself.

No cracked beam.

No ominous noise.

No one ignored an obvious problem.

It was a heavy move.

Professionals had planned it.

The interface genuinely benefited from another person watching.

I could do that.

A woman in a gray work coat came through the yard gate carrying three lengths of chalk and a folded sling sheet.

Dorn lifted a hand.

“Ressa.”

She looked at me.

“Greg?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Everyone needed another word.

She was perhaps thirty. Shorter than me. Strong hands. Hair braided tight against the back of her head. Bronze badge under the coat.

Not Antonius Vale.

Not related until proven otherwise.

Excellent.

Dorn said, “He's looking before he signs.”

Ressa nodded.

“Better.”

I liked her immediately.

Dangerous.

“What do you want from clearance?” I asked.

“Stop me before I create work for a healer.”

“Specific.”

She unfolded the sling sheet on the cart bed.

The frame had four lifting points. Two primary slings. Two balancing lines. Center of mass estimated slightly riverward because one side still carried mounting plates.

“Estimated?” I asked.

“Measured frame dimensions. Known plate weights. We did not disassemble it to weigh every piece.”

“Reasonable.”

“Yes.”

“Expected tilt?”

“Two fingers river side until tension equalizes.”

“Allowed swing?”

She pointed to the chalk lane.

“Inside that.”

“How much does wind matter?”

“Tomorrow morning forecast is low. Yard flags decide before lift.”

“No forecast in my head.”

“Good.”

There it was again.

Ressa traced the move.

“First lift is vertical from barge. Hold. Check all four points. Marn brings it quay side. Hold. I inspect balance. Dorn backs cart to first mark. Hold. Marn travels load over lane. Greg takes near clearance. Dorn backs to final mark. Hold. Then lower.”

“How many holds?”

“As many as needed.”

“Minimum?”

“Three planned.”

“Maximum?”

She looked at me like Hessa did when I asked for cast quotas.

“Enough.”

I sighed.

“Professionals are conspiring.”

“Yes.”

“Can I stop during a planned move without explaining first?”

“Yes.”

“Then explain after?”

“If I ask.”

“What if I see something outside my piece?”

“Call stop. Name it. Owner decides.”

Good.

“What if I think rigging is wrong?”

“Stop. Tell me. I decide.”

“Cart?”

“Stop. Tell Dorn.”

“Hoist?”

“Stop. Tell Marn.”

“What if the whole plan is wrong?”

“Then I'll be annoyed tomorrow instead of dead.”

Excellent.

Dorn said, “Dry run?”

Ressa looked at the empty cart.

“Yes.”

I had not signed yet.

She did not care.

We walked the move without the load.

Marn was called down from the hoist platform.

Old Marn was indeed old. White beard. Missing the tip of one ear. Forearms covered in faded blue marks that might have been old work tattoos or bad decisions.

He listened while Ressa described the dry run.

Then said, “No load.”

“Yes.”

“No hoist travel.”

“Yes.”

“Then this isn't my dry run.”

Ressa pointed at me.

“His.”

Marn looked at me.

“Greg.”

Apparently my name had become yard equipment.

“Yes.”

“What do you need?”

“Where do you look during lateral travel?”

“Head carriage.”

“Can you see the cart?”

“Front half.”

“Load underside?”

“Until post takes it.”

“Then?”

“No.”

“Ressa?”

“Slings and frame.”

“Dorn?”

“Left wheel, rear mark, Ressa when I can.”

There.

Actual blind space.

Mine tomorrow.

We moved the empty cart along the chalk route.

First mark.

Stop.

Second.

Stop.

Final.

The drainage lip was closer to the rear wheel than I liked.

Not unsafe.

Close.

“How much margin?” I asked.

Dorn measured with his boot, then crouched and used a short rule.

“Eight inches empty.”

“Loaded suspension drop doesn't move wheel laterally.”

“No.”

“Any steering correction during final back?”

“Shouldn't need one.”

“Shouldn't?”

“If first mark is right.”

Ressa said, “If he needs one, we stop.”

Good.

At the final position I walked around the cart.

The timber plates over the drain sat under the front-left wheel path, not final resting point.

I crouched.

One plate had fresh chalk across its edge.

“Inspection mark?”

Dorn nodded.

“Yard checked for movement under loaded barrel cart this morning.”

“Same weight?”

“Less.”

“How much less?”

He told me.

Not close enough to count as proof for tomorrow's load.

I looked at Ressa.

She said, “Tomorrow before lift, yard puts test blocks on the cart and rolls it across the plates.”

I stopped.

Already planned.

“Full expected wheel load?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Ressa smiled.

“There's the word.”

“Fuck all of you.”

Marn went back to his platform.

The dry run ended.

Nobody had discovered a fatal flaw.

We had discovered two places where stops mattered and confirmed that the yard already had a load test planned for the only surface section I disliked.

That was what preparation looked like when competent people had done it before I arrived.

Dorn said, “Well?”

“I'll take it.”

“Good.”

I pointed at him.

“No.”

He grinned.

We went back to the Guild together because he had another posting to close.

I signed the job.

The clerk told me the pay.

I asked her to repeat it.

Not because it was enormous.

Because it was better than lane watch and worse than I had started imagining during the walk back.

Reasonable.

Heavy transfer paid for responsibility, not heroism.

Good economy.

“Half day?” I asked.

“Expected.”

“If it runs long?”

“Hourly after fourth.”

“If canceled?”

“One copper show fee if you arrive before cancellation.”

“Wind?”

“Yard decides.”

“If yard delays two hours?”

“You wait one. Then you can leave unless Ressa renegotiates.”

I looked at her.

“This is unusually clear.”

“People complained.”

“Useful.”

“Complaining?”

“Sometimes.”

“Anything else?”

I almost asked whether other people had been preferred for jobs lately.

Pointless.

I almost asked whether north arch had requested me again.

Also pointless.

I had a job tomorrow.

Enough.

“No.”

The clerk looked suspicious.

I left before she could find me another form.

Tomorrow, second bell.

West river yard.

Clearance support.

Preferred, not required.

Paid.

Ordinary.

Good.

No.

Dorn had seen it.

Marn knew the hoist.

Tomorrow we would check again before moving anything.

Enough.

Too much onion.

Not enough salt.

Ate it anyway.

“Work tomorrow.”

It offered no objection.

Tomorrow had a job.

Five days had Hessa.

Next week had Alden and West Storehouse.

Pell had a regulator that might become two more.
