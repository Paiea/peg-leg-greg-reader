PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 84

THE PASSENGER

The horse had fallen over during the night.

This was not surprising. It had six legs and none of them appeared to agree about the ground.

I found it on its side beside the downstairs table while eating breakfast. One tail had caught against the wall. The other pointed accusingly toward the door.

I set it upright.

It fell over again.

The keeper watched from the stove.

“Movement representation,” she said.

“Shut up.”

My residual limb was more swollen than yesterday morning and less swollen than last night. Acceptable. Dressing dry. No heat. Right shoulder sore in the familiar band between neck and arm. Hands tender enough that the first grip on the crutches made me notice them and not tender enough to change anything.

Date cost.

Worth it.

I was deciding whether that counted as an observation or an accounting category when somebody knocked twice and came in before the keeper answered.

Sevren.

He had changed clothes. Same courier bag. Less mud. This seemed unfair.

“You busy?” he asked.

“No.”

The answer came out quickly enough that I distrusted it.

Sevren looked at the notebook beside my bowl.

“You sure?”

I put a hand over it.

“Yes.”

“Good. Come with me.”

“No.”

He blinked.

I blinked.

Interesting.

“What?” he asked.

“You gave no information.”

“I was going to.”

“Then do that first.”

He pulled out the chair opposite me and sat without asking the keeper. She put another bowl on the table anyway. He accepted this as his due, which explained why she liked him.

“North gate,” he said. “Small delivery. Cart out, cart back. I have a seat I don't need and you looked at my boots yesterday like you wanted to interrogate the road.”

“I did not.”

“You watched me leave.”

“Four seconds.”

“That is romantic for you.”

“Lysa would object.”

“Good. You learned something.”

I ignored that.

“How far?”

“Not far.”

“That is not a distance.”

“About an hour out with the cart.”

“Destination?”

“Roadside dye works past the north toll. Not a town. Barely a collection of buildings. I deliver a sealed packet, get a signed receipt, ride back with the same carter after he unloads two barrels.”

“What barrels?”

“I don't care.”

“You should if you're riding with them.”

“No, you should if you're riding with them. I am a courier.”

Fair.

“Why do you have a spare seat?”

“Carter's brother isn't coming.”

“Why?”

“Greg.”

“Relevant to whether the seat is structurally available.”

“He has a fever.”

“Then I don't want the seat.”

Sevren stared.

I stared back.

Then he understood.

“Oh. No. He hasn't been in the cart today. He's home. The carter told me at the yard.”

“Good.”

The keeper slid bread toward Sevren.

“You taking him somewhere?” she asked.

“North road.”

She looked at me.

I disliked this because she knew too much about the staircase and not enough about roads, which did not prevent the look from containing an opinion.

“Cart,” Sevren said.

“Seat?”

“Yes.”

“Back today?”

“Yes.”

She looked at me again.

I said, “I am nineteen.”

“Unfortunately.”

Sevren ate bread.

I should ask Nerin.

No. Maybe. An hour sitting in a cart was not medically identical to an hour sitting at Vale. Road vibration. Getting in and out. North gate paving. Unknown seating. Unknown place to rest. Return dependent on carter. If I went and the limb swelled badly, I would still be an hour from Carrow.

Good questions.

Not infinite questions.

“When do you leave?” I asked.

“Half an hour.”

Of course.

I looked at the crutches. Then the horse. Then Sevren.

“Can the cart turn around without me?”

“Yes.”

“Can I stay seated while you deliver?”

“Yes.”

“Is there somewhere dry at the destination?”

“Warehouse awning. Office too, probably.”

“Probably?”

“I've never been there.”

That mattered less than I wanted it to.

I stood.

The keeper said, “You haven't finished breakfast.”

I sat.

Fine.

I finished breakfast.

*

The north gate was farther than the barber and easier because I did not walk there.

Sevren had arranged a hired cart from a freight yard two streets over. Not for me. For the barrels and whatever return load the carter expected. My seat was a plank across the front with a thin folded sack pretending to be padding.

The carter looked at my crutches, then at the seat, then at me.

“You need help up?”

“No.”

I considered the height.

“Yes. Not lifting. Hold the crutches.”

He did.

Good.

Right foot on the iron step. Both hands on the cart edge. Push. Turn. Sit. The missing left foot attempted to participate in the turn and contributed nothing. My hip twitched. I stopped before the movement became larger.

Sevren had already climbed up on the other side.

He said nothing.

Also good.

The carter handed me the crutches. I laid them along the side with the tips forward and kept one hand through both cuffs so a rut could not throw them into the street.

Then the cart moved.

I had been in carts since the amputation. Hired rides through Carrow. Infirmary. Guild. Vale. Home. This was different only because the gate was ahead.

Which was stupid.

A gate was wood, iron, stone, guards, traffic, and paperwork. Carrow did not become a different world because we passed under an arch.

Still.

The north gate approached.

Two freight wagons waited in one lane. A drover argued with a guard over three goats and a fee I could not hear. A woman with a basket walked through the pedestrian side without stopping. The city wall looked less impressive from inside because buildings had grown against it and people used the base for shade.

Our carter held up a stamped chit. The guard glanced at the barrels, counted us, and waved us through.

That was it.

Carrow ended.

Not dramatically.

The paving continued for another hundred yards before becoming packed road. Houses thinned. Workshops spread farther apart. A cooper's yard. Two sheds with hides stretched under roofs. A field behind a low stone wall.

Then there was space between things.

I had known there was space between things.

I had memories of roads longer than this kingdom. I had crossed mountain passes in weather that killed pack animals. I had slept in ditches, camps, inns, barracks, wagons, one abandoned shrine, and once inside a grain bin because the alternative had been being stabbed.

Those were not this road.

This road had wet ruts from yesterday's rain and white flowers in the ditch. The cart wheel hit a stone. My whole body moved upward half an inch.

My residual limb objected.

Ah.

Road.

I shifted my weight.

Sevren noticed.

“Bad?”

“Not yet.”

“Useful distinction.”

“Yes.”

He leaned back against the cart side and closed his eyes.

“You're not watching the road?” I asked.

“I've seen it.”

“You're a courier.”

“Exactly.”

I looked at the road for him.

Fields. Ditches. A low orchard. Two boys carrying a ladder between them. A woman driving a narrow cart toward Carrow with baskets covered in cloth. A mile marker with the top corner broken off. Three riders passed us going south, one with a Guild badge on his shoulder and none interested in me.

Good.

The world outside Carrow was disappointingly occupied.

I had expected this.

I still found it satisfying.

*

The first problem was the plank.

The second was that the first problem had been obvious.

A plank seat on a city cart for twenty minutes was a plank seat. A plank seat for an hour over ruts became a technical argument conducted through the right side of my ass.

I moved the folded sack.

Worse.

Moved it back.

Better.

Sevren opened one eye.

“Comfortable?”

“No.”

“Good cart.”

“This is a good cart?”

“Wheels are still attached.”

My standards had drifted.

The residual limb did not like repeated vertical jolts. Not pain exactly at first. Pressure and swelling, then a hot pulse after a deeper rut. I braced with my right foot and immediately discovered that bracing every rut would exhaust the right thigh before we arrived.

So I stopped trying to defeat the road.

This was irritating.

I held the cart edge loosely instead. Let my trunk move. Protected the limb without locking the rest of me around it. Better.

Not comfortable.

Better.

Sevren said, eyes still closed, “You look less stupid.”

“You weren't looking.”

“I checked.”

“Why didn't you tell me?”

“Tell you what?”

“To stop bracing.”

“I don't know anything about your leg.”

“You know carts.”

“Yes.”

“And you knew I looked stupid.”

“Yes.”

“That was actionable information.”

He opened both eyes.

“You want me to say something every time you look stupid?”

I considered the scope.

“No.”

“Good.”

He closed his eyes again.

I hated him.

Friendship.

*

We stopped once before the dye works because the carter needed to piss.

I did too.

This created logistics.

The road had a ditch, a hedge, and no conveniently placed private room. Before the injury I would have stepped down, walked twenty yards, solved the problem, returned.

Now getting down meant getting back up. Ground beside the cart was damp and uneven. Hedge gap narrow. Crutches would sink if I planted them in the soft shoulder.

I looked at the road ahead. Then behind.

Sevren hopped down.

“You staying?”

“No.”

He waited.

I disliked waiting people.

“Find me hard ground.”

He looked around, pointed ten yards back toward the mile marker.

“Stone pull-off.”

Good.

The carter moved the cart ten yards after he returned, because apparently civilization could be constructed from requests. The pull-off was crushed stone. I got down with the carter holding the crutches again. No lifting. Right foot. Hands. Turn. Crutches.

The privy problem remained a hedge problem.

I solved it without writing anything down.

Progress.

When I came back, Sevren was sitting on the broken mile marker eating an apple.

“Where did you get that?”

He pointed at the orchard.

“You stole it?”

“No.”

“You crossed a wall?”

“There was a basket by the gate. One copper.”

“You bought one apple for one copper?”

“Three apples.”

“Where are the other two?”

He tossed me one.

I caught it against my chest with one hand and nearly dropped a crutch.

“Fuck.”

“Good catch.”

“Bad throw.”

“You caught it.”

I put the apple in my teeth long enough to get back to the cart.

Undignified.

Efficient.

The apple was excellent.

*

The dye works announced itself by smell before buildings.

Sharp mineral bite. Wet wool. Wood smoke. Something sour enough that my nose tried to reject it.

Then the road curved and the place appeared below us beside a narrow stream.

Not a town.

Sevren had been accurate.

Four long sheds. One stone office. Drying frames in a field. Cloth hanging in strips under roofed racks, reds and browns and one violent yellow that seemed personally offended by nature. Workers moved between vats and sheds carrying poles, baskets, ledgers, buckets.

Nobody stopped because we arrived.

The carter drove to a loading shed.

Sevren jumped down with the sealed packet.

“You coming?”

I looked at the step. Then the yard. Packed gravel. Mostly level. Office perhaps sixty yards away.

“Do you need me?”

“No.”

“Then no.”

He nodded and left.

That felt strange for approximately three seconds.

Then I sat in the cart and watched people who did not need me.

A worker rolled one of our barrels down a ramp with another man controlling a rope from above. They argued about whether the barrel had been overfilled. A woman came out of the nearest shed with both forearms stained red to the elbow and shouted for somebody named only by profanity. A boy swept wet grit away from a drain. Two older men examined cloth in sunlight and disagreed about color with the seriousness of surgeons.

I understood perhaps ten percent of what mattered.

Excellent.

The barrel came off the cart.

Nobody asked me to inspect it.

Also excellent.

I ate the apple.

A few minutes later, the woman with red arms walked past, glanced at my crutches, glanced at the cart, and said, “Waiting on courier?”

“Yes.”

She nodded and kept walking.

No pity. No curiosity. No invitation into dye chemistry.

Perfect professional interaction.

I almost laughed.

Sevren returned with a folded receipt tucked into his bag.

“Done.”

“That was fast.”

“Yes.”

“What was in the packet?”

“Documents.”

“What documents?”

“I don't know.”

“You carried them an hour.”

“Yes.”

“And didn't ask?”

“No.”

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

There was a whole profession in that answer.

I wanted to ask what he was responsible for if the documents were wrong. Whether he verified seals. What happened if the recipient refused. Whether he knew contents when hazardous. How liability worked. How routes were priced. Whether distance or urgency mattered more. Whether he could decline a destination. Whether couriers were expected to recognize political names. Whether he had ever carried something he later wished he had not.

Good questions.

Some of them.

“Did they sign?” I asked.

He tapped the bag.

“Yes.”

Enough for now.

The carter still had loading to do.

One barrel off. Two bundles of wrapped cloth on. Then another smaller crate. We waited under the warehouse awning because the cart bed was being rearranged.

There was a bench.

I loved the dye works.

I sat.

Residual limb throbbing. Swelling more noticeable. Shoulder fine because I had barely used the crutches. Right thigh tired from bracing before I learned not to. Hands better than a walking day.

Tradeoffs.

Sevren leaned against a post.

“So?”

“So what?”

“You wanted road.”

“I did not say that.”

“You stared at my boots.”

“That remains weak evidence.”

“Do you hate it?”

I looked past him.

The stream moved behind the sheds. Cloth lifted in the wind. A wagon on the road above us passed without turning in. Somewhere inside the long building, somebody dropped something metal and swore.

“No,” I said.

Sevren nodded as if I had confirmed the weather.

That was all.

Good.

*

The ride back hurt more.

Not dramatically.

Cumulative systems were rude that way.

I knew how to sit now, which helped. The road had not improved. By the time the north wall appeared in the distance, the residual limb felt too full and my lower back had joined the complaint because apparently it feared exclusion.

I could still think.

Unfortunately.

The city looked different from outside.

Not profoundly. Walls. Roofs. Smoke. Towers I had seen from streets but not arranged like this. The north gate was a dark cut in stone with wagons feeding toward it from both directions.

Carrow looked finite.

That was the difference.

Inside, it occupied everything because I moved through pieces of it. Streets connected to streets. Jobs connected to people. Guild to lodging to Vale to green door to infirmary to market.

From the road, it had an edge.

Beyond the edge was more road.

I knew this.

Old Greg knew this with thousands of miles of evidence.

Nineteen-year-old Greg had just spent one hour proving it again.

Different evidence.

The guard waved us through.

Carrow swallowed the cart.

*

Sevren got off two streets before my lodging because he had another delivery.

Of course.

“You're not coming back?” I asked.

“No.”

“I thought same cart.”

“You have same cart. I have work.”

Right.

He slung the courier bag across his chest.

“Tomorrow?” he asked.

“For what?”

“Nothing. I'm asking whether you'll be alive.”

“Probably.”

“Good.”

He jumped down.

I said, “Sevren.”

He looked back.

“Thanks.”

“For the terrible seat?”

“Yes.”

He grinned.

Then he left.

No promise. No new route. No invitation to become a courier. He had six deliveries yesterday and apparently more today. His life did not pause because I had discovered roads.

Good.

The carter took me to the lodging for another copper because the freight yard was farther than I wanted to crutch after the ride.

I paid without calculating whether I could have saved it.

Mostly.

*

The keeper saw me enter and said, “You look terrible.”

“Road.”

“That a place?”

“Yes.”

She pointed at the cot.

I obeyed before she could become pleased about it.

Crutches down. Blue shirt off because I was sweating. Dressing check.

Dry.

Swollen more than morning. No heat. No spreading redness. No drainage. Sore. Not alarming by the standards I had been given.

I elevated the limb.

The six-legged horse watched from the table.

Show-off.

I drank water.

There were things I could write down.

Cart seat bad over distance.

Bracing every rut worse.

Crushed stone better than road shoulder for crutches.

Need predictable return transport.

An hour out was not the same as an hour seated in a room.

All useful.

I opened the notebook.

Then stopped.

The first thing I wrote was not any of those.

NORTH ROAD: WHITE FLOWERS IN DITCH.

I looked at it.

Stupid.

Accurate.

Below it I wrote:

DYE WORKS YELLOW IS OFFENSIVE.

Also accurate.

Then, because I was not completely irresponsible, I added:

CART VIBRATION COSTS MORE THAN EXPECTED.

There.

Balance.

I closed the notebook.

Tomorrow I would probably hurt.

Tomorrow I might have work.

Holl might send data. Edrin might send nothing. Alden might appear with a bad idea. Lysa existed whether or not we had another date scheduled. Hessa still had not cleared magic. Vale still owned part of my future in copper-sized pieces.

Carrow was still here.

So was the road.

Neither had asked me to choose.

Good.
