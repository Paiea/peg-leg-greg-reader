PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 85

THE ARBITER

My leg objected to the north road before breakfast.

Not dramatically. Dramatic would have been easier to classify.

The residual limb was swollen enough that the dressing felt tighter, sore along the lower margin, and warm only in the ordinary sense that the rest of me was also warm. No new redness. No drainage. No smell. No fever. I checked twice because the first check had produced an answer I disliked rather than an answer I distrusted.

Still dry.

Good.

My right thigh hurt in places I had not assigned responsibility to yesterday. Lower back too. Hands were better than after a long crutch day. Shoulders were better. Apparently travel redistributed suffering instead of eliminating it.

Useful.

I wrote that down beneath DYE WORKS YELLOW IS OFFENSIVE.

The keeper put porridge in front of me and looked at the notebook.

“You writing about your holiday?”

“It was four hours.”

“Long holiday.”

“I delivered nothing.”

“You ate an apple.”

“That was not labor.”

“You watched other people labor.”

“Educational.”

She snorted.

I had planned to spend the morning downstairs. Planned was generous. I had decided not to go anywhere until the swelling reduced and then had immediately begun constructing exceptions.

Guild if requested.

Infirmary if necessary.

Food if unavailable here.

Alden if he arrived with blood.

Lysa if she arrived without blood.

Fire.

Structural collapse.

Unusually interesting noise.

The last category made the entire system suspect.

Somebody knocked.

The keeper looked at me.

I looked at the door.

“Interesting noise,” she said.

“Shut up.”

Holl came in carrying a wooden box under one arm and three folded sheets in the other hand.

He stopped when he saw me at the table.

“Good. Sitting.”

“I do that now.”

“So I heard.”

He put the box on the table hard enough to make my cup jump.

The keeper looked at the cup, then at Holl.

“Sorry,” he said.

She went back to the stove.

Holl sat opposite me and pushed the papers across the table.

“You said write it down.”

“I did.”

“No. I did.”

Right.

Prospective records.

Worker. Lot. Throughput. Ambiguous pieces. Returns or banding errors.

I had not thought about them yesterday.

That was probably healthy.

I unfolded the first sheet.

Holl had made columns.

Bad columns.

They were still columns.

“You did this yourself?”

“Iris started it.”

“She started it. I changed it because she writes too small.”

“Your writing is larger.”

“Better.”

“Those are not synonyms.”

He ignored me.

I read.

Six working days. Four lots. Two regular testers plus Holl on close pieces. Pieces sorted into three behavior bands. Set-asides counted. Rechecks counted separately on two days and not on the others because apparently measurement systems also required supervision.

There were return marks at the bottom.

Three.

I stopped.

“Three customer returns?”

“Three pieces returned. Two customers.”

“Why?”

“Wrong behavior.”

“Wrong band?”

“Yes.”

“Confirmed?”

Holl tapped the box.

I looked at it.

“No.”

“What?”

“You came here with rocks.”

“Yes.”

“My leg hurts.”

“You're sitting.”

“That is not the same as volunteering my table for magic testing.”

Holl stared at me.

I stared back.

This was satisfying.

He leaned back.

“Fine.”

That was less satisfying. I had expected resistance and prepared several excellent arguments.

“What do you want from me?” I asked.

“Look at the records.”

“Only?”

“Only.”

I pulled the second sheet closer.

There were numbers.

Numbers were dangerous because they looked like answers before anyone had asked a question.

Lot one: low ambiguity. Fast throughput. No returns.

Lot two: high ambiguity. Slow throughput. One returned piece.

Lot three: moderate ambiguity. Fast throughput. Two returned pieces.

Lot four: very high ambiguity. Very slow throughput. No returns yet.

I read again.

Then the worker lines.

Iris was faster than the other regular tester.

Not slightly.

Enough that I would have noticed if I had been paying her.

Her ambiguity rate was also higher.

Interesting.

The other tester set aside fewer pieces and processed fewer per hour.

Holl's rechecks converted some ambiguous pieces into saleable stock and rejected others.

“Which worker made the three returned calls?” I asked.

Holl pointed.

Two Iris. One other tester.

“Were those pieces marked ambiguous before sale?”

“No.”

“Close?”

“Don't know.”

“You don't know?”

“I didn't record every piece's original tray position and prayer history, Greg.”

“Reasonable.”

He narrowed his eyes.

“That sounded insulting.”

“It wasn't.”

It mostly wasn't.

I went back to the sheets.

The obvious answer was Iris was faster because she threw more uncertain pieces at Holl.

The second obvious answer was that this was bad.

The third obvious answer was that I had learned nothing from the last year.

“What happens to set-asides?” I asked.

“Holl tests them,” Holl said.

“You are Holl.”

“Yes.”

“What happens after you test them?”

“Band, reject, or hold.”

“Hold for what?”

“Better lot. Customer question. Sometimes I sell them as mixed suppression if somebody doesn't care.”

“At what price?”

“Less.”

“How much less?”

“Depends.”

“On?”

“Customer.”

Of course.

I looked at the lot totals again.

“Your expensive resource isn't the fixture.”

Holl's face changed slightly.

Not agreement. Attention.

“Keep going.”

I hated when people said that because it made me want to perform certainty.

I waited.

The keeper put bread between us without being asked. Holl took a piece. I took a piece. Nobody paid her immediately, which meant either trust or criminality.

“Maybe,” I said, “your expensive resource is you.”

“Obviously.”

“Not your skill generally. Your close-piece time.”

He chewed.

I pointed at lot four.

“This lot had the worst ambiguity and no returns.”

“Yet.”

“Yes. Yet. But you spent...”

I counted marks.

“Nearly twice as much recheck time on it as lot three.”

“Bad stone.”

“Maybe.”

“It was bad stone.”

“Fine. Bad stone. But if Iris is faster because she sends more close calls to you, that might be good or bad depending on what her time costs, what your time costs, what rejects cost, and what a wrong band costs.”

Holl stopped chewing.

I continued because stopping now would have been cowardice.

“And returns may not be the right error measure.”

“They're errors.”

“They are some errors. A customer can receive a piece at the edge of a band and never notice. Another customer can return a technically correct piece because their use is sensitive. A wrong piece might be consumed before anyone complains. A rejected piece might have been saleable. You don't see false rejects in returns.”

Holl looked at the sheets.

I looked too.

There it was.

The dangerous feeling.

I understand this.

Therefore I know what should happen next.

I could feel the sentence assembling.

Change the fixture.

Change the thresholds.

Give Iris a separate close category.

Price lots by expert-time burden.

Test a sample of rejects.

All plausible.

None mine yet.

“What decision are you trying to make?” I asked.

Holl looked up.

“Thought that was your question.”

“It is. Answer it.”

He considered.

“Whether to change anything.”

“Too broad.”

“Whether Iris is too loose.”

There.

Not the fixture.

Not the entire business.

Iris.

I read the numbers again with the actual question attached.

Different document.

“Then this doesn't answer it.”

Holl grunted.

“It tells you she's faster and sends you more close pieces. It tells you two returned pieces came from her calls. It doesn't tell you whether she makes more wrong calls per hundred clear decisions than the other tester.”

“Can calculate.”

“Not from this.”

“Why?”

“Because the lots differ.”

He frowned.

I tapped lot two, then three.

“If she handled more of the ugly material, comparing raw returns is nonsense. If she handled easier material, same problem the other direction. Also you recheck her ambiguous pieces. Those are not her final calls.”

Holl leaned forward.

“What do you need?”

Need.

Another dangerous word.

I nearly answered with a list.

Instead I asked, “What would you do if she is too loose?”

“Tell her to tighten.”

“And if that slows her down?”

“Fine if returns stop.”

“What if returns don't change?”

“Then I tell her to go faster again.”

I smiled despite myself.

“Good experimental design.”

“Don't call it that.”

“Fine. Business annoyance.”

“Better.”

I looked at the box.

“What are those?”

“The three returns and six pieces I pulled because I wanted comparison.”

“No testing.”

“You said.”

“Can I look at them without mana?”

Holl shrugged.

“Rocks don't bite.”

“Several rocks bite.”

“Not these.”

He opened the box.

Nine pieces of dull gray kestrin sat in cloth dividers. Cheap suppression stone looked disappointing even when it was causing expensive questions. The pieces differed in shape, surface, thickness. Two had chalk marks. One had a chipped corner.

I did not touch them.

“Which are returns?”

Holl pointed.

I looked.

Nothing visible distinguished them from the others.

Good.

Or useless.

Often the same thing.

“Do customers buy pieces or behavior?” I asked.

“Behavior.”

“You price by band.”

“Yes.”

“Do they care how close a piece is to the band edge?”

“Some.”

“Do you know which before sale?”

“Usually.”

Usually.

I rubbed my thumb against the bread crust.

“Then maybe there are two questions.”

Holl groaned.

“I came here for fewer questions.”

“Terrible choice.”

“What two?”

“Is Iris making worse calls? And are you selling the same tolerance to customers who don't actually need the same tolerance?”

He stared at me.

I immediately regretted the second question.

Not because it was bad.

Because it was interesting.

Interesting questions were how I acquired unpaid employment.

Holl reached for the papers.

“That's useful.”

“Maybe.”

“You always say maybe after the useful part.”

“Because you haven't tested it.”

“I'm not testing customers.”

“You already do. With invoices.”

He gave me a flat look.

I ate bread.

The keeper came past, saw the stones, and stopped.

“No magic.”

Holl and I both said, “No magic.”

She looked at me specifically.

“I said no magic.”

“You say many things.”

Then she left.

Holl smiled.

“Strong institutional authority.”

“You don't know the half of it.”

I pushed the papers back toward him.

“Don't change Iris from this.”

“I wasn't going to fire her.”

“I didn't say fire.”

“You thought it.”

“I thought you might blame the worker because worker is an easy variable.”

Holl's expression hardened.

“She is an easy variable.”

“Yes.”

“And sometimes the worker is the problem.”

“Yes.”

That mattered.

I had a tendency to become suspicious of obvious answers after discovering hidden ones. This was merely the inverse stupidity.

Holl tapped the page.

“So how do I know?”

I looked at the totals.

“You own the process.”

“I know.”

“You know the cost of a wrong band.”

“Yes.”

“You know what amount of recheck time annoys you enough to matter.”

“Yes.”

“Then pick a narrow comparison you can actually run without ruining the work.”

He waited.

I waited too.

He frowned.

“You're not going to tell me?”

“I can suggest.”

“Suggest.”

“Same lot. Split enough ordinary pieces between Iris and the other tester that material difference matters less. Keep their ambiguous piles separate. You recheck a small sample of each person's clear calls too, not only the pieces they flag. Then compare wrong clear calls, ambiguity, throughput, and your time.”

Holl considered it.

“Costs product.”

“Some.”

“Costs me.”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“I don't know.”

He nodded.

That was the correct response to ignorance. Disturbing.

“And customer tolerance?” he asked.

“Ask them what decision the stone supports.”

“They'll tell me they need good stone.”

“Yes.”

“Useless.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“So maybe ask what happens if the suppression is a little high or low.”

He looked at me.

“That sounds like work.”

“It is your business.”

“Thought you liked work.”

“I like other people's work at a safe distance.”

He laughed once.

The residual limb throbbed hard enough that I shifted in the chair.

Holl noticed.

Not dramatically. His eyes went down, then back up.

“You done?” he asked.

I wanted to say no.

The box was open.

Nine stones.

Three returns.

A business problem with actual data and a worker whose performance might be misread by the wrong denominator.

This was excellent.

Also my leg hurt.

“Yes.”

The word annoyed me less than expected.

Holl folded the sheets.

“That's it?”

“You asked me to look.”

“I did.”

“I looked.”

“What do I owe you?”

I blinked.

“For what?”

Holl stared at me.

Right.

Professional interaction.

I had somehow forgotten money while discussing somebody else's money.

“How long have you been here?” I asked.

The keeper answered from the stove.

“Forty minutes.”

Holl looked at her.

She shrugged.

“I have ears.”

I considered.

This was not Guild work. Holl had come to me. No transport. No magic. Judgment, not testing. Forty minutes.

“One copper.”

Holl reached into his purse.

Then stopped.

“Too little.”

I looked at him.

“You're arguing upward?”

“I pay Iris more than that for an hour when she's testing.”

“She's doing skilled production work.”

“So were you.”

“I ate your bread.”

“My bread?” the keeper said.

“Our bread,” I corrected.

“Two copper,” Holl said.

I almost objected.

Then didn't.

“Fine.”

He put two copper on the table.

The keeper took one.

I stared at her.

“Bread,” she said.

“That was not one copper of bread.”

“Table fee.”

“You have never charged a table fee.”

“New policy.”

Holl was already closing the box.

“You're encouraging her.”

“Yes.”

“Get out.”

He stood.

At the door he looked back.

“Same lot. Sample clear calls too.”

“Yes.”

“Don't change Iris yet.”

“Correct.”

“Ask customers what happens if they're wrong.”

“Approximately.”

He nodded.

Then he left with his stones and bad columns.

The room became quiet again.

I looked at the remaining copper.

One copper net after extortion.

Still more than yesterday's professional income.

Yesterday's professional income had been negative.

I had paid for transport to go nowhere useful and look at yellow cloth.

Today a testing shop owner had brought work to my breakfast table because something I had asked him to record had produced a question he wanted help answering.

The two facts did not cancel each other.

That seemed important enough that I refused to write it down.

I elevated my leg on the other chair.

The keeper put another piece of bread beside me.

“Free?” I asked.

“No.”

I ate it anyway.

*

By afternoon the swelling had reduced.

Not gone. Reduced.

I stayed downstairs.

This was partly discipline and partly because the stairs had become less interesting now that I knew I could use them under controlled conditions. Competence had ruined a perfectly good obsession.

I reread Holl's numbers from memory.

That was a mistake.

I did not have the papers.

Good.

I could reconstruct the broad pattern but not the exact counts. Also good. It prevented me from solving a business that was currently walking away from me in a wooden box.

I opened my notebook instead.

NORTH ROAD: WHITE FLOWERS IN DITCH.

DYE WORKS YELLOW IS OFFENSIVE.

CART VIBRATION COSTS MORE THAN EXPECTED.

Below that I wrote:

HOLL: COMPARE DECISIONS, NOT JUST PEOPLE.

I stared at it.

Too neat.

I crossed out HOLL.

Then I crossed out the whole sentence.

The keeper saw me.

“Bad?”

“Annoying.”

“What was it?”

“Something true enough to become false if I liked it too much.”

She considered that.

“Sounds like you.”

“Yes.”

I turned the page.

Blank.

Good.

Someone outside shouted about onions.

A cart rolled past.

Upstairs, a door opened and shut.

My leg hurt less than it had that morning.

I had one copper from work I had not gone looking for.

I had spent yesterday on a road for no reason that would survive an invoice.

Tomorrow remained offensively unassigned.

I left the page blank.
