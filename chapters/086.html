PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 86

THE PLAYER

The blank page lasted until nearly noon.

This was longer than expected.

I had assumed tomorrow would become assigned through ordinary violence. A Guild messenger. Holl returning with numbers despite being told to collect more than six days. Antonius deciding a one-hour task had siblings. Alden throwing something at a window. The keeper inventing a fee for remaining indoors.

Nothing happened.

My residual limb was less swollen than yesterday. The road soreness had settled into the dull kind that stopped being medically interesting and became personally annoying. Dressing dry. No heat. No redness. No drainage. Right thigh still sore. Lower back better. Hands almost ordinary if I did not squeeze anything hard.

I ate breakfast.

Then more breakfast because the keeper had made too much and claimed this was somehow my problem.

Then I sat.

The city continued without consulting me.

A wagon rattled past carrying empty baskets. Someone upstairs argued about a missing stocking. Two men outside spent five full minutes disagreeing about whether a barrel belonged at the front or back of a cart, then placed it in the middle. A woman came in to ask the keeper whether she knew anyone with a room for three nights. She did. Not here.

I considered going upstairs.

No.

I considered the Guild.

Dangerous. If I went there without a purpose, I would acquire one.

Pell and Vessa were still under explicit instructions not to receive me. Their instructions, not mine, but enforcement seemed likely.

Edrin had nothing for me.

Holl had work he needed to do without my face appearing beside it.

Antonius knew where I lived.

Lyssa was working.

Sevren was probably already somewhere else because this was the defining feature of couriers.

Jorren was at the Guild yard.

That was not a reason.

I stood.

The keeper looked over.

“Guild?”

“No.”

“Where?”

“I don't know.”

Her eyebrows went up.

I disliked being interesting before leaving the room.

“Out,” I said.

“Strong plan.”

“Yes.”

I took my crutches and left before she could charge me for it.

*

Carrow was easier when I was not trying to cross it efficiently.

This was an irritating discovery because efficiency had previously been one of walking's major advantages.

With two crutches, a missing left foot, and no appointment, I could choose streets according to grade, crowd, surface, shade, benches, and whether a delivery wagon had converted the pavement into a temporary wall. There was no penalty for arriving late because there was nowhere to arrive.

I went south because the north road still existed and I did not need to prove it twice.

A baker had set cooling racks near an open window. I stopped long enough to smell bread and not buy any because I had eaten twice.

A cooper was shaving a hoop. Three children were using a gutter channel as a boat race. A dog slept with its head on a doorstep where anyone with sense would have moved it, which meant everyone stepped around the dog instead.

I did too.

At the next corner I heard somebody shout, “Too high.”

Then a hollow leather thump against wood.

Then Alden laughed.

I changed direction.

This was not an accident.

The south Guild yard had a side court I had seen before and ignored because it contained nothing worth being injured over. Today the main practice floor was occupied by shield work, so half a dozen Bronzes had migrated into the side court with two leather throwing balls, a chalked wall, and apparently no supervision.

Jorren stood with his back against the fence eating something from paper.

He saw me first.

“No.”

“I haven't asked anything.”

“You came toward Alden voluntarily.”

“That is not a question.”

“It is a diagnosis.”

Alden turned.

He had one of the leather balls in his right hand and sweat darkening the collar of his shirt.

“Greg.”

“No.”

His smile widened.

“I haven't asked anything.”

“You were going to.”

“Yes.”

“Then no.”

“Coward.”

I stopped beside Jorren.

“What is the game?”

Jorren closed his eyes.

Alden tossed the ball once and caught it.

“Three marks.”

There were three chalk circles on the wall at different heights and distances from the center line. One low left. One chest-high center. One higher right.

“Rules?” I asked.

“Hit them.”

“In order?”

“Yes.”

“How many throws?”

“Three.”

“Scoring?”

“One point if you hit one.”

I waited.

Alden waited.

“That is the entire scoring system?”

“Yes.”

“No bonus for center?”

“No.”

“Distance?”

He pointed at a chalk line six paces from the wall.

“Same line.”

“Ball rotation?”

Jorren made a sound into his food.

Alden said, “You throw it.”

“Grip restrictions?”

“No.”

“Bounce permitted?”

“No.”

“Then why are there six people arguing?”

“Because Lio says the top mark is too small.”

Lio, already known to me and currently holding the second ball, said from the wall, “It is too small.”

“It is the same size it was yesterday,” Alden said.

“That does not make it good.”

This seemed reasonable.

I looked at Jorren.

“You play?”

“No.”

“Why are you here?”

“Lunch.”

He lifted the paper.

Apparently he had achieved what I was attempting.

Alden threw.

Low left. Hit.

Center. Hit.

High right.

The ball struck the chalk circle near its upper edge and dropped.

Alden turned toward me before it hit the ground.

“Three.”

“Two and a disputed boundary.”

“Chalk moved.”

“Then your scoring surface is degrading during use.”

“Three.”

“Two.”

Lio said, “Three.”

“You just complained about the target.”

“I can dislike the target and still understand circles.”

Fair.

Alden held the ball out to me.

“No.”

“You walked over.”

“I was observing.”

“You've observed.”

I looked at the line.

Six paces.

The side court had packed dirt, reasonably flat. The wall was old timber backed by stone. The leather ball was roughly the size of my fist and stuffed enough to deform when it hit. No blades. No magic. No moving machinery. No road.

Still standing to throw would require one crutch or balancing on the right leg. Bad. Sitting would change the distance and release height. Also the chair would need to be behind the line.

I could ask for a chair.

That sounded like participation.

“No,” I said again.

Alden tossed the ball to Lio.

“Fine.”

This was unexpectedly annoying.

They played another round.

Lio hit two. A woman I knew by face but not name hit one. Another Bronze missed all three and blamed the ball. Alden hit two cleanly and one that I would have counted if I were not invested in his suffering.

I watched his shoulder.

He did not throw hard. That was the first thing.

The target was close enough that force mostly created rebound. The better throws left his hand smooth, with less visible effort than the misses. His feet did more than I expected. Small shift. Hip. Trunk. Shoulder. Release.

Old knowledge supplied a dozen throwing mechanics from weapons, stones, darts, improvised objects, and one ridiculous winter campaign involving frozen turnips.

None of those bodies were sitting down.

Interesting.

Alden caught me looking.

“Chair?”

I hated him.

“Yes.”

Jorren laughed so hard he nearly dropped lunch.

*

The chair came from the training annex.

Not a special chair. A normal wooden chair with one leg slightly shorter than the others because Carrow believed furniture should participate in uncertainty.

Alden set it behind the chalk line.

I moved it myself.

“Back another half pace,” I said.

“Why?”

“My hand starts closer to the wall than yours when seated.”

He considered that.

Then dragged the chalk line back with his heel instead.

“That works too.”

“Less furniture.”

I sat.

Crutches went on the ground to my right within reach, not leaning where somebody could kick them. Right foot flat. Left knee bent comfortably. Residual limb clear of the chair edge.

The game became different immediately.

Not harder exactly.

Different geometry.

Alden handed me the ball.

Leather. Dense stuffing. Slightly uneven seam. Enough weight to matter. Not enough to hurt the wall or anybody with reasonable luck.

“Low left,” he said.

“I know the order.”

“Wasn't sure after the questions.”

I threw.

The ball hit the wall a foot above the low circle.

Everyone became quiet for exactly long enough to be rude.

Alden said, “Good wall.”

“Shut up.”

I had thrown mostly with the arm.

Stupid.

My old body expected the left leg to receive part of the rotational shift even though I was sitting. Not weight-bearing, exactly. More like the movement map had included a base wider than the one physically available. My trunk had stopped halfway through because some part of it expected support that was not there.

That was a theory.

Observed fact: terrible throw.

I adjusted my right foot.

Center target.

Second throw hit low and right.

Better.

Third missed the high mark by less.

“Zero,” Alden said happily.

“Yes.”

“You want advice?”

“No.”

“Good. I didn't have any.”

I stood by pushing through the chair with one hand, caught myself, then stopped.

No. Why stand?

The next player could wait.

I stayed seated while Lio retrieved the balls.

Jorren came over chewing.

“You're making the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where the game stops being a game.”

“It has three targets and measurable outcomes.”

“Exactly.”

“I missed all of them.”

“Yes.”

“That is offensive.”

“Good.”

Alden called, “Again?”

I said, “Yes.”

Jorren pointed his food at me.

“Game.”

“I know.”

“No notebook.”

“I didn't bring it.”

He stared.

I had not realized this until he did.

Interesting.

“No notebook,” I agreed.

Second round.

Low left.

I let my torso turn farther before release and kept the right foot planted without trying to make it replace both legs.

Hit.

The ball landed inside the chalk and bounced almost straight back.

I caught it against my chest.

Alden's expression changed.

“Accident.”

“Yes.”

Center.

Miss.

High right.

Hit edge.

“Two,” Lio said.

Alden frowned.

“That last one was boundary.”

“Chalk moved,” I said.

Lio pointed at me.

“He learns.”

The next round became competitive.

This was not my fault.

Alden had three points. Lio had two. I had two. The unnamed woman had two and clearly did not care about any of us, which made her dangerous.

I tried more force.

Worse.

I tried less.

Better.

I changed grip.

No meaningful difference.

I moved the chair three inches left.

Alden objected.

“Line is the line.”

“The chair remains behind it.”

“You're changing position.”

“You change your feet.”

“That is different.”

“Explain.”

He opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Jorren said, “Don't help him.”

Alden kicked the chair back three inches.

I stared at him.

“Now that's interference.”

“Play.”

I played.

The low target became easy first.

Not easy. Easier.

The center target exposed timing. The high target required more shoulder and less force than I thought. When I tried to generate power from the trunk, I shifted too far left and the chair rocked.

Once.

The missing foot planted itself in my body map to catch the shift.

Nothing touched the ground.

My left hip moved anyway.

The chair tipped half an inch.

My right hand shot to the seat edge and stopped it.

The ball dropped from my other hand.

Nobody laughed.

That was worse.

“I'm fine,” I said.

Alden nodded once.

Jorren had stopped chewing.

I reset the chair.

Pessa's voice came from behind us.

“You reached past what you had.”

Of course.

I turned.

She stood at the court entrance with a practice spear over one shoulder. She was sweaty enough to have been working.

“I know,” I said.

“After.”

I disliked the accuracy.

“Yes.”

She looked at the chalk circles, the ball on the ground, my chair, then Alden.

“What is this?”

“Game,” Alden said.

Pessa looked at me.

“Game,” I said.

“Good.”

That was all.

She pointed the spear at Alden.

“You're late.”

He looked toward the main yard.

“For what?”

She smiled.

Alden stopped smiling.

“Right.”

He handed me the ball.

“Hold that.”

“No.”

He was already following Pessa.

I now held the ball.

This was how property disputes began.

Lio said, “Your throw.”

Apparently it was also how games continued.

*

Without Alden, the game improved.

Not because he was bad.

Because he was annoying enough that beating him had become the objective, and his absence returned the objective to hitting chalk with leather.

I made three.

Then one.

Then two.

The unnamed woman made three twice in a row, accepted this with no visible emotion, and left when a Guild bell sounded.

Lio made two and complained that the ball was becoming soft.

I squeezed it.

It was becoming soft.

“Stuffing shifted,” I said.

Lio said, “Don't.”

“What?”

“Turn it into work.”

“I was describing the ball.”

“Greg.”

Apparently I had a reputation.

I threw again.

Missed low.

Center hit.

High hit.

The important part was not the score.

The important part was that by then my right shoulder had begun to tire in a way I recognized before it became pain. My hands were fine. My residual limb was quiet because I had been sitting. Right thigh did almost nothing. Trunk did more.

Different cost.

I noticed it.

Then I kept playing for one more round.

Not because I had failed to notice.

Because I wanted one more round.

This distinction felt dangerously like philosophy, so I refused to examine it.

I scored one.

Terrible decision.

Worth it.

Jorren finished his lunch and finally walked over.

“My turn.”

I handed him the ball.

“You said you don't play.”

“I said I wasn't playing.”

“That was forty minutes ago.”

“Yes.”

He stood behind the line.

Low left. Hit.

Center. Hit.

High right.

Hit.

Three throws. Three clean impacts.

No wasted motion.

I stared.

Lio stared.

Jorren handed the ball back.

“You asshole,” I said.

He smiled.

“I hit people for a living.”

“That is not the same skill.”

“Apparently close enough.”

“Again.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Lunch is over.”

He walked away.

I hated friendship.

*

I left before my shoulder made the decision for me.

This was partly because I was learning.

Mostly it was because Lio needed the chair back inside and I refused to be carried with it.

Standing after sitting that long required a moment. Right foot placed. One hand on chair. One on crutch. Rise. Balance. Second crutch. Good.

The residual limb had not swollen much while I played. It noticed the first ten crutch steps afterward, though. Blood shift. Pressure. Familiar pulse.

Manageable.

The right shoulder complained from throwing, not crutching. That was new enough to be interesting.

I did not have a notebook.

Excellent.

I stopped at a food stall and bought something fried around onions because the smell defeated whatever remained of my judgment.

The vendor wrapped it in paper.

“Careful, hot.”

I put the paper between my teeth while moving both crutches three steps to the side, then sat on a low wall.

This technique lacked dignity.

It worked.

I ate.

Alden found me halfway through.

He was sweaty again, which suggested Pessa had discovered what he was late for.

“How many?” he asked.

“Three.”

His face fell.

“Once.”

“Still.”

“Jorren made three first try.”

Alden's face changed in a much more satisfying way.

“What?”

“Yes.”

“He doesn't play.”

“That was also my understanding.”

Alden looked back toward the Guild as if betrayal might still be visible through two buildings.

“I hate him.”

“Yes.”

He took a piece of my fried onion without asking.

I slapped his wrist.

He got the onion anyway.

“Tomorrow?” he asked.

I almost asked what time.

Almost asked whether Pessa was training him first, whether the court would be free, whether the chair would be available, whether the same ball would be used, whether my shoulder would recover, whether repeated seated throwing had any useful transfer to knife work, combat, balance, future prosthetic use, crutch defense, or spellcasting.

All excellent questions.

None required yet.

“Maybe,” I said.

Alden nodded.

Then he stole another onion.

This time I caught his wrist.

He twisted free.

Progress for both of us.

*

Back at lodging, the keeper looked at my empty hands.

“No work?”

“No.”

“No road?”

“No.”

“No girl?”

“Lyssa has a name.”

“So no girl.”

“No.”

She looked at my shoulder when I lowered myself into the chair.

“What did you do?”

“Played a game.”

“With what?”

“Ball.”

She waited.

I waited.

“That all?” she asked.

“Yes.”

This answer felt suspiciously incomplete.

It was also true.

She put stew in front of me.

I ate.

Afterward I opened the notebook.

NORTH ROAD: WHITE FLOWERS IN DITCH.

DYE WORKS YELLOW IS OFFENSIVE.

CART VIBRATION COSTS MORE THAN EXPECTED.

The next page was blank.

I could write shoulder fatigue.

Seated rotation.

Reach boundary.

Phantom support assumption.

Chair position.

Release angle.

Three-mark scoring distribution.

Jorren is a bastard.

That one had merit.

I picked up the pencil.

Then put it down.

Tomorrow, if I went back, I could remember how the game worked.

If I forgot something, somebody would tell me.

Probably loudly.

I closed the notebook.

My right shoulder hurt.

My left foot felt as though it was tucked under the chair even though the chair had nothing under it except floor.

I moved the chair anyway.

The sensation did not change.

Fine.

I stole bread from the kitchen.

The keeper saw me.

She did not stop me.

Three points.
