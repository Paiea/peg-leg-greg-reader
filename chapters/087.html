PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 87

THE BLUFFER

My right shoulder objected to breakfast.

Not dramatically. It did not seize, tear, burn, or produce any of the symptoms that would justify using the word injury. It simply informed me, each time I lifted the spoon, that throwing leather balls at chalk circles had involved muscles I had not recently been using for throwing leather balls at chalk circles.

This was useful information.

It was also irritating because porridge was not heavy.

I changed hands.

The keeper watched me do it.

“No.”

“I haven’t asked anything.”

“You’re thinking about yesterday.”

“I think about most days.”

“You’re going back.”

“Maybe.”

She pointed the bread knife at my right shoulder. “No throwing.”

“I can play left-handed.”

“No.”

“That seems prejudicial.”

“That seems breakfast.”

I considered explaining that bilateral skill development had legitimate value.

Then I looked at the bread knife.

I ate.

The residual limb was better than it had been after the north road. Not normal, because normal had become a word with too many historical meanings, but the skin was dry, the swelling lower, and the deep ache from the cart had retreated into the sort of complaint that became louder only when I checked whether it was still there.

It was.

My missing left heel also itched.

That was less actionable.

By noon I had accomplished nothing except washing, eating, reading three pages of an old Guild notice I had already read, and deciding twice not to go watch Alden train.

The second decision was harder because it occurred while I was already halfway to the Guild.

I stopped at the corner.

The Guild was left.

The green door was right.

Straight ahead was a cooper arguing with a delivery boy about whether six hoops constituted a complete order when two of them were the wrong width.

I stood there long enough that the delivery boy noticed.

“Not you,” he said.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You looked like you were going to.”

“I was considering it.”

“Don’t.”

The cooper pointed at him. “See?”

I went right.

This was maturity.

Possibly.

The green door was open despite it being too early for most of the people who treated the place as an evening destination. Nessa was behind the counter with a rag over one shoulder and an expression suggesting she had already been disappointed by several objects.

Octavia sat at the back table with four cards face down in front of her and a little pile of copper bits at her elbow.

There were two other people at the table. I knew both by face and neither by name, which was ideal. Names created obligations.

Jorren occupied a chair beside them without cards.

He had a cup.

This was also ideal.

“You’re early,” Nessa said.

“For what?”

She looked at Octavia.

Octavia looked at me.

Jorren drank.

I disliked coordinated silence.

“What?”

“Sit,” Octavia said.

“No.”

“Good. Sit.”

“That is not how that works.”

“It worked.”

I looked down.

I had already moved toward the table.

Annoying.

The chair beside Jorren was ordinary, solid, and positioned so I could get both crutches against the wall without anyone stepping on them. I sat because standing indefinitely to prove that I had not been manipulated would have been a particularly expensive form of independence.

Octavia pushed four cards toward me.

“I don’t know this game.”

“Yes.”

“That usually matters.”

“Less than you think.”

Jorren said, “It matters.”

Octavia ignored him.

I looked at the cards.

They were narrow pasteboard, worn soft at the corners, each marked with one of four suits I recognized from other Carrow games. Cups. Nails. Birds. Towers. The values ran one through nine, except apparently not all values were present in all hands, which was either because the deck was incomplete or because I did not yet understand the deal.

Probably the second.

“How much?”

“One bit to stay in,” Octavia said.

“What am I buying?”

“The right to be wrong in public.”

“That seems overpriced.”

“One bit.”

I put down one bit.

Nessa called from the counter, “He’ll pay more.”

“I heard that.”

“You were meant to.”

Octavia dealt.

The game was called Four Doors, which was already suspicious because there were no doors involved.

Each player received four cards. One stayed hidden until the end. Three could be exchanged, one at a time, through a common row in the center. The goal was not simply to make the highest total. Matching suits mattered. Pairs mattered differently. Towers broke one kind of tie. Birds broke another. A hand that looked mediocre could become strong if the hidden card matched correctly.

I asked seven questions.

Octavia answered five.

“Why won’t you answer the other two?”

“Because one is strategy.”

“And the other?”

“Also strategy.”

“That seems arbitrary.”

“Yes.”

The first hand cost me one bit.

The second cost me two because I had misunderstood how aggressively one of the unnamed men valued a visible pair of nails.

The third I folded early.

Octavia looked disappointed.

“I had nothing.”

“You had a face.”

“I generally do.”

“It was a very informative face.”

I looked at Jorren.

He nodded.

“What did it say?”

Jorren considered. “You were about to explain why folding was correct.”

“That is not a face.”

“It is your face.”

I disliked this game.

I stayed.

By the fifth hand I understood enough to identify the first layer.

The cards mattered.

The visible row mattered.

What people took mattered.

What they declined mattered.

The order mattered.

Bet size mattered.

Speed mattered.

Eye movement mattered.

Posture mattered.

Breathing probably mattered, although trying to monitor breathing in three other people while remembering which towers had already appeared was excessive unless the expected return justified it.

It did not.

Probably.

Octavia won four bits from the man across from her by looking bored.

I watched carefully.

She did not look bored.

She performed bored.

The distinction was small enough that I only noticed because I had been watching her before she received the card that changed her hand.

Her shoulders had been loose.

Then they became loose on purpose.

Interesting.

The next hand I received two birds, a six of cups, and a useless tower.

Not useless objectively.

Useless to me.

The center row offered another bird.

I took it immediately.

Octavia glanced at me.

Mistake.

Not taking the bird. The speed.

I had announced that I liked it.

I could correct that.

On the next exchange, I waited.

Not naturally. I counted to three.

One.

Two.

Three.

Then I took a card I did not care about.

Octavia's mouth moved.

Not a smile.

Worse.

Recognition.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You did something.”

“I breathed.”

“You breathed judgmentally.”

Jorren said, “She does.”

Octavia flicked one copper bit into the center.

The man across from her matched.

The woman beside him folded.

I matched.

I had a good hand.

Not excellent.

Good enough that the probability depended heavily on what Octavia had hidden, which meant the probability did not actually depend on what she had hidden because I did not know it.

This was the problem with probability when people used it conversationally. They usually meant confidence wearing arithmetic's coat.

Octavia exchanged one card.

Slowly.

I watched her fingers.

Nothing.

Her eyes.

Nothing.

Her shoulders.

Loose.

Naturally loose or deliberately loose?

I had one useful piece of evidence.

She had noticed me trying to manipulate timing.

Therefore she knew I was thinking about behavior.

Therefore any behavior she showed me might be chosen.

Which meant I needed to model what she believed I believed about what she believed I was trying to observe.

No.

That road ended in old men writing books about poker.

I had read some of those books.

Most were terrible.

Different game.

Same disease.

I looked at my cards.

Three birds.

Good.

I put in another bit.

Octavia matched.

The other man folded.

Now it was us.

She looked at me.

I looked back.

There was an obvious move available.

Appear uncertain.

I knew how to appear uncertain because I was uncertain almost constantly.

The difficulty was appearing uncertain in the correct way.

Actual uncertainty made me look at variables.

Performed uncertainty should probably look at the money.

I looked at the money.

Octavia said, “There.”

“What?”

“That.”

“What?”

“You moved your eyes.”

“Eyes move.”

“Not like that.”

I stared at her.

She grinned.

“You're pretending.”

“I am playing cards.”

“Yes.”

“That seems included.”

“It is.”

I considered the problem.

Then I realized I had stopped considering the hand.

This was probably also information.

I pushed in another bit.

Octavia did not.

She folded.

I won.

I felt immediate satisfaction.

Then suspicion.

“You folded because I had the better hand?”

“Yes.”

“Not because of the eye thing.”

“The eye thing told me you wanted me to think you were weak.”

“That should imply strength.”

“Usually.”

“So why fold?”

“Because your first bird was fast.”

I looked at the discarded row.

The first bird.

Five exchanges ago.

She had retained it.

Not the card. The fact.

“You knew I wanted birds.”

“I knew you were pleased by one.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.”

“But enough.”

“For that hand.”

I looked at Jorren.

He was smiling into his cup.

“This game is stupid.”

“Yes,” he said.

I played again.

The next hand I decided not to manipulate anything.

This lasted perhaps twelve seconds.

Then the man across from me raised, and I deliberately scratched my jaw because I had once read that inexperienced gamblers touched their faces when uncomfortable.

Octavia laughed so hard she had to put her cards down.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That is obviously something.”

“You scratched.”

“My face itched.”

“No, it didn't.”

“You cannot know that.”

“You looked at me first.”

I had.

Damn.

The man across from me folded.

“Why did you fold?”

He shrugged. “Didn't like mine.”

“You weren't reacting to me?”

“No.”

This was insulting.

I lost that hand to Octavia.

I lost the next one because I spent too much attention deciding whether the woman beside her was genuinely impatient or performing impatience and failed to notice that I had discarded the only tower that would have protected my pair.

“Cards,” Octavia said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I am learning.”

“You're studying the room.”

“The room is playing.”

“The cards are also playing.”

“The cards are cardboard.”

“They're beating you.”

Jorren made a small choking noise into his cup.

I pointed at him. “You are not participating.”

“I know.”

“You don't get to enjoy this.”

“I paid for the cup.”

“That does not include me.”

“It does today.”

Nessa called, “Table fee.”

I looked at her.

She held up one finger.

“No.”

“Outstanding balance.”

“You stole a copper yesterday.”

“Collected.”

“You invented the fee after I earned the money.”

“Administrative timing.”

Octavia said, “She's very powerful.”

“I have noticed.”

Nessa smiled without warmth.

I returned to the game.

The next hand, I got nothing useful.

Not secretly useful.

Actually bad.

A two of cups, a three of nails, a four of towers, and a seven of birds with no useful relationship between them.

I exchanged.

Still bad.

Again.

Worse.

The rational choice was to fold.

I knew this.

Octavia knew I knew this.

The others probably knew because I had spent the first three hands asking about expected losses from staying in.

I looked at my cards.

Then at Octavia.

She was watching me.

Not the hand.

Me.

I put in one bit.

Her eyebrows rose.

Interesting.

The man across from her folded.

The woman matched.

Octavia matched.

I exchanged my three of nails for a five of cups.

Still bad.

The woman took a tower.

Octavia declined the row entirely.

I put in another bit.

Now Octavia looked at my hands.

Not my face.

My hands.

I kept them still.

This was easier seated. There were advantages to having fewer available dramatic movements.

The woman folded.

Octavia stayed.

Of course she did.

We reached the last exchange.

I took nothing.

Octavia took nothing.

I had a terrible hand.

I pushed in two bits.

Jorren lowered his cup.

Octavia stared at me.

I had intended to look calm.

That was impossible because I was having fun.

Apparently those were different expressions.

She looked at the center.

At me.

At my cards.

At the discarded row.

Then she folded.

For half a second I did not move.

Then I collected the bits.

Octavia pointed at me. “Show.”

“No.”

“Show.”

“Rules say I don't have to.”

“I know the rules.”

“Then this seems resolved.”

“Greg.”

“No.”

Jorren said, “Don't.”

Octavia looked at him.

He shrugged. “He'll become unbearable.”

“I am already unbearable.”

“Worse.”

Octavia leaned back.

“Fine.”

I gathered the bits carefully.

My heart was beating faster than it had any right to.

Not fear.

Not exactly.

The peculiar bodily excitement of having stepped off a ledge that was only one inch high.

I had lied.

Badly, probably.

But successfully.

No.

Not lied.

Represented.

Also no.

I had made a choice intended to alter another person's model of my hand.

That was lying.

Cards made ethics conveniently cheap.

I smiled.

Octavia saw it.

“You had garbage.”

I said nothing.

Her eyes narrowed.

I waited.

This was difficult.

The urge to explain arrived immediately.

I wanted to tell her exactly why the hand had worked, which observations had mattered, which had not, why I had stayed in despite negative expected value, and why her attention shifting from my face to my hands had convinced me that she was uncertain enough to push.

I wanted to explain that the bluff had not been a general success. It had been local. Against her. In that hand. After I had established a pattern of overanalysis and obvious behavioral manipulation.

I wanted to explain all of it.

I did not.

Octavia's eyes narrowed further.

“Oh, fuck you.”

I laughed.

Jorren slapped the table once.

Nessa looked over.

“What?”

“He didn't explain,” Jorren said.

Nessa frowned. “Is he sick?”

“I can explain.”

Octavia pointed at me. “No.”

The prohibition made it much harder.

I sat there with the explanation pressing against my teeth.

This was absurd.

Then Octavia started laughing.

Not because of the bluff.

Because she could see me suffering.

“You're awful,” I said.

“Yes.”

We played another hand.

I lost.

Then another.

Lost that too.

The bluff had not unlocked secret mastery.

This was disappointing but probably healthy.

By the time Lysa came through the green door, I was down three bits overall and had developed a completely unjustified hatred of the four of towers.

She stopped beside the table.

“What are you doing?”

“Losing.”

Octavia said, “Learning to lie.”

Lysa looked at me.

I said, “That description lacks context.”

Octavia made a delighted sound.

Lysa pulled out the empty chair beside me.

“I want the context.”

“You cannot have it.”

She sat.

“Why?”

“I am practicing.”

“Lying?”

“Not explaining.”

She looked at Octavia.

Octavia nodded solemnly.

Lysa looked back at me.

“How's it going?”

“Poorly.”

“Why?”

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

Lysa smiled.

“That badly?”

“Yes.”

She stayed for three hands.

She did not play.

She watched.

This was worse than Octavia watching because I cared what Lysa thought and because I had recently kissed her enough times to make her opinion physically salient.

I received a pair of cups.

Good enough to stay.

I exchanged once.

Improved.

Octavia watched my face.

I ignored her.

Lysa watched my cards only when I tilted them enough for her to see, which was probably accidental until I noticed her doing it, at which point it became impossible to know.

I won one bit.

“Very impressive,” Lysa said.

“Thank you.”

“Was that lying?”

“No.”

“Could've fooled me.”

I looked at her.

She smiled.

I liked her.

This was inconvenient.

The game continued.

My right shoulder began to ache from holding the cards too high.

That was ridiculous.

I lowered them.

My residual limb was fine. My hands were fine. My right leg had started to stiffen from sitting, so I shifted it forward and back under the table.

No catastrophe.

No revelation.

Just body.

After another hand, Lysa stood.

“I have to go.”

“Where?”

“Work.”

“Oh.”

She looked at me.

I realized the question had sounded disappointed.

I was disappointed.

That was allowed.

Probably.

“I'll see you later?” she asked.

“Yes.”

No model.

No calendar check.

No calculation of whether later meant tonight or tomorrow or an unspecified socially acceptable interval.

Yes.

She bent and kissed my cheek.

Octavia made a noise.

Lysa pointed at her without looking.

“Don't.”

Octavia raised both hands.

Lysa left.

I watched the door for slightly too long.

Then I looked back at my cards.

Octavia was smiling.

“No.”

“I didn't say anything.”

“You were going to.”

“I was breathing.”

“Judgmentally.”

Jorren said, “She does.”

I lost another bit.

Eventually my shoulder moved from complaint to warning.

Not injury.

Enough.

I put my cards down.

“Done.”

Octavia looked at the pile in front of me.

“You're down four.”

“Three.”

“Four.”

“Nessa stole one.”

“Different ledger.”

“Same purse.”

Jorren stood.

“You going?”

“Yes.”

“Guild?”

I considered.

Alden might still be there.

The throwing game might still be there.

My shoulder had already voted.

“No.”

“Home?”

I looked outside.

Late afternoon light had turned the street gold along one edge and dirty gray along the other. A cart rolled past with empty baskets. Two children chased each other around a post. Someone farther down the street was singing badly while hammering something metal.

I did not want to go home yet.

“No.”

Jorren waited.

I waited too.

He shrugged.

“Food?”

“Yes.”

That was enough.

We went to find something fried.

I did not explain why.

This lasted almost six minutes.

Personal record.
