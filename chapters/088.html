PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 88

THE OCCUPANT

The keeper asked whether I still wanted my room.

This was a reasonable question.

I disliked it immediately.

“What?”

“Your room.”

“I know which room.”

“Good.”

She was standing at the bottom of the stairs with a folded sheet over one arm and a bucket beside her foot. I was sitting at the kitchen table with bread, cheese, and the six-legged clay horse from the night market.

The horse had migrated.

I had not moved it.

This meant the keeper had.

I pointed at it.

“Why is that here?”

“Rent.”

“That is not rent.”

“Decoration fee.”

“You cannot charge me for an object I own.”

“I can charge the object.”

The horse remained silent.

Coward.

The keeper nodded toward the stairs. “You still want it?”

My room.

Upstairs.

Bed. Window. Trunk. Two shirts I did not particularly like. One shirt I did. A stack of paper. A chipped cup. Three objects I had kept because they might become useful and had not yet justified their continued existence.

My life, apparently.

“Yes.”

“You haven't slept there in four nights.”

“Five.”

She waited.

I hated when people let my corrections improve their argument.

“The downstairs room is working,” she said.

“It is storage.”

“You sleep in it.”

“That does not alter its architectural identity.”

“It has a bed.”

“It has boxes.”

“Your upstairs room also has boxes.”

“Fewer.”

She shifted the sheet to her other arm.

“I'm asking because rent comes tomorrow.”

Ah.

Money.

The least philosophical of all forces.

“How much difference?”

She named it.

I knew the amount already. I had been paying it.

That was not what I had asked.

“How much cheaper downstairs?”

She named that too.

I calculated.

Then I stopped.

Then I calculated anyway because refusing arithmetic on principle was stupid.

The difference was not trivial.

It was also not ruinous.

I could keep the upstairs room.

Probably.

Exact purse remained a thing I had avoided reconstructing because the answer changed every time I ate, rode somewhere, lost at cards, bought a blue shirt, or allowed Nessa to invent taxation.

I had enough.

That was a number.

Not a precise one.

Still useful.

The keeper watched my face.

I wondered whether Octavia had permanently damaged my relationship with faces.

“What?”

“You're doing sums.”

“Yes.”

“Want downstairs?”

“No.”

That came out before the rest of the sums finished.

Interesting.

The keeper nodded.

That was all.

No argument.

No warning about stairs.

No speech about practicality.

She picked up the bucket.

I became suspicious.

“You agree?”

“With what?”

“Keeping the room.”

“You pay for it.”

“Yes.”

“So keep it.”

“You just asked whether I wanted it.”

“Yes.”

“And now you have no opinion.”

“I have lots of opinions.”

“About this.”

She considered.

“Your room has better light.”

That was not the opinion I expected.

She started up the stairs.

“Wait.”

She stopped on the second step and looked back.

“I want to go up.”

“Now?”

“Yes.”

She looked at my shoulder.

My right shoulder was better than yesterday. Not perfect. Raising my arm high still produced a dull objection, but normal crutch use had been fine all morning.

She looked lower.

The residual limb had behaved. Dry. Less swollen. No heat. No new redness. The north road had mostly stopped collecting interest.

Then she looked at my face.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You are all doing that now.”

“Doing what?”

“Looking at my face.”

“I've always looked at your face.”

“Not analytically.”

“I promise I am not doing that.”

“That sounds like something someone doing it would say.”

She went upstairs.

I finished breakfast.

Not because she had won.

Because stair practice immediately after eating bread too fast was a poor decision.

There were fourteen steps.

Twelve, landing, two.

The rail ran ash-smooth beneath my left hand, three posts, wall return. Lerris Vane had built it properly. The stringer had been inspected. Nothing was secretly waiting to kill me.

This was useful to remember because my body had developed opinions about stairs independent of engineering.

The keeper came back down with the old sheet.

“You still going?”

“Yes.”

“Then stop staring.”

“I am assessing.”

“You know the stairs.”

“That does not eliminate assessment.”

“It has been seven minutes.”

I stood.

Crutches first.

Right foot planted.

Left knee bent, residual limb clear.

The ascent had become familiar enough to have sequence but not familiar enough to become automatic. That was probably good. Automatic was where my missing foot occasionally rejoined the workforce without authorization.

I put one crutch where I wanted it, hand to rail, weight through right leg, then up.

Pause.

Reset.

Again.

The first four were easy.

Easy was relative.

Easy meant I did not have to negotiate with each muscle individually.

By six, my right thigh was working.

By eight, my left hand had tightened on the rail.

At ten, the keeper said, “Slow.”

“I am slow.”

“Slower.”

This was insulting because she was correct.

I stopped.

Not dramatically.

Just stopped.

My right leg trembled once, then settled.

The old version of me would have categorized the tremor.

Fatigue.

Motor control.

Load.

Recovery interval.

Potential failure mode.

The current version did all of that too.

He was not dead.

He had simply acquired a chair-shaped appreciation for pauses.

There was no chair.

I leaned into the rail and crutch.

The missing left foot felt planted one step below me.

It was not.

I looked down at the actual geometry.

Right foot.

Wood.

Crutch.

Rail.

No left foot.

Good.

“Ready?” the keeper asked.

“Yes.”

Two more.

Landing.

Turn.

The turn remained stupid.

Stairs should not require choreography.

Two final steps.

Then upstairs.

I stood in the narrow hall breathing harder than fourteen steps deserved.

The keeper did not comment.

This was kindness.

Or boredom.

Either was acceptable.

My room smelled closed.

Wood, old cloth, a little dust, the faint dry smell of paper.

The window was shut.

The bed was made badly.

My trunk sat at the foot of it.

Nothing had happened.

This surprised me.

Not intellectually.

I had not expected burglary, fire, structural collapse, spontaneous fungal occupation, or the development of a small upstairs government.

But unused rooms acquire a strange quality. They stop feeling like places and begin feeling like stored versions of places.

I moved to the bed and sat.

The mattress compressed.

Still mine.

Apparently.

The keeper put the clean sheet on the trunk.

“Need anything?”

“No.”

“Coming down before dinner?”

“Yes.”

She looked at me.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That was a look.”

“That was me checking whether you were going to try descending alone.”

“I wasn't.”

“Good.”

She left.

I waited until her steps disappeared.

Then I said, “I could.”

The room did not believe me.

I opened the window.

Carrow entered immediately.

Not the city.

The noise.

Cart wheels.

Someone calling prices.

A dog objecting to something with moral certainty.

Hammering from two streets over.

A woman laughing below.

The smell of bread, smoke, horse, river damp, and something fried badly enough that it might still be worth eating.

My upstairs window had a different city than the downstairs storage room.

This was obvious.

Height changed sightlines.

The storage room had wall, alley feet, and whatever portion of the sky happened to survive between buildings.

Up here I could see roofs.

Not many.

Enough.

A slice of Guild roof farther away.

A chimney with three cracked bricks.

Laundry moving between buildings.

Two birds fighting over something on a gutter.

I sat on the bed and watched them.

One bird won.

The other returned six seconds later.

Poor strategic learning.

I approved.

My papers were where I had left them.

I pulled the stack from the trunk.

Old notes.

Work.

Magic.

Money.

Names.

Questions.

The kind of paper archaeology that could consume an afternoon if I let it.

I found one page from before West River.

A list.

Not important enough to remember writing.

Things to ask Edrin.

A Guild job possibility.

Something about Pell and Vessa's regulator.

A note to price better paper.

At the bottom:

STAIRS AT LODGING?

I stared at it.

I had apparently once considered stairs a property feature.

Technically they still were.

I turned the page over.

Blank.

Good.

I put it aside.

There were newer notes too.

NORTH ROAD: WHITE FLOWERS IN DITCH.

DYE WORKS YELLOW IS OFFENSIVE.

CART VIBRATION COSTS MORE THAN EXPECTED.

I had written those downstairs.

They looked different up here.

Not because the words changed.

Because I had a window.

I moved the papers.

The blue shirt hung from a peg.

I touched it.

Still blue.

Excellent.

Good.

The damaged left boot was not here. Tam had the salvage leather.

My right boot sat under the bed beside nothing.

That arrangement looked wrong.

I looked away.

Then back.

Still wrong.

Fine.

Some wrong things were just true.

I took the right boot out, checked the sole, found nothing requiring attention, and put it back.

The empty space remained.

I considered moving something into it.

No.

That would be worse.

I lay back on the bed.

This was not medically optimal posture for everything.

It was, however, extremely good.

My right shoulder relaxed.

My right leg stretched.

The residual limb rested without pressure.

The ceiling had a water stain shaped like a coastline from a country that did not exist.

Or perhaps did.

My future geographic memory was unreliable enough that I could not safely rule out ceiling countries.

I closed my eyes.

I woke to someone knocking.

Not the door downstairs.

My door.

I opened my eyes.

Light had moved across the floor.

I had slept.

This was unacceptable.

“Who?”

“Me.”

Lysa.

I sat up too fast.

My residual limb swung.

My missing foot struck the floor.

The sensation was complete enough that my stomach dropped before my eyes corrected it.

Nothing hit.

Nothing hurt.

Old map.

I breathed once.

“Wait.”

I looked at myself.

Hair.

Fine.

Shirt.

Not blue.

Why was I not wearing the blue shirt?

Because I had not expected Lysa.

This was a weak excuse.

I crutched to the door and opened it.

She stood in the hall with one hand against the wall and a folded paper packet under her arm.

“You're upstairs.”

“Yes.”

“I noticed.”

“How?”

“Stairs.”

“Strong evidence.”

She looked past me.

“I've never seen your room.”

This was true.

That seemed suddenly important.

Not objectively.

Still.

“Do you want to?”

She smiled.

“Yes.”

I moved aside.

This required more geometry than the gesture deserved.

Crutch.

Door.

Right foot.

Do not block entry with body.

Do not create dramatic invitation accidentally.

Lysa entered.

She looked around.

There was not much to look at.

Bed.

Trunk.

Window.

Peg.

Paper.

Cup.

One chair.

The six-legged horse was downstairs, which damaged the room's cultural standing.

“This is nicer,” she said.

“Than storage?”

“Yes.”

“That is an extremely low comparison.”

She went to the window.

I watched her look out.

This was different from looking out myself.

Possibly because she had hair that moved when the air came in.

That was not analytically relevant.

I sat on the bed.

“What is that?”

She held up the paper packet.

“Food.”

“Why?”

“You said I'd see you later.”

“I did.”

“This is later.”

Good.

Very strong system.

She opened the packet.

Fried dough.

Honey.

I stared.

“You brought this upstairs?”

“I carried it.”

I looked at my crutches.

Then at the food.

Then at her.

“Yes,” she said.

“I didn't say anything.”

“You did with your face.”

Octavia had ruined everything.

Lysa sat beside me.

Not touching at first.

Then our shoulders touched.

My right one complained faintly.

I shifted.

She noticed.

“Throwing?”

“Yes.”

“You went back?”

“No.”

“Cards?”

“Yes.”

“How do cards hurt your shoulder?”

“I was holding them too high.”

She laughed.

I ate fried dough.

Honey got on my thumb.

Lysa took the piece from my hand and ate the rest.

This was theft.

I permitted it.

We talked about nothing important.

Her work.

A customer who had changed her mind three times about a hem and then blamed the cloth.

A man downstairs who had tried to sell the keeper onions too soft to survive the day.

Jorren's theory that fried fish became health food if eaten while walking.

My counterargument that walking while eating fried fish introduced risk without altering nutrition.

Lysa rejected both positions.

The light moved.

At some point she put her head against my shoulder.

Left shoulder.

Competent woman.

I looked out the window.

The roofs had gone orange.

I thought about rent.

Not the amount.

The room.

I could have moved downstairs.

Cheaper.

Easier.

More practical.

I had not.

There were reasons.

Privacy.

Light.

Air.

Belongings.

A door that was mine to close.

A place Lysa had not seen before because it belonged to a part of my life that had become temporarily difficult to reach.

All defensible.

I could make the case.

I did not.

Lysa said, “What?”

“Nothing.”

“You did a whole thing.”

“I did not.”

“You went quiet.”

“That is allowed.”

“It is.”

I waited.

She waited.

I realized she was not asking.

Good.

I ate another piece of fried dough.

Eventually the keeper called from below.

“Greg!”

“Yes?”

“You coming down?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

I looked at the window.

Then at Lysa.

“Soon.”

The keeper made a sound that traveled up the stairs and conveyed skepticism without language.

Lysa smiled.

“You need help?”

“No.”

That answer came too quickly.

I corrected it.

“Not with the stairs. I need her spotting.”

Lysa nodded.

No offense.

No insistence.

No attempt to become useful.

Also good.

We stood.

I took the crutches.

At the door, Lysa kissed me.

Not my cheek.

Brief.

Then again, less brief.

My body did not attempt to step anywhere.

Progress.

Or sitting had trained it.

No.

Do not build theory from two kisses and a card game.

She pulled back.

“I'll go first.”

“Why?”

“So I'm not standing behind you on the stairs.”

That was sensible.

I disliked how many people around me were sensible without needing my assistance.

She went down.

The keeper came up to the landing.

I followed with the rail, crutch, right foot, pause.

Descent still required more attention than ascent.

At the landing my right thigh trembled.

I stopped.

Nobody spoke.

Then I continued.

At the bottom, I sat immediately.

Not because I had failed.

Because there was a chair.

The keeper handed me water.

Lysa took the remaining fried dough.

“Hey.”

“You were busy.”

“That does not transfer ownership.”

“It transferred possession.”

I looked at the keeper.

“Do something.”

“Table fee.”

“This is not a table.”

“Stair fee.”

“You cannot keep inventing fees.”

She smiled.

Lysa laughed.

I drank the water.

Upstairs, my room remained upstairs.

Fourteen steps away.

Still inconvenient.

Still mine.

Rent due tomorrow.

I would pay it.
