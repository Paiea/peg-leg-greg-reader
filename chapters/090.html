PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 90

THE BUYER

I wanted a chair.

Not a special chair.

This distinction became important.

I wanted an ordinary wooden chair with four legs, a back, a seat, and no moral purpose.

The chair in my upstairs room had all of those things except comfort.

Comfort had not mattered much before because I had mostly used it for putting clothes on.

Now I was spending more time upstairs.

Reading.

Writing.

Looking out the window.

Occasionally allowing a woman to bring me fried dough.

The chair had developed ambitions beyond its construction.

After twenty minutes, the front edge pressed behind my right thigh. After thirty, my lower back began negotiating. The left side of the seat did nothing useful for the residual limb, which was not the chair's fault because the chair had been built for a customer with the standard inventory of lower legs.

Still.

I wanted a better chair.

This was how I ended up in a furniture shop arguing against improvements.

The shop sat three streets east of the lodging house, open to the road through a pair of wide doors. Half the front room was chairs. The other half was tables pretending not to be chairs.

Wood shavings curled across the floor.

A woman at a workbench was fitting a stretcher between two chair legs.

I knew her by face.

She knew me by face.

This was increasingly common in Carrow and mildly alarming.

“You're the stair rail,” she said.

“I am not.”

“The one with the rail.”

“Yes.”

“Lerris made it.”

“Yes.”

She looked at my crutches.

Then at me.

“Chair?”

“Yes.”

She stood.

I immediately became suspicious.

“Ordinary chair.”

She stopped.

“I haven't said anything.”

“I am establishing scope.”

“Sit.”

She pointed to a chair.

I sat.

It was too low.

“Not that one.”

“You told me to sit.”

“I was learning.”

“About me?”

“About the chair.”

Good answer.

I stood.

This took more effort than I wanted it to.

Low chairs were traps disguised as furniture.

Right foot under me.

Hands.

Push.

Crutch.

Balance.

The woman watched.

I disliked that.

Not because she was staring.

Because she was competent enough that I could see her measuring without measuring.

“How high's yours?”

I told her approximately.

She held a hand beside her hip.

“That?”

“Probably.”

“Probably is bad furniture.”

“I can measure it.”

“Do that.”

She moved to another chair.

Higher.

Flat seat.

Straight back.

Arms.

“No arms.”

“Why?”

“They interfere with crutches.”

“Getting up?”

“Sometimes. Also placing them beside me.”

She looked at the chair.

Then moved it away without argument.

Excellent.

Third chair.

No arms.

Higher seat.

Slight backward angle.

I sat.

Better.

Not good.

The front edge still met my right thigh in the wrong place.

I shifted.

She watched.

“What?”

“You're sitting crooked.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

I looked down.

My left knee was bent. The residual limb stayed clear of the seat edge, but to do that comfortably I had rotated slightly right.

“Because if I sit square, the left side presses here.”

I touched the back of my thigh above the knee.

“Not the end?”

“No.”

She nodded.

This was good.

No dramatic pause.

No expression about tragedy.

Just location.

She crouched and looked at the seat.

“Could cut the left front corner back.”

“No.”

She looked up.

“Why?”

“I want a chair.”

“That would still be a chair.”

“I want an ordinary chair.”

“Ordinary chairs fit ordinary bodies badly all the time.”

“That is not helping your sales argument.”

“It is helping my chair argument.”

I stood.

She moved to a fourth.

“Try.”

I did.

This one had a woven seat.

Higher again.

The weave gave slightly under my weight.

Better on the right thigh.

Worse under the left because the edge sagged differently when I shifted.

“No.”

She nodded.

Fifth.

Wood.

Broad seat.

Almost level.

Back upright enough that I could write at a table without folding forward.

I sat square.

Interesting.

The seat was deep enough that the left thigh rested without the front edge reaching the sensitive area behind the knee. The residual limb hung free without contact.

My right foot planted.

I could stand from it without using the back of my right calf against the seat.

I stood.

Sat.

Stood again.

The woman said, “That's annoying.”

“What?”

“You found one.”

“You wanted me to find one.”

“I wanted to sell you work.”

“Ah.”

She was honest.

I liked her.

This was dangerous.

“How much?”

She named a price.

I disliked her.

“That is a chair.”

“Yes.”

“Not a horse.”

“No.”

“It has four legs and no ability to move itself.”

“Correct.”

“That should reduce cost.”

“Horse eats.”

“This does not.”

“Exactly.”

I looked at the chair.

Good chair.

Too expensive.

Not impossibly expensive.

More than I wanted to spend.

“How much for the ugly one?”

She looked around.

“Which ugly one?”

I pointed.

A chair near the wall had a broad seat, one repaired back spindle, and a dark stain across one side that looked like someone had died while eating stew.

“That isn't ugly.”

“It is visibly guilty.”

“Old tavern chair.”

“Why is it here?”

“Repair.”

“For the tavern?”

“No. They replaced the set. I bought six.”

“How many left?”

“Two.”

“Why?”

“Because they're good chairs.”

I crutched over.

She moved it clear for me.

I sat.

The seat was broad.

Height good.

Back slightly too curved.

The repaired spindle did not matter.

The stain was definitely stew.

Probably.

I shifted left.

No edge pressure.

Right foot planted.

I stood.

Easy enough.

“How much?”

She named a lower price.

Still not low.

“Why is the new one so much more?”

“New wood. New labor. Matching stock. Finish.”

“This has labor.”

“Repair labor.”

“And old wood.”

“Yes.”

“Which has survived long enough to demonstrate structural competence.”

She stared at me.

“That is the worst way anyone has ever asked for a discount.”

“It is a valid point.”

“No.”

“Old furniture has evidence.”

“Old furniture has old joints.”

“Which you repaired.”

“One spindle.”

“What about the joints?”

“Checked.”

“So evidence.”

She folded her arms.

I had become a customer.

This was different from being useful.

I had no jurisdiction.

No professional standing.

No reason she should care what I thought except that I had money and she wanted some of it.

This was refreshing.

“How much if I carry it?”

She looked at my crutches.

I looked at the chair.

“That was a test.”

“You failed it.”

“I agree.”

“Delivery's one bit.”

“One bit?”

“Three streets.”

“I can see my lodging from the corner.”

“Then carry it.”

I looked at her.

She smiled.

Octavia had also ruined smiles.

“Half bit.”

“There is no half bit.”

“Then free.”

“One bit.”

“I will ask Jorren.”

“Then Jorren can carry it.”

“He might.”

“Good.”

This was not going well.

I stood beside the chair.

I wanted it.

This fact had become irritatingly simple.

There were other chairs.

I could keep using the one upstairs.

I could ask the keeper whether there was another chair somewhere in the lodging house.

I could buy nothing.

I could wait.

I could spend the money.

The chair was not an investment.

It would not increase earning capacity in any defensible way.

It would not unlock a professional category.

It was not medical equipment.

It was not magical.

It was not even attractive.

It was comfortable.

I sat again.

Yes.

Damn.

“I'll take it.”

The woman nodded.

“One bit delivery.”

“I object.”

“Noted.”

“I am not paying delivery.”

“Then don't.”

“I want the chair.”

“Then solve your problem.”

I stared at her.

She returned to the workbench.

Conversation over.

I sat in my purchased chair while she worked.

This was strange.

I had not paid yet.

Possession was conceptually unstable.

“Can I leave it here?”

“For how long?”

“Until I find someone to carry it.”

“Today.”

“Yes.”

“Fine.”

“What if tomorrow?”

“Storage fee.”

“You people are inventing fees.”

“Who is people?”

“Nessa. Keeper. You.”

“Sounds like a you problem.”

Possibly.

I paid for the chair.

Not delivery.

Principle mattered.

Then I left to find Jorren.

He was not at the Guild.

This was inconvenient but reasonable because Jorren did not exist to transport my furniture.

Alden was there.

Worse.

He was coming out of the side yard with a practice shield under one arm and sweat darkening his shirt.

He saw me.

“Game?”

“No.”

He looked offended.

“I bought a chair.”

His expression changed.

“What?”

“I need to move a chair.”

“Why?”

“Because I bought it.”

“Why?”

“To sit.”

He stared.

This was fair.

“Where?”

I told him.

“Where to?”

“Lodging.”

He looked at my crutches.

“Yes,” I said.

“I didn't say anything.”

“You did.”

“With my face?”

“Everyone is doing this now.”

Alden laughed.

“I have training.”

“Now?”

“In a little.”

“How little?”

He looked toward the yard.

“Enough to carry a chair?”

“Probably.”

“Then no.”

“What?”

“You will be late.”

“I can carry a chair three streets.”

“I believe you.”

“Then?”

“I do not want Pessa arriving at my lodging to kill me because I converted your training time into furniture logistics.”

“She won't kill you.”

“Good.”

“She'll make me run.”

“Worse.”

Alden considered.

“After?”

“When?”

“After training.”

“That is an actual time category?”

“No.”

“Excellent.”

He went back inside.

I stood outside the Guild.

I could wait.

I did not want to wait.

This was partly because the chair was mine now and remained somewhere else.

Ownership creates ridiculous urgency.

I went to the green door.

Octavia was not there.

Nessa was.

“No.”

“I haven't asked.”

“You need something carried.”

I stared.

She pointed at my face.

“Fuck all of you.”

She laughed.

“Jorren?”

“Not here.”

“Sevren?”

“Road.”

“Octavia?”

“Freight office.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you here?”

“I was checking.”

“For people.”

“Yes.”

“Very moving.”

I left.

I considered the freight office.

No.

Octavia had work.

Sevren had work.

Jorren had somewhere he was not.

Alden had training.

Lyssa had work.

This was becoming a survey of other people's schedules.

Good.

Healthy.

Annoying.

I went back to the furniture shop.

The woman looked up.

“No porter?”

“No.”

“One bit.”

“No.”

I sat in the chair.

She returned to work.

I watched her fit the stretcher.

She used a mallet, not hard, tapping the joint into alignment before checking the square.

Competent.

I liked watching competent people work even when they were extorting me.

After several minutes, she said, “You planning to live there?”

“Temporarily.”

“Storage fee.”

“I paid for the chair.”

“Not the floor.”

“This is coercion.”

“This is a shop.”

I looked toward the street.

A handcart passed.

Not useful.

Two laborers passed carrying a rolled rug between them.

Potentially useful.

No.

They were working.

A boy passed with nothing.

Too small.

A woman carrying three baskets.

Obviously no.

I could hire someone.

That would cost money.

Probably more than one bit.

The furniture woman knew this.

Her delivery price was reasonable.

That made the argument worse.

“I will pay one bit.”

She nodded.

I hated her.

“Who delivers it?”

“My nephew.”

“When?”

“After lunch.”

“It is after lunch.”

“Then later after lunch.”

“That is not a time.”

“You bought a chair, not a clock.”

I closed my eyes.

“Fine.”

“Where?”

I gave the lodging house.

She knew it.

Of course.

Carrow was not enormous.

“Upstairs?”

I opened my eyes.

“Yes.”

“One bit is door.”

“No.”

She pointed at the chair.

“Three streets.”

“Yes.”

“Door.”

“Yes.”

“Stairs are different.”

“How much?”

She named another bit.

“No.”

“Then door.”

“I need it upstairs.”

“Then two.”

“That doubles delivery.”

“Stairs.”

“There are fourteen.”

“That's stairs.”

“Twelve, landing, two.”

She stared at me.

“That is still stairs.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

I did not like her.

I liked her less because she was right.

Carrying a chair upstairs was not the same job as carrying it three streets on level ground.

I had spent weeks learning that stairs were not decorative vertical hallways.

Of course labor changed.

“Two bits.”

“Yes.”

“Delivered upstairs.”

“Yes.”

“No damage to rail.”

She looked offended.

“I'm furniture.”

“That does not answer rail.”

“It answers enough.”

“Does your nephew know how to carry through a turn?”

She put the mallet down.

“Do you want the chair?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want it upstairs?”

“Yes.”

“Then stop interviewing the stairs.”

I paid two bits.

This felt like defeat.

It was not.

I had bought a service.

That was different.

Probably.

I went home.

The chair arrived an hour later.

The nephew was a man older than me.

This was one of those family words that survived age.

He carried the chair upside down over one shoulder.

I stayed out of the way.

The keeper opened the door.

“What's that?”

“Chair.”

“We have chairs.”

“I bought this one.”

She looked at it.

Then at me.

“Why?”

“It is better.”

“For what?”

“Sitting.”

She considered the existing downstairs chair.

Then the new one.

“Fair.”

The nephew carried it upstairs.

I did not follow immediately.

There was no reason.

He knew the room.

The keeper showed him.

I heard the chair touch the wall once.

Not hard.

Then steps.

Then nothing.

Then steps down.

He emerged empty-handed.

“Done.”

“Rail?”

He looked at me.

“Still there.”

Good.

I thanked him.

He left.

The keeper looked at me.

“You paid delivery?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“No.”

She smiled.

“Too much.”

“No.”

“Then you'd say.”

“I bought labor.”

“That bad?”

“Go away.”

She did.

I waited until my legs, plural in the grammatical sense, felt ready.

Then I went upstairs with the rail.

Slow.

Known sequence.

No drama.

At the top, the chair sat by the window.

It looked wrong.

Not because it was ugly.

It was.

Because it looked like it had always been there.

Broad seat.

Old repaired spindle.

Dark stew stain.

I moved it six inches.

Better.

Sat.

Right foot planted.

Left thigh supported without pressure where I did not want pressure.

Back against wood.

Window open.

Carrow beyond it.

Good.

I had spent money on a chair and then spent more money because I could not carry it home or upstairs.

There were several ways to interpret this.

Dependence.

Adaptation.

Luxury.

Logistics.

Waste.

Preference.

Economic participation.

I could make any of them work.

The chair did not care.

I sat in it until dinner.
