PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 95

THE TASTER

Pessa listened to my report with the expression of someone receiving weather.

“Slightly more swollen this morning,” I said.

“Yes.”

“You haven't looked.”

“You're standing differently.”

I looked down.

This was useless.

“How?”

“More weight right.”

“I always have more weight right.”

“More.”

“Hessa checked it.”

“Good.”

“She said no training.”

“I said no training.”

“She agreed with you.”

“Good.”

“I drew mana.”

Pessa looked at me then.

Not dramatically.

Enough.

“With Hessa?”

“Yes.”

“Once?”

“Yes.”

“Any pain?”

“No.”

“After?”

“No.”

“Leg?”

“Same.”

“Hands?”

“Fine.”

“Good.”

That was apparently the entire magical discussion.

I waited.

Pessa waited.

“You don't want to know what it felt like?”

“No.”

“That is almost offensive.”

“Did Hessa care what it felt like?”

“Yes.”

“Then tell Hessa.”

“I did.”

“Good.”

Distributed authority remained terrible.

“When can I train?”

“When the swelling settles.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Maybe.”

I stared.

She stared.

I left before the word became violent.

Jorren was outside the Guild eating something red.

Not red food.

Red substance attached to food.

He had a folded piece of flatbread in one hand and the expression of a man who had made a decision too late to reverse it.

His eyes were watering.

“What happened?”

He swallowed.

“Nothing.”

“Your face disagrees.”

“Try this.”

“No.”

He held out the flatbread.

“No.”

“You don't even know what it is.”

“I know what your eyes are doing.”

“That could be emotion.”

“You don't have those.”

He laughed and then coughed.

The coughing became worse.

I waited.

When he recovered, he pointed down the street.

“New pepper stall.”

“New?”

“New to me.”

There was a cart under a striped awning with three clay pots set into a bed of hot sand.

Steam.

Bread.

A handwritten board.

MILD.
HOT.
LIAR.

I looked at Jorren's food.

“Liar?”

He nodded.

“Why?”

“I asked for hot.”

“And?”

“He said I looked like a liar.”

“Reasonable.”

“He gave me the third one.”

“You ate it.”

“I had already paid.”

This was Jorren.

“How much?”

“One bit.”

“Worth it?”

He considered while tears ran down his face.

“Yes.”

That was also Jorren.

I had nowhere to be.

This was dangerous.

Not in the way Hessa meant.

I went to the stall.

The man behind it was old enough to have stopped caring whether customers approved of him and young enough that this was probably personality rather than age.

I did not ask his name.

He looked at my crutches.

Then at my face.

Then at Jorren.

“Another liar?”

“I am generally accurate.”

“Liar.”

Jorren made a sound that might have been agreement.

I looked at the three pots.

“What is the difference?”

“Pepper.”

“That is not enough information.”

“Then mild.”

“I didn't say I wanted mild.”

“You asked questions.”

“That implies caution?”

“It implies mild.”

Jorren leaned against the cart.

“He does this.”

The stall man pointed at the board.

“Mild, hot, liar.”

“What kind of pepper?”

He stared at me.

I had made a mistake.

Not social.

Culinary.

“Red,” he said.

Jorren laughed.

I looked into the pots.

The mild was brown-red.

Hot was darker.

Liar looked almost black at the edges where oil gathered.

It smelled excellent.

Onion.

Rendered fat.

Something sour.

Cumin?

No.

Not cumin.

A memory arrived from another life.

Chili.

Not one dish.

A thousand arguments pretending to be one dish.

Beans or no beans.

Tomato or no tomato.

Ground meat.

Chunks.

Powder.

Fresh peppers.

People becoming morally invested in stew.

Humanity had survived many things it did not deserve to survive.

“Hot,” I said.

The stall man nodded once.

Respect restored.

He filled a small clay cup.

Not much.

Good.

I paid.

Jorren watched.

“You're going to regret that.”

“You are eating liar.”

“I regret that.”

“You said worth it.”

“Both can be true.”

Also reasonable.

I sat on the low stone edge of a fountain across from the cart.

The fountain was dry.

Probably decorative failure.

Good seat.

Crutches beside me.

Cup in both hands.

Steam.

I took a bite.

Heat.

Not temperature.

Pepper.

Immediate.

Then meat.

Onion.

Sourness.

A sweetness underneath that I could not place.

Then more pepper.

My nose began running.

Jorren sat beside me.

“Well?”

I swallowed.

“Good.”

“That's all?”

“What do you want?”

“You made a face.”

“I am eating.”

“You made the thinking face.”

“I have one face.”

“No.”

This was becoming a conspiracy.

I took another bite.

The heat built.

Not unbearable.

Enough that each bite made the next one worse.

Excellent design.

“What is the sweet thing?” I asked.

Jorren shrugged.

“Food.”

“Helpful.”

“Ask him.”

“I did. He said red.”

“That was pepper.”

“He will say sweet.”

Jorren nodded.

Likely.

I ate.

People moved around us.

Guild traffic.

A pair of porters arguing about rope.

A woman carrying six loaves stacked against her chest.

Two children chasing each other around the fountain until someone shouted from somewhere I could not see.

A Bronze with a dented breastplate.

A clerk.

A dog.

Not the onion dog.

Different dog.

Carrow had many dogs and insufficient standards.

Jorren finished the last of liar.

He was sweating.

“Do you have work?” I asked.

“Later.”

“What?”

“South warehouse.”

“What are you doing?”

“Moving sacks.”

“That sounds like moving sacks.”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

He looked at me.

“Why?”

“Curiosity.”

“You're going to calculate something.”

“I might.”

“No.”

I ate another bite.

Fine.

“What time?”

“After midday bell.”

I looked at the sun.

“You have maybe an hour.”

“Yes.”

“What are you doing until then?”

“This.”

He pointed at the food.

“You're done.”

He looked at his empty hand.

“Then nothing.”

That sounded suspicious.

“What do you mean nothing?”

“I mean I have an hour.”

“Yes.”

“And no work for an hour.”

“Yes.”

“So nothing.”

I understood every word individually.

The sentence remained structurally unsound.

“You could do something.”

“I am.”

“What?”

“Sitting.”

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

I returned to the stew.

This was apparently friendship.

After several minutes, Jorren said, “How's the magic?”

I nearly answered with everything.

The copper strip.

The organized phantom.

Hessa's stopping conditions.

The distinction between draw and shaping.

Instead I said, “I drew mana once.”

Jorren nodded.

“Good.”

“That's all?”

“What else?”

“I don't know.”

“You want me to be impressed?”

“Yes.”

“I'm impressed.”

“You don't sound impressed.”

He turned to me.

“Greg.”

“What?”

“You did a tiny magic thing while sitting in a chair with Hessa watching.”

“Yes.”

“That is good.”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to shout?”

“No.”

“I can.”

“No.”

He inhaled.

I hit him with my elbow.

He laughed.

“Fine.”

I smiled despite myself.

The stall man called across the street.

“Thinking boy.”

I looked over.

He held up a spoon.

“What?”

“You keep staring at the pot.”

“I am trying to identify something.”

He pointed the spoon at me.

“Come.”

I looked at Jorren.

He shrugged.

I collected the crutches.

Standing from the fountain edge was harder than the chair because the stone was low.

Right foot close.

Push.

Both crutches.

No problem.

The residual limb felt full but not worse.

I crossed.

The stall man put a drop of something dark on a wooden spoon.

“Taste.”

“What is it?”

“Taste.”

This was how people died.

Probably not in public pepper stalls.

I tasted.

Sweet.

Dark.

Sour.

Fruit.

“Plum?”

He grunted.

“Dried?”

Another grunt.

“Cooked down?”

He took the spoon away.

“That is three questions.”

“Those were guesses.”

“Too many.”

Jorren had followed.

“It's plum?”

The stall man looked offended.

“No.”

I laughed.

“What is it?”

“Black pear.”

I stopped.

I knew black pears.

Small.

Ugly.

Mostly used dried because fresh they had the texture of wet sand and the flavor of regret.

“You put black pear in pepper stew.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He stared.

I knew the answer before he said it.

“Good.”

Fair.

He dipped another spoon into the mild pot and held it out.

I tasted.

Same sweetness.

More obvious without the heat.

Black pear.

Once known, impossible to miss.

Interesting.

The stall man pointed at the liar pot.

“Now.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“I bought hot.”

“Taste.”

Jorren said, “Do it.”

“You are not a neutral party.”

“I want to watch.”

Exactly.

The stall man offered the spoon.

This was not a challenge.

It was absolutely a challenge.

I tasted.

For perhaps one second, nothing happened.

Then my mouth became a workplace accident.

I closed my eyes.

Jorren started laughing.

The stall man said, “Liar.”

I could not speak.

This was fortunate because several words were available and none improved the situation.

Heat climbed into my nose.

My eyes watered.

My scalp became involved.

Why was my scalp involved?

I breathed through my mouth.

Mistake.

I breathed through my nose.

Worse.

Jorren handed me water.

The stall man slapped his hand away.

“No water.”

I pointed at him.

He gave me a piece of flatbread.

I ate it.

Slight improvement.

Then another.

Better.

The stall man nodded.

“Now you know.”

I swallowed.

“What pepper?”

“Red.”

I hated him.

Jorren was bent over the cart laughing.

I sat on the fountain again.

This time I required no philosophical interpretation.

Sitting was good.

My mouth slowly returned from war.

Jorren wiped his eyes.

“You looked so confident.”

“I was not confident.”

“You took the spoon.”

“That is not confidence.”

“What is it?”

“Nineteen.”

He laughed again.

That was probably the truest thing I had said all day.

We sat until the bell.

Not discussing magic.

Not discussing training.

Not discussing work.

At some point the stall man brought over two small pieces of bread with black pear paste on them.

No charge.

He handed one to Jorren and one to me.

“Why?”

“Thinking boy learned.”

“What did I learn?”

He walked away.

Jorren bit his.

“Black pear is good.”

“I knew black pear existed.”

“Did you know it was good?”

I looked at the paste.

No.

Not like this.

I ate it.

Sweet.

Sour.

Smoky from the pot.

Good.

“Apparently not.”

The midday bell sounded.

Jorren stood.

“Work.”

I looked at him.

“You're going to move sacks after liar?”

“Yes.”

“You may die.”

“Then I won't have to move all the sacks.”

He started toward the south road.

No invitation.

No expectation that I follow.

He had work.

I did not.

“Jorren.”

He turned.

“The stall man is wrong.”

“About what?”

“I am not a liar.”

Jorren looked at me for several seconds.

Then he said, “You told Pessa you weren't going to negotiate.”

I had told Hessa that.

Not Jorren.

“Who told you?”

He grinned and kept walking.

I stared after him.

Carrow was a surveillance state.

I bought another cup of hot.

Not liar.

I had limits.

This was progress of some kind.

Probably not the kind Hessa was measuring.
