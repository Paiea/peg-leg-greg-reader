PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 98

THE DRESSER

The next morning, I did not go to Hessa.

This was because she had told me not to.

I would like that entered into the record.

I also did not go to Pessa.

My residual limb was nearly back to its usual morning size, but nearly was not the condition she had given me.

This left me with an entire day and no professional supervision.

Dangerous.

I went upstairs after breakfast and discovered I owned four shirts.

I knew this already.

Apparently I had not understood it.

One was blue.

One had once been white and had entered a long negotiation with gray.

One was brown.

One was technically wearable if I wanted to look like I had escaped through a hedge.

I sat in the chair by the window with the four shirts arranged across the bed.

This was inventory.

Not architecture.

I hated that Pessa had infected my vocabulary.

The blue one was best.

This created a problem because I had worn it enough recently that even I had noticed.

I picked up the gray-white shirt.

The cuff was fraying.

The brown one had a small repair near the shoulder.

The hedge shirt had no defense.

I had money.

Not infinite money.

Enough money to own more than four shirts.

This thought felt suspiciously luxurious.

In another life I had owned too many clothes while insisting I did not care about clothes. This was a common male ritual. You bought six versions of the same acceptable thing and then claimed freedom from fashion.

I had apparently reinvented it badly.

I put on the blue shirt.

Then changed my mind.

Brown.

No.

Blue.

This was absurd.

I went downstairs in the blue shirt because indecision had limits.

The keeper looked up.

“Going somewhere?”

“To buy a shirt.”

She looked at my shirt.

“Yes.”

I stopped.

“What does that mean?”

“Nothing.”

“It meant something.”

“Buy two.”

“I am buying one.”

She returned to whatever she was cutting.

“Buy two.”

I left before the lodging became hostile.

The cloth market was three streets farther than I remembered.

Or the streets were the same and I was slower.

Both could be true.

I had passed the stalls dozens of times before the accident without treating them as a destination. Cloth had been background. Bolts. Awning. Dyed wool. Linen. People holding fabric against themselves and making serious faces.

Now I had to enter.

The first stall was too expensive.

I knew because nothing had prices.

The second had prices and therefore loved humanity.

I stopped there.

A woman behind the table was folding shirts into rectangles so exact they seemed accusatory.

She looked at me.

“Work?”

“What?”

“Work shirt?”

“Oh. No.”

“Good shirt?”

“I don't know.”

She stopped folding.

This answer was apparently unacceptable in commerce.

“What is it for?”

“Being a shirt.”

She stared.

I tried again.

“I want another one.”

“What do you have?”

“Four.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

“Blue. Brown. Gray-white. One bad.”

“What color blue?”

“Blue.”

She closed her eyes briefly.

I was losing.

She reached under the table and put three shirts in front of me.

Green.
Dark red.
Cream.

“No.”

“You haven't touched them.”

“The cream will become gray-white.”

She removed it.

Good.

I touched the green.

Linen blend, I thought.

Maybe.

The weave was slightly coarse but not unpleasant.

The red was softer.

“Price?”

She told me.

The red cost more.

Of course softness had monetized itself.

“Why?”

“Better cloth.”

“How much better?”

She looked at my crutches.

Then at me.

“Are you always like this?”

“Yes.”

“Green.”

“That does not answer the question.”

“It answers the shirt.”

I looked at the green again.

It was not a color I usually wore.

Did I usually wear colors?

I had four shirts.

Statistically, I did not have enough data to have a usual.

“Can I try it?”

She pointed behind a hanging cloth.

There was a narrow changing space.

I looked at the opening.

Then at the crutches.

Then at the shirt.

The space was not impossible.

It was irritating.

I could stand inside with both crutches, but removing my current shirt while holding them would require either a third hand or a new religion.

“Chair?”

I asked.

She looked behind her.

“No.”

“Wall?”

She pointed to the wooden side of the neighboring stall.

“That.”

Good.

I went behind the cloth.

Leaned one crutch carefully against the wall.

Then the other.

Right hand on wall.

Blue shirt off.

This was more complicated than it deserved to be because standing on one foot while removing clothing changed balance every time an arm went overhead.

I knew this now.

Not philosophically.

Physically.

I sat on the floor.

This was easier.

Also dusty.

Fine.

Green shirt on.

Sleeves.

Head.

Pull down.

I stood using the wall and one crutch.

The shirt fit.

Mostly.

The left side hung differently because my hips did not stand level when I was tired.

No.

That was not the shirt.

I adjusted.

The shoulders fit.

Sleeves slightly long.

I pushed them up.

The fabric caught under my right arm when I planted the crutch.

There.

Problem.

I moved twice.

The seam rubbed exactly where the crutch and my body had recently become acquainted.

I took it off.

Blue back on.

Returned.

“No.”

The seller looked at the green shirt.

“Too small?”

“No. Underarm.”

She looked at my crutches.

“Ah.”

That was all.

No pity.

Excellent.

She took the green away and pulled another from beneath the table.

Darker green.

Different cut.

“Try.”

“Price?”

“Same.”

Better.

Back behind cloth.

Floor again.

I had accepted the floor.

The darker green shirt had a slightly wider body and the sleeve seam sat differently.

I stood.

Crutch.

Plant.

Move.

Again.

Better.

Not perfect.

Clothes were not designed around crutches.

They were also not designed around carrying sacks, climbing rigging, forging metal, pregnancy, rain, or being shaped like Jorren.

Civilization persisted.

I came out wearing it.

The seller looked.

“Turn.”

I turned.

This required three movements instead of one.

Right foot.
Crutches.
Body.

Not Pessa training.

Just turning in a shirt.

“Again.”

I turned the other way.

She tugged the back hem.

“Shoulders good.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you here?”

“To benefit from your expertise.”

She snorted.

Progress.

I looked down.

Dark green.

I liked it.

That arrived before the calculations.

Interesting.

“How much?”

She repeated the price.

I had heard it the first time.

I was stalling.

Not because of money.

Because buying a shirt solely because I liked it felt strangely exposed.

A shirt was useful.

Obviously.

I could justify it.

I had only four.
One was bad.
One was fraying.
Laundry existed.
Blue was overused.

All true.

None were why I wanted this one.

“I'll take it.”

“Good.”

I started removing it.

“You can wear it.”

“I have a shirt.”

“Yes.”

“I paid for this one.”

“You haven't.”

Right.

I paid.

Then I wore it.

The blue shirt got folded.

Badly.

The seller watched me attempt to tuck it under one arm while using two crutches.

“No.”

“I can carry it.”

“You can drop it.”

“I can also carry it.”

She took the shirt, folded it into a much smaller rectangle, wrapped it in paper, tied string around it, then made a loop.

She held it up.

I understood.

The loop went over the top of one crutch below the grip.

The parcel hung against the shaft.

Not swinging much.

“Huh.”

“One bit.”

I looked at her.

“For string?”

“For being smarter than you.”

“That pricing model has dangerous scalability.”

“One bit.”

I paid.

Worth it.

Outside, I walked half a street before realizing I was looking at myself in windows.

Not every window.

Enough.

Dark green.

It looked good.

I was nineteen.

This fact had been medically true for some time and personally true only intermittently.

A pair of girls about my age passed going the other direction.

One looked at me.

Then away.

Maybe shirt.

Maybe crutches.

Maybe face.

Maybe nothing.

My brain tried to build a model.

I killed it.

Not everything was Holl's testing room.

I continued.

At the Guild, Alden was coming out.

He stopped.

“What happened to you?”

I looked down.

“What?”

“You're green.”

“It is a shirt.”

“I know.”

“Then why ask?”

He walked around me once.

I turned my head to follow him and immediately noticed the balance shift.

Fuck Pessa.

Alden nodded.

“Good.”

“What?”

“The shirt.”

“Oh.”

He started away.

“Wait.”

He looked back.

“You like it?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He stared at me.

I had become the shirt seller.

“Because it looks good.”

“That is not specific.”

“Greg.”

“What?”

“It's a shirt.”

He left.

Correct.

I went inside.

Merrin looked up from the desk.

“New?”

“Yes.”

“Nice.”

“Thank you.”

That was easier.

I should have started with Merrin.

No.

That would have required buying the shirt before receiving validation.

Important sequence.

Probably not.

I sat at an empty table.

Not for work.

The parcel with my blue shirt hung from the crutch.

I untied it.

The knot was simple.

I retied it.

Wrong.

Tried again.

Better.

Useful.

I looked at the dark green sleeve.

Still liked it.

No deeper meaning appeared.

I waited.

Nothing.

Excellent.

Jorren came in ten minutes later.

He saw me.

Stopped.

Looked at the shirt.

Then at my face.

“No.”

I narrowed my eyes.

“What?”

“I am not discussing it.”

“Why?”

“Because you will ask questions.”

“I have not asked anything.”

“You're about to.”

He walked past.

“Do you like it?”

“No.”

I turned in the chair.

“You're lying.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because this is fun.”

He kept walking.

I looked down at the shirt.

Still green.

Still good.

I smiled.

Then I did absolutely nothing useful for almost half an hour.

The shirt remained worth buying.
