PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 124

THE HORSE

The yellow bird was still on the table in the morning. This seemed correct. I left it there. My hands were normal. Right calf normal. Residual limb quiet. Phantom foot present in the ordinary useless way, heel resting on a floor that did not exist.

No magic today. That was less offensive than yesterday. Not good. Less offensive. I ate. The keeper looked at my shirt. Gray. He frowned.

“What?”

“I've lost the system.”

“There was no system.”

“Green was theatre.”

“Once.”

“Blue was theatre too.”

“Yes.”

“Now gray.”

“I own several shirts.”

“This is becoming difficult.”

“For you.”

He pointed at me with a spoon.

“Where are you going?”

I considered lying for no reason.

“Theatre.”

He looked triumphant.

“That proves nothing.”

“It proves gray is theatre.”

“No.”

I left before he could classify trousers. The walk west was ordinary. That mattered because tomorrow morning would not be. Hessa. Thread. No. Theatre. I could think about a thread tomorrow. Probably for several hours while Hessa prevented me from touching one.

The theatre doors were open. Inside, something was roaring. I stopped. Not a beast. A man. Then another roar. Worse.

Someone shouted:

“Again.”

The roar came a third time. I went in. The servant actor was standing center stage with both hands around his mouth. He saw me.

“Gregory!”

The roar stopped.

“That was you?”

“Yes.”

“It was bad.”

“Fuck you.”

Teren, in his chair, looked over a page.

“Blue Shirt is back.”

“Gray.”

“Worse.”

The unnamed woman near the stage edge looked at me once.

“Ramp.”

“Yes.”

Nobody asked why I was there. Good. The broad grooved scenery ramp was partly blocked by a painted panel showing a stone garden wall. The soot-shirt worker was holding one end while another worker adjusted the bottom brace.

She saw me.

“Wait.”

I waited. They shifted it.

“Go.”

I went. This was apparently my role in stage traffic now. Wait. Go. Crutch. Foot. Chair. Useful vocabulary. I reached the audience-side floor. My previous chair was occupied by a basket of fake fruit.

The bad rocking chair was empty. I looked at it.

Teren said:

“Paper is gone.”

“I remember.”

He tossed me a folded scrap. I caught it badly against my chest.

“Left front.”

“Yes.”

I folded it twice and put it under the chair leg. Stable. Mostly. The roaring resumed. I watched. The servant actor tried again. This time he lowered the pitch. It sounded like a sick cow.

The woman said:

“No.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you do it?”

“Commitment.”

“That is not a defense.”

Someone behind the scenery laughed. I looked at Teren.

“What are they doing?”

“Horse.”

I looked back at the servant actor.

“No.”

“Exactly.”

Onstage, a second actor crossed from the painted doorway carrying a letter. The woman pointed.

“From ‘I heard him at the south road.’”

The actor began.

“I heard him at the south road. He was riding like the dead had sent him back.”

The servant actor crouched just offstage and made the sick-cow sound. The actor onstage did not react. The woman stopped him.

“You heard a horse.”

“I heard him.”

“Then hear him.”

The actor reset. Again.

“I heard him at the south road. He was riding like the dead had sent him back.”

The roar. This time the actor looked toward the wing before continuing. Better. Still not a horse.

I said:

“Why is the horse roaring?”

Everyone looked at me.

The servant actor said:

“Because nobody can find the hoof board.”

“The what?”

Teren pointed with his page toward a pile backstage.

“Board with the cups.”

I stared. That explained nothing. The soot-shirt worker disappeared behind the garden wall.

The woman said:

“Again without it.”

The actor reset. The servant actor inhaled.

“No,” the woman said.

He lowered his hands.

“You have taken my only art.”

“You have several arts. Most are unfortunate.”

They ran the line without sound. The actor looked toward the wing. Nothing happened. It was worse. The woman frowned.

“Need something.”

“I said that,” the servant actor said.

“You are not something.”

“I am deeply wounded.”

The soot-shirt worker returned dragging a narrow board with four shallow wooden cups mounted beneath it. Two were cracked. One hung sideways.

“Found it.”

She put the board on the floor. The servant actor brightened.

“Horse.”

“That is not a horse,” I said.

He looked at me.

“You keep saying that.”

The worker picked up one end of the board and rocked it against the floor. Clop. Clop-clop. Clop. Oh.

“That is stupid.”

“Yes,” she said.

She rocked it faster. The uneven cups struck in sequence. The sound was not accurate. It was also immediately a horse. Annoying. They reset. The actor onstage began.

“I heard him at the south road. He was riding like the dead had sent him back.”

Clop. Clop-clop. Clop. The actor turned toward the wing. The servant actor stepped backward as though making space for something entering fast. The woman raised two fingers.

From behind the garden wall, another worker snapped a gray cloth low across a gap and released it. A dark shape crossed the opening for less than a second. Not a horse. Not even close.

The actor flinched aside. Teren turned a page. I saw a horse. Fuck. The scene continued.

“He nearly rode me down.”

The servant actor shouted from the wing:

“He still might.”

Audience laughter would go there. Probably. The woman let the scene continue another few lines. Then stopped.

“Again.”

I looked at the board. Then the cloth. Then the gap. The cloth had no legs. No head. It was approximately horse-colored. Maybe.

“That's it?”

The soot-shirt worker looked at me.

“What else do you want?”

“A horse.”

“No.”

“A better silhouette.”

“No.”

“The cloth is wrong.”

“No.”

“It doesn't have the correct shape.”

She picked up the board.

“Does it sound enough like a horse?”

“Not exactly.”

“Good.”

“That is not what good means.”

She walked away.

Teren said:

“You're doing the face.”

“Everyone has started saying that.”

“Because you have the face.”

I watched them run it again. Clop. Gray movement. Actor reaction. Horse. Not real. Not even a convincing illusion. There was barely any illusion at all. The audience would know there was no horse.

Of course they would. They were in a theatre. That somehow made the problem worse. The actor missed his next line. Teren gave him the first three words. He caught it. The horse disappeared from my attention.

The scene lived. I hated this place. I stayed. After two runs, one of the wooden cups split completely.

The sound became:

Clop. Crack. Clop. The servant actor stopped.

“Dead horse.”

The woman said:

“Keep going.”

He looked at the broken board. The actor onstage had already started the line.

“I heard him at the south road.”

The soot-shirt worker kicked a small box. The servant actor grabbed it. He struck the side twice with his palm. Thump-thump. Wrong. The worker snapped the cloth. The actor looked. The servant actor clicked his tongue against his teeth.

Then slapped the box again in a faster rhythm. Not horse. Horse. The scene continued. Nobody stopped. I had been waiting for them to stop. They did not.

At the end the woman said:

“Better.”

The servant actor held up the broken cup.

“This died.”

“I saw.”

“Need another.”

“Later.”

She pointed at the actor.

“You looked too soon.”

“I heard the box.”

“You knew the box was coming. He didn't.”

She pointed toward the imaginary person inside the play. The actor frowned. Again. No lecture. No table. Again. The soot-shirt worker carried the broken board toward a worktable. I followed. Not immediately. That would have looked eager.

I waited maybe six seconds. Still eager. Fine. The board was simple. Four cups. Different depths. Mounted on short pegs beneath a narrow plank so rocking transferred weight unevenly. One peg had split.

“I can fix that,” I said.

The worker looked at me.

“No magic.”

I blinked.

“How do you know?”

“You talk.”

“That does not answer the question.”

“Hessa talks too.”

This was alarming.

“What did she say?”

“Nothing to me. Somebody said you're on no-magic.”

Carrow had achieved information transmission without my consent.

“I meant the peg.”

“You can fix wood?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She handed me the broken piece. I sat at the worktable. There were tools. Normal tools. Knife. Small hand drill. Cord. Glue. Wood scraps. The peg did not need magic. Good. I chose a scrap.

The original peg had been too thin. That was obvious. I cut a replacement thicker. The worker watched for approximately three seconds, decided I was not going to remove a finger, and left. Nobody supervised.

Also good. I shaped the peg. Too thick. Adjusted. Better. The hole in the cup was damaged. I could widen it. If I widened it, the cup would sit differently. That would alter sound.

I compared the other three. Different depths. Different angles. Not random. Probably. I measured by eye. Then with the edge of the knife. Then found a bit of cord and used that. Teren appeared beside the table.

“What are you doing?”

“Repair.”

“You look angry.”

“The cups are different.”

“Yes.”

“Intentionally?”

“No idea.”

He left. Useless. I tested the cup. The crack changed the sound. It should be replaced too. There were spare cups somewhere. Probably. I searched the shelf under the table. Three cups. One too deep.

One too shallow. One nearly right but heavier. I tapped each. The heavier one had better tone. Not the same tone. Better. This was now a design problem. I forgot the horse scene for several minutes.

The servant actor appeared.

“Horse doctor.”

“No.”

“Can it live?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Not yet.”

He picked up the heavier spare cup.

“This one.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Different weight.”

He rocked it against the table. Clop.

“That sounds horse.”

“It changes the sequence.”

He rocked it again. Clop.

“Horse.”

“You are not helping.”

“I am the customer.”

“No.”

He left with the cup. I followed.

“You cannot just take it.”

He could. He had. The soot-shirt worker had already mounted the spare cup with cord. Not even the new peg. Cord. Ugly. Loose. Temporary. The board rocked. Clop. Clop-clop. Clop. The sequence was slightly different.

I could hear it. Nobody cared. The woman pointed.

“From south road.”

They ran it. The horse worked. Better than before. Not because the sound was more accurate. Because the actor waited this time. The gray cloth passed. The servant actor stepped aside late enough that it looked like something had actually forced him to.

The actor's eyes tracked the gap. The horse existed for about two seconds. Then it did not. The scene continued. I stood with my replacement peg in one hand. Unused. The soot-shirt worker looked at it.

“Good peg.”

“Yes.”

“Put it in the repair box.”

“It is for that board.”

“Later.”

“The cord will shift.”

“Probably.”

“The cup angle is wrong.”

“Probably.”

“It changes the sound.”

“Gregory.”

“Yes.”

“We have horse.”

I looked at the stage. They did. Fuck. I put the peg in the repair box. The worker went back to the wall flat. No philosophy. No explanation. Theatre had simply stolen my problem before I solved it.

I sat again. Teren tossed me a page.

“Hold.”

I held it. He had two versions open and was comparing a cut.

“What am I holding?”

“Page nine.”

“Why?”

“Because I have page eight and page ten.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It literally is.”

Fine. I held page nine. The woman cut two lines from the servant actor. He objected.

“They are my best lines.”

“They are your longest lines.”

“Same.”

“No.”

He appealed to Teren.

Teren said:

“I like the second one.”

“Traitor.”

The line stayed cut. Then came back. Then moved. I lost track. At some point Teren took page nine back without thanking me. Also normal. The horse board failed again after six runs. The cord slipped.

The cup rotated. I noticed first. I almost stood. Could not quickly anyway. The servant actor noticed second because the sound went wrong. He changed his hand position and made the next beat with the side of the board instead.

The actor reacted. Gray cloth. Horse. Scene continued. Nobody looked at the repair box. I did. The peg was still there. This felt personal. At break I retrieved it. The soot-shirt worker was eating something wrapped in paper.

I held up the peg.

“Now?”

She chewed.

“Sure.”

Victory. I installed it. The cup sat correctly. Mostly. I adjusted the angle. Rocked the board. The sequence was cleaner.

Closer to an actual four-beat pattern, though not exact because the mounted cups could not produce independent timing without more complex rocking. I could fix that. No. I stopped. The worker watched me stop.

“Done?”

“Yes.”

She rocked it. Clop. Clop-clop. Clop. She nodded.

“Good.”

That was satisfying. Then she took a small knife and shaved one side of my new peg. I stared.

“What are you doing?”

“Too tight.”

“It is secure.”

“It sticks.”

“That is secure.”

“We need to swap cups fast.”

“Why?”

“Different floor.”

I looked at her.

“What?”

“Stone stage sounds different than wood. Sometimes we use the shallow cup.”

Oh. She shaved it again. The peg still held. Looser. Removable. She handed me the knife.

“You want pretty or useful?”

“Those are not opposites.”

“Here they are.”

Then she walked away before I could argue. I hated her too. I did not know her name. This was becoming inconvenient. The afternoon continued. No grand event. No missing actor. No fire. No magical discovery.

There was an illusion of a window that turned blue when evening arrived in the play. I noticed it only because it flickered once. A worker behind the flat swore. The blue vanished. The scene kept going.

Someone adjusted a lamp. The painted window looked dimmer. Apparently that was enough until break. I wanted to know what failed. I did not ask. Not because I had developed restraint. I was still holding page nine.

Later, the blue returned. No announcement. No demonstration. Nobody cared except the person running it. Interesting. No. Not yet. The servant actor found me before I left.

“Horse doctor.”

“Stop calling me that.”

“Horse Gregory.”

“Worse.”

He handed me the board.

“What?”

“You made it work.”

“It already worked.”

He looked confused.

“Yes.”

That was somehow more insulting.

“Take it.”

“Where?”

“Back table.”

“You can carry it.”

“I am beloved.”

“That does not affect your arms.”

“It affects my obligations.”

I balanced the board awkwardly across my lap while seated. Then realized I could not carry it while using both crutches. He was already gone.

“Fuck.”

Teren passed. I pointed at the board. He took it without comment and put it on the back table. Then came back.

“Tomorrow?”

“Hessa.”

“Morning.”

“Yes.”

“After?”

“I don't know.”

“Good.”

“You all do that.”

“Do what?”

“Nothing.”

Teren looked at me for a moment. Then shrugged.

“Good.”

I left. My palms were warmer than yesterday by the time I reached home. Right calf mildly tired. Residual limb quiet. The phantom heel returned briefly on the stairs while the keeper spotted me.

Ordinary. No magic. The yellow bird was still on the table. I put the new image beside it in my head. Not literally. Horse board. Gray cloth. Actor looking toward nothing. Horse. The board had been broken.

They had used a box. The illusion window had failed. They had changed the lamp. Nothing had stopped. Tomorrow Hessa would ask me to make something happen correctly. Probably. Maybe. I looked at the yellow bird.

Then at my notebook. I did not write about the horse. I went to bed. For several minutes, I could still hear the wrong hoofbeats. They sounded enough like a horse.
