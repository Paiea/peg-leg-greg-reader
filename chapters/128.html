PEG-LEG GREG
ART
CHAPTER 128

THE MESSENGER

I went to the theatre because I wanted to. This was becoming suspicious. No Hessa. No magic. No Guild task. No courier cart. The theatre door was open.

Inside, somebody was shouting:

“Not the blue one.”

A different voice shouted:

“They are both blue.”

“Not that blue one.”

I entered. Two painted doors stood onstage. Both blue. One darker. Probably. The soot-shirt worker was carrying a brass handle in her teeth. She saw me. Pointed at the darker door. Then pointed at the floor beside it.

I went there.

“What?”

She removed the handle.

“Hold this.”

She gave me the door. Not metaphorically. It was lighter than it looked. Canvas over frame. I braced it with one hand while keeping a crutch under the other arm. Bad arrangement. She looked.

“No.”

“Yes.”

“No.”

She took the door back.

“Sit.”

There was a chair near the stage edge. I sat. She put the door beside me.

“Hold from there.”

Better.

“Why?”

“Because I need the hinge.”

“Which hinge?”

“The one you are covering.”

I moved my hand. She took the hinge.

“Excellent.”

“That was not useful.”

“It was eventually.”

Good morning. Teren was at his usual place with pages. He looked up.

“Gregory.”

“Teren.”

“Can you read?”

“Yes.”

“Today?”

“Probably.”

He held out three folded pages. I took them.

“What am I?”

“Messenger.”

I looked at the page. The title at the top was not the play title. Just scene number and a word I did not know. I pointed.

“What is that?”

“Scene change note.”

“It looks like a name.”

“It isn't.”

“What does it mean?”

“Blue room after court.”

“That is four words.”

“It saves space.”

“It failed.”

“For you.”

“Then it failed.”

Teren took the page back, looked at the note, and said:

“No.”

He gave it back.

The servant actor called from the floor:

“Gregory has discovered notation.”

“I can read notation.”

“Today?”

“Fuck you.”

The woman said:

“Please keep him. He improves morale.”

Teren said:

“He is not mine.”

Good. Correct. I looked at the page again. There was a messenger. Four lines.

“No.”

Teren blinked.

“What?”

“I mean what am I doing?”

“Reading messenger.”

“That sounds like what I am.”

“For six minutes.”

“Why?”

“Because we are working the entrance.”

I looked toward the stage. The recurring woman was there in a dark skirt over rehearsal clothes. The servant actor was lying on the floor. Not dead. Probably. Another man stood by the blue door.

Actual actors. No missing person. No emergency. Teren pointed.

“You enter from there.”

He pointed to the darker blue door.

“Walk to the table.”

There was no table.

“There is no table.”

“There will be.”

“Where?”

The woman pointed at a chalk rectangle on the floor.

“There.”

“Why not put the table there?”

“Because they're painting it.”

Reasonable. I looked at the page. Messenger entered. Crossed to table. Delivered letter. Four lines. Exited.

“Why me?”

Teren had already looked back at his pages.

“Because you are here.”

That answer was becoming dangerous. The servant actor sat up.

“Gregory has been promoted.”

“To what?”

“Temporary furniture.”

“I am messenger.”

“Temporary messenger furniture.”

The woman said:

“Start before he develops standards.”

I stood. Crutches. Pages. Letter. Too many things. Teren noticed.

“Pages stay with me.”

Good. He took them back.

“You know the lines?”

“No.”

“You just read them.”

“That is not the same thing.”

He held them where I could see. Fine. The letter was a folded scrap. I tucked it under two fingers on my right crutch handle.

Teren said:

“No.”

Everyone said no here.

“What?”

“You need to hand it over.”

“Yes.”

“You have trapped it.”

I looked. True.

“Pocket.”

I put it in my shirt pocket.

The woman onstage said:

“Messenger does not arrive with royal correspondence folded into his armpit.”

“It is my pocket.”

“Worse.”

“Why?”

“Because then I watch you dig for it.”

“You are supposed to be listening to the message.”

“I can do both.”

“That seems like your problem.”

“It becomes yours when I laugh.”

The servant actor said:

“She will.”

“I won't.”

“You already are.”

She was. Barely. Fine.

The servant actor said:

“Put it in your teeth.”

I looked at him. He grinned.

Soot-shirt worker, still by the door, said:

“Don't.”

Fine. I held the letter in my right hand and used the crutch grip with the heel of that hand. Possible. Awkward. Teren watched.

“Can you cross like that?”

“Yes.”

“Do it.”

I entered. Wrong door.

“Other blue,” three people said.

I backed out.

“Both blue.”

The woman said:

“Not to us.”

Second attempt. Correct blue door. Across stage. Three unrailed steps existed at the front. I did not use them. No reason. The chalk table was farther than it looked from the wing. The stage also had more traffic than it appeared to from a chair.

A stool waited just outside my path. A rolled carpet occupied part of the wing. Someone crossed behind the painted wall carrying a lamp. None of this was in the four lines. I reached the chalk table.

Stopped. Read from Teren.

“My lady, a message from Lord…”

I looked at the name. Teren supplied it.

“Varen.”

“Varen.”

The woman held out her hand. I continued reading. She kept holding out her hand. I kept reading.

“Letter,” she said.

“Oh.”

I gave it to her. The servant actor made a small choking sound. Probably laughter. I finished the lines.

“Then go,” Teren said.

“I have another line.”

“After she reads.”

I looked at the page. Yes. Pause. Right. The woman opened the letter. Read silently. Looked at me. I said my final line. Too late.

“Again,” Teren said.

No criticism. Just again. I went back. Third attempt. Correct door. Cross. Line. Letter before she had to ask. Pause. Final line. Exit. I turned toward the wrong side.

“Other way,” Teren said.

I changed direction.

The servant actor said:

“Messenger knows the kingdom. Not the building.”

“Fuck you.”

The woman laughed.

Teren said:

“Again.”

Fourth. I was enjoying this. That was inconvenient. The problem was not the lines. The problem was everything around the lines. Door. Crutches. Letter. Where she stood. When her hand appeared. How long she read.

Where I was supposed to leave. I had four lines and somehow there were twelve failures available. Excellent. Also my right crutch made a sound on one board. Small. Wood tip against a slightly raised seam.

Click. Every crossing. I noticed it on the fifth attempt. Of course I did. I looked down.

Teren said:

“No.”

“I haven't done anything.”

“You are about to.”

“The board is raised.”

“Yes.”

“My crutch catches it.”

“Does it stop you?”

“No.”

“Then later.”

“It changes the sound.”

The servant actor said:

“Messenger has hooves.”

I looked at him.

“No horse.”

Nobody understood why that was funny to me. Better. I crossed again. Click. The woman did not react. Neither did anyone else. I tried placing the crutch half an inch left next time. No click.

That made my step worse.

Teren said:

“Why did you do that?”

“Board.”

“Stop.”

Fine. Click returned. Apparently messenger had one hoof. Again. This time I remembered the exit. The woman did not hold out her hand. I almost handed her the letter anyway. Stopped. She looked at me.

I looked at her. She said her line. Then held out her hand. I gave it.

Afterward I said:

“You changed it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I was late.”

“You did that intentionally?”

“No.”

I stared. She shrugged.

“I forgot.”

“That is worse.”

“Did you survive?”

“Yes.”

“Then again.”

Teren said:

“From the argument before entrance.”

I started to leave.

“No. Stay.”

“I am not in the argument.”

“You are outside the door.”

“So I wait?”

“Yes.”

“Doing what?”

“Being outside the door.”

“That is not an action.”

The servant actor said:

“Think messenger thoughts.”

I hated him. I stood behind the blue door. The argument began. Louder than expected. The woman crossed. The man by the door slammed a cup onto the nonexistent table. There was no cup.

He slapped his palm against the chalk rectangle instead.

Teren said:

“Cup.”

Soot-shirt worker tossed him a wooden cup. He caught it. Argument continued. I waited for my line. No. Entrance. I waited for the argument to end. It did not.

The woman shouted:

“Then let him come tell me himself.”

That sounded like entrance. I entered.

Teren said:

“Too early.”

I stopped. The actors did not. The man turned toward me.

“Who the hell are you?”

Not on my page. I looked at Teren. He made a small circular motion. Continue. Right. The woman looked at me too. I had no line yet.

The servant actor, now standing near the wall, whispered:

“Messenger thoughts.”

Fuck him. I looked at the letter. Then at the man.

Then said:

“I was told to deliver this.”

Not the line.

The man snapped:

“Then deliver it.”

I crossed. The woman held out her hand. I gave her the letter. She opened it.

Teren said:

“Now.”

I found the actual first line.

“My lady, a message from Lord Varen.”

The woman looked at the letter already in her hand. Then at me.

“That much I gathered.”

Everyone laughed. I did too. Which made the next line impossible.

Teren said:

“Again from entrance.”

I went back.

“Was that wrong?” I asked.

“Yes,” Teren said.

The woman said:

“It worked.”

Those were different answers. Good.

“Which matters?”

“Right now?” Teren said. “Wrong entrance.”

The woman said:

“Right now? Do it again.”

No philosophy. Fine. We did it again. I waited longer. Correct entrance. Correct line. Correct letter. Correct pause. Correct exit. Boring. Not actually. Cleaner. Then the blue window failed. Same painted window from before.

Or one like it. The evening blue appeared across it. Flickered. Went gray. Nobody stopped. Soot-shirt worker pulled one side lamp lower. Another worker shifted a pale panel. The woman continued arguing with the man.

The window stayed gray. The scene still happened. I watched for half a second too long. Teren tapped my page.

“You're next.”

Right. Messenger. I entered. Correct door. Forgot the letter. I reached the chalk table. Hand empty. The woman held out hers. Nothing. I stopped. Teren said nothing. The servant actor's face became delighted. I could go back.

No. Too late. I looked at the woman. She looked at my empty hand.

Then she said:

“Well?”

I put my hand into my shirt pocket. Nothing there. Obviously.

I said:

“I was told to place it directly in your hands.”

Her eyebrows moved.

“Then?”

“I appear to have been lied to.”

The servant actor folded in half. The woman held it. Barely.

The man said:

“Search him.”

That was not on anything.

“No,” I said.

“Suspicious.”

“I am on crutches.”

“Perfect disguise.”

The woman lost. She laughed.

Teren said:

“Stop.”

Everyone stopped. I expected correction. Teren pointed at the wing.

“Letter.”

The soot-shirt worker held it up. I had left it on the chair.

“Yes.”

“Get it.”

I went.

The servant actor said:

“Coward.”

“You were not being searched.”

“I would have committed.”

“To what?”

“Anything.”

Probably true. I returned with the letter.

Teren said:

“Again.”

We did it. No one explained what I should have done when the prop vanished. Apparently the answer was not whatever I had done. Also apparently it had not killed the scene. Both could be true.

At the next pause I asked the woman:

“What was I supposed to do?”

“When?”

“Without the letter.”

“Oh.”

She thought.

“Depends.”

“That is not useful.”

“It is exactly useful.”

“What would you have done?”

“If I were you?”

“Yes.”

“Probably swear.”

“Onstage.”

“Quietly.”

I waited. She relented.

“Could have said it was stolen. Could have made him send you back. Could have crossed without handing anything and let me decide. Could have stopped if Teren stopped us.”

“Which is correct?”

“Whichever lets us get to the next thing without making a worse problem.”

“That is not correct.”

“No.”

She smiled.

“It's rehearsal.”

Then somebody called her and she left. That was all the instruction I got. I disliked it. I also wanted another missing letter. The blue window came back while we were halfway through. Nobody applauded.

It was simply blue again. I noticed the effect more this time. Not how. Just that the room looked later. The painted wall had not changed. The window had. The woman's face picked up a little blue on one side.

Then I had to enter. I entered. After six minutes, which was actually closer to thirty, Teren took the pages.

“Done.”

“That was not six minutes.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“We kept using you.”

“That is not a unit of time.”

“It is here.”

The servant actor came over.

“You were terrible.”

“Yes.”

“Better than last time.”

“I did not have a last time.”

“You read merchant.”

“That was seated.”

“Still terrible.”

“Thank you.”

He clapped my shoulder. Not hard.

“Tomorrow?”

There it was again. Everyone in this city had one question.

“I have Hessa.”

“After?”

“I don't know.”

“Fine.”

He left before I could decide whether that was invitation. The woman was arguing with soot-shirt worker about the blue door. Not the window. Door.

“It reads black from the side.”

“It reads blue from front.”

“Half the house is side.”

“Not half.”

“Enough.”

They moved the door. Turned it. Moved a lamp. Still blue. Different blue. I watched. Soot-shirt worker saw me.

“No.”

“I did not say anything.”

“You leaned.”

This was spreading.

“I was looking.”

“Dangerous.”

The woman said:

“Gregory.”

“Yes?”

“Stand there.”

She pointed to a place off the stage. I went.

“Blue?”

I looked. The door was almost black.

“No.”

“Thank you.”

Soot-shirt worker moved the lamp.

“Now?”

“Blue.”

“Done.”

“That seems audience-specific.”

They both looked at me. I regretted speaking.

The woman said:

“It seems blue from where people sit.”

“Yes.”

“That is generally where we need it.”

Right. I shut up. Soot-shirt worker grinned.

“Learning.”

“No.”

“Good.”

They went back to work. I stayed. Not because needed. That distinction was getting weaker. Later somebody gave me a piece of bread. I did not know whose. I ate it. A young crewman carried the chalk table in while I was chewing.

Actual table. I moved my chair without being asked. He put it exactly over the rectangle. Almost. One leg missed by two inches. I noticed. He noticed me noticing.

“Don't.”

“I didn't.”

“Good.”

He shoved the table once. Done. The servant actor dropped the cup again. Different scene. This time nobody laughed because apparently he was supposed to. I could not tell anymore. That was fun. My palms got warm.

I sat before they hurt. Nobody commented. The blue window worked. Then failed once. Then worked. Nobody asked me to care. I did not. Mostly. When I finally left, the soot-shirt worker was changing the hinge on the darker blue door.

I said:

“Wrong blue?”

She said:

“Fuck off, Gregory.”

I laughed all the way to the street. No magic. Tomorrow Hessa. But not yet. For now I had been a messenger for thirty minutes. Badly. I wanted to do it again.
