CHAPTER 158

THE RETURNER

I woke because Pell was washing a shirt in our drinking water.

I watched him for several seconds.

"That's drinking water."

"Was."

"You have a basin."

"Full."

"Of what?"

"Socks."

I lay back down.

The ceiling smelled like bread.

The room smelled like wet wool, soap, and two men who had spent too much
time in costumes.

Outside, somebody was moving a cart through mud.

Nothing hurt in a new way. My shoulders were sore. My hands had the
familiar rubbed places from the crutches. My residual limb was fine when
I checked it, which made it the least complicated part of the morning.

I cleaned and rewrapped it.

Pell wrung out the shirt.

"Show tonight."

"I know."

"Advocate."

"I know."

"From."

I threw a sock at him.

It was his sock.

That made it less satisfying.

He put it in the basin.

"Thanks."

Downstairs, nobody was waiting for me.

Again.

Marek was eating something fried from a paper twist and arguing with a
boy about whether the King had won last night.

The boy said no.

Marek said the King was still King at the end.

The boy said that was not winning.

Marek looked genuinely troubled by this.

Nessa had three costumes spread over two chairs and half the bakery
counter. Rinna was writing numbers on the back of an old flour invoice.
Iven had coffee or something close enough to coffee that he was
pretending. Orin was absent.

Serra sat by the window with her hair loose, reading a folded note.

She folded it before anyone came near.

Not because of me.

Just because someone came near.

Davin was not there.

Teren was.

That was worse.

He looked rested.

"How?"

He looked at me.

"What?"

"You slept."

"Yes."

"How?"

"Bed."

I hated management.

He pointed toward bread.

"Eat."

"Advocate?"

"Tonight."

"I know tonight."

"Then why ask?"

I took bread.

Bessa came through the front door carrying a board with ticket marks.

Marek stopped arguing with the boy.

"How many?"

"Thirty-seven."

He smiled.

"For tonight?"

"No, Marek. For a funeral next spring."

"Good funeral?"

Bessa walked past him.

"Thirty-seven," he told me.

"I heard."

"Yesterday was twenty-three."

"I remember numbers."

"You forgot three wagons."

"Different numbers."

He accepted this.

Lorn was awake.

I found him in the room above Bessa's sister's shop because Rinna sent
me with bread and because apparently being Advocate meant I had
inherited errands.

Bessa's sister sold candles.

I learned this because the room below Lorn contained enough wax to make
the stairs smell sweet.

Lorn was sitting up in bed under two blankets.

He looked terrible.

Less terrible than last night.

"You're alive."

"Unfortunately."

I gave him bread.

He looked at it.

"Is that payment?"

"No."

"Then good."

His pages were on the chair.

My pages.

His pages.

I had developed opinions about ownership too quickly.

He pointed.

"Bring them."

I did.

He flipped through the marks.

"What happened here?"

"Iven."

"He killed half the role."

"Orin helped."

"Animals."

"You said keep the magistrate."

"I did not say remove jurisdiction."

I stared at him.

"You like jurisdiction?"

"It's the best part."

"Orin called it three minutes of furniture."

"Orin falls down before knives touch him."

Fair.

Lorn found the bridge section.

He looked at my circles.

FROM had been circled enough times that the paper was nearly torn.

"You said to."

"Once."

"Rinna said twice."

"Rinna wasn't onstage."

"Everyone knows."

Excellent.

He read farther.

"You made him angry."

"Who?"

"Advocate."

"I was tired."

"He's not angry."

"He accuses the Chancellor of treason."

"Forgery."

"Both."

"See? You're learning."

"What is he, then?"

Lorn looked at me as if this were obvious.

"Scared."

I waited.

"He walks into a king's hall and says the king's seal is being used to
steal from merchants. If the king decides the seal is fine, Advocate is
calling the king a thief. If the king decides it isn't, Advocate has
just told the room the king can't control his own house."

"That sounds like anger."

"That sounds like wanting to go home alive."

I looked at the first page.

That was irritating.

I had played him like someone who wanted the argument.

Lorn took the bread.

"Don't copy me."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good. I'm better."

"You're in bed."

"Temporarily."

I left before I started defending a role I had played once to a man who
had played it for six months.

Outside, the rain had stopped.

The town looked better for it.

Not beautiful.

Better.

Mud still ran along the gutters. Water dripped from awnings. People had
opened shutters and put goods back outside.

I had nowhere I was required to be for several hours.

This felt suspicious.

I walked.

Not far.

The market occupied a narrow square three streets from the hall.
Vegetables, cloth, cheap metal goods, paper, cooked food, secondhand
shoes, two cages of chickens, one man selling carved saints, and a woman
selling buttons by weight.

I bought paper.

One sheet cost less than I expected.

Ink cost more.

I bought neither ink nor pen because the paper seller had a writing
table and charged a copper to use both and send a folded letter south
with a carrier leaving before evening.

One copper.

I had more than that now.

Not enough that copper meant nothing.

Enough that I could spend one without having to invent a reason.

I sat.

Lyssa,

The roof did not fall in.

This seemed like the most important opening.

I stared at it.

Then added:

I was not on it. I held a lantern. Completely different employment.

We did a show under it anyway. Then another problem happened and I
became an Advocate with forty-three lines, except apparently lines can
be removed by coworkers if they dislike them. I was paid the same five
copper.

I stopped.

That sounded like complaint.

It was complaint.

Fine.

I wrote about the bridge.

Not all of it.

Enough.

I wrote that I had said to when I meant from and that apparently an
entire criminal case could depend on a direction.

I wrote that the red dress was finally finished.

Then crossed out finally because Lyssa had no idea about the red dress.

Then wrote it again with an explanation that made the red dress sound
more important than the play.

I wrote that the bread was good.

I did not write about Drell.

I thought about him only after noticing I had not.

That was interesting for perhaps one second.

Then a chicken escaped.

The market became more interesting.

A boy caught it against a cabbage stall.

I finished:

We perform again tonight. I think we leave after that, but nobody has
told me anything useful, which may mean I am learning how this works.

I miss you.

Greg

I looked at the last line.

Left it.

Paid the copper.

The paper seller sanded the letter, folded it, tied it, and wrote CARROW
on the outside.

"Name?"

"Lyssa."

"Family?"

"No."

She waited.

I realized this was not enough information to deliver a letter in a
city.

I gave her the street and the place Lyssa usually worked near, plus the
description that would actually help someone find her.

The woman added notes.

"Carrier gets it to Carrow. After that, somebody asks."

"How long?"

"Two days if road's good. Four if not."

"Fine."

She put my letter into a leather pouch with perhaps twenty others.

That was it.

One copper.

I walked away.

At a button stall I saw a narrow brass clasp shaped like two leaves
meeting.

Lyssa would probably call it unnecessary.

It cost two copper.

I walked away.

Then came back.

"One?"

"Two."

"It's small."

"So are buttons."

"One."

"Two."

"One and a button."

She stared at me.

"What button?"

"I don't know. You're the button woman."

"Two."

I paid two.

Apparently theatre money could become brass leaves.

I put the clasp in my inner pocket with the remaining coins.

Not ceremonial.

Just hers, probably.

If she hated it, I could use it to hold Advocate pages together.

Back at the bakery, Orin had appeared.

He had a bruise on his jaw.

Nobody asked.

Marek did.

"What happened?"

"No."

"Was it a person?"

"No."

"A door?"

"No."

"A husband?"

"No."

"Wife?"

"No."

"Animal?"

"No."

"Furniture?"

Orin looked at him.

"No."

Marek smiled.

"Furniture."

Orin took his bread and left.

Serra was gone.

Iven was asleep in a chair.

Nessa had found another problem with the red dress.

Not the dress itself.

A glove.

This was acceptable.

The dress had graduated.

Rinna counted coins into two piles, moved three from one to the other,
then covered both when Marek came near.

"I wasn't."

"You were."

"I was walking."

"Elsewhere."

He walked elsewhere.

I looked at my Advocate pages for perhaps twenty minutes.

Not memorizing.

Mostly.

I restored two lines I liked.

Then removed one because Lorn was right and it made the Advocate sound
too eager.

I practiced the first entrance once with Iven after he woke.

"Your Majesty, I appear on behalf of the merchants of the eastern road."

"Fine."

"Whose three wagons of wool were seized at the north gate."

"Fine."

"Under an order bearing your seal."

"Fine."

"The soldiers were diverted FROM the bridge."

He stared.

"That is later."

"I know."

"Then why?"

"Checking."

"Stop checking."

Good advice.

I ignored it privately.

By late afternoon the confidence bucket was gone.

The roof man had taken it.

Bessa had sold sixty-four tickets.

Marek knew.

Of course.

Teren and Bessa had apparently reached terms because they were no longer
arguing about terms. They had moved on to arguing about whether two
children counted as one seat if they shared.

Bessa said no.

Teren said he did not care.

Bessa said then stop talking.

He did.

I respected her.

The second show began without rain.

That alone changed it.

People arrived less wet and louder.

Some had seen us the night before.

I knew because one man near the front said, "Bridge," when I came out as
Advocate.

Not loudly.

Loud enough.

I heard him.

Fuck.

I entered on time.

Order came free cleanly.

Seal remained unbroken.

First line correct.

North.

Wool.

Three.

Marek looked at me.

"And you have come to complain about my handwriting?"

Same line as yesterday.

He knew I knew it.

The audience from yesterday knew it too.

I had an answer ready.

That was the problem.

I said, "Your handwriting has seized three wagons."

Nothing.

Not dead.

Just nothing.

Yesterday's stealing wool had been better.

I felt the urge to fix it.

Did not.

The Advocate was scared.

Not angry.

I held the order with both hands for half a second before remembering I
needed a crutch.

Awkward.

Real.

"The men at the gate believed this was yours."

Marek's King leaned forward.

"And you do not?"

I looked at the seal.

"I would prefer not to."

That was not written.

The room went quiet.

Not bad quiet.

Different.

Marek stopped smiling.

"Careful."

There.

That was the scene.

For perhaps thirty seconds I forgot about yesterday.

Then I reached the bridge.

I knew FROM.

I knew it so well I nearly smiled.

The Chancellor said the soldiers had been moved for the crown's
protection.

My line.

"They were diverted from the bridge."

Correct.

Normal volume.

Good.

The man in front whispered, "From."

His wife hit his arm.

I almost laughed.

Did not.

Then the Chancellor changed his next line.

Yesterday he had denied issuing the order.

Tonight he said, "And who told you I gave such an instruction?"

That was not my page.

I knew the answer.

The seal.

No.

The witness.

Was there a witness?

I looked at Serra.

She was not in this scene yet.

Marek waited.

The Chancellor waited.

I said, "The man who obeyed it."

Good enough.

"Name him."

Fuck.

I did not know his name.

Maybe he had one.

Maybe not.

I said, "Bring him here and I'll ask."

Audience laughed.

The Chancellor smiled.

"Convenient."

"Very."

Then Marek cut across us.

"Enough."

He restored the scene himself.

Good.

I had preserved bridge and lost witness.

Progress.

Shopkeeper was cleaner.

No jurisdiction.

Not once.

Iven tried to tempt me.

He said, "By what authority do you demand payment?"

I looked at him.

He looked innocent.

Audience waited.

I said, "Mine."

Small laugh.

"Is that legal?"

"Pay me and find out."

Better.

Backstage I hit him with the cloth cap.

"Never again."

"You said that yesterday."

"So did you."

Sword was worse.

Not terrible.

Worse.

I expected the belt to catch.

It didn't.

I expected Serra to call me stubborn.

She didn't.

I expected Marek to enter with the extra sword.

He did not.

The scene felt strangely empty because all my remembered accidents were

I started anticipating Serra.

She changed the rhythm.

Slower.

I stopped.

She waited.

Then gave the line.

No joke.

No rescue.

Better.

The line was there.

I did not say it.

Audience laughed.

Serra said, "Convenient."

That word again.

Different scene.

Different actor.

I laughed inside.

Not outside.

The second Advocate entrance went almost correctly.

Almost.

I did not announce FROM.

I did not stop Orin.

I also played the Advocate so carefully afraid of the King that the

I could hear him anyway.

The final Shopkeeper appearance came.

I remembered it.

That alone felt suspicious.

I entered.

Three lines.

All correct.

Nobody laughed.

Not once.

Iven said, "Good."

I stared.

"Nothing happened."

"Exactly."

Applause.

Louder than last night.

More people.

"Eighty-two."

Of course.

Bessa was already smiling before the bows finished.

Teren was not.

That meant money was happening.

Backstage, people drank water and changed clothes and found missing

The blue poison jar was exactly where it belonged.

Empty.

Five copper.

Still five.

"Advocate improved," he said.

There.

Balance restored.

"Yes."

"Tomorrow."
