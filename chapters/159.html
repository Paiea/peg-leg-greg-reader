CHAPTER 159

THE PRIVATE PLAYER

Bessa tried again before breakfast.

"Ninety."

Teren was tying a ledger shut.

"No."

"Ninety-five."

"No."

"One hundred if I put benches along the rear wall."

"No."

Marek looked up from a roll.

"One hundred?"

Bessa pointed at him.

"Stay."

Teren said, "No."

Marek looked wounded.

"I have value."

"You have a wagon."

"Less value."

Bessa ignored him and followed Teren through the bakery while he
collected three different things from three different people.

"Better gate."

"Maybe."

"Full house."

"Maybe."

"You know my roof."

"Unfortunately."

"It stayed up."

"So far."

"That's performance."

I looked at Teren.

He did not look at me.

Bessa had stolen my argument.

She finally stopped at the door.

"When you come back, I want first date."

"If we come back."

"When."

Teren opened the door.

Bessa said, "Coward."

"Manager."

Then he went outside.

That appeared to be the end of negotiations.

For perhaps six minutes.

Packing had started before I woke.

I knew this because my bag was no longer beside my bed.

I found it downstairs in the wrong wagon.

Again.

Rinna found me finding it.

"Other."

"I know."

"Then why is it here?"

"I didn't put it here."

She looked at Pell.

Pell was carrying three hats and a loaf of bread.

"No," he said.

Nobody had accused him.

Rinna moved my bag.

I held the wagon flap.

This counted as improvement.

The company came apart from the town in pieces.

Nessa collected the red dress, two gloves, one shoe that belonged to
nobody currently present, and a length of blue ribbon she claimed was
ours.

The baker claimed it was his daughter's.

Nessa returned it immediately.

Rinna settled something with the bakery woman using coins I did not see
clearly. The woman then pushed a sack of yesterday's rolls into Rinna's
arms.

"Stale."

"Food."

"Exactly."

Rinna accepted.

Marek tried to take one.

Rinna slapped his hand.

"Travel."

"I am traveling."

"Later."

Davin arrived with the wagons already harnessed.

I did not know when he had left.

The long iron-cornered box came out last.

Davin and two local men moved it upright, slow and careful. Nobody asked
what was inside. Nobody joked. They secured it against the wagon frame.

I watched the knot.

Did not touch it.

Davin noticed me not touching it.

"Good."

That was all.

Orin was ready before Marek.

This disturbed everyone.

He had a coat today.

Both shoes matched.

The bruise on his jaw had darkened.

Marek stared.

"What happened to you?"

"No."

"You can't answer that with no."

"No."

"Was it furniture?"

"No."

"Was it attractive furniture?"

Orin climbed into the wagon.

"No."

Serra came from somewhere down the street carrying a small wrapped
parcel and the remains of the white flowers. Two blossoms had survived.
One was tucked through a buttonhole in her coat.

She said goodbye to nobody I knew.

A woman across the street raised two fingers.

Serra raised two back.

Then she climbed aboard.

Iven was annoyingly ready.

Pell was not.

Pell was missing.

We discovered this when Teren counted people.

Not loudly.

He looked at the wagons.

"Where is Pell?"

Nessa said, "Bakery."

We checked the bakery.

No Pell.

Rinna said, "Hall."

Marek volunteered to retrieve him.

Teren said, "No."

"Why?"

"You'll disappear too."

"Unfair."

"Accurate."

I went because I could not carry anything useful and therefore
apparently had time.

Pell was at the hall.

He was under the stage.

Of course.

"Why?"

"Fish."

I closed my eyes.

"The wooden fish."

"Yes."

"Why is it under the stage?"

"Don't know."

"Why do we need it?"

"Don't know."

"Then leave it."

He looked offended.

"It's ours."

That was apparently enough.

We found the fish wedged behind a support where someone had pushed it
with a boot.

Its spring still worked.

Pell carried it back like rescued livestock.

Bessa saw us crossing the square.

"One hundred!"

"No," I said.

"I wasn't asking you."

"Good."

We left at nine.

Approximately.

Nine according to Teren.

Marek said it was still morning and therefore exact time was oppressive.

The road east was better than the ridge road for the first hour, then
became worse in a more expensive way.

Stone paving appeared in stretches, cracked where wagon wheels had
broken the edges. Drainage ditches had been dug properly and then
neglected. Large houses appeared farther apart from one another behind
walls, hedges, or fields.

The rain held off.

That helped.

Marek slept.

That also helped.

I sat near the rear of the second wagon with Serra, Pell, and a costume
trunk that smelled faintly of damp wool.

The brass clasp remained in my inner pocket.

I checked once after a hard rut.

Still there.

My money was divided now. Most in the inner pocket. A few copper
somewhere easier to reach. The five from last night had joined the rest.

Serra noticed me checking.

"Rich?"

"No."

"Then stop touching it."

"I wasn't touching money."

She looked at me.

I stopped touching the clasp too.

Pell slept with his head against the costume trunk.

The wooden fish was on his lap.

Every large bump made it twitch.

After the fourth time I covered it with a cloth.

Serra said, "Coward."

"It watches."

"It doesn't have eyes."

I uncovered it.

It had painted eyes.

Serra looked.

"Fair."

We stopped once for water.

Teren spoke with Davin and Rinna away from the wagons. I could not hear.

Orin ate one of the stale rolls.

Marek woke long enough to ask whether we had arrived.

"No," six people said.

He went back to sleep.

By early afternoon the road narrowed between two stone walls.

The house appeared gradually.

First the gate.

Then trees.

Then a long pale roof beyond them.

Not a palace.

Too practical.

The main building had been expanded at least twice. The central portion
was older stone with tall windows and a steep roof. A newer brick wing
extended east. Another lower range connected to stables and service
yards.

I looked at the join between the stone and brick.

Teren, riding ahead, turned in his seat.

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You leaned."

"I looked."

"Same thing."

The gatekeeper knew we were coming.

That was new.

At public halls we usually arrived and began proving we belonged there.

Here a man in a dark coat checked a written list.

"The players?"

Teren said, "Yes."

The man counted wagons.

Then people.

His eyes stopped on me for a fraction longer than anyone else.

Not rude.

Recognition.

"Good."

I did not like that word.

He waved us through.

The service yard behind the house was busier than the front had
suggested.

Cooks, stable workers, servants carrying linen, a boy rolling a barrel,
two women stripping leaves from herbs at an outdoor table, three dogs
who believed the wagons were personally offensive.

Nobody stopped working because actors arrived.

A woman in a dark green dress came down the service steps.

Not a servant, I thought.

Then she took a crate from the boy because he nearly dropped it.

Maybe.

She handed it to another worker.

"Teren?"

He climbed down.

"Lady Merra."

There.

Not servant.

Probably.

She was perhaps forty, with gray beginning at her temples and sleeves
plain enough to work in. She looked at the wagons before she looked at
us.

"You're late."

"Road."

"It's dry."

"Now."

She considered that.

"Fair."

Teren said, "Balance first."

She smiled.

"No."

"Then we're early."

"You're already in my yard."

"Still portable."

Marek woke.

He whispered, "Money."

Iven put a hand over his mouth.

Lady Merra heard anyway.

"Your king?"

Teren said, "Unfortunately."

She looked at Marek.

Marek removed Iven's hand.

"Majesty, privately."

"Not yet."

She looked back at Teren.

"Half after setup. Half after performance."

"You paid half already."

"Of the original."

"Which bought travel."

"Which bought arrival."

"Arrival happened."

"Setup hasn't."

Teren's expression changed slightly.

Not angry.

Working.

"What changed?"

"Guest count."

"How much?"

"Thirty-two."

"You said twenty."

"People marry."

Nobody reacted.

I looked at Serra.

She looked at me.

Lady Merra continued.

"My sister's daughter announced her engagement three days ago. The
dinner grew."

There.

Celebration.

Human-sized.

Teren said, "Thirty-two isn't a problem."

"The room is."

"Show me."

She did.

We entered through the service side, not the front.

The house was warm enough that my damp shirt became immediately
uncomfortable.

The performance room had probably never been intended for performance.

Long salon.

Polished floor.

Tall windows.

Fireplace at one end.

A small raised platform at the other, perhaps meant for musicians or
speeches.

Chairs were arranged in two shallow rows with tables behind them.

Too close.

Everything was too close.

I could have reached the front row with a crutch.

Marek whispered, "Excellent."

Serra said, "No."

The ceiling was high.

That was the only generous thing about it.

Teren walked the room once.

"Full play won't fit."

Lady Merra said, "I don't want the full play."

Of course.

"What do you want?"

"The King."

Marek straightened.

"The Princess."

Serra did not.

"The talking sword."

There.

Everyone looked at me.

I looked at the floor.

Lady Merra said, "And the creditor."

"Shopkeeper," I said.

"That."

Teren asked, "Who told you?"

"My nephew saw you in Carrow."

Which nephew?

Not important.

"He said the sword was a man with one leg."

There it was.

Not cruel.

Not even inaccurate enough to argue with.

Still.

Lady Merra looked directly at me now.

"You're Gregory?"

"Greg."

"Can you do both?"

I said, "Apparently."

Marek laughed.

Teren did not.

Lady Merra said, "Good. My father will like you."

That was worse somehow.

"Why?"

"He likes unusual things."

I looked at Teren.

Teren said, "No."

Lady Merra looked at him.

"No what?"

"No handling."

"I wasn't going to handle him."

"Good."

I looked at Teren again.

"Handling?"

"Later."

Lady Merra folded her arms.

"My father is seventy-one, half deaf, rude, and paying for the
engagement dinner. He likes performers who answer him."

"Answering is fine," Teren said.

"If he asks for another scene?"

"Depends."

"If he asks the sword to insult him?"

Marek said, "I can do that."

"No," Teren said.

Marek looked wounded.

Lady Merra smiled.

"What exactly did I buy?"

Teren began counting on his fingers.

"Forty minutes. Selected scenes. Six players. Music between if needed.
One additional piece if requested before we finish."

"An hour."

"Forty-five."

"Fifty."

"Food."

"Already included."

"For everyone."

She looked toward the yard.

"Actors and crew."

"Everyone."

"Fine."

"Wine?"

"For dinner guests."

"Beer?"

"For workers."

Marek whispered, "Workers."

Iven stepped on his foot.

Lady Merra said, "Fifty minutes."

"Forty-five plus one requested piece."

"Done."

"Balance before the requested piece."

She laughed.

"You are tiresome."

"Paid."

"Not yet."

They shook hands.

I had just watched Teren sell us without selling all of us.

That was interesting.

I did not say it.

The house steward appeared.

This man was definitely a steward because he looked offended by the
existence of unlisted objects.

His name was Harl.

He wanted the wagons moved.

Davin wanted the long box kept under cover.

Harl wanted nothing blocking the service arch.

They solved this without me.

The box went into a dry carriage bay, upright.

Nobody recognized it.

Nobody opened it.

Good.

"The actors can eat in the side room," Harl said.

I went with the actors.

Nobody corrected me.

The side room had a long table, bread, cold sliced meat, cheese, apples,
pickled onions, and a pot of something thick with barley.

Marek stopped in the doorway.

"Oh."

Rinna said, "Behave."

"I am."

"You haven't started yet."

The food was better than our last three meals.

Not impossibly better.

Just enough butter to reveal money.

The bread was fresh.

There was meat nobody had brought as a gift after a show.

There was more cheese than twelve people needed.

A servant set down another plate.

Rinna looked at it.

Then at the first plate.

Then at the servant.

"Does that stay?"

"Until dinner."

"And after?"

The servant shrugged.

"Kitchen decides."

Rinna nodded.

She had learned something.

Nessa touched the tablecloth.

Not dramatically.

Two fingers.

Then she looked at the curtains.

Pell followed her eyes.

"No."

"I didn't."

"You did."

"Fabric."

"Not ours."

"Obviously."

Marek had already found the beer.

Teren took it from him.

"After."

"I am a worker."

"Debatable."

Lady Merra entered before we finished eating.

"Change."

Teren looked up.

"What?"

"My father wants the sword at dinner."

"You bought after dinner."

"He wants it during."

"No."

She looked surprised.

Not offended.

Just unused to no arriving so quickly.

"It would amuse him."

"It would put Gregory between tables with people eating."

She looked at me.

I had not considered that.

Crutches, chairs, wine, servants, plates.

Bad.

"He can sit."

Teren looked at the salon again through the open door.

"At the end of the room."

"Near my father."

"End."

"Near."

"Then move your father."

She laughed.

Actually laughed.

"Nobody moves my father."

Teren said, "Then your father waits."

That could have gone badly.

Instead Lady Merra looked delighted.

"I see why my nephew liked you."

"He didn't see me."

"He complained about you."

"Better."

She left.

Marek said, "I want private money forever."

Teren gave him back his bread, not his beer.

Setup took less equipment and more thought.

No scenery.

Nothing tall.

No rear entrance.

The salon had two doors, but one led directly into a corridor guests
would use. The other led toward the side room.

Serra and Marek could use both.

I could not cross behind anything because there was nothing to cross
behind.

For Sword, they put my mounting board beside the fireplace.

I stared.

"Hot."

Davin moved it three feet.

"Better."

For Shopkeeper, a table and chair.

Lady Merra's steward wanted one of the house tables.

Teren said no.

Harl said ours looked poor.

Teren said, "Shopkeeper."

Harl looked at the battered table.

"Fair."

The guests began arriving before we were ready.

That was also different.

Public audiences waited outside.

Private audiences belonged to the building.

They wandered through.

A man with silver at his collar stopped to watch Pell pin a sleeve.

Two girls stared openly at me.

One whispered something.

The other whispered back.

Then both ran away.

A servant carrying wine said, "Sword?"

I said, "Sometimes."

He nodded.

"Good."

Again.

I was beginning to dislike being good before doing anything.

An older man entered using two sticks and no patience.

Seventy-one, presumably.

Lady Merra's father.

He had white hair, a heavy waistcoat, and the expression of someone who
had spent decades discovering that people eventually moved if he
continued forward.

Everyone moved.

He stopped in front of me.

Not close enough to be rude.

Close enough to inspect.

"You're the sword."

"Sometimes."

"One leg."

"Consistently."

He barked a laugh.

Good.

Not mine.

His.

"How'd you lose it?"

There were thirty possible answers.

None belonged in the salon.

I said, "Badly."

He laughed again.

Lady Merra appeared behind him.

"Father."

"What?"

"Don't interrogate the actors."

He looked at her.

"I'm paying them."

Teren appeared from the side room.

"You're paying for a performance."

The old man looked at him.

"Difference?"

"Several silver."

Silence.

Then the old man laughed harder than before.

"Fine."

He pointed one stick at me.

"Insult me later."

Teren said, "If the scene earns it."

The old man looked delighted.

I was less certain.

He walked away.

I said quietly, "Several silver?"

Teren said, "Not yours."

"Still."

"Work."

Then he left.

Forty-five minutes.

Selected scenes.

No Advocate.

Thank every god.

Sword.

Shopkeeper.

Marek's King.

Serra's Princess.

Orin in two short pieces.

Iven in Shopkeeper and another scene.

Music between.

One requested piece after payment balance.

This was not the play.

It was pieces of the play arranged around people who had eaten too much.

Dinner ran late.

Of course.

We waited in the side room dressed and ready while servants carried
plates past us.

The smell became cruel.

Marek ate another apple.

Nessa took it from him after one bite because his costume had pale
cuffs.

He finished it by leaning forward while she held it.

Nobody found this strange.

The household staff moved faster as the guests moved slower.

I watched a young servant balance six plates along one arm.

That was more impressive than anything I had done as Advocate.

When the salon finally cleared enough for chairs to turn toward us, the
guests brought wine with them.

Thirty-two had become thirty-five.

Harl looked offended by arithmetic.

Lady Merra looked pleased.

Teren looked at the room.

Then at us.

"Short."

"How short?" Marek asked.

"Until they stop listening."

"That is not time."

Iven said, "Theatre."

I hated him.

We began with the King.

Not me.

Good.

The guests talked through the first minute.

Not all.

Enough.

Marek pushed louder.

Teren, from the side, shook his head once.

Marek stopped pushing.

Serra entered.

The room changed.

Not silent.

More directed.

She had the red dress.

Apparently rich people liked red too.

Then Sword.

I was mounted beside the fireplace.

Three feet away from an old man holding wine.

Lady Merra's father.

Of course.

Serra took me.

The old man said, "That's him."

Serra looked at him.

Then at me.

"Apparently."

The room laughed.

That was not written.

Fine.

I said, "I was hoping for a less observant audience."

The old man laughed first.

Others followed.

Serra carried me into the scene.

Different distance.

Different danger.

I could see every face.

One woman was still eating a pear.

A man near the window whispered to someone through my line.

A child sat on the floor because chairs were apparently optional when
wealthy.

Serra asked what sort of sword complained about being stolen.

I said, "An expensive one."

Lady Merra's father called, "How expensive?"

There.

Customer.

Audience.

Patron.

Old man.

I looked toward Teren.

He did nothing.

That meant answer.

"More than your daughter told you."

The room exploded.

Lady Merra covered her face.

Her father slapped one stick against the floor.

Teren looked at the ceiling.

I had insulted him.

Apparently the scene had earned it.

We continued.

Not cleanly.

The old man interrupted twice more.

Serra answered one.

Ignored one.

Marek entered and immediately began playing to him.

That worked until it didn't.

The rest of the room started disappearing.

Teren coughed once.

Marek heard.

He turned back into the scene.

Interesting.

Shopkeeper came later.

The table looked poor enough to satisfy Harl.

Iven entered.

Lady Merra's father recognized me without the sword.

"Creditor!"

I said, "Unfortunately."

He said, "Charge him double."

Iven looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because I can afford it."

I said, "Then you're next."

That got him.

The room laughed.

Iven said, "Take him first."

"Older debt."

The old man raised his wine.

Fine.

The scene moved.

Private audiences did not know when to stay out of it because staying
out had never been part of the agreement.

That was the difference.

Or one difference.

A woman asked Iven directly whether his character had a wife.

He answered, "Not anymore."

I had never heard that.

Neither had he, probably.

She asked why.

He pointed at me.

"Debt."

I said, "I don't take wives."

"Good policy."

"Expensive."

Jurisdiction was dead.

Expensive survived.

Fine.

We reached the end.

Applause came from thirty-five people and six servants standing in the
corridor.

The servants were louder.

Marek bowed toward them.

Correct.

Lady Merra approached Teren before anyone could request anything.

She handed him a small purse.

He weighed it.

Opened it.

Counted.

Not secretly.

Silver flashed.

More than one.

Less than a fortune.

Enough that I understood why we had left Bessa's ninety.

Teren closed the purse.

Lady Merra said, "One more."

"One."

"My father wants the sword."

"Of course."

"Five minutes."

"Seven."

She blinked.

"You negotiate upward?"

"He'll interrupt."

She laughed.

"Seven."

Teren looked at me.

"Sword."

I said, "Do I get more?"

Lady Merra looked interested.

Teren said, "Two extra copper."

I stared.

"For seven minutes?"

"For requested piece."

Marek said, "I can be Sword."

"No," three people said.

Lady Merra's father shouted from the salon:

"Bring me the expensive one!"

I held out my hand.

"Two copper."

Teren put two copper into it before the scene.

That was new.

I pocketed them.

"Now?"

"Now."

I went back to the fireplace.

Apparently private money was different.
