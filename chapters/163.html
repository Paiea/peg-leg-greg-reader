CHAPTER 163

THE MILL PLAYER

The road had opinions.

Mostly about wheels.

Every hundred yards it found a new way to express them.

The wagon dropped into another rut. Marek's head struck the sideboard.

He opened one eye.

"Money?"

"Still maybe."

"Wake me at yes."

He closed it.

Across from him, Serra had her folded note tucked under one thigh while
she mended a tear in her glove. She had not opened it since the bridge.

I looked away before she noticed me noticing.

The road climbed.

That was worse.

The ruts became shelves cut into hard dirt, some filled with yesterday's
water. Davin walked beside the lead wagon more often than he rode,
choosing where the wheels went one bad stretch at a time. When he lifted
one hand, the driver stopped. When he pointed, the wagon moved six
inches left or right.

Nobody argued.

I had seen Teren argue with hall owners, patrons, market masters,
actors, Rinna, weather, and once a chair.

He did not argue with Davin about roads.

That seemed sensible.

By late afternoon the land dropped again and we heard the mill before we
saw it.

Not a pleasant waterwheel turning beside flowers.

A mill.

The first sound was a deep wooden knocking under a constant rush of
water. Then came a grinding vibration I could feel through the wagon
boards. The road curved around a low hill and the settlement appeared
below us, spread along a narrow river that had been forced to behave.

A stone channel split away from the river upstream. Water ran fast
between retaining walls, crossed beneath a timber walkway, and struck a
broad wheel beside the largest building. The wheel was dark with spray.
Beyond it stood two long timber structures, a stone storehouse, sheds,
workers' cottages, a stable, and several grain barns with high doors.

Flour dust had gotten everywhere.

Roof edges.

Window ledges.

Cart wheels.

People.

A man came out of a doorway carrying a sack and looked as though someone
had thrown him into bread.

Greg noticed the sluice gate first.

Of course Greg noticed the sluice gate first.

I leaned.

Teren, riding ahead, turned around.

"No."

"I'm looking."

"That's how it starts."

"I don't even know what I'm looking at."

"Better."

We reached the bottom.

A wagon loaded with grain blocked the main yard.

Davin stopped us before the bridge.

"Wait."

The bridge was wide enough.

I could see that.

I almost said it.

Then a second grain wagon came out of the mill and took the entire
bridge while turning.

Fine.

We waited.

A woman walking beside it pointed at my crutch.

"Don't put that on the white boards."

I looked down.

Several boards along the edge of the yard were dusted pale.

"Why?"

"Chute."

"What chute?"

She pointed under them.

I looked.

There was a gap.

Not large.

Large enough for the bottom of a crutch to disappear into somewhere I
did not want to follow.

"Good."

"Not really."

She kept walking.

Local competence.

Annoying.

Useful.

When the bridge cleared, Davin took the wagons across one at a time.

The long box moved with them.

Upright.

Dry.

Unopened.

Nobody in the mill yard looked twice.

They had grain to move.

"Players?"

Teren said, "Yes."

"Vessa's in the count room."

"Which?"

"Count room."

Teren started walking.

Rinna followed.

Marek sat up.

"Money?"

Teren did not turn.

"Not yet."

Marek lay back down.

I got off the wagon because my back wanted something different.

Pell handed me a bundle of costume cloth.

"Why?"

"Keep it off the flour."

"Where?"

He pointed at the flour.

"Not there."

Then he ran toward Nessa.

Good.

I stood in an industrial yard holding fabric.

A cart came toward me.

I moved.

Another cart came from the other direction.

I moved again.

The woman who had warned me about the chute passed back through.

"You're in the loading lane."

"Where isn't?"

She pointed toward a strip beside the stone wall.

I went there.

The cloth remained off the flour.

Success.

A bell rang.

I nearly dropped it.

Doors opened all across the mill.

Workers came out.

Not everyone.

Enough to suddenly make the yard belong to a shift instead of wagons.

Some headed toward the cottages. Some went to a pump. Three men sat
directly on grain sacks and started eating. A woman washed her face in a
bucket and made the water white.

Iven came over.

"Show?"

"I don't know."

"Why are you holding that?"

"I don't know."

He nodded.

"Good."

The count room door opened.

Teren came out.

Rinna did not.

Behind him was a broad woman with cropped brown hair and a flour streak
across one cheek. She wore a dark vest over rolled sleeves and had two
pencils tucked through the same buttonhole.

"Vessa," Teren said.

She looked at us.

Not me.

All of us.

"Which one's the manager?"

Marek pointed at himself.

Teren said, "Me."

Vessa looked at Marek.

"Why did he point?"

"Illness."

"Permanent?"

"Probably."

Marek said, "I can hear you."

Vessa nodded.

"Good."

She was the one who had answered.

Not no meant exactly what it sounded like.

She could offer us a place.

She could offer food.

She could offer some money.

She could not offer enough money.

"Owner won't pay for a troupe," she said.

Teren said, "Then why did you write?"

"Owner isn't the only person here."

"Who is paying?"

"Me. Partly."

"Personally?"

"Mill account."

"Owner knows?"

"Owner knows the account exists."

Teren stared at her.

Vessa stared back.

I liked her.

Rinna came out carrying a scrap of paper.

"How much?"

Vessa named the number again.

This time I heard it.

Twenty copper guaranteed.

For the company.

Not each.

Twenty.

More than the market.

Less than a full public show if everyone had to be paid normal rates.

Teren said, "No."

Vessa said, "I know."

That was apparently why it had been not no.

She continued.

"Workers asked."

"All of them?"

"No."

"How many?"

"Enough to be annoying."

A man carrying a sack shouted from across the yard, "We heard that."

Vessa shouted back, "Work."

He kept walking.

Teren asked, "Door?"

"No door."

"Collection?"

"Maybe."

"Maybe isn't money."

"Neither is an empty evening."

Rinna looked at the paper.

"Lodging?"

"Store loft."

"Dry?"

"Yes."

"All."

"Food?"

"Tonight."

"Breakfast?"

"No."

"Free until the owner discovers thirst."

Rinna ignored that.

"Animals?"

"Stable space. Oats not included."

Davin, who had appeared behind us without sound, said, "How much?"

Vessa named a number.

He looked at Rinna.

Rinna frowned.

Teren said, "What do they want?"

Vessa shrugged.

"Something after second shift."

"Full play?"

"They don't know what that means."

Marek looked offended.

"Everyone knows what a play is."

A worker passing with a handcart said, "Is it singing?"

Marek said, "No."

The worker said, "Good."

Marek looked more offended.

"Meal room. Benches. One end clear if we move tables."

"Workers?"

"Seats."

"And if they don't?"

"Forty."

Teren looked at Rinna.

Rinna looked at Davin.

"Thank you," Teren said.

"Tomorrow worse if rain."

We all looked up.

Clouds.

Not dramatic ones.

Just enough.

Vessa said, "You stay, you're under roof."

Rinna asked, "Twenty copper, dinner, loft, stable space."

"Yes."

"Plus collection."

Vessa said, "I won't force them."

Teren said, "Neither will we."

"Then yes."

"Collection belongs to company."

Vessa shrugged.

"Workers' money belongs to workers until they give it to you."

"Good."

"How long?"

"Forty minutes."

Vessa laughed.

"Twenty-five."

"Thirty-five."

"Twenty-five."

"Thirty."

"Done."

Thirty minutes after second shift.

Twenty copper guaranteed.

Dinner.

Dry floor.

Whatever the workers voluntarily put into a collection afterward.

No normal show rate.

Again.

Marek said, "What do we get?"

Teren turned.

"Food."

"I am beginning to suspect this industry is mostly food."

Rinna said, "You're still here."

"Evidence."

Iven looked at me.

I looked at him.

We both said, "No."

Jurisdiction stayed dead.

Teren decided individual pay would depend on the collection.

That made Marek unhappy.

"Guaranteed?"

"No."

"We have guaranteed company money."

"Company has guaranteed company money."

"I remain company."

Rinna said, "You remain expensive."

"Privately."

"Always."

Teren clapped once.

"Unload what we need. Nothing else."

The meal room was exactly what Vessa had promised.

A long room with plank walls, low beams, six heavy tables, benches, two
small windows, and flour dust in places flour dust should not have been.

No stage.

No wings.

No curtain.

One door at each end.

The room smelled like bread before bread became food.

Greg immediately looked at the beams.

No.

Not a puzzle.

Just beams.

Pell sneezed four times.

Nessa said, "Costumes stay covered."

Pell sneezed again.

"Yes."

Teren walked the room.

"Mixed bill."

Marek said, "King."

"Short."

"King."

"Short King."

"Insulting."

"Survive."

Teren pointed.

"Sword. Shopkeeper. Death."

Orin, standing in the doorway, said, "No."

That was all.

Teren accepted it.

No death piece.

"Am I in it?"

"No."

"Good."

Less good.

Vessa gave me and Iven four workers to help rearrange the room.

I made the mistake of assuming they wanted me to tell them where the

They did not.

A narrow man named Joss had apparently arranged this room for every

"Maybe there?"

Dry floor.

Mostly.

Flour mill.

Sawdust.

I had enough problems.

My crutch went with it.

Not charming.

Just stupid.

Serra found it.

"Missing something?"

"No."

She looked at my leg.

"Fair."

"Thank you."

"Don't do that onstage."

Her folded note was no longer visible.

None of my business.

The mill ran around us until the second bell.

That was the strangest part.

We were preparing entertainment inside somebody else's workday.

Sacks moved.
