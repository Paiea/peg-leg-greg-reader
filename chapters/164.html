PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 164

THE PROSPECTOR

The mill started before I did.

I woke to grain moving under my head.

Not metaphorically.

A sack hit the floor below the loft hard enough to shake dust out of the
boards. Someone shouted a number. Someone else shouted a different
number. The wheel was already turning, the grinding vibration had
returned, and pale flour floated through a blade of morning light beside
the loft door.

Pell slept through all of it.

Iven did not.

He was sitting against a sack with his boots on, eating an apple.

"Breakfast?"

"Mine."

"Helpful."

"You asked what it was."

Below us, a man shouted, "Need the loft!"

That woke Pell.

He sat up.

"Fire?"

"No."

"Fish?"

"No."

He went back down.

I kicked his boot.

"Loft."

"Oh."

The company came apart from sleep quickly.

Blankets rolled.

Bags closed.

Costumes disappeared into covers before flour could settle on them.
Nessa found a white handprint on one bundle and stared at Pell until he
denied touching anything.

"I was asleep."

"You have hands while asleep."

"Unfair."

Rinna climbed the ladder carrying the wooden collection bowl from last
night.

The nail was still in it.

Marek had apparently not invested it.

"Out," she said.

Nobody asked why.

The mill needed the loft.

We left.

Vessa passed us in the yard with a ledger under one arm and a man
talking at her about the grain-lift rope.

He said, "I told Brann."

"Tell him again."

"He knows."

"Then make knowing expensive."

She saw Teren.

"Road east is clear."

Davin said, "Mostly."

Vessa looked at him.

"You've been?"

"Asked."

"Who?"

"Cart."

She accepted that.

Teren said, "Thank you."

Vessa nodded once.

Then the rope man said something else and we stopped existing.

Good.

Joss was moving tables back into the meal room.

I passed the door.

He pointed at one bench.

"Wrong."

"I didn't touch it."

"I know."

That somehow felt worse.

Breakfast had not been included.

Rinna made this everybody's problem in an organized way.

We still had bread from the road food, several apples, hard cheese, and
the last of something that might once have been roast meat. Marek
reached for the meat.

Rinna slapped his hand.

"That's noon."

"What is morning?"

"Bread."

"Morning deserves dignity."

"Buy dignity."

Marek checked his purse.

Morning lost.

A woman near the mill gate sold hot oat cakes from a covered basket. One
copper bought two.

Greg had money.

That fact was still strange enough that I stood there for a moment
deciding whether hot food was worth one copper.

Then I bought it.

No debate.

No calculation about whether I could survive without it.

One cake went into my mouth.

The other went into my pocket for later.

That felt more significant than it deserved.

Marek watched.

"You have wealth."

"I have breakfast."

"Same thing."

"You have two copper."

"Reserved."

"For what?"

"Opportunity."

Orin walked past us.

"No."

Marek pointed after him.

"He fears enterprise."

We left the mill before eight.

Nobody waved.

The wheel kept turning.

A grain wagon was already waiting for the bridge before our last wagon
crossed it.

The road east really was better.

Not good.

Better.

For almost half an hour we managed a speed that made the wagons feel
like transportation instead of furniture. Then a flock of sheep occupied
the road and reduced us to walking behind them while one boy and an
elderly dog argued about direction. Davin did not complain. Teren did
not complain. Marek complained enough for everyone.

The sheep ignored him.

Eventually the boy got them into a field and lifted one hand in apology.

Davin lifted his back.

That seemed to settle the matter.

There were fewer ruts deep enough to have names, and stretches had been
laid with crushed stone. We made enough speed that Marek stayed awake.

This proved unfortunate.

He spent the first hour attempting to determine what kind of town we
were approaching.

"Rich?"

Teren said, "No."

"Poor?"

"No."

"Violent?"

"Probably."

"Useful?"

"Unknown."

"Women?"

Serra said, "Almost certainly."

"Excellent."

She threw an apple core at him.

He caught it.

"Breakfast."

The land opened as we traveled. Grain fields gave way to pasture and
fenced lots. We passed cattle moving west, then sheep, then three wagons
loaded with empty cages that smelled as if the cages had recently
contained something deeply committed to being alive.

By midday we began passing people headed the same direction.

Carts.

Farmers.

A family walking beside two goats.

A man leading a bull with the expression of someone who had made several
poor choices and was committed to all of them.

Market day tomorrow.

The road knew before the town did.

Rinna sat beside Teren with a folded scrap on her knee.

"No answer," she said.

"I know."

"Could have changed."

"Could have."

"Could be full."

"Could be."

"Could be nothing."

Teren looked at her.

"We try."

Rinna folded the paper.

That was apparently the entire strategy.

At a crossroads we stopped for water.

There was a stone trough, a shrine, a blacksmith's shed, and a woman
selling boiled eggs under a cloth.

Marek spent one copper.

Reserved opportunity became egg.

He ate it in three bites.

"Investment?"

"Protein."

"Profitable?"

"Immediately."

Davin checked the wheels.

Rinna checked the horses.

Pell discovered a burr attached to his trousers and accused the
countryside.

Nessa removed it.

Serra sat on the low shrine wall writing something.

Not reading.

Writing.

I saw the folded paper beside her.

I looked toward the road.

None of my business.

I took out the oat cake I had saved.

It had flattened into a warm disk with pocket lint on one edge.

I ate the other edge.

I thought about Lyssa.

Not because of anything important.

Because she would have told me not to eat pocket lint and then watched
to see whether I did.

I touched the inner pocket where the brass clasp sat wrapped in scrap
paper.

Still there.

My letter might be in Carrow by now.

Or in a wagon.

Or under somebody's soup.

I had no idea.

For the first time it occurred to me that Lyssa could answer and have
nowhere sensible to send the answer.

I asked Teren.

"How do letters find you?"

He looked at me.

"They usually don't."

"Helpful."

"If I know a route, I leave names at halls and inns."

"And if you don't know the route?"

"They don't."

"So people just miss letters?"

"Yes."

"That seems badly designed."

"The road?"

"Mail."

"The road too."

He drank water.

"If you're writing again, send to Carrow and tell them to hold replies
at East Market Hall."

"Will we be there?"

"Eventually."

"When?"

He looked at the wagons.

"No."

Fair.

I did not write another letter.

Not yet.

We reached the town in the middle of the afternoon.

It announced itself with fences.

Long ones.

Heavy ones.

Pens spread along the western edge in a rough grid, some empty, some
holding sheep, pigs, goats, or cattle. The smell arrived before the
buildings did. Beyond the pens stood a broad market square, two inns, a
row of shops, a smithy, a low brick counting house, and a whitewashed
building with tall double doors.

The assembly room.

Probably.

A painted sign beside the road said KEST.

Below that, someone had nailed a smaller board:

MARKET TOMORROW.

Below that, someone had written:

NO PIGS IN EAST YARD.

A pig stood in the east yard.

Good town.

We pulled into a wagon field behind the first inn.

A man came out before the wheels stopped.

"Stabling?"

Teren said, "Maybe."

"Maybe costs one copper."

"Looking costs?"

"Free."

Teren looked.

The man looked back.

Rinna climbed down.

"Water?"

"Included if stabled."

"Oats?"

"Separate."

"Loft?"

"Full."

"Rooms?"

"Nearly."

"Market."

"Exactly."

Rinna's face did a small thing.

Money.

We had arrived on the expensive side of tomorrow.

Teren said, "Assembly room."

The innkeeper pointed across the square.

"Ask Noll."

"Where?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Whether he owes you money."

Marek said, "I like him."

Teren ignored Marek.

"Who is Noll?"

"Keeper."

"Where?"

"Try cattle office."

Teren started across the square.

Then stopped.

He pointed at me.

"Come."

I looked behind me.

Nobody else.

"Why?"

"Door."

"What door?"

"Assembly room."

"I can see it."

"Good. Check it."

Apparently I had been promoted to door.

We crossed the square.

Market preparation was already swallowing the town.

People marked spaces with chalk.

A woman hammered stakes beside a cloth awning.

Two boys rolled barrels toward an inn.

Cattle complained from the western pens.

A man carrying six wooden stools nearly walked into me, then blamed me
with his face.

I moved.

The assembly room sat beside the counting house.

The front doors were broad.

Too broad to be interesting.

Teren tried one.

Locked.

He knocked.

Nothing.

A woman arranging baskets across the street shouted, "Noll!"

Teren shouted back, "Where?"

She pointed vaguely east.

"Office!"

"Which office?"

"Cattle!"

Good.

We went to the cattle office.

Noll was not there.

A clerk with ink on both hands said, "Market office."

We went to the market office.

Noll was not there.

A boy said, "Pens."

"Which pens?"

The boy looked offended.

"Cattle."

Back to cattle.

Teren stopped in the middle of the square.

"Do you know what he looks like?"

"No."

"Good."

"How are we finding him?"

"Eventually he'll become annoyed enough to find us."

That sounded like experience.

He handed me a folded note.

"If you see Noll, give him this."

"How will I know?"

"Ask."

Then Teren left me.

I had not understood the assignment.

I thought I was checking a door.

Now I was hunting a man nobody could locate.

Touring.

I asked the basket woman.

"Noll?"

"Red vest."

"Thank you."

"Unless he changed."

"Today?"

"Since noon."

Fine.

I asked a cattle drover.

"Noll?"

"Short."

I asked a woman carrying a chicken upside down.

"Noll?"

"Annoying."

We were narrowing him down.

At the east pens I found a short man in a red vest arguing with three
people at once.

One wanted another livestock space.

One wanted money back.

One wanted pigs moved.

The pig problem had spread.

I waited.

He noticed me.

"What?"

"Noll?"

"No."

I looked at the red vest.

He looked at it too.

"Brother."

"Where's Noll?"

"Assembly room."

Of course.

I went back.

The doors were open.

Teren stood inside with a tall bald man holding a ring of keys.

Noll.

He was not short.

He wore no red.

I handed him the note.

He opened it.

"This was for me?"

"Apparently."

"You've been carrying it?"

"For twelve minutes."

Teren said, "Excellent."

I looked at him.

"You knew?"

"No."

"Worse."

Noll read.

"No."

There it was.

Clean.

Teren said, "Room?"

"No."

"Tomorrow?"

"Committee has it."

"For?"

"Market supper."

"Night?"

"Market supper."

"After?"

"Cleaning."

"Before?"

"Setup."

Teren looked around.

The assembly room was better than the mill meal room and worse than an
actual theatre.

High ceiling.

Small platform at one end.

Windows along both walls.

A side door.

A kitchen hatch.

Stacked benches.

No backstage.

No curtain.

No reason for us to be there.

Teren said, "Tonight."

Noll looked at him.

"Tonight what?"

"Room."

"No."

"Why?"

"Empty."

"That's why."

Noll frowned.

"Nobody's coming tonight."

"Could."

"Who?"

"People."

Noll stared.

Teren waited.

I leaned against the wall.

This was not acting.

Probably.

Noll said, "Room costs eight copper."

"Four."

"Eight."

"No audience."

"Your problem."

"No heat."

"Six."

"Eight."

"Benches included?"

"Obviously."

"Kitchen?"

"No."

"Side room?"

"No."

"Seven."

Noll said, "Eight."

Teren said, "No."

Then he walked out.

I followed.

That was apparently negotiation.

Outside, I asked, "Are we done?"

"No."

"You said no."

"He said eight."

"You said no."

"Yes."

"That sounds done."

"It isn't."

I hated this job.

Rinna found us near the wagon field.

"Room?"

"Eight."

"No."

"Yes."

"Lodging?"

"Bad."

"How bad?"

She told him.

Teren swore.

Not loudly.

The private-house silver was no longer abstract.

Neither was the mill money.

Rooms, oats, stabling, food, room rental.

A town could eat a job before the job existed.

Rinna said, "We can camp outside."

"Weather?"

Davin looked up.

"Dry."

"For once."

"Maybe."

Teren rubbed his face.

"Market committee?"

"Tonight," Rinna said. "Counting house. Six."

"Who told you?"

"Man with pigs."

"Reliable?"

"He had three."

That was enough.

We waited.

That was the work.

Not rehearsing.

Not performing.

Waiting for six.

The company spread through Kest because there was nothing else to do.

Nessa and Pell found a cloth seller.

Davin went somewhere involving horses.

Orin disappeared.

Marek discovered a dice game behind the second inn and, to my surprise,
did not join it.

He stood watching.

"Growth?" I asked.

"Capital preservation."

"You have one copper."

"Exactly."

Serra bought a cup of something hot and sat on a fence watching cattle.

I sat beside her.

Not too close.

The fence was wide.

"You know anyone here?" I asked.

"No."

That was it.

Good.

A cow sneezed on Marek when he walked past.

Better.

At six the market committee met.

Teren went in.

Rinna went with him.

We did not.

I bought a bowl of beans for one copper because dinner was not included
anywhere and nobody had promised it would be.

The bowl was hot.

I could afford it.

I ate beside the wagons while Pell tried to teach the wooden fish to
bite on command.

"Doesn't it already?"

"Sometimes."

"That's not teaching."

"It knows independence."

The fish snapped.

Pell smiled.

I moved my hand.

At dusk Teren came back.

Marek stood.

"Money?"

"Maybe."

Marek looked at me.

I said, "Don't."

Teren held up a paper.

The committee would not pay a guarantee.

They would let us use part of the south market edge tomorrow after
livestock judging.

No charge.

We could collect.

No room.

No company fee.

No food.

No lodging.

Rinna said, "That's not work."

Teren said, "Could be."

"Could be twelve copper."

"Could be forty."

"Could be six."

"Yes."

"Dast?"

He looked east.

"Still after."

Rinna folded her arms.

The assembly room doors across the square opened.

Men began carrying tables inside for tomorrow's supper.

Eight copper.

No audience.

The market square would cost nothing.

Maybe audience.

Maybe money.

Maybe not.

Teren looked at us.

"Tomorrow. One set first."

Marek said, "One?"

"If they stop, two."

"If they pay?"

"If they pay."

"And if they don't?"

"We leave."

Simple.

Rinna sighed.

"Where do we sleep?"

Teren pointed west.

Beyond the livestock pens, outside the town wall, was a flat field
already filling with market wagons and temporary camps.

Free.

Probably.

Marek looked at it.

"Private money was better."

I said, "Private beds were better."

"We had a loft."

"Private floor was better."

"Fair."

We moved the wagons before dark.

Davin chose a place away from the lowest ground.

Rinna paid for oats.

Nothing else.

No room.

No warehouse.

No stable loft.

We ate what remained of the road food and what people bought for
themselves.

The town kept building tomorrow around us.

Lanterns appeared.

Animals settled badly.

People shouted across camps.

Somebody sang near a wagon until somebody else told them to stop.

I lay under the wagon with my blanket and looked at the dark shape of
the assembly-room roof beyond the pens.

We had come east for work.

We had found no guarantee.

We were staying anyway.

One set.

Then maybe another.

Then maybe Dast.

Maybe.

I put a hand over the pocket with Lyssa's clasp.

One more stop had become one more morning.

That was probably how it happened.
