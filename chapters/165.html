PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 165

THE DRAW

A cow woke me by objecting to commerce.

It began before sunrise and continued until I accepted that whatever
argument the cow was making had already been lost. By then three other
cattle had joined it, somebody was hammering stakes into the ground, two
wagon wheels were grinding over stone nearby, and a man somewhere beyond
our camp was shouting that six was not eight.

Another man shouted back that the animal had four legs.

This did not settle the matter.

I opened my eyes under the wagon.

Market day.

The flat field beyond Kest had changed while we slept. Cooking smoke
hung low between wagons. Traders folded blankets, tightened harness,
checked ropes, cursed animals, counted baskets, and shouted names I did
not know. A boy ran past carrying a chicken. The chicken appeared to
disagree with the route.

Pell was asleep beside one wagon wheel with the wooden fish tucked under
his arm.

Iven stepped over him.

"Dead?"

"Probably."

"The fish?"

"No."

Pell opened one eye.

"Rude."

Breakfast cost money.

Rinna solved part of this by buying a pot of oat porridge from a camp
cook for the company, which meant everybody got something warm and
nobody got enough to mistake it for generosity. She paid from company
copper. Marek watched the transaction closely.

"How much?"

"Not yours."

"I am learning."

"Learn farther away."

I bought nothing else.

My bowl was enough.

Marek ate his, then stood beside the pot until Rinna noticed.

"No."

"I haven't asked."

"You leaned."

I looked at Iven.

He looked at me.

Apparently leaning had become legally binding.

Davin had already moved the wagons closer to the south side of town
before most of us finished eating. The long iron-cornered box rode where
it always rode, upright, tied, dry, uninteresting to everybody except
the people who had to move around it.

Pell reached toward one of Davin's ropes.

Davin said, "No."

Pell withdrew his hand.

"Good," I said.

Davin looked at me.

I stopped helping.

By full morning, Kest had become louder than the mill.

The livestock pens filled first.

Cattle in the western runs.

Sheep farther south.

Pigs near the east yard despite the sign.

Goats wherever goats had decided they belonged.

The broad market square filled with cloth awnings and temporary tables.
People sold harness, knives, pots, onions, boots, cheese, salt, cheap
ribbons, baskets, wooden tools, live birds, dead birds, and things in
jars I could not identify from a safe distance.

The company had been given part of the south market edge.

That sounded larger when Teren said it yesterday.

In practice, it was a strip of packed earth between a sheep pen, a cart
lane, and a woman selling onions.

The onion woman looked at our wagon.

"No."

Teren said, "We have permission."

"Not there."

"Committee said south edge."

"That's south lane."

She pointed with an onion.

"Cart comes through there."

Teren looked at the lane.

It looked exactly like empty ground.

A cart came through it.

Teren moved.

The woman nodded.

Local expertise.

A market official arrived wearing a green sash and carrying a stick with
chalk marks on it. He was called Harven. That was all I learned about
him because he was already late for something else.

He pointed.

"From that post to the trough."

Teren said, "That's narrow."

"Yes."

"Collection?"

"Fine."

"Bench?"

"No."

"Mounting board?"

Harven looked at the Sword board.

"What is it?"

"Performance."

"No nails."

"We don't nail it."

"Good."

"How long?"

"Judging clears by eleven. You have until the wool auction."

"When?"

"Depends on sheep."

Then he left.

Marek watched him go.

"I respect that."

"You don't understand any of it," Serra said.

"Exactly."

We waited for sheep.

That was most of the morning.

There were too many people to perform and nowhere to perform because the
south edge belonged to livestock judging until livestock judging stopped
belonging to it. Teren walked the boundary twice. Rinna talked to the
onion woman. Nessa kept costumes inside the wagon because the ground was
already filthy. Orin vanished and returned with a paper cone of roasted
seeds.

Nobody asked where he had been.

Serra stood at the sheep pen watching a judge put his hands on an animal
with deep professional seriousness.

"Can he tell?"

I asked.

"Tell what?"

"Which sheep."

She looked at me.

"Yes."

"Helpful."

"You asked."

A goat began chewing the corner of a cloth sign.

Its owner removed the sign from the goat.

The goat looked offended.

At ten thirty, Teren told me to watch the collection bowl.

"We aren't collecting."

"Watch it anyway."

The wooden bowl sat on the wagon step.

"What am I watching for?"

"Children."

That made sense five minutes later.

A girl picked it up.

I said, "That's ours."

She looked inside.

"It's empty."

"Still ours."

"Why?"

"Future money."

She considered this.

"Bad bowl."

Then she put it down.

I had successfully defended company assets from a seven-year-old critic.

At eleven, the sheep moved.

Not all at once.

Nothing at Kest happened all at once.

One pen emptied. Another filled. Men dragged temporary barriers away.
Harven reappeared, pointed at our strip, and said, "Now."

Teren said, "How long?"

Harven was already walking.

"Sheep."

Useful.

The company moved.

Fast.

Davin and Pell carried the mounting board.

Nessa brought only what costumes were needed.

Rinna put the collection bowl near the front edge and then moved it
because the onion woman said a cart wheel would crush it.

I carried a bundle of cloth to the wrong side.

Nessa said, "Other."

I moved it.

"Other."

"There are only two."

"Behind."

There were three.

Fine.

Teren chose the first set while we were setting it.

"King opening. Short. Sword. Orin closes."

"No Shopkeeper?" I asked.

"Not first."

Marek smiled.

"Headliner."

"No," Teren said.

"King."

"Noise."

Marek's smile weakened.

"Royal noise."

"Get dressed."

We had no curtain.

No backstage.

No raised platform.

The crowd did not know a performance was beginning because the crowd did
not care.

Marek solved this by climbing onto the low mounting block and shouting:

"Citizens!"

Three people looked.

Teren closed his eyes.

Marek continued.

"I have been robbed!"

More people looked.

A cattle buyer shouted, "Again?"

Marek pointed at him.

"Of respect!"

The cattle buyer laughed.

That helped.

Serra entered from beside the onion stall wearing the Princess coat.
Iven came in from the wagon lane. Orin appeared from somewhere behind a
cheese seller.

The scene began.

Not the play.

A piece of it.

Compressed.

Loud enough to compete with sheep.

I stood beside the mounting board waiting for Sword.

At first maybe fifteen people stopped.

Then twenty.

Then fewer because a line of cattle moved past and took half the view
with them.

Marek raised his voice.

A horse near the trough raised its head.

Teren made a small downward motion.

Marek ignored him for one line.

Then heard himself.

He lowered it.

The horse went back to being a horse.

By the time Serra came for the Sword, we had a real half-circle.

Not a hall audience.

Nobody sat.

People held baskets.

One man kept negotiating the price of a bridle while watching over the
seller's shoulder.

A woman bounced a baby.

Two boys had climbed the sheep fence.

The onion woman watched while still selling onions.

Serra stopped in front of me.

"I require a weapon."

I said, "You require judgment."

A man walking past laughed without stopping.

No copper.

Serra said, "I have judgment."

"Then why are you here?"

That got more.

Someone dropped a copper into the bowl.

I heard it.

That was different.

Applause could happen at the end.

Money made a sound immediately.

Serra took the Sword.

The woman who had bought onions said, "Is that the sword?"

The onion seller said, "Apparently."

I almost looked at her.

Did not.

Serra continued.

I answered.

The crowd grew.

Then a goat put its head through the fence behind me and bit the bottom
corner of the mounting cloth.

I felt the pull.

I looked down.

Wrong.

Serra was giving me a line.

I missed it.

She waited half a beat.

"Do you object?"

The goat pulled again.

"To theft," I said.

The crowd saw the goat.

That helped too much.

A boy shouted, "He's eating him!"

I said, "Slowly."

Laugh.

Copper.

Another copper.

The goat's owner arrived and dragged it backward.

The cloth came free with a wet corner.

Nessa made a sound from behind the wagon that I would hear later in my
sleep.

I should have checked the cloth before we started.

I had put the mounting board too close to the pen.

Nobody else had.

Me.

The scene continued.

The goat disappeared.

The laugh did not matter anymore.

I had a line.

I said it.

The first set lasted maybe eighteen minutes.

Orin closed with a short piece about a magistrate, a stolen chicken, and
three witnesses who all claimed to be the chicken's owner.

I was not in it.

That gave me time to watch the bowl.

People laughed.

Some paid.

Many did not.

One man watched almost the entire thing, laughed twice, then walked away
carrying a new shovel.

A woman who had arrived halfway through dropped two copper.

A boy dropped a smooth white stone.

I looked at him.

He said, "Lucky."

I left it.

Future money had diversified.

At the end, Teren said, "Thank you."

No request.

No announcement.

No promise of another set.

People broke apart because market people had somewhere else to be.

The half-circle dissolved in less than a minute.

Rinna picked up the bowl.

Teren looked at her.

She counted with one hand inside it.

Marek leaned.

Rinna turned away.

He leaned farther.

"Enough?" Teren asked.

Rinna counted again.

"Fourteen."

Marek said, "Fourteen!"

Rinna said, "You are not helping."

"Fourteen is helping."

Teren looked at the crowd moving through the market.

"Again?"

Rinna looked toward the wagon field.

"Oats already paid."

"Food."

"Yes."

"Dast."

"Maybe."

She handed him the bowl.

"Again."

That was it.

We had earned a second set.

Not much more.

Enough.

Teren said, "Twenty minutes."

Marek said, "Rest?"

"Advertise."

"How?"

"Be visible."

Marek put the crown back on.

"Finally, management recognizes my gifts."

Teren walked away.

The second set was different because the market had changed.

The wool auction had started near the center square.

Half the people who had watched us were gone.

Different people came south.

More traders.

Fewer children.

More people carrying money and less interest in giving it away.

Teren cut the King.

Marek objected.

"No King?"

"No."

"What opens?"

"Shopkeeper."

Marek stared at him.

"I have been replaced by debt."

I said, "Common."

He pointed at me.

"See? He's already working."

Shopkeeper in a livestock market was dangerous because everybody
understood debt.

Iven entered first carrying the old coat.

I followed with the battered table.

The onion woman said, "Him again."

I said, "Unfortunately."

That got three people.

Not enough.

Iven slapped a folded paper on the table.

"I have paid."

"You have presented paper."

"It says paid."

"I can write king."

Marek shouted from behind the wagon, "No."

The crowd laughed.

He was not in the scene.

Teren looked at him.

Marek raised both hands.

Iven said, "You heard the King."

"I heard unemployment."

A cattle seller barked a laugh.

Someone stopped.

Then someone else.

The set built slower.

The first crowd had formed around noise and the goat.

This one formed around people hearing other people laugh.

That took longer.

It also held differently.

A man in a leather apron argued with Greg from outside the scene.

"If he paid, he's paid."

I said, "You know him?"

"No."

"Then why are you risking your reputation?"

"I don't have one."

"Excellent. Stand for office."

The man laughed.

Iven said, "Can he forgive the debt?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Poor judgment."

The leather-apron man shouted, "I have that!"

"Still no."

Copper hit the bowl.

The second set became better than the first.

Then I ruined part of it.

Not onstage.

Between pieces.

Teren told me, "Take the bowl to Rinna."

I heard, "Take the bowl."

I picked it up.

Rinna was not where she had been.

I carried it behind the wagon looking for her.

A trader immediately shouted, "Where's the bowl?"

I stopped.

The crowd had people reaching for money.

I had removed the thing they were supposed to put it in.

Teren stared at me.

I put it back.

Not where it had been.

The onion woman pointed.

"Cart."

I moved it again.

A cart came through thirty seconds later.

Good.

Rinna found me.

"Why did you move it?"

"Teren."

Teren said, "I said take it to Rinna."

"You weren't there."

Rinna looked at me.

"So you brought it nowhere."

"Yes."

"Useful."

"No."

She took the bowl.

That was the entire review.

Sword came second.

The goat was gone.

Good.

The mounting cloth had a wet chewed corner.

Bad.

Nessa had folded it under so nobody could see.

Pell had moved the board farther from the pen without being told.

Better.

This Sword did not get the goat laugh.

It got something else.

A woman near the front recognized the piece from the first set.

When Serra said, "I require a weapon," the woman quietly said,
"Judgment."

Greg heard her.

So did Serra.

Serra changed.

"I require a weapon because judgment has failed me."

I said, "Again?"

The woman laughed.

That was enough.

We did not repeat the first exchange.

The scene moved.

A little later a man asked what the Sword cost.

Greg said, "Depends who is buying."

"Me."

"No."

"Why?"

"You asked."

That worked.

Not huge.

Enough.

The second set ended with Serra and Orin doing a short argument piece I
had never seen before.

Not the widow.

This one involved a landlord, a missing roof tile, and an accusation
that rain was a tenant responsibility.

I watched from the wagon.

Orin was better than me.

Not in a way I could fix by noticing.

He could let silence sit until the crowd leaned toward him.

Then Serra would cut it.

They had done this before.

Probably many times.

I watched.

A man beside me said, "You with them?"

"Apparently."

"Where next?"

I looked east.

"Maybe Dast."

"Fair?"

"Maybe."

He nodded.

"Good fair."

"You've been?"

"Every year."

That got my attention.

"Covered market?"

"Some."

"Performance?"

He looked at me.

"I sell harness."

"Right."

He pointed east.

"If you're going, don't take north ford. Bridge at Lerren's faster this
week."

"Why?"

"Ford deep."

Davin needed that.

I found him after the set.

He listened.

"Who?"

"Harness seller."

"Name?"

I looked back.

The man was gone.

"I don't know."

Davin stared.

"He sells harness."

"Good."

That was not praise.

The second collection was better.

Rinna counted twenty-one copper.

Thirty-five total.

Plus one smooth stone.

Marek held up the stone.

"Reserve."

"Put it down," Rinna said.

He did.

Thirty-five copper was not a hall gate.

It was not private silver.

It was more than six.

It was enough that nobody looked angry about staying.

Teren paid the performers who had worked both sets.

Greg got two copper.

Marek got one.

He stared at it.

"Why one?"

"You weren't in second."

"I advertised."

"You shouted once."

"Royal marketing."

"One."

Serra got two.

Iven two.

Orin two.

Other shares moved through Rinna's hands. Pell got something for work.
Nessa did. Davin did not ask in front of me.

The company kept the rest.

Rinna counted it again, then put it away.

"Food," she said.

Teren nodded.

"Dast?"

"Still possible."

The word had changed.

Not confirmed.

Possible.

A woman from the market committee found Teren while we were packing.

Not to pay us.

To tell us we had left the south edge clean.

Teren said, "Good."

She said, "If you're back next month, ask before market week."

"Why?"

"Cheaper rooms."

Then she left.

That was as close as Kest came to inviting us back.

The onion woman sold Rinna a sack of onions for road food.

Not free.

A little cheaper because the market was ending and she did not want to
carry them home.

Soft economy could apparently include not wanting to lift onions twice.

Pell tried to buy the lucky stone from the bowl.

"It isn't ours?" he asked.

"It was given."

"Then ours."

"Why would you buy it?"

"Accounting."

Rinna gave him the stone.

He put it in his pocket.

"Profit."

Nobody stopped him.

By late afternoon, the livestock market had started taking itself apart.

Pens emptied.

Animals left.

Temporary awnings folded.

The assembly room was filling for supper we were not attending.

Noll stood in the doorway directing tables.

He saw Teren.

"Eight."

Teren said, "No."

Noll nodded.

Apparently their negotiation had survived the day.

We did not stay another night.

That mattered.

Kest had paid enough to justify the morning.

Not enough to justify buying another Kest evening.

Rinna bought bread and cheap dried meat.

Davin watered the horses.

We loaded.

The long box went last.

Upright.

Dry.

Davin tied it.

Nobody cared.

Teren stood beside Rinna with the folded paper from Lady Merra's dinner
guest.

"Dast," he said.

"Six days became?"

"Four from here if road holds."

Davin said, "Three and half."

Rinna looked at him.

"With what road?"

"Bridge."

I said, "Lerren's."

Davin looked at me.

I explained the harness seller.

He asked the name.

"I still don't know."

"Ford?"

"Deep."

Davin nodded once.

Apparently anonymous harness information had passed inspection.

Rinna said, "Fair still no guarantee."

Teren said, "No."

"Covered market?"

"Possible."

"Contact?"

"Card says ask for Bellan."

"Answer?"

"No."

Marek climbed onto the wagon.

"We try."

Rinna looked at him.

"Don't."

He smiled.

Teren looked east.

Then at the money Rinna had put away.

Then at the road.

"Dast."

That was the decision.

Not because Kest had been good.

Because Kest had been enough.

We rolled out while the market was still disappearing behind us.

I sat with my crutches tied beside the wagon and two new copper in my
purse.

The brass clasp stayed in my inner pocket.

Carrow was west.

Lyssa was west.

Hessa was west.

Dast was east.

I knew all of that.

The road bent.

Kest vanished behind cattle pens and evening dust.

Nobody asked when I was going home.

Neither did I.
