PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 170

THE CHASER

Davin woke us before the geese.

That was how early it was.

South Three had not gone quiet so much as changed shifts. Cooking fires
from the night were gray holes in the ground. A man three spaces over
was already loading folded cages onto a wagon. Someone farther down the
field coughed for what felt like several minutes without stopping. The
fair itself made a low sound beyond the dark, boards moving, animals
objecting, people beginning again.

"Up," Davin said.

Marek opened one eye.

"Why?"

"Road."

"Still there?"

Davin walked away.

I sat up under the wagon and hit my head.

My bag had spread.

Teren had been right.

Not dramatically. One shirt hung over a wheel because I had washed the
collar. My spare stocking was under the wagon tongue. The little packet
with the clasp was not spread anywhere. That was inside my coat, wrapped
and safe.

I gathered everything else while Pell crawled out from under the second
wagon with a blanket around his shoulders.

"Fish," he said.

"What?"

"Have you seen it?"

"No."

He looked under my wagon.

"If it bites me, I'm leaving it in Dast."

"It likes you."

"Then it can miss me."

The fish was in the prop basket.

Pell claimed he had checked there first.

Nobody believed him.

Rinna handed out bread while we packed. There was no ceremony to
breakfast. Bread, water, the last of a hard cheese. Nessa ate standing
while folding cloth. Serra had her hair tied back and was helping Rinna
count food without being asked. Orin appeared from somewhere with his
coat buttoned wrong, fixed it, and started carrying stools.

I watched him for a moment.

He noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

Davin had the first wagon half loaded before the sky changed color.

The long box went where it always went. Upright. Tied. Dry. Davin
checked the rope, pulled once, and moved on.

A field worker came through carrying a board of wooden tokens.

"South Three?"

Rinna held up ours.

"S3-41."

"Leaving?"

"Yes."

"Token."

She gave it to him.

He dropped it into a sack without looking.

That was Dast's farewell.

We rolled out before sunrise.

For the first hundred yards, I thought Davin had been excessively
cautious.

Then three grain wagons entered the field behind us at once.

By the time we reached the northwestern road, carts were already queuing
at the turn.

Marek looked back.

"We escaped."

"We left," Serra said.

"Less dramatic."

"Yes."

The road northwestern out of Dast climbed.

Not sharply. It simply refused to give back any height once it took it.
The fair fields fell behind us in long brown-green strips, then vanished
behind hedges and low stone walls. The road itself was broad enough for
wagons to pass without negotiation most of the time. Old stone markers
stood at intervals, worn on the corners from weather and perhaps people
sitting on them.

For the first hour, we were not alone.

A cattle buyer passed us riding a gray horse and leading another. Two
wagons loaded with empty barrels went north. A family with three
children and a rolled canvas awning had been camped somewhere near Dast
and now traveled ahead of us until they turned west.

The musicians passed going the other direction.

Not the good singers from the west platform. The fiddle-and-drum pair
from the road and fair.

The drummer lifted two fingers.

Marek called, "Cowards!"

The fiddler called back, "Paid!"

Marek considered this.

"Professionals."

Then they were gone.

Teren had Orin's paper.

I had seen it the night before. I had not read it.

After the road thinned, Rinna asked for it.

Teren handed it across the wagon.

She read while walking beside the wheel.

"Founder Hall," she said.

Orin nodded.

"Who's Harel Venn?"

"Organizer."

"You know him?"

"No."

Rinna looked at Orin.

"You know the man who sent this."

"Yes."

"Name?"

"Corst."

"Trade?"

"Food."

Marek said, "That's not a trade."

Orin looked at him.

"Everything you've eaten came through somebody."

"Then I retract my objection."

Rinna tapped the paper.

"Corst says Venn wanted players for Founder's supper. Three silver
company. Third night after Dast second market day."

"Tonight is first," Teren said.

"Tomorrow second. Supper the next."

"If the date is right."

"Why wouldn't it be?"

Orin shrugged.

"Corst."

That explained nothing to me.

Apparently it explained something to Rinna.

She folded the paper.

"Food?"

"Not written."

"Lodging?"

"Not written."

"Length?"

"Not written."

"Useful man."

"He knew the price."

"That's why we're going."

Teren took the paper back.

Rinna said, "If it's gone, we lose a day."

"If it's there, three silver."

"Yes."

That was the discussion.

We chased it.

Around midmorning the road divided at a stone post.

One branch continued northwest. The other went almost straight north
between orchard walls.

The sign had once been painted.

It was no longer interested in helping.

Teren got down.

"Rellin?"

Nobody answered.

A cart came from the orchard road carrying empty fruit crates.

Teren raised a hand.

"Rellin?"

The driver pointed northwest.

"Old road."

"How far?"

"Depends."

I had learned to hate that answer.

"On?"

"Bridge."

Of course.

Teren asked, "What's wrong with the bridge?"

"Nothing."

"Then why?"

"Queue."

He drove on.

Marek said, "I appreciate his commitment to precision."

Davin looked northwest.

"Bridge."

"Yes," Teren said.

Davin climbed back onto the wagon.

We went toward the bridge.

The queue began before we could see water.

Six wagons.

Then nine.

Then a cart with two goats tied behind it joined after us and became our
problem because one goat kept trying to eat the rear wagon rope.

Pell moved the rope.

The goat moved.

Pell moved the rope again.

The goat moved again.

"Persistent," I said.

"Help."

"How?"

"Be less edible."

The bridge appeared around a bend.

It was stone, narrow, and entirely intact.

The problem was a crew replacing the timber rail on one side. They
allowed traffic in one direction at a time while three men carried long
pieces across the roadway.

No danger.

No collapse.

Just work.

A man with a white rag tied around his arm walked the line counting
wagons.

"Two at a time," he said. "Wait for the board."

"What board?" Marek asked.

The man pointed to a square painted board beside the bridge.

One side was white.

One side was black.

"White, go. Black, wait."

Marek nodded.

"Elegant."

"Black," the man said.

"We're waiting."

"Then you're learning."

Rinna sent me to buy food.

There was a woman beside the queue with a basket.

Of course there was.

Boiled eggs, small loaves, onions, dried pears.

"How much for six eggs?"

"Three copper."

"Four."

"Three."

"I meant four eggs."

"Two copper."

"Six is better."

"For you."

I bought six.

Rinna had given me three copper.

I returned with six eggs and no change.

She looked at them.

"Three?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Marek appeared.

"Egg."

"No."

"I will pay."

That changed things.

"One copper."

He stared at me.

"The woman charged three for six."

"Market conditions."

"You are becoming evil."

"Professional."

He took one anyway.

I told Rinna.

She gave me the look.

I gave Marek the egg.

He ate it before she could decide whether I had committed theft.

The bridge queue took almost an hour.

Davin used the time to check a wheel.

I watched the lane while he did it.

This time I knew what he meant when he said, "Rear."

A wagon came too close.

I moved our water bucket.

The driver nodded once.

That was all.

When our board turned white, Davin was ready.

We crossed.

The river was wider than I expected, brown-green and slow beneath the
arches. Men on a barge below were unloading cut stone. One shouted up at
the bridge crew. The bridge crew shouted back.

I could not hear the words.

They seemed satisfied with them.

The road after the river changed.

More orchards.

Apple, mostly, though I only knew that because fruit still hung on some
of the higher branches. Low stone walls divided fields. Farmhouses sat
farther back from the road than the villages east of Carrow. Press sheds
appeared beside some yards, broad-roofed buildings with dark doors and
stacked barrels outside.

By afternoon the fair traffic had almost disappeared.

Rellin traffic replaced it.

Barrels going northwestern.

Empty carts returning.

A wagon carrying long boards.

Two men walking with pruning hooks over their shoulders.

People had destinations.

We were one more wagon among them.

At a roadside well, Teren asked a woman filling two clay jugs how far to
Rellin.

"Tomorrow."

"How much tomorrow?"

She looked at the wagons.

"For you? Noon."

Davin said, "Morning."

The woman looked at him.

"If you don't stop."

Davin nodded.

Teren said, "We stop."

Davin nodded again.

That was settled.

We could have pushed.

Three silver made pushing tempting.

The horses made the decision instead.

By late afternoon Davin found a field edge where travelers were allowed
to stop beside an old toll house that no longer collected tolls. The
roof of the house had fallen in on one side. The yard remained flat. A
stone trough had water. A painted notice said travelers could use the
ground but not the building.

Marek read it.

"Why?"

A man sitting against the intact wall said, "Falls on you."

"Ah."

Local expertise.

We stayed outside.

Rinna paid one copper to a farmer's boy for a bundle of hay that Davin
inspected before accepting. Oats came from our own sack.

Dinner was bread, onion, the remaining eggs, and dried pear.

I washed my shirt properly at the trough.

Nessa caught me hanging it over a wagon rope.

"Not there."

"Why?"

"Costume."

There was nothing on the rope.

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

I moved my shirt.

Five minutes later she hung two pieces of costume there.

"That was theft."

"Planning."

I found another rope.

My bag had been packed for a trip.

I had not understood how long a trip was.

The shirt was the first problem.

The second was stockings.

The third was that I had begun keeping bits of food in pockets that had
not been designed for food.

The fourth was that I no longer knew which town had put the first stain
on my spare trousers.

I sat beside the wagon and repaired a loose seam in my bag badly.

Serra sat across from me repairing something else well.

After a while she said, "Give me that."

"I'm doing it."

"I can see."

I handed it over.

She pulled three of my stitches out.

"Those were structural."

"They were criminal."

She restitched the seam.

I watched.

"Where'd the thread go?"

"What thread?"

"The red one."

She looked at her finger.

There was no red thread.

"Oh."

That was the answer.

I waited.

She kept sewing.

I let it go.

Marek was playing cards with the man from the ruined toll house.

Not for money.

I checked.

He saw me check.

"Character assassination."

"Capital preservation."

"Exactly."

Orin was asleep.

Teren and Rinna were talking quietly beside the first wagon.

I heard "Rellin," "three silver," and "if."

Davin had gone to look at the horses again.

Pell was making the fish bite a stick.

Nessa told him to stop.

He stopped until she turned around.

The road had become ordinary enough that I could imagine waking beside
it forever.

That was not a good thought.

It also was not entirely a bad one.

I touched the inside of my coat.

The clasp was there.

The letter was gone west.

I wondered where it was.

Then Marek accused the toll-house man of inventing a card rule and I
forgot to wonder.

We left before full light the next morning.

Davin had been right.

Without stopping, Rellin was morning.

The road rose one last time through orchards and then leveled along a
ridge.

We saw the town before we reached it.

Rellin was stone.

Not all of it, but enough that the first impression stayed.

A low old wall enclosed the center, not defensive anymore. Houses had
grown through and beyond it. Pale stone buildings clustered around a
square with a square tower at one end. Newer timber shops leaned against
older walls. Beyond the town, orchards ran down the slope in rows.

Barrels were everywhere.

Outside shops.

On carts.

Stacked beneath sheds.

A wagon passed us carrying apples high enough that I could smell them.

Marek inhaled.

"Founder's supper."

"Those are apples," I said.

"Foundation of supper."

A painted board at the road read RELLIN.

Below it, another smaller board read:

FOUNDING WEEK.

Not Founder's supper.

Week.

Teren looked at it.

"Good?"

Rinna said, "More expensive."

Fair.

We entered through an opening where the old wall had been cut wide
enough for carts.

Nobody charged us.

Nobody redirected us.

That made me suspicious after Dast.

The streets were narrow in the old center. Davin stopped before we got
properly trapped.

"No."

Teren looked ahead.

"Hall?"

"Walk."

The wagons went to a yard outside the wall near a cooper's shed after
Rinna negotiated something I did not hear.

Teren took Orin's paper.

Then he looked at me.

"Come."

"Why?"

"You can carry things."

"What things?"

"Yourself."

We went into Rellin.

Orin came too.

That surprised me.

"Do you know where Founder Hall is?" Teren asked him.

"No."

"Useful."

"Corst said square."

We found the square.

It was not large.

The tower I had seen from the ridge belonged to an old stone civic
building with three arched doors. A fountain occupied the center. People
were hanging green-and-gold cloth from windows. Two boys carried a
wooden sign between them that said FOUNDING SUPPER and argued about
which end was up.

Founder Hall was not the civic building.

It was behind it.

Of course.

A woman arranging small potted trees along a passage told us.

"Players?" she asked.

Teren stopped.

"Maybe."

"Harel's been swearing about players."

That sounded promising.

"Where's Harel?"

"Inside."

That sounded more promising.

We found Harel Venn standing in a hall full of tables.

He was not happy to see us.

He was a heavy man in a brown waistcoat with a strip of green cloth
pinned crookedly at the shoulder. He held a list in one hand and a spoon
in the other.

"You."

Teren held out the paper.

"East Market company."

Harel looked at it.

Then at Orin.

Then at me.

Then back at Teren.

"You're late."

Teren said, "Message was late."

"I sent it four days ago."

"We got it two days ago."

"Corst."

Orin said, "Yes."

Harel closed his eyes.

I began to understand Corst.

"The job?" Teren asked.

Harel opened his eyes.

"Half gone."

Rinna would have hated that answer.

Teren said, "Which half?"

"I hired musicians yesterday."

"Then you don't need us."

"I need the supper piece."

"Three silver?"

"No."

There it was.

Teren did not move.

Harel looked at his list.

"Musicians took one silver eight. I have one silver two left in
entertainment."

I did not know exactly how he was dividing silver in his head.

I knew it was less than three.

Teren said, "For what?"

"Twenty-five minutes after the second course. Not during food. Before
speeches."

"How many people?"

"Seventy. Maybe eighty."

"Room?"

Harel pointed around us.

Founder Hall was long, stone-walled, timber-roofed, with tables arranged
in two rows and a clear strip at one end. No stage. A broad fireplace.
Two side doors. A gallery above one wall that looked decorative until
three kitchen workers carried baskets through it.

"Food?" Teren asked.

"For performers working the supper, yes. Kitchen meal after."

"Lodging?"

"No."

"Horses?"

"No."

"One silver two for twenty-five minutes."

"Yes."

Teren looked at Orin.

Orin looked at the spoon.

Teren looked at me.

I said, "I'm not management."

Harel said, "Good."

Teren looked back at Harel.

"Two silver."

"I don't have two."

"Then one silver five."

"I have one silver two."

"Then you have musicians."

"I also have seventy people expecting something after the second
course."

"One silver four."

Harel stared at him.

"I have one silver two."

Teren said nothing.

Harel said nothing.

A kitchen worker crossed behind him carrying a tray of onions.

Another person entered through the side door and asked Harel where the
green tablecloths were.

Harel said, "Gallery."

"They're not."

"Then cellar."

"Which cellar?"

"Dry."

"There are two."

Harel closed his eyes again.

The person waited.

Harel pointed left.

They left.

He looked at Teren.

"One silver two. Food. Twenty minutes."

"You said twenty-five."

"I changed my mind."

Teren said, "Twenty."

"Yes."

"Payment before."

Harel's face changed.

"Half before."

"All before."

"Half."

Teren held out the old message.

"Three silver."

Harel looked at it.

"Half before. Half when you finish. Food for twelve."

Teren said, "Fourteen."

"We're feeding seventy."

"Then fourteen won't kill you."

Harel looked at the spoon.

"Fourteen."

Teren folded the paper.

"Done."

That was it.

We had chased three silver for a day and a half.

We found one silver two.

And lunch after dinner.

Outside Founder Hall, Teren handed Orin the old message.

"Find Corst next time."

Orin folded it.

"Maybe."

I laughed.

Teren looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You sound pleased."

"We have work."

"For less."

"Still work."

He considered me.

"Go get Rinna."

"Where?"

"Wagons."

"I know where the wagons are."

"Good."

I started back through the passage.

Behind me, Founder Hall kept moving. Tables. Cloth. Kitchen people.
Harel swearing about a cellar. Someone testing the bell that would
apparently divide courses.

We had arrived.

The job had survived.

Not intact.

That seemed normal too.
