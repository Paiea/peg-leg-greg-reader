CHAPTER 172

THE CUSTOMER

The cooper was heating a barrel hoop when I woke.

Not in a forge.

The iron ring lay on three bricks in the yard. He touched a blackened
copper tab to it, said something I could not hear, and a dull red line
traveled around the hoop.

He watched it.

When the color reached the other side, he picked the hoop up with tongs
and carried it into the shed.

Nobody applauded.

Davin was already awake.

"Magic," I said.

"Hoop," he said.

"Both."

He looked at me.

"Move."

I moved.

The company was packing around me.

Rellin had gone gray with early morning. The old wall held the last of
the night in its cracks. Beyond it, chimney smoke had started rising. A
cart of apples rattled through the gate. Somewhere farther down the
road, a bell rang twice and then stopped.

Pell was rolling the mounting cloth.

"Do we own magic?" I asked.

He looked up.

"What?"

"For shows."

"Yes."

Nessa, kneeling beside the costume chest, said, "No."

Pell frowned.

"We do."

"We own prepared effects."

"That's magic."

"Not the way he means."

"I don't know how he means."

Neither did I.

I said, "The throw clasp."

Nessa closed the chest.

"What about it?"

"Do we have one?"

"No."

"Anything like it?"

"Not like it."

Pell said, "We have the blue lamp piece."

Nessa looked at him.

"What?"

"The blue lamp piece."

"That's not a throw."

"I didn't say it was."

"What does it do?" I asked.

Pell stood.

"Blue."

I waited.

"That's it?"

"It makes a lamp blue."

Nessa said, "It makes one lamp look colder from the house."

"Magic?"

"Prepared glass and a little cue."

"Who cues it?"

"Usually Davin."

From beside the wagon, Davin said, "No."

Nessa raised her voice.

"Who cued it last?"

"Rinna."

Rinna, counting food at the other wagon, said, "Once."

Pell pointed at me.

"See?"

I had seen the blue lamp.

I remembered it.

I had thought it was blue glass.

Apparently it was blue glass.

And magic.

"How much mana?"

Nessa stood.

"Very little."

"How little?"

"Greg."

"What?"

"We are leaving."

That was fair.

I found Teren near the horses.

"When?"

"Forty minutes."

"I want twenty."

"For what?"

"Instrument shop."

He looked at me.

"Why?"

"Throw clasp."

That got a different look.

Not surprise.

Recognition.

"You're buying one?"

"I don't know."

"Then don't."

"Useful."

"They're expensive."

"How expensive?"

"That's what shops are for."

I waited.

He sighed.

"Twenty minutes."

"Thirty?"

"Twenty."

"I have crutches."

"You had them when you asked."

Rinna said, "Take Pell."

Pell looked delighted.

Nessa said, "No."

Rinna looked at her.

Nessa looked at Pell.

"Bring him back."

"I was planning to."

"I wasn't talking to you."

Pell grinned.

We went.

The blue shutters were easy to find once we reached the square.

The shop was not.

The shutters belonged to two narrow windows over a doorway so dark I
thought the place was closed.

Pell walked in.

A little brass bell rang.

Then rang again from somewhere behind us.

I stopped.

Pell did not.

Inside smelled like wood, varnish, metal, and old dust.

Instruments hung from the walls. Some complete. Some not. A fiddle body
without a neck. Three drums stacked under a bench. Reeds in narrow
boxes. Strings looped over pegs. Brass fittings filled shallow trays.

A man sat at a workbench with his back to us.

"Varo?"

"No."

He did not turn.

"Do you know Varo?"

"No."

Pell looked at me.

The man said, "She's upstairs."

"Ah."

He turned then.

He was younger than I expected. Maybe thirty. Maybe younger. There was
silver dust on one sleeve.

"What?"

"Throw clasp."

"Buy?"

"Look."

"No."

Pell laughed.

I said, "Maybe buy."

"Then look."

He pointed to a glass-fronted cabinet.

There were six silver clips inside.

They did not look magical.

That annoyed me.

One was plain. One had two tiny loops worked into the side. One was
blackened at the tips. Two were mounted on little wooden blocks. The
last was larger and shaped to fit around something round.

"Which is throw?"

"Four."

"Which four?"

"The four."

Pell leaned closer.

"That's a reed seat."

The man nodded.

"Good."

I had no idea which one Pell meant.

A woman came down the stairs carrying a bundle of thin wooden strips.

"Why is an actor touching my cabinet?"

I was not touching it.

I looked at my hands anyway.

The younger man said, "He's looking."

The woman put the strips on the bench.

"Actors look with fingers."

"I'm not."

"Yet."

"Varo?"

"Unfortunately."

She looked at Pell.

"You're too young to buy anything."

"I'm with him."

"That makes it worse."

I liked her.

I said, "I saw a throw clasp last night. Lap strings. Founder Hall."

"Yessa's."

"I didn't get her name."

"You got mine."

"Yes."

"What do you want it to do?"

That was exactly the wrong question.

"Move sound."

Varo stared at me.

Pell said, "He means throw."

"I know what he means. He doesn't."

I said, "Make the sound come from somewhere else."

"How far?"

"I don't know."

"Which direction?"

"I don't know."

"What room?"

"I don't know."

"What instrument?"

"I don't have one."

Varo looked at Pell.

"Is he always like this?"

Pell said, "More now."

"Good. Means he's learning."

I almost smiled.

Then Varo added, "Slowly."

She opened the cabinet.

"Yessa's clasp is built for her board. You put it on a fiddle, it does
badly. You put it on a voice, it does nothing. You put it in a street
and ask it to throw through six carts, you wasted money."

She took out the plainest clip.

"Prepared channel. Maker sets the bias. Player gives it a cue. Tiny draw
if the preparation is good."

"What if the player can't draw?"

"Then buy a charged one."

I looked at her.

"Charged?"

"Costs more. Lasts less."

"How much?"

"For you?"

"Why for me?"

"You ask expensive questions."

Pell covered his mouth.

Varo said, "Basic fitted throw, one silver six. Better one, more.
Charged, depends. Custom, don't bother."

One silver six.

I stopped wanting to touch it.

"That's more than I have."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"You won't buy the wrong one."

She put it back.

I looked at the cabinet again.

"What is it changing?"

"Sound."

"Yes, but the sound itself or where people think it is?"

Varo paused.

Not long.

Long enough.

"Where did you get that?"

"Yessa said those aren't always different."

"Yessa says things when she doesn't want to explain."

"Do you?"

"Constantly."

Pell was looking at a small brass disk on the counter.

Varo slapped his hand away without looking.

"That's hot."

"It isn't."

"It was yesterday."

Pell withdrew his hand.

I said, "So?"

Varo leaned against the bench.

"A throw doesn't make a second sound. It doesn't pick up a note and
carry it across a room like a bucket. It changes the path enough that
your ear lies to you."

"My ear?"

"Your head. Your ear is trying."

"That's illusion."

"Sometimes."

"What other time is it?"

"Acoustics."

That was not satisfying.

Varo did not care.

She picked up one of the wooden strips and checked its edge against the
light.

"You actors always want magic to be the interesting part."

Pell said, "I'm crew."

"You people are worse."

I said, "How much is the magic?"

"In what?"

"The effect."

Varo looked at me again.

"Wrong question."

"Why?"

"Because if the room is wrong, more magic gives you expensive wrong."

That sounded like Hessa.

Not the words.

The refusal.

I frowned.

Varo frowned back.

"See? Now we're both unhappy."

Pell said, "Show him the bell."

"No."

"Bell?"

"No."

The brass bell over the door rang.

Then the sound came from behind me again.

I turned.

Varo had not moved.

The younger man was smiling.

"Bell," Pell said.

I looked up.

The bell was ordinary brass.

A tiny black strip had been fixed to the inside of the door frame.

"Throw?"

"Cheap one," Varo said.

"How cheap?"

"Not for sale."

"Why?"

"It lies badly."

"It worked."

"You turned around."

"Exactly."

"You're standing three feet from it and weren't expecting it. Put twenty
people in here twice and half of them point at the door."

That was interesting.

"Shortcut," Pell said.

Varo nodded.

"The room does most of it."

I looked at the narrow shop.

Hard walls. Low ceiling. Shelves. Hanging instruments.

"You put the false sound where the room already makes sense."

Varo said, "Sometimes."

"So you need less."

"Sometimes."

"Could you do that with a voice?"

"Yes."

"An actor?"

"Yes."

"Onstage?"

"Yes."

"Offstage?"

"Obviously."

Pell said, "We do."

I looked at him.

"What?"

"We do."

"When?"

"Ghost piece."

"I haven't seen the ghost piece."

"You weren't in it."

"Who throws?"

"Nobody."

I waited.

Pell grinned.

"Tube."

Varo laughed.

I said, "Tube."

"Under the floor if we have one. Behind cloth if we don't. Little metal
mouth. Actor speaks into the other end."

"That's not magic."

"Correct."

"Then why did you say we do?"

"Because it sounds like throw."

Varo pointed at him.

"Keep that one."

Pell looked pleased.

I was annoyed again.

Not at him.

At the answer.

A tube could do what I had spent the last ten minutes asking magic to
do.

Not everywhere.

Not as cleanly.

But enough, in the right place.

Varo picked up another fitting.

"Most stage work is cheating."

Pell said, "Craft."

"Same thing if you're paid."

I said, "What about a veil?"

Varo's expression changed.

"Why?"

"Nessa said throw isn't veil."

"Nessa's right."

"What does veil do?"

"Ask Nessa."

"You're the shop."

"I'm the instrument shop."

That was fair too.

She held up the fitting.

"This keeps a reed from splitting when somebody cues badly. You want
stage veils, find a stage maker."

"Where?"

"Not in Rellin."

"Carrow?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"I don't live there."

I thought of East Market Hall.

Of ropes.

Paint.

Lamps.

Things I had stopped seeing because I knew where they were stored.

"What do theatres use?"

"Whatever works."

"Magic."

"Sometimes."

"Mechanisms."

"Usually."

"Both."

"Best when nobody can tell."

That stayed.

Not because it was profound.

Because she said it while scraping varnish from a brass fitting with her
thumbnail.

I looked at the cabinet.

One silver six.

No.

"Do you have anything inert?"

Varo said, "Everything is inert if you don't use it."

"I mean something prepared but not active."

"Why?"

"I can't draw unsupervised."

The shop changed.

Only slightly.

The younger man stopped smiling.

Varo put the fitting down.

"Guild?"

"Restricted."

"Then don't buy a clasp."

"I wasn't going to activate it."

"People who say that in shops activate things."

"I don't."

"Good."

She opened a drawer and took out a narrow piece of silver-colored metal.

Not a clasp.

A failed clasp, maybe. Flat on one end, bent on the other.

"This was cut wrong."

"What does it do?"

"Nothing."

"Prepared?"

"Started."

"Meaning?"

"Channel was marked. Bias wasn't set. Scrap."

"How much?"

"One copper."

Pell said, "That's garbage."

Varo said, "Yes."

I bought it.

Pell stared at me.

"She said garbage."

"I heard."

Varo took my copper.

"No activation."

"It can't activate."

"No filing."

"Why?"

"You'll ruin the mark."

"It's garbage."

"It's educational garbage."

Pell made a sound.

I put the piece into my pocket.

Not with the clasp for Lyssa.

Different pocket.

"Can Hessa look at it?"

Varo shrugged.

"I don't know Hessa."

"She's Guild."

"Then she can frown at it professionally."

That sounded possible.

We left.

Outside, Pell said, "You paid for broken metal."

"Yes."

"I could have found you broken metal."

"Prepared broken metal."

"Still broken."

"Educational."

"Garbage."

"You're young."

"I'm right."

He was.

We reached the wagons late.

Not very late.

Late enough.

Teren looked at me.

"Twenty minutes."

"Shop was educational."

"Did you buy garbage?"

Pell said, "Yes."

Teren closed his eyes.

Nessa said, "What?"

I showed her the scrap.

She took it.

Turned it over.

Looked at the faint marks.

"Throw blank."

"Failed."

"Obviously."

"Varo said the channel was marked but the bias wasn't set."

Nessa handed it back.

"Then it's useless."

"For doing it."

"Yes."

"What about looking?"

She frowned.

Not like Hessa.

More efficiently.

Then she reached into the prop chest.

I expected blue glass.

Instead she pulled out a small wooden mouthpiece attached to six feet of
narrow leather tube.

"Ghost."

Pell looked triumphant.

Nessa said, "This is what you learn first."

I held it.

"Tube."

"Tube."

"No magic."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because it works."

She took it back.

Then she lifted a little square of smoky glass wrapped in cloth.

"Blue lamp."

The glass had a thin pale line worked around one edge.

"Prepared?"

"Yes."

"Who made it?"

"No idea."

"How does it work?"

"Rinna touches the cue mark after the lamp is lit."

"Mana?"

"Small."

"Could I?"

"No."

That answer came quickly.

I nodded.

Nessa wrapped it again.

"But you can hang it."

"What?"

"You can learn where it goes."

That was different.

She pointed toward the rear wagon.

"After we stop."

Teren said, "We are stopping now."

Nessa looked at him.

"Later."

"Good."

We left Rellin.

The southwest road dropped out of orchard country in long shallow turns.

For the first mile, barrels passed us going the other way.

Then fewer.

Then none.

The land opened.

Low stone walls continued between fields, but the orchards thinned into
pasture and grain.

At a roadside shrine, a little glass bead burned with steady amber light
beneath a stone roof.

No candle.

No smoke.

Marek did not look at it.

Serra did not look at it.

I did.

Then I nearly stepped into a rut.

Davin said, "Road."

I watched the road.

At midday we stopped beside a stream.

Rinna gave us bread and the last of the supper cheese.

Marek complained there was no hot food.

Nobody answered.

He ate.

Serra sat twenty yards away under a tree reading something.

Orin had gone upstream.

Pell was throwing pebbles into the water and trying to hit the same
patch twice.

Nessa sat beside the prop chest.

I sat near her.

She looked at me.

"No."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"Yes."

She sighed.

"Get the lamp."

I did.

The blue lamp piece was smaller in daylight.

Less impressive.

Just smoky glass with a pale worked line around it.

Nessa made me hold it by the cloth.

"Why?"

"Oil."

"From my hand?"

"Yes."

"Does that matter?"

"Eventually."

She showed me the hook.

Not the magic.

The hook.

How the glass sat in front of the lamp without touching the hot chimney.
How the angle mattered. How it looked from the front. How it looked from
the side.

"If they see the edge, it's bad," she said.

"Because they see the magic?"

"Because they see glass."

"Oh."

"If the cue works perfectly and they see a square of glass hanging in
front of the lamp, you've made blue glass."

"Which it is."

"Exactly."

She had me hang it from a wagon lantern that was not lit.

I did it wrong.

She moved it half an inch.

The edge disappeared behind the lamp frame.

"Again."

I did it again.

Wrong differently.

Pell came over.

"He's bad."

"Go away."

He stayed.

Third time, I got it.

Nessa nodded.

"Fine."

"Now cue?"

"No."

"I know."

"Good."

She took the glass off.

I said, "How much of the effect is the cue?"

"Small."

"How much is the glass?"

"Some."

"How much is where you hang it?"

"Enough."

"How much is the audience not looking at the lamp?"

Nessa looked at me.

Pell stopped throwing stones.

"More," she said.

That was all.

She wrapped the glass.

I took the failed throw blank out of my pocket.

Nessa looked at it.

"Hessa will hate that."

"Why?"

"Because you bought it."

"She might not."

"She'll frown."

"Probably."

"What are you asking her?"

I looked at the scrap.

I had been asking myself that since the shop.

Not how to make a throw.

Hessa did not know stagecraft. Or if she did, she had never shown any
interest in teaching me.

That wasn't her job.

The troupe knew how to make an audience look away from a lamp.

Varo knew how to make an ear lie.

Hessa knew what happened when I drew mana and failed to make anything
outside myself do what I wanted.

Those were not the same problem.

Maybe they touched.

"I don't know yet."

Nessa took the lamp back.

"Good."

We packed.

By afternoon, clouds had gathered behind us.

Davin noticed first.

Of course.

We kept moving.

Teren rode on the front wagon for a while, then walked.

At a fork, he stopped to read a weather-darkened board.

One road went west.

One southwest.

"Which?" Marek asked.

Teren looked at Rinna.

Rinna looked at Davin.

Davin looked at the sky.

"Southwest."

"Work?" I asked.

Teren said, "Town tomorrow."

"Show?"

"Maybe."

Marek groaned.

I said nothing.

The southwest road still bent west.

Carrow was no longer behind everything.

It was somewhere ahead of some of it.

I touched my coat.

The brass clasp for Lyssa was still there.

In my other pocket, the failed throw blank clicked against two copper.

I moved it so it would not scratch them.

We walked.

At the next rise, I could see the road for a long way.

Fields.

A stand of dark trees.

Two wagons ahead of us.

A little village farther off, its lamps beginning to come on one by one.

One lamp brightened green before settling to yellow.

Nobody stopped.

Neither did we.

I had spent one copper on something that could not do magic.

I had learned how to hang blue glass.

Somehow I had more questions than before.

That felt promising.

Also expensive.
