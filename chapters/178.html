PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 178

THE ENSEMBLE PLAYER

Lyssa came home with somebody else's shoes.

Not wearing them.

Carrying them by the laces.

I was still at the table with Hessa's question facedown when the door
opened and Lyssa came in sideways, one shoulder against the frame, a
paper parcel under one arm and a pair of brown shoes dangling from two
fingers.

She stopped when she saw me.

"You live here now?"

"Apparently."

"Terrible."

"Whose shoes?"

"Jessa's."

"Why?"

"Because she went home barefoot."

"Why?"

Lyssa put the shoes on the table.

"Because her feet hurt."

"That doesn't answer why you have them."

"She left them at Marra's."

"That still doesn't."

"I am returning them tomorrow."

"Now we have a story."

She looked at the paper in front of me.

"What is that?"

"Nothing."

"Guild nothing?"

"Yes."

She reached for it.

I put my hand on top.

"No."

"Then definitely Guild nothing."

"It's a question."

"To who?"

"Hessa."

"The woman who makes you not do magic?"

"She lets me do magic."

"While making you not do magic."

"That's unfair."

"Did anything move?"

"That was yesterday."

Lyssa nodded.

"So no."

"Correct."

She put the parcel beside Jessa's shoes and started unpinning her hair.
The brass leaves were not in it tonight. I noticed that and then felt
stupid for noticing.

"Where were you?"

"Marra's. Then Jessa's sister's. Then the dyer."

"At night?"

"The dyer closes when the work is done."

"What work?"

"Blue."

"That's not work."

"It was six lengths of blue."

"More work."

She opened the parcel.

Inside was cheese.

I respected the parcel more.

"Did you buy that?"

"No."

"Steal it?"

"Payment."

"For blue?"

"For carrying the blue because Jessa took her shoes off."

I looked at the shoes.

"This city has complicated trade."

"You came home with broken magic."

"Educational garbage."

"Where is it?"

I pointed toward my bag.

Lyssa went to the bag.

"No."

She stopped.

"You said it doesn't do anything."

"It doesn't."

"Then why no?"

"Because Hessa said don't alter it."

"I was going to look."

"That's how it starts."

Lyssa stared at me.

Then she laughed.

"You're scared I'll accidentally do magic."

"No."

"You're scared I'll make your trash less educational."

"Yes."

She left the bag alone.

We ate cheese.

Later, she found the question anyway because I forgot it on the table
when I washed.

She read it upside down.

"Can I produce a repeatable external change small enough to isolate?"

"Stop."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know."

"Good question."

"Give it back."

She did.

Then she read the second line before letting go.

"How little does it have to change before it counts?"

"That's not exactly what it says."

"It's exactly what it says."

"It has more words."

"Why?"

"Hessa."

"Does she charge by the word?"

"No."

"Then you're doing that yourself."

I folded the paper.

"Tomorrow is theatre."

"Today was theatre."

"Tomorrow is also theatre."

"Do you get a day off?"

"I had several before I met them."

"Those don't count."

She was right.

That was annoying.

In bed, I found out she had been using my side for folded clothes.

I moved them.

She moved two back.

"Those are clean."

"So?"

"Your road coat touched the floor in four towns."

"More."

"Exactly."

I put the road coat on the chair.

She moved the clean clothes.

Compromise.

I slept better than I had on the road.

I also woke with Lyssa's elbow in my throat.

Home.

East Market Hall had lost a room overnight.

Not literally.

The room still existed.

A group of six men with rolled papers had rented it.

Rinna was standing in the corridor arguing with one of them.

"I was told noon."

"You were told the room was yours after noon."

"It is after noon."

"It is eleven forty."

Rinna looked toward me.

"What time is it?"

I stopped.

The man looked at me too.

This was dangerous.

"Almost noon."

Rinna smiled.

The man did not.

"Greg," she said, "go away."

I did.

Inside the hall, Pell was repairing the latch.

He had removed it entirely.

"That seems worse."

"It's temporary."

"Door doesn't close."

"That's why it's temporary."

Nessa walked past carrying a painted column.

"Don't talk to him."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

Pell said, "He started it."

Nessa kept walking.

The painted column was stone from ten feet away and wood from two.

I followed it.

Not Nessa.

The column.

She set it beside a shallow doorway built into a painted wall.

The doorway bothered me.

It looked wrong.

The painted wall had lines that narrowed toward the back. The doorway
itself was only deep enough for a person to stand inside and turn
sideways. Somebody had painted another door beyond it.

Up close, the second door was obviously paint.

The floor between them was paint.

The little shadow under the far lintel was paint.

"That's bad," I said.

Nessa looked at me.

"What?"

"The door."

"What door?"

"The back one."

"There is no back one."

"Exactly."

"Good."

"No. It looks fake."

"From here."

She walked away.

I stayed.

Then I went into the seats.

The back door became a door.

Not a good door.

A door.

The real front frame cast an actual shadow across the painted floor. The
painted shadow farther back continued it. The real column hid one edge.
The narrowing lines did the rest.

I moved three seats left.

Worse.

Three seats right.

Better.

Pell appeared beside me.

"What are you doing?"

"Door."

"What?"

I pointed.

He looked.

"That's been there forever."

"I know."

"You've seen it."

"I know."

"Why are you looking now?"

"Go fix your latch."

He left.

I went back to the stage.

There was a wooden crate in front of the column.

I moved it.

The doorway looked more real from here.

Nessa came back.

"Why?"

"Why what?"

"The crate."

"It was blocking the column."

"Put it back."

"Why?"

"Because from the house it blocks the bottom."

"The bottom is real."

"Yes."

"Why hide the real part?"

"Because it looks wrong."

That offended me.

I went back into the seats.

The crate hid where the real floor met the painted floor.

Without it, the seam made the whole thing shallow.

With it, the seam disappeared.

The fake door got deeper.

I put the crate back.

Nessa said nothing.

That was worse than being told.

The day's bill was not yesterday's.

Teren had crossed Shopkeeper out.

I stared at the board.

"What?"

"Not tonight."

"Why?"

"Veya wants the room."

"Veya is not Shopkeeper."

"No."

"What am I?"

"Third Man."

"What is Third Man?"

"Third man."

"How many lines?"

"Seven."

"That's not a role."

"It has a name."

"What name?"

"Third Man."

I looked at him.

He was already reading something else.

"Anything else?"

"Shopkeeper tomorrow."

"Probably?"

"Probably."

I had been demoted by a woman who was not even taking my part.

The piece was called The Petition.

I had seen part of it before we left, but not enough to matter.

Third Man entered with two other petitioners, argued about who had been
waiting longest, delivered a complaint about a goat, and left.

The goat was not present.

Good.

My seven lines were easy.

The problem was that my fifth line was a cue.

Nessa told me that.

Not Teren.

"Don't change it."

"I don't change lines."

She looked at me.

"Not that one."

"Why?"

"Door."

"What door?"

"Side."

She pointed.

A young actor named Sellen was supposed to enter on the word "neighbor."

My line was:

My neighbor's goat has eaten half my fence and all my patience.

Sellen entered on neighbor.

"That's obvious," I said.

"Good."

"Why not enter after goat?"

"Because then he crosses Veya."

"After fence?"

"Music."

"What music?"

"You'll hear it."

I did.

There was a three-note sting from the balcony after fence.

Not magic.

A little reed.

Everything was already full.

We ran it once.

I said the line.

Sellen entered on neighbor.

The reed sounded after fence.

Veya turned.

Nobody hit anyone.

Teren said, "Again."

We did.

Then somebody needed the stage for the rented room's chairs, which did
not make sense until I learned they were borrowing ours.

The six men with rolled papers came through.

One of them pointed at the painted doorway.

"Can that move?"

Nessa said, "No."

"It has wheels."

"No."

"It visibly has wheels."

"No."

They took chairs.

The doorway stayed.

I liked Nessa.

The afternoon broke into pieces.

I carried chairs.

Then costumes.

Then a bucket of paint with a prepared lid that tightened itself when
the rim was pressed twice. Pell pressed it three times and could not
open it.

Nessa made him carry it to the paint woman.

I did not help.

Marek arrived, changed his shirt, ate something from his pocket, and
left again.

"Where are you going?"

"Back."

"Back where?"

He pointed behind himself.

"That's not a place."

"It is if you're going there."

He disappeared through the side door.

I did not see him again until the show.

Serra came in once, spoke to Rinna, took a wrapped bundle from the
office, and left.

She saw me.

"Back?"

"Apparently."

"Good."

That was all.

I wanted to ask where she had been.

I did not.

At four, Lyssa came through the hall carrying Jessa's shoes.

I was holding a painted bench.

"What are you doing?"

"Returning shoes."

"Here?"

"Jessa's sister is meeting me by the west market."

"This is east."

"I know where markets are."

"Why are you here?"

"Shortcut."

She looked at the bench.

"What are you doing?"

"Acting."

"That looks heavy."

"It's a demanding role."

Nessa said from behind me, "Greg."

Lyssa smiled.

"I like her."

"You don't know her."

"I know enough."

She left through the opposite door.

Did not stay.

Did not ask about the show.

Good.

I carried the bench.

By evening, The Petition had changed one chair, two cups, and a coat.

My seven lines had not changed.

I knew them.

That was the problem.

The audience was good.

Not loud.

Interested.

The first petitioner got a laugh I had never heard in rehearsal.

The second got another.

I came in.

We argued.

Veya, playing the clerk, asked what business could possibly justify
three grown men shouting in her office.

The first petitioner answered.

The second answered.

Then me.

"My neighbor's goat has eaten half my fence and all my patience."

Sellen entered.

Perfect.

The audience laughed at the goat.

I should have stopped.

Instead, because the laugh was there and the road had taught me what to
do with a laugh, I looked toward the side door and added:

"And apparently he's followed me."

Bigger laugh.

Good laugh.

Sellen stopped.

Bad.

He had entered as the neighbor's son.

His first line was:

My father sent me because the goat is innocent.

Except I had just implied he was the goat.

The audience looked at him.

Sellen looked at me.

There were several things he could do.

Most were bad.

He chose one.

"My father sent me because the goat is innocent," he said.

The audience laughed again.

Different laugh.

Sellen kept going.

The reed missed its entrance because the balcony player was laughing
too.

Veya waited.

The pause stretched.

Then she slapped the desk.

"Your goat may defend itself after the people."

That was not her line.

It fixed the order.

Sellen moved.

The reed player found the three notes late.

The scene continued.

It worked.

That was the worst part.

Backstage, I was pleased for almost thirty seconds.

Then Sellen came off.

"Don't."

"It worked."

"For you."

There it was.

He was not angry enough to fight.

That would have been easier.

"You entered."

"Yes."

"You covered."

"Yes."

"The audience liked it."

"Yes."

"So..."

He stared at me.

"So I had to become a goat."

I laughed.

He did not.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Say the line."

"Right."

He walked away.

Teren appeared.

"Good laugh."

I smiled.

"Don't do it again."

Smile gone.

"It worked."

"Sellen already told you why it didn't."

"Yes."

"Good."

He left.

I stood there.

Pell walked past.

"Goat."

"Fuck you."

He laughed.

The next piece used the painted doorway.

I was not in it.

I helped shift the crate.

Then I watched from the side.

Up close, the painted hall beyond the doorway was terrible.

One brushstroke made a wall.

Another made a shadow.

The far door was four dark lines and a brass-colored dot.

An actor crossed through the real frame, stopped where the real floor
ended, and turned as if somebody farther inside had spoken.

He looked into painted space.

The audience looked with him.

Nobody laughed.

The room behind him did not exist.

The crate covered the seam.

The real frame cast the first shadow.

The actor supplied the rest.

I knew that.

I still wanted to see who was in the painted room.

That was becoming a problem.

After the show, Rinna paid me one copper.

"One?"

"Third Man."

"Seven lines."

"Exactly."

"I carried a bench."

"Free."

"Ghost yesterday."

"Different day."

"Door."

"What door?"

I gave up.

One copper.

Still money.

Sellen got paid too.

I considered apologizing again.

Then decided he had specifically told me not to be sorry.

I said, "Neighbor."

He looked at me.

"Goat."

"Fair."

That seemed to fix enough.

Pell was still working on the latch.

The door now closed.

It did not open.

Progress.

Nessa had received a folded note sometime during the second piece. She
read it, put it in her pocket, and left before cleanup finished.

Nobody told me why.

Teren and Veya were arguing quietly near the office.

Not about me.

I could tell because neither looked at me.

That was relaxing.

I moved cups.

Stacked chairs.

Put the crate back where it belonged.

Not where it looked best from the stage.

Where it looked best from the seats.

I was learning which direction mattered.

Outside, Marek was waiting with food.

"For me?"

"No."

He ate it.

"Why are you waiting?"

"Not for you."

"Good."

A woman came around the corner.

Marek handed her a folded paper.

She handed him a small wrapped parcel.

Neither introduced me.

They left in different directions.

I went home.

Lyssa was there this time.

Jessa's shoes were gone.

"Successful?"

"What?"

"The shoes."

"Yes."

"Good."

She was sitting on the floor with cloth spread over her knees, picking
stitches out of one seam.

I put my one copper on the table.

"Rich."

"Third Man."

"What's that?"

"Apparently one copper."

"Good role."

"I was a goat."

She looked up.

I explained.

Not all of it.

Enough.

She laughed harder than the audience had.

"You're horrible."

"It got a laugh."

"At him."

"Yes."

"You're horrible."

"I said sorry."

"Did that help?"

"No."

"Good."

I sat.

The question paper was where I had left it.

Lyssa had put a cup on the corner so it would not curl.

She had not written on it.

I appreciated that.

Tomorrow was Hessa.

I read the question again.

CAN I PRODUCE A REPEATABLE EXTERNAL CHANGE SMALL ENOUGH TO ISOLATE?

Still ugly.

Still probably useful.

I thought about adding something about the doorway.

I did not.

The doorway was not Hessa's problem.

The fact that four painted lines could become a room if one real frame
told the truth first was Nessa's problem.

Maybe nobody's problem.

Maybe just a door.

Lyssa held up the seam she was unpicking.

"Does this look straight?"

"No."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because I'm taking it out."

I looked again.

The stitches were very straight.

The cloth underneath them was not.

"Then the stitches are straight."

"Wrong place."

"Oh."

She kept cutting.

I did not say anything else.

Tomorrow Hessa wanted something real to move.

Tonight Lyssa was removing perfectly good stitches because they made the
dress wrong.

At the theatre, a real floor had made a fake room worse until we hid
part of it.

I was beginning to suspect that being technically correct was not as
useful as I had been promised.

That sounded like something Hessa would hate.

I did not write it down.
