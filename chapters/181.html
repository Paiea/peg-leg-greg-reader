CHAPTER 181

THE CALLER

Lyssa was gone before I woke.

There was blue thread on the table.

Not all of it. One loose piece, maybe the length of my hand, had escaped
whatever bundle she was carrying and caught beneath the cup.

I picked it up.

It was blue.

I had no further useful observations.

I left it under the cup.

Outside, Carrow had already decided to be loud. A cart wheel complained
somewhere toward the market. Somebody was selling hot cakes badly enough
that I could hear him from the end of the lane. Two women were arguing
over a basket. A boy ran past with three prepared lamp wicks bundled
under one arm and nearly hit me.

He apologized without slowing.

East Market Hall's charcoal sign was gone.

Mostly.

GREG remained as a gray stain beside the latch.

I rubbed it once with my thumb.

"Don't start."

Rinna was inside the door.

"I wasn't."

"You were touching it."

"That's not starting."

"Good. We need you."

That was rarely good.

"What am I?"

"Calling second and third."

"What happened to whoever calls second and third?"

"Not here."

"Who?"

"Jori."

"Do I know Jori?"

"No."

"Then how do I know his job?"

"You don't."

She handed me a folded page.

I looked at it.

There were lines.

There were marks beside the lines.

There were names I knew and names I didn't.

"Rinna."

"Yes."

"I don't know this."

"I know."

"That's comforting."

"You're not calling first."

"Why?"

"Because first is complicated."

"Also comforting."

She pointed toward the stage.

"Pell will show you second. Veya knows third. Do not invent anything."

"I don't invent things."

Rinna looked at me.

"At work."

She left.

Pell was under the stage.

I knew because his voice came through the ghost tube.

"Greg."

I looked toward the dark corner of the hall.

"Under here."

I looked down.

There was a square opening beside the stage steps.

"Why are you under there?"

"Tube."

"Why?"

"Ghost."

"I understand the nouns."

His head appeared.

"We moved the outlet."

"Why?"

"People could see it from the left benches."

"I thought they couldn't."

"They could."

"Did someone complain?"

"No. Nessa sat there."

That was worse.

He disappeared again.

I lowered myself onto the stage edge and then down one step at a time.
Crutches were useless for the first part. Pell took one without asking
and leaned it against a beam.

The space beneath the stage was high enough for Pell to crouch.

It was not high enough for me to do anything gracefully.

Dust stuck to my palms.

"Second piece," he said.

"What is second?"

"The Dead Brother."

"That's cheerful."

"Ghost is the brother."

"Who is dead."

"Usually."

"Usually?"

"Don't worry about it."

I worried about it.

Pell showed me the page.

There were four marks that mattered.

Curtain open.

Lamp lower.

Ghost.

Door.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"Why does Rinna have a whole page?"

"Other people."

"Ah."

He tapped ghost.

"When Nessa says, 'I heard him on the stair,' you point at me."

"Why?"

"So I talk."

"You need me to point at you?"

"I can't hear her well from here."

"Then how do you know when to talk normally?"

"Someone points."

"Jori."

"Usually."

"Who isn't here."

"Yes."

I looked at the tube.

"So my job is pointing."

"Second piece."

"Excellent."

"And curtain."

"Less excellent."

Pell grinned.

We climbed out.

The curtain was a problem.

It was not heavy. It was awkward.

The pull ran along the wall beside the stage, then around a wooden wheel
overhead. Jori apparently stood there, watched the stage, watched the
side door, listened for Nessa, and somehow did not let actors walk into
the curtain.

I tried once.

I let the curtain out too quickly.

It slapped the stage.

Everyone stopped.

Nessa looked up from the coat she was pinning.

"No."

"I noticed."

"Again."

Pell reset it.

I tried again.

Too slow.

The curtain crawled.

Sellen, waiting behind it, said, "I will die before the scene begins."

"You're not in this one."

"I'll die anyway."

"Quiet," Nessa said.

We tried again.

This time the curtain opened at a speed nobody commented on.

Apparently that was success.

My right shoulder already hurt.

"You pull with both hands?" I asked.

Pell said, "Jori does."

"I don't have both hands if I'm standing."

"You can sit."

There was a low box beside the wall.

I sat.

That changed the angle enough that I could brace my good foot and pull
without fighting the crutches.

"Better," I said.

Pell shrugged.

Nobody congratulated me for solving sitting down.

That was also good.

The third piece was The Petition.

Veya took over.

"You know your own entrance."

"Yes."

"You're not in it tonight."

I stared at her.

"What?"

"You're calling."

"But I'm Third Man."

"Not tonight."

"Why?"

"Because Jori isn't here."

"Can I be both?"

"No."

"I could."

"No."

"I know the scene."

"Good. Then you know when Sellen enters."

I looked at the page.

There was a mark beside my old line.

NEIGHBOR.

"You want me to call Sellen."

"I want you to point at Sellen."

"Why is all theatre pointing?"

"Because if you shout his name the audience hears you."

That was fair.

Sellen was across the backstage passage putting on the neighbor coat.

He looked at me.

I pointed.

"Not now."

"I was practicing."

"Don't."

Veya took the page from me and drew a small line.

"Watch me. When I put the petition down, point to the reed. After the
reed, Sellen. Do not anticipate."

"I don't anticipate."

Sellen laughed.

"Nobody asked you."

"You're not in the scene."

"I know."

That hurt more than it should have.

Before we opened the doors, Nessa made me walk the backstage route once
without the page.

Not for education. She needed to know whether I could get from the
curtain box to the side gap quickly enough without blocking the costume
change.

I could, barely.

The narrowest place was between a scenery brace and a basket of folded
cloth. My left crutch fit. My right one caught.

"Move the basket," I said.

"No."

"Why?"

"Quick change."

"Move the brace."

"No."

"Why?"

"Wall."

I looked at the passage.

"So I don't fit."

"You fit differently."

That was not inspiring. It was also true.

I turned sideways, planted both crutches ahead, and moved through in two
short hops.

Nessa watched.

"Do that."

"I hate that."

"Do it anyway."

So I did.

By the third trip my palm was sore where the grip rubbed. I wrapped a
strip of scrap cloth around it and kept going.

The hall filled around us.

Nessa had the wrapped parcel from yesterday open on a table. It
contained three narrow rolls of pale cloth and a packet of black hooks.

That was all.

No mystery.

She was arguing with the paint-wrist woman about whether the hooks were
too bright.

Teren had won something from the baker.

I could tell because there were trays of small rolls in the back room.

"Four bookings?" I asked.

"Three evenings and an afternoon," he said.

"That's four."

He pointed at me.

"Don't."

I took a roll.

He slapped my hand.

"After."

"You're cruel."

"Work."

At the front door, a woman asked me whether the ghost was real.

I had been sent to help for ten minutes while Rinna found change.

I looked at her.

Her daughter was holding her hand.

Behind them, three men were arguing about whether two copper was too
much.

Yesterday I would have said Pell.

Today I said, "You'll hear him."

The girl looked delighted.

The woman bought two tickets.

I felt guilty for almost half a second.

Then one of the men asked, "Is it frightening?"

"Not very."

Rinna, behind me, kicked my crutch.

"Ow."

"Sell."

"It's not frightening."

"People like frightened."

"Do you want me to lie?"

"No. Stop finishing every answer."

The men bought tickets anyway.

One said, "If it's bad, I'm blaming you."

"That's fair."

Rinna sighed.

Someone behind us said, "Move the ticket box with magic."

I turned.

The young actor from yesterday was carrying two lamp stands.

"No."

"Bad magic."

"Yes."

He kept walking.

That was the entire consequence of my possible breakthrough.

The first piece began.

I was not calling it.

Jori's replacement for first was a woman named Hara who had apparently
done the job before. She stood where I would stand later, barely moved,
and somehow knew what everyone needed.

I watched her instead of the play.

She did not look at the page much.

That was annoying.

At the end, she handed me the cord.

"Don't wrap it around your wrist."

"Why?"

She showed me a pale scar across hers.

"Ah."

Then she left to change costume.

I did not ask why the caller was also acting.

Apparently everyone was everything.

Second piece.

The Dead Brother.

I sat on the low box.

Crutches leaned within reach.

Page on my knee.

Pell was under the stage.

Nessa was onstage in a dark dress I had seen hanging from a nail that
morning.

The room quieted.

Curtain.

I pulled.

Good enough.

Lamp.

The lamp boy lowered the left lamp after I pointed.

Too far.

Nessa's face nearly vanished.

I pointed up.

He stared at me.

I pointed up harder.

He raised it.

Nessa kept speaking.

Nobody died.

Not yet.

A chair crossed.

Door closed.

Nessa stopped beside the painted stair.

"I heard him on the stair."

I pointed at Pell.

Nothing.

I pointed again.

Nothing.

My stomach dropped.

Then I realized I was pointing at the floor.

Pell was under the stage.

He could not see through wood.

There was a narrow gap beside the curtain.

That was where Jori pointed.

I leaned.

Too far.

My crutch tip slid into the edge of the curtain.

The fabric twitched.

Nessa looked toward the dark stair.

Not at me.

I got the crutch free.

I shoved my hand into the gap and pointed down.

The ghost spoke.

"Nessa."

I knew it was Pell.

I knew exactly where he was.

I knew the leather tube ran beneath my feet and ended behind the dark
flat beside the stair.

My eyes went to the stair anyway.

Nessa froze.

Not before.

After.

The room froze with her.

"You're dead," she whispered.

Pell answered through the tube.

"I know."

Someone in the audience made a small sound.

Not a scream.

More like they had forgotten to breathe and remembered badly.

Then I remembered the door.

I pointed.

The door operator did not move.

I pointed again.

Wrong person.

The operator was on the other side.

I had been told that.

I had also forgotten it.

I reached across and tapped the wooden frame twice.

That was apparently not the cue.

The door stayed open.

Nessa crossed toward it.

She was supposed to close it on the next line.

It was already open too wide.

For one terrible second, I could see the whole mechanism behind the
painted stair from where I sat.

The audience could not.

Probably.

Nessa reached the door, caught it herself, and slammed it.

The sound was better than the planned one.

The audience jumped.

Pell whispered through the ghost tube.

"Good."

I wanted to kill him.

The rest of the piece worked.

When the curtain closed, Nessa came offstage and walked directly to me.

"Wrong side."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Now."

"Good."

She kept walking.

That was my review.

Pell crawled out from under the stage.

"You pointed at the floor."

"You were under the floor."

"Yes."

"So it was reasonable."

"No."

"I hate theatre."

"No, you don't."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because you keep lying."

Third piece.

The Petition.

I was still annoyed that I was not in it.

The new Third Man was one of the actors I had seen arguing about a coat
yesterday. His name was Tovin.

He had my goat.

Not physically.

The goat remained theoretical.

I sat with the page.

Veya began.

First petitioner.

Drainage.

Second petitioner.

Pig.

Then Veya put the petition down.

I pointed at the reed.

The reed sounded.

I pointed at Tovin.

He entered.

"My neighbor's goat has eaten half my fence and all my patience."

Wrong.

He had reversed it.

It was still a sentence.

Sellen looked at me from the wing.

I almost laughed.

Tovin continued.

Sellen entered on neighbor anyway.

The scene worked.

Different.

Not broken.

I had spent two days learning exactly how the cue fit and now somebody
else had said it wrong and the building had not fallen down.

I was offended.

Sellen did not use the father line.

He used another line instead.

The audience laughed.

Less.

Then Veya got a bigger laugh with a look.

I sat behind the curtain and pointed when I was supposed to point.

At some point I realized I was enjoying myself.

That seemed unfair.

After the third piece, my shoulder hurt enough that I stopped pretending
it didn't.

I leaned both crutches against the wall and rubbed it.

Davin appeared beside me.

I had not seen him all day.

"Pulling?"

"Curtain."

He looked at the cord.

"Bad angle."

"I sat."

"Better."

"Thank you."

He touched the wooden wheel overhead, frowned, and walked away.

"Are you fixing it?"

"Tomorrow."

"What's wrong with it?"

"Worn."

"Was it worn yesterday?"

"Yes."

"Why tomorrow?"

He looked back.

"Because today is today."

Then he disappeared toward the rear yard.

I accepted this.

Cleanup took longer because someone had spilled lamp oil near the prop
shelf.

Nothing caught fire.

That was the good news.

The bad news was that Nessa made everyone stop carrying cloth through
the area until it was sanded and wiped.

The rolls came down.

The hooks went back in the parcel.

The fake loaf went somewhere I did not see.

Rinna paid me one copper.

"Caller."

"I was bad."

"You were adequate."

"I missed the ghost."

"It arrived."

"I missed the door."

"It closed."

"Nessa closed it."

"Yes."

"That means I was bad."

"That means the show happened."

She put the copper in my hand.

"Jori back tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

I stared at her.

She smiled.

"Don't."

Pell had not rewritten the sign.

Instead, somebody had drawn a tiny piece of paper beside the gray GREG
stain.

It had little lines around it like it was flying.

I left it.

Outside, Carrow was cooling.

I found Lyssa two streets from home carrying a bundle of blue thread.

Not one bundle.

Six.

They were looped over her arm.

"You carried blue thread."

"I told you."

"I didn't understand the scale."

"Move."

I moved.

She shifted two bundles onto me.

"I have crutches."

"You have an arm."

"Pell said that about a tube."

"Who?"

"Nobody important."

We walked home.

"What did you do?"

"Called."

"Called what?"

"Ghost."

She stopped.

"You summoned a ghost?"

"No."

"Good."

"It was Pell."

"I don't know who that is."

"Exactly."

She started walking again.

I carried blue thread.

At home there was a note under the door.

Guild paper.

I knew before I opened it.

Lyssa set down her bundles.

"Paper?"

"Different paper."

I unfolded it.

Hessa's handwriting.

TOMORROW. AFTER MIDDAY.

Below that:

NEW FINS.

Then:

DO NOT BRING PELL.

I stared.

Lyssa leaned over my shoulder.

"Who is Pell?"

"I may have mentioned him."

"To Hessa?"

"Possibly."

"Why?"

"I don't remember."

She took the note.

"She knows you."

"Unfortunately."

Lyssa handed it back.

I put it beside my question paper.

One movement.

Not repeated.

One fin easier.

Tomorrow, new fins.

My shoulder hurt.

There was blue thread on the table.

Somewhere at East Market Hall, Pell was probably still under something.

I had spent the day making a ghost arrive late, failing to close a door,
and watching another man say my line wrong.

Tomorrow Hessa wanted me to try not to move paper incorrectly.

Both sounded like work.
