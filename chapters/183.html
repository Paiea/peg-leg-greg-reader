CHAPTER 183

THE WORKER

I spent breakfast staring at the corner of the paper under Lyssa's book and not touching it. Lyssa counted that as progress only after telling me to stop staring and go to work.

Three short pieces of blue thread remained tied around folded scraps on the table. Lyssa's explanation was that the rest had gone to people who needed blue thread. Marra had some. Jessa had one. The others were, apparently, people.

The cup and last night's copper were still there too. The copper felt heavier because I had earned it, which was not how weight worked. I took my crutches and went to work before I could investigate the feeling further.

At East Market Hall, Pell met me beside the repaired latch and demanded magic. I told him the useful version: I had moved paper under glass while Hessa supervised, and I was not doing it here. He tried to expand that into feathers, string, fake petitions, and finally 'things.' I refused the categories until Nessa found him something real to carry.

Teren asked the questions that mattered to theatre. Could I do it on cue? Here? Without Hessa? On anything larger than paper? No, no, no, and no. He nodded, decided the magic was not his problem, and handed me a rope for the doorframe instead.

That was how my first established external magical effect entered the theatre.

I carried rope.

The red doorframe had split through an old peg hole in one foot. Davin was absent, so Nessa bound a replacement block in place while I kept the frame upright. My shoulder objected to the awkward hold; she told me to sit closer, which irritatingly solved most of it.

By then Pell's version of my magic had reached Sellen, who asked whether I could move the doorframe with it. I could not. This disappointed him and improved nothing. Nessa tightened the repair while I learned that theatre gossip traveled faster than useful information.

The new block held. Another worker and I carried the frame to its mark, and the curtain wheel Davin had fixed opened without its old scrape. Nobody remarked on either repair once it worked. That seemed to be the standard.

The afternoon audience began arriving before we were ready.

They were louder than the evening people.

Children came in pairs and groups, some with adults and some with older siblings who had apparently been told to keep them alive until supper. Market workers arrived still wearing aprons. One baker came with flour up both forearms.

Rinna put me at the door because she had not learned.

"Two copper full bill," I said. "One for children."

A woman with three children pointed at the painted sign.

"Magic?"

I looked.

Someone had added a small star beside THE LITTLE KING.

"I don't know why that's there."

The oldest child looked at me.

"Are you the magician?"

"No."

"Pell says you are."

Of course he did.

"I moved paper."

The child waited.

"With magic," I added.

"Can you make something disappear?"

"Yes."

His face changed.

I took the handbill from his hand, put it behind my back, and handed it to Rinna.

The child stared at me.

Rinna stared at me.

"That's not magic," he said.

"No."

He seemed disappointed.

His mother laughed and paid four copper for all of them.

Rinna waited until they were inside.

"That was terrible."

"They bought tickets."

"Do it again."

"I hate this job."

"No, you don't."

I hated when people knew that.

The afternoon problem was not the children.

It was the lamp.

The Little King used a small prepared lamp with a pale blue glass cap. Not the same blue-lamp piece I had hung before. This one was supposed to brighten briefly when the king opened a wooden box.

It did not.

The first test gave a weak gray flicker.

The second gave nothing.

Pell found me.

"Magic."

"No."

"Fix."

"No."

"Bad."

"Find Nessa."

Nessa was already there.

She took the lamp from the boy holding it, checked the worked line under the cap, then called for Rinna.

Rinna touched the prepared strip with one finger.

Nothing.

"Spent," she said.

"Can it be charged?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Now?"

"No."

Pell looked at me.

I pointed at Rinna.

"See?"

"Your magic is still bad."

"My magic has nothing to do with this."

Nessa removed the blue cap.

"Plain lamp."

Pell said, "But the king's box."

"Plain lamp."

"What about the flash?"

"No flash."

"What if Greg moves paper?"

Nessa looked at him.

Pell left.

The piece ran with a plain lamp.

The king opened the box.

Nothing brightened.

A little girl in the front row still gasped because the actor looked into the box as if he had seen something wonderful.

I was carrying a stool behind the side cloth and almost stopped to watch.

Nessa tapped the stool.

"Move."

I moved.

The Miller's Son was next.

I had one line.

Not Third Man.

Not Shopkeeper.

Not anything I had performed before.

I was a clerk.

Teren had given it to me twenty minutes before the audience came in.

My entire job was to enter with a folded page, tell the miller's son that the north wheel belonged to the widow until the debt was paid, hand him the page, and leave.

"Which widow?" I had asked.

"The widow."

"From the other piece?"

"No."

"Does she have a name?"

"No."

"Why does she own a wheel?"

"Debt."

That had been enough for me.

I liked the clerk.

He knew what he was doing.

I entered.

The miller's son was played by Hara.

She looked younger than she had yesterday because someone had put her hair loose and given her a badly fitted brown coat.

I knew she was Hara.

She looked seventeen.

That was annoying.

I gave her the page.

"The north wheel belongs to the widow until your father's debt is paid."

Hara stared at me.

I waited.

She was supposed to say, "My father is dead."

She did not.

A child coughed.

Hara looked down at the page.

Then back at me.

"My father is dead."

Too late.

I had already started turning.

I stopped.

My crutch squeaked on the floor.

A boy laughed.

I turned back.

"That does make collection difficult."

The audience laughed.

Hara's eyes changed.

Not angry.

Interested.

"Will you tell the widow?"

"No."

Bigger laugh.

That was not the scene.

I knew it immediately.

I had done it again.

Hara folded the page.

"Then I'll tell her myself."

That got us back.

I left.

Backstage, Teren was waiting.

"It worked," I said before he could speak.

"For you."

I closed my mouth.

He had not been there for the last version of this conversation, but apparently theatre had one answer.

Hara came off two minutes later.

"You left."

"You forgot your line."

"I remembered it."

"After I left."

"You were supposed to wait."

"I did wait."

"You turned."

"Because you didn't say anything."

"That was my silence."

"It was a long silence."

"It was my silence."

Teren pointed between us.

"Both."

Hara frowned.

"Both what?"

"Both wrong."

Then he walked away.

I looked at Hara.

She looked at me.

"Your father is dead," I said.

"I know."

We both started laughing.

The Red Door used the repaired frame.

That was its entire reason for being called The Red Door as far as I could tell.

A woman came home.

The door was locked.

Her brother was inside.

He would not open it.

They argued through an empty frame that had no wall attached to it.

From backstage, it looked ridiculous.

From the side gap, I could see the brother standing three feet behind the red frame with nothing between him and his sister.

From the audience, the frame and a strip of painted cloth made a house.

The repair held.

Halfway through, one of the children yelled, "Go around!"

The woman onstage stopped.

The brother stopped.

The audience laughed.

She looked at the painted wall.

Then at the child.

"I've tried."

The child accepted this.

The play continued.

The short song happened because the children had not behaved and Teren apparently wanted them tired.

Afterward, the hall looked like a meal had exploded.

Cups under benches.

A heel of bread behind the third row.

One child's scarf tied around the leg of a chair.

A wooden horse that belonged to nobody in the company.

Rinna held it up.

"Whose?"

Three children shouted, "Mine."

She put it on the ticket table.

"Fight later."

We cleaned.

Pell found the old magic sign stain and added a second tiny paper beside the first.

This one had legs.

"Why?"

"It can move."

"Paper does not have legs."

"Yours does."

"No."

He added a hat.

"Stop improving it."

"Magician."

"I'm not a magician."

"You did magic."

"Yes."

"You moved a thing."

"No."

He put down the charcoal.

"You moved paper."

"Yes."

"Paper is a thing."

I looked at him.

He smiled.

I did not have Hessa's patience.

Rinna paid me one copper for the afternoon clerk role and crew work.

"One?"

"One."

"I also did door."

"You sold tickets."

"I repaired scenery."

"Nessa repaired scenery."

"I held scenery."

"One."

I took it.

"Fair."

"Yes."

"Are we paid tonight too?"

"If you work tonight."

"What am I?"

"Third Man."

"My goat."

"Your goat."

That made two shows in one day.

The afternoon crowd left.

The hall did not empty completely.

A few company people lay down backstage. Hara ate half an onion with bread. Sellen disappeared. Jori sat on the low box with his cue page over his face.

I went outside.

Carrow was still doing everything else.

A fish seller poured meltwater from a shallow cooling tray into the gutter. A delivery boy pushed a handcart too fast around a corner and lost three cabbages. Two women argued over a bolt of yellow cloth. A prepared bakery stone showed a dull orange line beneath a pan of rolls.

Nobody knew I had moved paper.

That was good.

I bought one roll.

The baker did not ask me to move it.

Also good.

When I returned, Teren had changed the evening bill.

The Petition was gone.

Third Man was gone with it.

I stared at the board.

"What happened to my goat?"

Teren did not look up.

"Tomorrow."

"You told me tonight."

"I was wrong."

"What am I doing?"

"House."

"Door?"

"House."

"What does that mean?"

"Seats. Late people. Don't let anyone through the left passage during the second piece."

"Why?"

"Costume change."

I had become less important in under three minutes.

I worked the house.

It was worse than acting.

People wanted aisle seats after buying center tickets. A man arrived during the first piece and whispered that he had paid yesterday. A woman insisted the left passage was where she had entered last week.

"No."

"Yes."

"Not tonight."

"Why?"

"Costume change."

"Whose?"

"I don't know."

That was the correct amount of information.

She went around.

During the second piece, I stood at the back wall and watched a scene I had never seen before. Sellen played a physician. Veya played a woman pretending not to know him.

I did not know why.

It was good.

Nobody needed me.

Then a bench leg shifted.

I caught the end before it tipped.

The man sitting on it looked at me.

"Thank you."

"Don't move."

He froze.

I found a folded scrap of wood, shoved it under the short leg, and let go.

The bench stayed.

The scene continued.

That was my largest practical accomplishment of the day.

No magic.

After the evening show, Rinna gave me another copper.

"House."

"Bench."

"House."

"I saved four people."

"You stopped a bench wobbling."

"It was going."

"Two copper total today."

I put both coins in my pocket.

Pell came past carrying the ghost tube.

"Can you move coins?"

"No."

"Bad magic."

"Go away."

He did.

Lyssa was not home when I got there.

There was a note on the table.

BACK LATE. EAT.

Under it sat half a loaf and a small bowl of beans.

I ate.

Then I found paper.

Not the paper I had stared at that morning.

A different piece.

I wrote:

FORWARD?

Then underneath:

MY FORWARD OR BOARD FORWARD?

I looked at it.

That was ugly enough to be useful.

I did not draw.

I did not shape.

I did not put anything under glass.

I folded the paper and set it beneath Hessa's old note.

Then I put the two copper from the day beside yesterday's copper.

Three.

The coins did not move.

Neither did the paper.

Lyssa came in while I was washing the bowl.

She carried no blue thread.

"Where is it?" I asked.

"Where is what?"

"The thread."

"Gone."

"All of it?"

"Most."

"What happened?"

"People took it."

"Who?"

Lyssa looked at me.

"Still people."

I dried the bowl.

"I worked two shows."

"Good."

"I was a clerk."

"Good."

"I moved no paper."

"Very good."

"I held a door."

"That sounds more useful."

"It was."

She took off her shoes.

"How was the magic?"

"I told you. No paper."

"I mean yesterday."

"Oh."

She smiled.

I leaned against the table.

"It is still good."

"Good."

"Everyone keeps asking what I can do."

"What can you do?"

"Move paper."

"Then tell them."

"I do."

"And?"

"They ask what else."

"What else?"

"Nothing."

Lyssa nodded.

There was no cruelty in it.

That made it worse.

I looked at the folded question.

Tomorrow Hessa did not want me.

Tomorrow the hall did.

I had one small piece of real magic and no use for it.

I had a repaired curtain, a red door that stood if nobody kicked it, two copper from a day of work, and a question about which direction paper preferred.

That seemed like enough things to own at once.
