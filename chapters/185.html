PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 185

THE REGULAR

The new leather grip was already dirty.

I decided that meant it belonged to me.

It was also better.

My palm still had a sore patch from the curtain cord, but the crutch no
longer twisted a wad of cloth into it every time I leaned. I noticed
this on the way to East Market Hall because I was carrying nothing.

This lasted until the door.

Rinna handed me three empty lamp tins.

"Morning."

"Good morning."

"Find the other three."

I looked at the tins.

"Where?"

"If I knew, I would have six."

The hall was already awake.

Someone had opened the rear doors, and a pale strip of morning crossed
the floor through dust and paint smell. Jori was onstage with the
curtain half open. Hara stood in one stocking beside a costume basket,
arguing that two brown stockings were not the same brown. Veya was
reading a page while somebody pinned the back of her dress. Pell's legs
were visible beneath a table.

I stepped around them.

"Why do we need six?"

"The oil man is coming."

"I know."

"Then why did you ask?"

"I was hoping the number had changed."

"It hasn't."

Three tins knocked together under my arm.

I knew where the lamp shelf was.

That was new enough that I noticed it, then annoying enough that I
stopped.

Two more old tins sat behind the blue lamp pieces, both empty except for
a greasy smell and one dead moth.

Five.

"Five," I called.

"Six," Rinna called back.

"I understand counting."

"Then find six."

Pell's legs disappeared under the table.

I looked at the stage.

The sixth tin could have been anywhere.

That was the problem with a place where everything became something
else. A crate was a seat until it was a wall. A bowl was supper until it
was a crown. A lamp-oil tin was probably lamp oil until Pell saw it.

"Pell."

Nothing.

"Pell."

"What?"

"Where is the sixth oil tin?"

"Which one?"

"The sixth."

His head appeared beneath the table.

"That's not a description."

"The small square ones. We have five."

"We have more than five."

"Excellent. Where?"

He disappeared again.

I waited.

A hand emerged holding a wooden wheel.

"Not a tin."

"I know."

"Then why did you give it to me?"

"I didn't."

I put the wheel on the table.

Nessa came through carrying a folded painted cloth.

She handed one end to me without stopping.

I took it.

"Where?"

"Back rail."

I knew the back rail.

I followed.

The cloth was painted with pale stone blocks on one side. Nessa walked
backward, guiding it around a bench.

"Not on the floor."

"I know."

"Then lift."

I lifted.

We got it onto the rail.

She tied one corner, looked at my side, and said, "Higher."

I raised it.

"Other higher."

I raised the wrong hand.

"No."

"I know what other means."

"Apparently not."

We got the cloth hung.

She left.

I looked at the five tins still under my arm.

I had forgotten I was holding them.

That seemed dangerous.

I put them beside Rinna's table.

A young man I had seen twice but never spoken to came through the side
door carrying the fake loaf.

"Where?"

"Prop shelf. Second level, left."

He went.

Nessa's voice came from behind me.

"No."

I closed my eyes.

The young man stopped.

"Where?"

"Kitchen box today," Nessa said.

He turned around.

I pointed at Nessa.

"She changes things."

"It was on the shelf yesterday," I said.

"Yesterday it wasn't in The Empty Table."

"What is The Empty Table?"

"Third piece."

I looked at the young man.

"Kitchen box."

He went.

Nessa looked at me.

"I was right yesterday."

"Very useful today."

Then she left.

I knew enough to be wrong faster.

That was progress of a kind.

The oil seller arrived before we found the sixth tin.

He came through the front with a wooden carrier holding six sealed tins
and the expression of a man who had been told he would receive six old
tins.

Rinna met him.

"Five now," she said.

"Six old."

"Yes."

"Six new."

"I can see them."

"Six old."

Rinna looked toward me.

I disliked being included in this.

"Looking."

The seller put the carrier down but kept one hand on it.

"No old, one copper extra."

"For the tin?"

"For not returning it."

"It's my tin."

"Then return it."

Rinna looked at me again.

I went to find Pell.

He was no longer under the table.

The table had acquired a woman sewing a hat.

"Where's Pell?"

She pointed down.

I looked.

No Pell.

"Not there."

"He was."

"Everyone was."

I went backstage.

Jori was testing the repaired curtain wheel. It rolled quietly enough
that the cloth moved without the old scrape.

"Seen Pell?"

"Rear."

I went rear.

Davin was crouched beside a wagon wheel with a hammer and a block of
wood.

"Seen Pell?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

Davin pointed toward the hall.

I had just come from the hall.

"Recently?"

"No."

"Thank you."

He glanced at the new leather grip.

"Better."

"Yes."

That was the whole conversation.

I went back inside.

My right shoulder had started to complain before my palm did. This
seemed unfair but predictable.

Hara was now wearing both stockings.

They were not the same brown.

Nobody had died.

"Have you seen Pell?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

"He asked me for red powder."

"When?"

"Morning."

"It is morning."

"Earlier morning."

"What did he want red powder for?"

Hara shrugged.

That was enough.

Red powder meant props or paint or fake blood, depending on which person
had said it.

I checked the prop shelf.

No Pell.

I checked the side table.

No Pell.

Then I saw a square metal corner beneath a stack of folded rags.

I pulled them aside.

The sixth lamp tin sat open on the table.

It was full of nails.

I stared at it.

Pell appeared behind me.

"Those are mine."

"The nails?"

"Yes."

"The tin?"

"No."

"Good."

He reached for the nails.

I stopped him.

"Where do they go?"

"In the tin."

"Not anymore."

"Then where?"

I found a cracked wooden cup.

Pell looked offended.

"That's the blood cup."

"Is there blood in it?"

"No."

"Then it's the nail cup."

"It leaks."

"Nails don't."

He considered this.

"Fine."

We poured the nails into the cup.

One fell through the crack.

Pell picked it up.

"See?"

"It lost one."

"You found it."

"Still."

I took the tin.

"Rinna is waiting."

"Tell her I needed it."

"You didn't."

"I needed something like it."

"That's worse."

The oil seller got his sixth tin.

Rinna got six new ones.

Nobody paid an extra copper.

The entire crisis had lasted long enough for Jori to finish checking the
curtain, Hara to change stockings again, Nessa to hang two cloths, and
Teren to decide the afternoon bill did not need The Empty Table after
all.

The fake loaf moved back to the prop shelf.

The young man who had asked me where it went looked at me.

I pointed.

"Don't."

He laughed.

By midday I had swept the left passage, carried two benches, taken a
coat to costumes, returned the coat because it was the wrong coat, and
moved the ghost tube before someone put a basket on it.

Nobody told me to move the tube.

I just did.

Pell came around the corner carrying the basket.

He stopped.

"Where'd it go?"

"Tube."

"Oh."

He put the basket somewhere else.

That felt better than magic.

Not more important.

Just more useful before lunch.

Teren found me sitting on the low curtain box with my crutches against
my knee.

"Evening."

"Maybe?"

"Yes."

"That means yes?"

"It does now."

"Role?"

He handed me four pages.

"Witness."

"In what?"

"Third."

Then he walked away.

I stared after him.

"Third what?"

He did not answer.

Jori did.

"The Empty Table."

I looked toward the prop shelf.

The young man with the fake loaf was already laughing.

"Of course."

The Empty Table turned out to be about an inheritance.

There was a dead aunt, three cousins, a missing will, a servant who knew
too much, and a table nobody was allowed to touch because the aunt had
supposedly died beside it.

The table was not empty.

It had a fake loaf, a wooden knife, a cup, two folded papers, and a bowl
with one real onion in it.

I pointed this out.

Veya said, "The title is old."

"Was the table empty before?"

"No."

"Then why?"

"Greg."

"Yes?"

"Read."

I read.

The Witness had six lines.

I had seen the piece once from the back wall, but I had not known it was
called The Empty Table because nobody had needed me to know.

My job was to enter after the second cousin accused the servant, say I
had seen the aunt alive after sunset, identify which cousin had been at
the rear gate, then leave after everybody decided I was lying.

I liked the Witness.

He knew one thing.

Everyone hated him for it.

Simple.

We ran the entrance once.

Jori called it.

I entered from the wrong side.

Veya stopped.

"Other."

"I know."

"You said that this morning."

"That was cloth."

"This is you."

I went around.

The second entrance worked.

During the break I went outside for air.

Carrow was busy in the ordinary way that had nothing to do with me.

A fish seller was throwing gray water into the gutter. Two boys rolled a
barrel downhill until a woman shouted at them. A wagon blocked half the
lane while somebody argued over sacks. Across the market, I saw Lyssa.

She was talking to a woman I didn't know.

The woman held a folded strip of yellow cloth. Lyssa had one hand on her
hip and the other pointing toward the cloth as if the cloth had
personally caused trouble.

Lyssa looked up.

She saw me.

I raised a hand.

She raised two fingers.

Then the woman said something, and Lyssa turned back to her.

I went inside.

I did not know who the woman was.

I survived.

The baker rolls arrived before the evening show.

Teren stood beside the tray.

I approached carefully.

"No."

"I haven't done anything."

"You were going to."

"That is not evidence."

"It is experience."

He turned away to answer Jori.

I took a roll.

Nobody stopped me.

I stood there with it in my hand, suspicious.

Hara walked past.

"You look guilty."

"I succeeded."

"At bread?"

"Yes."

"Congratulations."

She took one too.

The evening crowd came in slowly.

I worked the door until Rinna told me not to.

A man asked where the privy was.

I knew.

A woman asked whether the left passage was open.

"No."

"Why?"

"Quick change."

"Whose?"

"I don't know."

She went around.

That was apparently the correct amount of truth.

A boy carrying two tickets asked me where the ghost was.

"Not tonight."

"Tomorrow?"

"I don't know."

He looked disappointed.

"Paper?" Pell said from behind me.

"Go away."

The boy looked at Pell.

"What paper?"

"Nothing," I said.

Pell went away.

The rumor was dying.

I appreciated his effort to save it less than he probably intended.

The first piece was a short comedy I did not know.

I carried a stool on, carried it off, and missed nothing.

The second was The Red Door.

The repaired frame held.

A child did not tell anyone to go around.

This felt like a loss.

Then came The Empty Table.

I waited on the correct side.

The leather grip had darkened under my hand from sweat and dust, but it
stayed smooth.

Jori pointed.

I entered.

Veya, playing the oldest cousin, turned on me.

"You were at the lane."

"I was."

"You saw her?"

"After sunset."

"Alive?"

"Angry."

A laugh.

That line was written.

Good.

"Who else was there?"

I looked at the younger cousin.

He looked guilty.

This was also his job.

"Him."

The younger cousin stood.

"You're lying."

"No."

"You've hated me since spring."

"I met you this morning."

That got a bigger laugh.

That line was also written.

I liked this play.

The servant crossed behind the table.

Veya said, "Then why were you at the rear gate?"

I answered.

"Because your aunt paid me to watch it."

The younger cousin came toward me.

That was where I was supposed to back away.

I did.

My crutch hit the edge of the table.

The fake loaf fell.

It landed on the floor.

For one very long moment, everyone looked at it.

The loaf was painted cloth over something light. From the house it
looked acceptable.

On the floor, upside down, it looked like a dead pillow.

The younger cousin looked at it.

I looked at it.

He said, "Even the bread wants out."

The audience laughed.

I bent automatically.

Veya said, "Leave it."

I stopped.

The scene continued.

The loaf stayed on the floor for another two lines until the servant
kicked it under the table without looking.

Backstage, Nessa was waiting.

"I know."

"What?"

"Don't hit the table."

"No."

I blinked.

"No?"

"Don't pick up the loaf."

"I didn't."

"You started to."

"It fell."

"The audience saw it fall. If you stop the scene to fix it, they watch
you fix it."

I looked toward the stage.

The servant was accusing Veya of hiding the will.

The loaf was gone.

"Right."

Nessa handed me a folded cloth.

"Costumes."

I took it.

"Which chest?"

"Wall."

I hesitated.

She looked at me.

"Actually wall?"

"Yes."

"Today?"

"Greg."

I went.

The rest of my work was ordinary.

That was the part I noticed most.

I knew where the folded cloth went.

I knew not to use the left passage during Hara's change.

I knew which stool had the bad leg and put it against the wall before
anyone sat on it.

I knew the lamp boy needed the long taper, not the short one, for the
last piece.

I knew the blue lamp was not a lamp anyone should light for ordinary
light.

I knew where Pell would be when he wasn't where he was supposed to be.

Usually.

I was wrong twice.

Once about a coat.

Once about a rope.

The rope had moved to the rear peg because tomorrow's setup needed the
front peg clear.

I said, "It was here."

Rinna said, "Yes."

That was all.

The building changed while I learned it.

Apparently that was allowed.

After the show, Rinna paid me one copper.

"Witness and crew."

"I dropped bread."

"You hit a table."

"The bread fell."

"Yes."

"The audience laughed."

"Not because you were good."

"I didn't say that."

"You were going to."

I put the copper away.

"Tomorrow?"

"No morning."

I waited.

"After midday," she said. "Maybe evening."

"I have Hessa after midday."

Rinna looked at me.

"Paper?"

"Direction."

"Is that better?"

"I don't know."

"Then come after."

"If we're done."

"With paper?"

"Yes."

She nodded.

"Come after paper."

That was the entire scheduling negotiation.

Magic had become something I had to tell work about.

I was not sure how I felt about that.

Mostly I felt tired.

When I left, the hall still had people in it.

Nessa and the brown-coat woman were arguing about a hem. Jori was
writing on tomorrow's cue page. Pell had reclaimed the cracked wooden
cup for nails and was pretending this had always been the plan. Teren
was eating the last baker roll.

I pointed at him.

"You stopped me yesterday."

"Yesterday was yesterday."

I hated this place.

I went home smiling.

Lyssa was already there.

That was unusual enough that I checked the room as if I might have
entered the wrong one.

She was sitting at the table with her shoes off and a wet cloth around
one ankle.

"What happened?"

"Nothing."

"That's wet."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"My ankle hurts."

"What did you do?"

"Worked."

I put my crutches down.

"Hostile."

"Accurate."

There was bread on the table.

I sat.

"Hessa tomorrow."

"I know."

"How?"

"You left the note under the cup."

"Right."

Lyssa looked at my coat.

"You smell like oil."

"Six tins."

"Of oil?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because there were five."

She waited.

I realized that did not explain anything.

"There were supposed to be six old tins. Pell put nails in one."

"Of course."

"You don't know Pell."

"I know enough."

I ate bread.

"My grip works."

"Good."

"One copper."

"You told me."

"It still works."

"Excellent."

"I was a Witness."

"To what?"

"An inheritance."

"Did you inherit anything?"

"One copper."

"Good inheritance."

"I also dropped bread."

"Real?"

"Fake."

"Then you didn't drop bread."

I thought about Hessa.

"I dropped the representation of bread."

Lyssa stared at me.

"No."

"Fair."

I got up to wash.

My coat had a pale streak of stage dust along one sleeve. There was
something black under my thumbnail, probably charcoal or paint. The
leather grip was dirty.

Tomorrow's note was still under the cup.

AFTER MIDDAY. BRING THE QUESTION.

I took out the question.

FORWARD?

MY FORWARD OR BOARD FORWARD?

I did not improve it.

Today I had found a lamp tin, been wrong about a loaf, been wrong about
a door, been right about a passage, carried a stool, played a Witness,
knocked fake bread onto the floor, and earned one copper.

Tomorrow I was going to ask paper which way forward was.

I put the question back under the note.

Then I went to bed before anything else could need carrying.
