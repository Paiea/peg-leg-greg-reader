CHAPTER 186

THE TESTER

Lyssa's ankle was still wrapped when I woke.

Not badly. She was already standing on it.

I pointed.

"No."

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"Sit down."

"That wasn't it."

"It was close."

She put bread on the table and sat long enough to cut it.

I had Hessa's note beside my cup and my question under it.

FORWARD?

MY FORWARD OR BOARD FORWARD?

Lyssa read it again.

"Paper today?"

"Direction."

"Which direction?"

"That's the problem."

She nodded as if this were a reasonable answer.

"Are you working?"

"Yes."

"With the ankle?"

"Yes."

"Hessa would object."

"Hessa doesn't supervise cloth."

"She should expand."

Lyssa gave me bread.

I was still smiling sometimes for no reason.

Not at paper anymore. I had learned that staring at paper made Lyssa
hostile.

I smiled at the bread instead.

She saw.

"Very small amount."

"Yes."

"Under glass."

"Yes."

"While supervised."

"Yes."

"You are still pleased."

"Extremely."

"Good."

Then she left before me.

I did not go to the hall.

That felt wrong.

I had spent enough mornings there that walking past the turn toward East
Market made me feel as if I had forgotten something. I checked my coat,
my question, and the copper in my pocket.

Nothing forgotten.

The Guild had me after midday.

Until then, I had nowhere I was required to be.

This was almost suspicious.

I bought a meat bun and sat near the lower market long enough to eat it
while it was still hot.

Nobody handed me anything.

Nobody asked where Pell was.

Nobody needed a fake loaf.

It was peaceful.

I disliked it after about ten minutes.

By the time I reached the Guild, Hessa was not ready.

Of course.

The clerk at the front knew me now.

That was not necessarily good.

"Wait."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"I hate when people do that."

"Sit."

I sat.

The chair was against the wall near the same long table where people
left things they did not want to hold while arguing. Today it had two
wrapped bundles, a cracked prepared plate in a shallow tray, and a box
with three seals hanging from strings.

The cracked plate had acquired another note.

DO NOT REPAIR UNTIL CLEARED.

Someone had written beneath it:

I WASN'T.

I liked the Guild slightly more.

The gray-coated woman passed through carrying a stack of papers.

She saw me.

"Paper man."

"No."

She kept walking.

That was worse than Pell.

Hessa appeared several minutes later with ink on two fingers and
irritation on her face.

"Come."

"Good afternoon."

"No."

I followed her.

"Bad afternoon?"

"Unfinished."

"That seems normal here."

She looked back at me.

"Do you want to wait?"

"No."

"Then don't improve it."

The room was not the same one we had used last time.

The old table was there, or one much like it, but the wall behind it had
shelves with shallow boxes, folded cloth, weights, and a stack of wooden
frames. Hessa had cleared the center.

The apparatus sat on the table.

I recognized it and didn't.

The paired wooden guides remained. The glass shield remained. The paper
fins were new again, cut from the same pale sheet and folded at the
bottom. Hessa had added a flat square board beneath the guides.

There were four black marks on the board, one near each edge.

One was a line.

One was two lines.

One was a dot.

One was a cross.

"Directions?"

"Marks."

"Those are directions."

"They will be if I tell you they are."

I put my crutches against the wall.

"That's annoying."

"Good."

She held out her hand.

I gave her my question.

She read it.

Then she read it again.

"Better."

I stared at her.

"This is the ugliest question I've brought you."

"Yes."

"And this is better."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She put the paper beside the board.

"What is your forward?"

I pointed away from my chest.

"That."

"What is board forward?"

I pointed toward the black line.

"That."

"If I turn the board?"

"The line turns."

"If you move?"

"My forward turns."

"Good."

She looked at the question.

"It asks two things I can separate."

"I liked my other questions."

"They were prettier."

I was offended because she was right.

Hessa checked the table legs.

Then the wedges.

Then the glass.

She removed the shield and set two fins into the guides.

"Do not touch the table."

"I know."

"Do not touch the chair after I set it."

"I know."

"Do not move your crutches once we begin."

"They're against the wall."

"Good."

She placed the chair.

I sat.

The table was slightly too far away.

I shifted the chair.

Hessa waited.

"Now?"

"Yes."

She moved it half a hand closer.

"Now?"

"Yes."

"Then stay."

I put my good foot flat.

The missing leg did what it always did, which was not participate.

Hessa looked down.

"Can you hold that position without bracing on the table?"

"Yes."

"Show me."

I held it.

My shoulder objected mildly.

"Fine."

She put the glass shield down.

The fins stood beneath it.

One on the left.

One on the right.

Tiny.

Ridiculous.

Real.

I wanted one to move so badly that I had to look at the table edge for a
moment instead.

Hessa mixed two small wooden squares beneath the table and set one
beside each guide with the marked sides hidden.

Same as before.

I would not know which fin she had designated until she showed me.

"Minimal first."

I nodded.

The draw came cleanly.

It was still small.

Still supervised.

Still familiar.

I had spent days not touching it, and the sensation returned with an
almost insulting lack of ceremony. A narrow inward gathering. The same
difficult-to-name awareness along the wrist edge. The same sense that
one side of the shape was easier to find than the other.

I held.

Hessa watched.

"Release."

I released.

She wrote.

That made twenty-five successful supervised minimal draws.

"Again. Shape only. No external target."

I drew again.

The deliberate shape took longer.

The wrist-edge asymmetry was there.

I kept myself from leaning toward it.

Hessa saw something in my face.

"Don't chase."

"I'm not."

"Good."

I formed the shape as evenly as I could.

Held.

Released.

Twenty-one successful supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Hessa wrote again.

Then she lifted the wooden square beside the left guide.

A black mark underneath.

"Left target."

I looked at the left fin.

"Direction?"

"Line."

The black line on the board pointed away from me.

Board forward and my forward were the same.

"That's not helpful."

"It is first."

"Right."

She checked the glass again.

"Begin when settled."

I drew.

Shaped.

The target sat beneath the glass.

Forward.

Barely.

Not move.

Not jump.

Not slide across the table.

Forward.

I tried to make the intention smaller than the thing itself.

The familiar asymmetry sharpened at the wrist edge.

The paper moved.

It was tiny.

A dry little scrape.

I opened my mouth.

Hessa raised one finger.

I shut it.

She leaned over the glass without touching it.

The left fin had shifted forward perhaps half the width of my smallest
fingernail.

The right fin had not visibly moved.

Hessa checked the wedges.

She checked the board.

She checked the guide.

She crouched to look at my foot.

"I didn't move."

"I know."

"Then why are you looking?"

"Because knowing is not checking."

I shut up.

She marked the board beside the fin.

Then she lifted the glass, removed both fins, and examined their bases.

"Nothing obvious."

"Good?"

"It means nothing obvious."

She wrote.

That was the tenth supervised external-effect attempt.

One movement.

In the intended direction.

Also away from me.

Also board forward.

It proved almost nothing about the question.

I hated useful preparation.

Hessa turned the entire square board.

The black line now pointed to my left.

The guides turned with it.

The chair did not.

"Same target side?"

"Different fin. Different assignment."

She changed both fins.

Mixed the wooden squares again.

This time the right fin was target.

"Direction?"

"Line."

I looked at the line.

My left.

Board forward.

Not my forward.

"Try."

I drew.

The effort felt the same until it didn't.

I shaped toward the line.

Left.

The wrist-edge sensation wanted to become important.

I refused to decide what it meant.

The fin did nothing.

I held until Hessa said, "Release."

Nothing.

Control nothing.

Eleventh supervised external-effect attempt.

Hessa inspected anyway.

"Again?" I asked.

"No."

She rotated the board back.

"Why?"

"Because I want a different question."

She stood behind me.

"Get up."

I reached for the crutches.

"Careful."

"I know how to stand."

"Not without shaking my table."

That was fair.

I stood without touching it.

Hessa moved the chair to the opposite side of the table.

This took longer than the magical part.

I had to get around the table without hitting the board, the glass, the
chair, the shelf, or Hessa. My crutch tip caught the chair leg once.

Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped.

She moved the chair herself.

"This is humiliating."

"This is furniture."

"Same thing sometimes."

When I sat on the opposite side, the board's black line pointed toward
me.

Board forward had not changed.

My forward had.

"Now they're opposite."

"Yes."

She changed the fins again.

New assignment.

Left target.

"Line again?"

"Line."

I stared at the target.

To move board forward, it would have to move toward me.

That felt wrong before I even drew.

I did not know if that mattered.

I drew.

Shaped.

The familiar asymmetry returned.

I tried to aim through it toward the line.

Toward me.

Nothing happened.

I held.

Nothing.

"Release."

I released.

Twelfth supervised external-effect attempt.

No movement.

Hessa inspected.

Nothing.

I leaned back.

"So it doesn't like toward me."

"No."

"It moved away from me before."

"Once today."

"And before."

"Under different arrangements."

"It moved forward twice before."

"Board forward."

"Which was also away from me."

"Yes."

I smiled.

Hessa did not.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

Everyone had become unbearable.

Hessa rotated the board again.

Now the line pointed to my right.

From my new seat, my forward was toward the opposite wall.

The line was sideways.

"Different intended direction," I said.

"Yes."

"Still board forward."

"Yes."

She changed the target assignment.

Right fin.

I settled.

My shoulder had begun to ache from holding myself too carefully. The
mana itself was not painful. The attention was worse. Every tiny thing
in the room had become a possible explanation.

My foot.

My breath.

The glass.

The table.

The paper base.

The board.

My crutch against the wall.

Hessa's sleeve.

I understood why she was annoying.

This did not make her less annoying.

I drew.

Shaped toward the line.

Right.

The paper moved.

Not right.

Away from me.

A tiny forward slide relative to my body.

Which, from the board's perspective, was sideways.

I saw it.

Hessa saw it.

Neither of us spoke.

She inspected.

Control unchanged.

Wedges unchanged.

No contact.

The target fin had moved less than the first one, but enough to see
against the ink mark.

Hessa marked it.

Thirteenth supervised external-effect attempt.

I waited.

That was difficult.

Finally I said, "That wasn't the line."

"No."

"It went away from me."

"Yes."

"So my forward."

"Once."

"Twice today if you count the first."

"The first cannot distinguish them."

"Right."

"And the failed attempt with the line sideways?"

"Nothing."

"And the attempt with board forward toward you?"

"Nothing."

I looked at the paper.

"Again."

Hessa looked at me.

"Please."

"No."

"Why?"

"You're tired."

"I'm not."

"Your shoulder has moved three times since you sat down."

I looked at my shoulder as if it had betrayed me.

"It hurts."

"Yes."

"That's not mana."

"No."

"Then it shouldn't count."

"It counts as you moving."

I hated her.

She removed the glass.

I watched the target fin as she lifted it.

It had moved perhaps a few grains' width.

That was all.

Enough to make my heart beat harder.

Not enough to make Hessa let me do it again.

She laid the fins side by side.

"What can I say?"

"About today?"

"Yes."

"You produced two observed movements during four external attempts."

"Both away from me."

"One was away from you and board forward."

"The other was away from me and not board forward."

"Yes."

"And the two failures were directions that weren't away from me."

Hessa looked at me.

"One was sideways relative to you. One was toward you."

"So."

"So today suggests movement may be easier away from your position."

I waited.

"That's it?"

"That is more than you knew this morning."

"Can I say direction follows me?"

"No."

"Can I say I can push away?"

"No."

"Can I say it prefers away?"

"Not yet."

"What can I say?"

She took my ugly question paper and wrote beneath it.

POSSIBLE BIAS: AWAY FROM GREG.

NOT ESTABLISHED.

I read it.

"That's terrible."

"It's accurate."

"It sounds weak."

"It is weak."

"I moved it twice."

"Yes."

"With magic."

"Yes."

That still worked on me.

The irritation fell away for a moment.

"Yes," I repeated.

Hessa capped the ink.

"Do not become stupid because I agreed with one word."

"I moved paper twice."

"Under this setup."

"Yes."

"In two arrangements."

"Yes."

"With two failures."

"Yes."

"And today's evidence suggests, but does not establish, that movement
may be easier away from your position."

"Yes."

"Good."

I looked at the board.

"What about the wrist thing?"

"No."

"I didn't even ask."

"You were going to connect them."

"They both have a side."

"Everything has a side if you describe it badly enough."

I laughed.

Hessa did not.

She wrote the counts.

Twenty-five successful supervised minimal draws.

Twenty-one supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Thirteen supervised external-effect attempts.

I pointed.

"Only one shaping?"

"One isolated shaping before external work. The external attempts are
already deliberate external shaping."

"Right."

"Do not inflate your own record."

"I wasn't."

She looked at me.

"At work," I added.

That almost got something from her.

Almost.

Hessa took a fresh scrap of paper.

She wrote:

IS THERE AN EASIER DIRECTION?

Then below it:

TEST AWAY FIRST.

I frowned.

"I want to know if I can choose."

"I know."

"That seems more useful."

"Later."

"Why?"

"Because if one direction is easier, I want to know that before you
spend six sessions failing to prove you can do something harder."

"That sounds reasonable."

"Yes."

"I don't like it."

"I know."

She handed me the paper.

"When?"

"Not tomorrow."

"Of course."

"I have other work."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"The gray woman called me paper man."

"She has other work too."

"I noticed."

Hessa began clearing the fins.

"Restrictions remain."

"No independent draw."

"Yes."

"No shaping."

"Yes."

"No paper."

"Yes."

"No testing whether things move away from me."

"Correct."

"What if Pell moves away from me naturally?"

Hessa stopped.

"Go to the theatre."

"That's where Pell is."

"Then suffer."

I left before she could improve the instruction.

East Market Hall was already in the middle of something when I arrived.

The front doors were open.

A bench sat sideways in the aisle. Two costumes hung from the ticket
table. Somebody was singing behind the curtain, badly and with
confidence.

Rinna saw me.

"Paper?"

"Complicated."

"Can you work?"

"Yes."

"Good. Chairs."

She pointed.

I moved chairs.

That was it.

Nobody asked whether I had discovered a fundamental property of magic.

I put six chairs against the right wall, moved two benches forward, and
carried a basket to costumes.

Pell found me halfway through the basket.

"Paper?"

"Yes."

"Better?"

"Maybe."

"Can you move it sideways?"

"Maybe."

He grinned.

"Better magic."

"Nessa needs you."

"Does she?"

"I don't know."

He looked toward backstage.

Then went anyway.

Useful.

Jori handed me a folded page.

"House tonight."

"Role?"

"House."

"That's not a role."

"It is if people keep sitting where they shouldn't."

Fair.

I worked the door.

A woman wanted the aisle seat because her husband had a bad knee.

I moved two people.

A man wanted to know whether The Red Door was on.

It wasn't.

A girl dropped a ribbon.

I picked it up.

The evening passed without requiring me to move anything with magic.

I earned one copper.

When Rinna paid me, she asked, "Direction?"

I looked at her.

"Paper may prefer away from me."

Rinna put the copper in my hand.

"Reasonable."

"What does that mean?"

"Most things do."

Then she called Jori over because tomorrow's bill had changed.

I went home.

Lyssa was not there yet.

I put Hessa's new paper under the cup.

IS THERE AN EASIER DIRECTION?

TEST AWAY FIRST.

Under my old question, Hessa's answer looked annoyingly small.

POSSIBLE BIAS: AWAY FROM GREG.

NOT ESTABLISHED.

I sat.

My shoulder hurt.

My palm hurt less.

The room contained paper, cups, coins, bread, cloth, a chair, and
several other things I was not allowed to attempt to move away from
myself.

I behaved.

Lyssa came in later carrying a bundle under one arm.

"Paper?"

"Yes."

"Which direction?"

"Maybe away from me."

She put the bundle down.

"Can you make it come back?"

"No."

"Then don't lose it."

I stared at her.

"That's almost exactly the problem."

"Good."

She started untying the bundle.

I looked at Hessa's question again.

Tomorrow the hall wanted me.

Hessa did not.

After that, apparently, we were going to find out whether my magic had a
favorite direction.

For now, it had moved two pieces of paper less than a fingernail each.

That was enough to keep me awake longer than it should have.

Not long enough to test anything.
