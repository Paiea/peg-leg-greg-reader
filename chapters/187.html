CHAPTER 187

THE SUBSTITUTE

We had no bread.

This was Lyssa's fault because I had thought she was buying bread.

It was also my fault because she had thought I was buying bread.

We established both facts before either of us had properly dressed.

"I bought it last time," she said.

"I don't remember that."

"You ate it."

"That doesn't establish who bought it."

"It establishes you were present."

Her ankle wrap was still on, though looser than yesterday. She stood
with most of her weight on the other foot while tying her hair back.

I pointed at the ankle.

She pointed at my crutches.

We abandoned that argument.

"I'll get bread," I said.

"Good."

"If I'm near Marra's, I am not carrying anything."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You were going to."

"I was going to tell you Jessa said your coat button is loose."

I looked down.

The second button was loose.

"That's worse."

"How?"

"I was prepared for a parcel."

Lyssa smiled.

She had work west again. I had the hall.

Hessa's papers remained under the cup.

POSSIBLE BIAS: AWAY FROM GREG.

NOT ESTABLISHED.

And beneath that:

IS THERE AN EASIER DIRECTION?

TEST AWAY FIRST.

I put the cup back over them.

Nothing moved.

Good.

Outside, a shop door opened away from me.

I noticed.

Then I was annoyed that I had noticed.

By the time I reached East Market Hall, I had bought bread, eaten part
of it, and stopped assigning directions to doors.

Rinna was waiting inside with a coil of narrow cord over one arm.

"You're late."

"I'm early."

"Good. You're here."

She handed me the cord.

"What's this?"

"Back drop."

"Today?"

Rinna looked at me.

"Yes."

That felt like progress.

Nessa was already on the rear platform with a folded length of blue-gray
cloth. She saw the cord in my hand and pointed upward.

"Back rail."

"I know."

"Today."

"I know that too."

She gave me one end of the cloth.

We raised it.

I got the first corner right.

The second sagged.

"Other peg," Nessa said.

I moved it.

"Not that other peg."

"There are too many pegs."

"There are six."

"Exactly."

She fixed it while I held the cloth.

Nobody explained what the drop was for.

I did not ask.

Across the hall, Jori was checking the curtain line. Hara stood in one
stocking and one bare foot, arguing with a costume worker about whether
brown meant brown or the other brown. Veya sat on the stage edge reading
a page while somebody pinned the side of her dress.

Pell was nowhere visible.

This should have worried me.

It did not yet.

Rinna passed me on her way toward the front.

"Tell Pell narrow tube tonight. Not long."

"Narrow tube."

"Tonight."

"Yes."

She kept walking.

I repeated it once to myself.

Narrow tube tonight.

Then Jori called, "Greg."

I looked over.

A young woman I had seen twice before was holding the fake petition
stack.

"Where does this go?"

I almost answered.

Then I remembered the loaf.

"Today?"

Jori looked pleased in a way I distrusted.

The young woman said, "The Petition."

"Prop box by left wing."

Jori nodded.

I pointed.

"Bottom shelf. Wax side up."

She carried it away.

I had become dangerously competent.

Nessa handed me a broom.

That corrected the feeling.

I swept the left passage.

I moved two benches.

I took a red coat from a chair to costumes and was told it belonged on
the chair.

"Today?" I asked.

"Today," the costume worker said.

I returned it.

Someone asked where the blue lamp was.

"Upper prop shelf, wrapped."

It was.

Someone asked where the bad stool was.

"Which bad stool?"

"The one with the loose back."

"Rear wall."

It was not.

Pell had moved it.

I found him under the stage with his feet sticking out.

"Narrow tube tonight," I said.

He slid halfway out.

"What?"

"Narrow tube. Tonight."

"Why?"

"Rinna."

"For what?"

"I don't know."

"Ghost?"

"Probably."

"Which narrow tube?"

I stared at him.

There were two.

Of course there were two.

"The narrow one."

"They're both narrow."

"One must be narrower."

"Yes."

"Use that one."

Pell considered this.

"Good."

I left.

That was when Hara called me to hold a costume basket.

Then Jori needed the second stool.

Then Teren arrived carrying three folded pages and said, "You're in
second if Evin isn't here."

I forgot about the tube.

"Who is Evin?"

"Clerk."

"In what?"

"Debtor's Uncle."

"I don't know Debtor's Uncle."

"You've seen it."

"Once."

"Good."

Teren handed me a page.

There were eight lines marked in charcoal.

I read the first.

"I have no authority to release the chest."

I looked at Teren.

"This is a terrible role."

"It's a clerk."

"Exactly."

"Learn it."

"What if Evin comes?"

"Then he does it."

Teren walked away.

I looked at the page.

Nobody had asked whether I wanted the role.

That was also progress.

Debtor's Uncle turned out to be a short comedy about a man who inherited
a chest from an uncle who owed money to everyone in the district. The
chest contained almost nothing. This did not stop six people from trying
to claim it.

The clerk existed to tell everyone no.

I liked him immediately.

Veya found me reading beside the left wing.

"Entrance after second knock."

"Which side?"

"Right."

"Today?"

She looked at me.

"Yes, Greg."

"I am learning."

"Slowly."

She walked away.

I read the lines again.

The second knock.

Right side.

No authority.

Chest remains sealed.

Receipt first.

No, your cousin cannot sign.

Eight lines.

Easy.

Which meant something else would go wrong.

At midday, Evin still had not arrived.

Rinna had not seen him.

Jori had not seen him.

Teren had apparently expected this because he did not look surprised.

"Why is he late?"

"His sister's boy is sick," Hara said while eating an apple.

"How do you know?"

"He told me yesterday."

"Did he tell Teren?"

Hara looked toward Teren.

"Apparently not."

Teren heard.

"He told me he might be late."

"That seems relevant."

"It was."

"Did you tell me?"

"I just did."

Fair.

We ran the entrance once.

I came in on the right.

The first knock sounded.

I started.

Veya said, "Second."

"I know."

"You moved."

"I didn't enter."

"You leaned."

"That's not entering."

"Do it again."

The second time I waited.

Second knock.

Enter.

I made it to the desk.

The chest was a square wooden box with iron-colored paint at the
corners. It was light enough that Sellen had carried it one-handed five
minutes earlier.

I was not supposed to touch it.

That helped.

My first line was to Veya.

"I have no authority to release the chest."

She answered.

I gave the second.

Sellen came in too early.

Veya told him to go back out.

He did.

Nobody treated rehearsal as sacred.

Somebody hammered backstage through half of it.

Nessa crossed behind us carrying a rolled painted wall.

Pell shouted something about a hinge.

Jori called, "Again from the knock."

We did.

By the third time, the clerk existed.

Not much.

Enough.

During the break, I went to find Pell.

He was not under the stage.

I remembered Rinna's message.

Narrow tube tonight.

Good.

I had delivered it.

Mostly.

At the rear wall, one of the tubes was already out.

It was the longer one.

I stared at it.

Pell came through the side door with a handful of chalk.

"Why is that out?"

"Ghost."

"Rinna said narrow."

"That is narrow."

"The other narrow."

He looked at me.

"You said narrower."

"Yes."

"This one is narrower than the long one."

"There are three?"

"Yes."

I closed my eyes.

Pell pointed toward the prop shelves.

"Long, narrow, narrower."

"Why?"

"Different rooms."

"Rinna said narrow tube, not long."

"That's narrow."

I had delivered exactly the words.

I had also known he had asked which one.

I had decided I was done.

That part was mine.

"Wait."

I found Rinna near the ticket table.

"Which narrow tube?"

She looked at me.

"The short one."

"Pell has the middle one."

"I told you narrow, not long."

"There are three."

"I know."

"I didn't."

"You do now."

"Do you want the short one?"

"Yes."

"Good."

I turned.

Rinna said, "Greg."

I looked back.

"I told you this morning."

"Yes."

That was all.

I went back.

"Pell. Short one."

"That's narrower."

"I don't care what it's called."

"Then how will I know which one?"

"Short."

"Good."

He put the middle tube away.

Nothing had broken.

Nobody died.

We lost perhaps four minutes.

I had been relied upon to carry six words across one building and had
managed to make them insufficient.

This seemed unfair.

It was not.

I went outside for bread.

The market was loud enough that the hall noise disappeared after twenty
steps.

A fish seller was shouting prices. Two boys rolled an empty barrel
between them and nearly hit a woman carrying eggs. A wagon had stopped
crooked beside a stack of sacks, and three men were arguing about
whether the wagon or the sacks had been there first.

I saw Lyssa across the street.

She was with the woman from yesterday, the one with the yellow cloth.

Today the woman had green cloth.

This seemed like a development.

Lyssa saw me.

I lifted the bread.

She looked at it, then at me.

I pointed at myself.

She nodded approval.

Neither of us crossed the street.

I did not know who the woman was.

I survived again.

Back at the hall, Evin still had not arrived.

Teren saw the bread.

"Where did that come from?"

"I bought it."

He looked disappointed.

"Why?"

"Because yesterday you oppressed me."

"The baker arrangement ended."

I stopped.

"What?"

"No rolls today."

Behind me Pell said, "What?"

Rinna said, "Paid work ended."

Pell came around the curtain.

"We're still working."

"Different problem," Rinna said.

Pell looked genuinely betrayed.

I tore my bread in half.

He held out his hand.

"No."

"You're cruel."

"You called me magician for three days."

"That was praise."

"It was inaccurate."

"Half."

"No."

He wandered away.

I ate both halves.

This was one of my better performances.

The first piece went on without me.

I moved a stool before it and moved the same stool back afterward.

Nobody applauded.

Evin had still not arrived.

Teren found me.

"Clerk."

"Yes."

"Second knock."

"Yes."

"Right."

"Today?"

He stared at me.

"Go."

The house was not full, but it was good enough that the room changed
when the curtain opened.

That still happened.

Empty hall became audience.

Painted wall became a room.

Sellen became a man with a dead uncle and six creditors.

Veya became his cousin, who claimed the chest contained family property
despite never having seen it.

I waited behind the right side.

First knock.

I stayed.

Second knock.

I entered.

The clerk's coat was too wide through the shoulders and slightly too
short in the sleeve. Nobody had altered it for me because I was not
supposed to own the role.

I crossed to the desk.

Veya demanded the chest.

"I have no authority to release the chest."

"Then find someone who does."

"I have no authority to find someone who does."

That got a laugh.

Not a large one.

Enough.

The scene continued.

Sellen tried to sign for his dead uncle.

"No, your cousin cannot sign."

"He's not my cousin."

I looked at Veya.

"I met you this morning."

The audience laughed harder.

That line belonged to Evin.

I had heard him do it once.

He did it better.

Mine still worked.

A creditor entered.

Then another.

The chest acquired more owners without moving.

My job was mostly to prevent everyone else from progressing.

I understood bureaucracy spiritually.

Near the end, Sellen put both hands on the chest.

I said, "Receipt first."

He said, "For my own uncle?"

"Especially for your own uncle."

That got another laugh.

Then Veya took the scene away from me with a long complaint about how
the dead man had owed her three spoons, a blanket, and an apology.

She was better.

The audience followed her.

Good.

I stood behind the desk and looked annoyed.

Also good.

The piece ended.

Backstage, Teren said, "Fine."

"Fine?"

"The show happened."

"I got laughs."

"So did the chest."

The chest had not said anything.

I chose not to argue.

Evin arrived during the next piece.

He was thin, red-faced from hurrying, and angry before anyone spoke to
him.

"Boy's fever broke," he said to Hara.

"Good."

"Cart wheel split."

"Bad."

"Took the south road."

"Worse."

He saw me in his clerk coat.

"Oh."

"Hello."

"Did you do it?"

"Yes."

"How bad?"

"Teren said fine."

Evin nodded.

"Good."

That was all.

For the evening repeat, Evin took the coat back.

Obviously.

I still felt a small, stupid disappointment when he did.

Then I watched him run the clerk scene and understood why it was his.

He did less.

That was the irritating part.

He barely moved behind the desk. He let everyone come to him. His first
"no" was almost bored. By the time he said, "I met you this morning,"
the audience was already waiting for him to refuse something.

His laugh was bigger than mine.

I hated him for approximately six seconds.

Then Jori handed me a cue page.

"Hold."

I held it.

"Not there."

I moved.

"Other side."

I moved again.

The evening continued.

The ghost used the short tube.

It worked.

Pell did not mention magic.

I noticed this halfway through the piece and was offended.

Then he whispered, "Move."

I looked at him.

"What?"

"You're on my cord."

I moved my crutch.

He pulled the cord free.

That was better.

Later, Nessa handed me a gray cloth.

"Back rail."

"Today?"

"Tomorrow."

I stopped.

She nodded once.

"Good."

I put it on the marked shelf instead.

That felt suspiciously like learning.

Then I carried the wrong rope to Jori.

"It was there yesterday," I said.

Jori pointed to the front peg.

"Today."

"Yes."

The building kept changing while I learned it.

By the end of the evening, my shoulder hurt, my right palm was tender,
and the new leather grip had another dark mark across it.

It still did not twist.

Worth one copper.

Rinna paid me two.

I looked at the coins.

"Two?"

"Morning, clerk, evening crew."

"I was only clerk once."

"You were only paid for it once."

"Good."

"Did you want less?"

"No."

She closed her box.

I put the coins away.

"Do you need me tomorrow?"

"Afternoon."

"Role?"

"Maybe."

"Don't."

Rinna smiled.

"Tell Pell the short tube goes back on the upper shelf."

I looked toward Pell.

He was six paces away.

"PELL."

He looked over.

"SHORT TUBE. UPPER SHELF."

Rinna said, "Excellent."

"I've developed a system."

Pell shouted, "WHICH UPPER SHELF?"

I looked at Rinna.

She closed her eyes.

I went home.

Lyssa was already there.

Her ankle wrap was off.

The ankle itself looked slightly swollen.

I pointed.

She said, "Don't."

"Better?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Your button is worse."

I looked down.

The loose button was now hanging by one thread.

"That also seems like work."

"Sit."

I sat.

She took the coat.

"How was paper?"

"Yesterday."

"I know. I wanted to see if you remembered."

"You're becoming cruel."

"Work."

She found a needle.

I watched her repair the button.

"Busy day?"

"Yes."

"Bad?"

"No."

I thought about it.

"Busy."

She nodded.

"Mine too."

"Yellow cloth woman had green cloth today."

Lyssa looked up.

"You saw us?"

"Across the market."

"That isn't her cloth."

"Whose is it?"

"Someone else's."

"Good."

She returned to the button.

I waited.

Nothing else came.

I had no idea who the woman was.

Still alive.

"I was a clerk."

"At the theatre?"

"Yes."

"What did you do?"

"Told everyone no."

"Good casting."

"I was excellent."

"How excellent?"

"Teren said fine."

Lyssa laughed.

"The real clerk came back."

"And?"

"He was better."

"That's rude."

"Very."

She bit the thread and handed me the coat.

"What did you earn?"

"Two copper."

"Better than fine."

I put the coat beside me.

On the table, Hessa's paper was still under the cup.

Nothing had moved.

I had thought about away twice during the day.

Once at the door.

Once when Pell finally walked away from me carrying the correct tube.

Neither counted.

I did not test anything.

Tomorrow the hall wanted me in the afternoon.

Hessa had not asked for me at all.

That was fine.

Probably.

I put one copper beside the bread money and kept the other.

Lyssa leaned back and stretched her sore ankle.

I stretched my shoulder.

Neither of us said anything useful about that.

People had needed me all day.

Cord.

Cloth.

A message.

A clerk.

A cue page.

A rope.

A tube.

A place to put something.

I had gotten some of it right.

I had gotten some of it wrong.

The show happened anyway.

That felt less impressive than moving paper.

It was considerably more tiring.

I went to bed before anyone could ask me where the short tube belonged.
