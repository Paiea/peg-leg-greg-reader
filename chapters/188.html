PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 188

THE HOUSEMAN

Lyssa was gone before I woke.

There was bread.

This was an improvement.

There was also a bowl on the table that did not belong to us.

I knew this because ours had a chip on the rim and this one did not. The
bowl contained nothing. Beside it was a folded scrap of paper.

MARRA.

That was the entire note.

I ate bread while looking at the bowl.

Then I looked at the door.

"No."

Nobody answered.

I finished eating.

The bowl was still there.

I carried it to Marra's.

This was how they got you.

The morning was cool enough that the leather on my right crutch did not
immediately grow damp under my palm. The soreness was better than
yesterday. Not gone. Better. The grip stayed where the cobbler had put
it, seam turned away from the tender place.

I passed the Guild street without looking toward it.

Mostly.

Hessa had not sent for me.

There was no reason to go in.

I had no paper.

I had no glass.

I had no permission.

A cart wheel rolled away from a boy in the street and struck a wall.

Away.

I kept walking.

Marra's green door was open.

Marra was kneeling beside a low stool with three skirts hanging from
pegs behind her. Jessa stood on the stool in her shift while another
woman pinned a dark hem around her.

I held up the bowl.

Marra looked at it.

"Good."

"Is that all?"

"Yes."

Jessa looked down at me.

"Your button?"

"Repaired."

"Show me."

"No."

"Then why did I tell Lyssa?"

"So she could repair it."

Jessa seemed dissatisfied by the successful completion of her own
instruction.

I put the bowl on a shelf.

Marra said, "If you see Lyssa, tell her west after second bell."

I stared at her.

Marra smiled.

"No."

"Why?"

"I came here to return a bowl."

"And now you know something."

"I refuse."

"Fine."

Jessa said, "Tell her west after second bell."

I left.

I had become a message with crutches again.

The hall did not want me until afternoon.

This left me with time.

I bought an onion.

I stood beside a fruit stall and considered buying an apple, decided
against it, then bought one because I had already spent too long
considering it.

I sat on a low wall near the lower market.

Nobody needed me.

A man repaired a basket across the street. Two women argued about fish.
A dog slept under a cart until somebody moved the cart, at which point
the dog moved three feet and went back to sleep.

I ate the apple.

Nobody handed me a rope.

Nobody shouted my name.

Nobody asked which tube was narrow.

It was peaceful.

I lasted perhaps fifteen minutes.

Then I went to the hall early.

Rinna was at the front table counting small coins into stacks.

She looked up.

"Afternoon."

"It is almost afternoon."

"It isn't."

"I can leave."

"Don't."

I leaned my crutches against the table.

"Role?"

"No."

I waited.

Rinna kept counting.

"That's all?"

"Unless something goes wrong."

I considered this.

"That sounds like a role."

"It isn't."

"It sounds like one."

"Carry the wall."

There was a wall.

Of course there was.

It was a narrow painted flat with a window on it, leaning against the
left side of the stage. Pell had one end.

I took the other.

"Where?"

"Rear."

"Today?"

Pell looked at me.

"Why do you keep saying that?"

"Experience."

We carried it rear.

The painted window showed a strip of dark blue sky and one pale square
that might have been another building. Up close, the paint was streaked
and the square looked crooked.

"Which piece?"

"The Miller's Son."

"I've seen that."

"Good."

"Is it still the same?"

Pell stopped.

"What?"

"Today."

He stared at me.

Then he laughed hard enough that the wall shook.

"Don't."

We set it down.

Nessa was at a worktable with a small lamp opened in front of her.

Not the blue lamp.

This one had a brass frame, cloudy glass, and a thin prepared strip set
into the base. The strip was dark at one end.

I knew enough to stop two steps away.

"It isn't lighting," Hara said.

She stood beside Nessa with one sleeve rolled up and a smear of paint on
her wrist.

"Did you change the oil?" Nessa asked.

"Yes."

"Wick?"

"Yes."

"Catch?"

"Twice."

Nessa touched the wooden side of the lamp, not the prepared strip.

"Who moved it?"

Hara pointed at Pell.

Pell pointed at me.

"I just arrived."

"Then not Greg," Nessa said.

This was the nicest thing anyone had said to me all day.

I leaned closer.

"Can I help?"

"No."

"Good."

Nessa looked at me.

"Get Sava."

"Who is Sava?"

"Rear yard. Gray apron."

I went.

Sava was a broad woman with a shaved strip above one ear and both hands
blackened with lamp soot. I had seen her before without knowing her
name.

"Nessa wants you."

"Why?"

"Lamp."

"Which?"

"Brass. Cloudy glass. Prepared strip dark on one end."

She wiped her hands on the gray apron.

"Good."

That was apparently enough.

I brought the right person.

This felt suspiciously mature.

Sava looked at the lamp, swore once, and asked for a narrow blade.

I left before anyone could mistake my presence for competence.

Jori found me near the curtain.

"Tonight you're rear."

"Rear what?"

"Rear."

"That's not a job."

"It is now."

He handed me a small wooden block.

I looked at it.

"What does this do?"

"Door."

"Which door?"

"The Miller's Son."

I looked toward the painted wall.

"The fake one?"

"The real fake one."

"Good."

Jori pointed behind the rear hanging.

There was a short board on the floor, another upright board, and a metal
latch fixed to nothing I could see.

Pell came through carrying a bucket.

"He's crowd."

Jori said, "You're crowd."

"I thought I was rear."

"Same place."

"How many jobs are in rear?"

"All the ones the audience shouldn't see."

That seemed excessive.

The Miller's Son had a scene I remembered badly. A young miller owed
grain to three men, money to two women, and an apology to his mother.
Near the end, people gathered outside his house while he tried to
convince Veya, playing his sister, that leaving through the window was
reasonable.

Apparently the people outside were mostly Pell.

"How many?" I asked.

"Seven," Pell said.

"How many people?"

"Two."

"Who's the other?"

"You."

I looked at Jori.

He was already reading his cue page.

Pell showed me the arrangement.

The rear side of the stage had two strips of flooring. One was bare
board. One had an old woven runner nailed down. There was a hanging door
panel that the audience saw from the other side. Beside it, a loose
shutter rested against the wall. A tin cup sat on a shelf.

Pell pointed.

"Three steps on board. Stop. Knock."

"Me?"

"You."

"Then what?"

"Move to runner. Different voice."

"What voice?"

"Not yours."

"I have several voices."

"No."

"You haven't heard them."

"I have."

That was probably true.

Pell crossed the boards with heavy steps.

He knocked twice.

Then he moved behind the hanging, changed the angle of his body, and
called, "Open up, Len!"

It sounded like one man.

He stepped onto the runner, softened his feet, leaned toward the
opposite side of the hanging, and said in a higher, irritated voice, "We
know you're in there."

Different man.

Not completely.

Enough.

Then he rattled the loose shutter while striking the wall once with his
heel.

From where I stood, it was obviously Pell making noise.

Jori said, "Greg, board."

I crossed three steps.

"Too even."

"I'm walking."

"You're counting."

"I was told three."

"Don't sound like three."

I tried again.

Apparently I had been walking like a man demonstrating the concept of
footsteps.

Pell shook his head.

"Angry."

I walked harder.

"Now you're a horse."

"Pick a species."

"Person."

I did it again.

Better.

"Knock," Jori said.

I knocked.

Veya, onstage beyond the hanging, turned toward the door.

I watched her.

"Greg."

I knocked again.

Jori looked at me.

"What?"

"Late."

"She looked at the door."

"That was your warning."

"I thought that was the cue."

"No. She looks. Then Sellen says the line. Then knock."

Pell said, "Not when they look. After."

"I understand."

"Good."

"Today?"

Pell started laughing again.

We reset.

Veya looked toward the door.

Sellen said, "Nobody knows I'm here."

Jori lifted two fingers.

I knocked.

Veya flinched.

The knock sounded better because she flinched.

That was annoying.

Pell immediately crossed behind me, gave two heavy steps from farther
left, and called, "Miller!"

Then I moved to the runner.

"Open up," I said in a low voice.

Pell stopped.

"No."

"What?"

"Never that voice."

"It was different."

"It was a dying cow."

"I don't know what cows sound like here."

"The same."

Jori said, "Use your voice. Turn away."

"That isn't a different person."

"Audience can't see you."

I turned my head toward the rear wall.

"Open up."

It sounded different.

Not much.

Enough.

Pell nodded.

"Better cow."

We continued.

He rattled the shutter.

I struck the wooden block against the upright board once.

Veya crossed away from the door.

Sellen looked at the window.

The two of them made the room feel crowded before we made another sound.

I noticed that.

Then Jori snapped his fingers at me because I had missed my next step.

"Work."

"Yes."

The lamp problem took longer.

Sava had the prepared strip out on a cloth. Nessa held the lamp body.
Hara stood nearby, increasingly impatient.

"Can it run tonight?" Hara asked.

"Not like this," Sava said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means no."

"What if we use the other catch?"

"No."

"What if we use the blue lamp?"

Nessa said, "Wrong light."

I had an idea.

This was dangerous.

I kept it.

Then Nessa looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

Sava asked me to fetch a plain taper lamp from the upper shelf.

I knew where that was.

I brought it.

Nessa set it behind a thin cream screen, moved it twice, and looked from
the house.

"Closer."

Hara moved it.

"Too close."

They moved it again.

The broken prepared lamp stayed on the worktable.

The ordinary taper became the replacement.

I did not know exactly what the prepared one had been meant to do.

Nobody explained.

The show was going to happen anyway.

At the front, a woman asked me whether The Red Door was on tonight.

"No."

"What is?"

"The Miller's Son, The Widow's Room, and something short after."

"What's short?"

"I don't know."

She looked at me as though I worked there.

This was unreasonable.

"Is it funny?"

"Probably."

Rinna, behind the table, said, "Yes."

I looked at her.

She said, "Sell the seat."

I looked back at the woman.

"Yes."

She bought two.

I was learning commerce.

Poorly.

Before the house opened, I remembered Marra.

I had not seen Lyssa.

I found a scrap of paper.

WEST AFTER SECOND BELL.

I wrote LYSSA above it.

Then I realized I had no reliable way to get it to her.

I put it in my pocket.

This was not useful.

The house filled enough that I stopped thinking about it.

I worked the aisle first.

A man wanted to move three seats because his wife could not see around a
tall hat.

The woman wearing the tall hat objected to being described as a hat.

I moved the man and his wife instead.

A boy dropped a wooden animal under a bench.

I retrieved it.

An older woman asked whether the hall was always this warm.

"Only when people come."

She stared at me.

I decided I should not work the house.

Then Jori called from the side.

"Rear."

Better.

The first piece did not need me.

I stood behind the hanging and listened.

Audience laughter came through cloth differently. Softer. Less like
people and more like weather.

Pell was repairing a strap beside me.

"Paper," he whispered.

"No."

"Didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"I was going to ask if you had paper."

"Why?"

He held up the strap.

"Need a shim."

I gave him a folded scrap from the prop shelf.

He tore it.

"Thanks."

That was the entire magic conversation.

The Miller's Son began.

I took my place.

The painted window was six feet from me and looked terrible.

From the house it had looked like night.

The replacement taper behind the cream screen made a pale square across
one side of the set. It was not the same as the prepared lamp. I could
tell because Hara had complained twice.

The audience did not know what had been planned.

The scene moved.

Veya and Sellen argued.

The miller lied.

His sister did not believe him.

Offstage, Pell held up one finger.

Not yet.

Veya looked toward the door.

Sellen said, "Nobody knows I'm here."

Jori's hand lifted.

I crossed three hard steps.

Not counting.

I knocked.

Veya flinched.

A murmur went through the house.

Pell moved left.

"Miller!"

I moved onto the runner and turned away.

"Open up."

Different enough.

Pell struck the shutter.

I hit the upright with the block.

Someone in the audience laughed.

Onstage, Sellen backed toward the painted window.

Veya caught his sleeve.

"No."

"They're at the door."

"There are six of them."

There were two of us.

Pell made another voice from farther right.

I moved behind the hanging and stamped once on bare board.

Veya turned toward that sound before I made it.

For half a second, I felt late.

Then I remembered.

After.

I waited.

Her head turned.

Sellen said, "The back."

I struck the wall.

The audience's attention shifted.

I could hear it.

Not a sound exactly.

A change.

Pell rattled the shutter.

The room outside the fake house filled with people who did not exist.

I nearly missed my last knock because I was listening to them.

Jori touched my sleeve.

I knocked.

The scene continued.

When it ended, Pell grinned.

"You were four."

"Four what?"

"People."

"I was at least five."

"No."

"Why?"

"Bad cow only counts half."

"I didn't use the cow."

"It remains."

We reset the block.

No one in the audience had seen me.

I had enjoyed that more than I expected.

The Widow's Room used the painted doorway but not the blue lamp. The
ordinary taper replacement stayed in Miller's Son storage.

The short final piece turned out to be Hara and Evin arguing over a
missing shoe.

It was funny.

I had no role.

I watched from the side until Nessa handed me a basket and said,
"Costumes."

I carried it.

Afterward, the hall became itself again.

People stood.

Benches scraped.

Coins clicked at the front.

Pell carried the short tube toward the wrong upper shelf.

I pointed.

"Other."

He changed direction without arguing.

That was disturbing.

Sava had wrapped the bad prepared strip separately and written something
on the cloth.

I did not ask what.

Hara was already complaining about tomorrow.

Jori rolled his cue page.

Veya took off one shoe and walked around in the other.

Teren was nowhere I could see.

Rinna paid me one copper.

I looked at it.

"Rear?"

"House and rear."

"I was four people."

"Then split it with them."

I closed my hand.

"One is fine."

"Good."

She reached under the table.

"For you."

It was a folded note.

My name was on the outside in Hessa's writing.

I opened it.

DAY AFTER TOMORROW.

AFTER MIDDAY.

AWAY FIRST.

Below that, smaller:

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

I read it again.

Rinna said, "Paper?"

"Day after tomorrow."

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"You can work tomorrow."

I looked at her.

She had already turned away.

Of course.

Outside, it was fully dark.

I had forgotten Marra's message.

I remembered it halfway home.

Second bell had been hours ago.

I stopped in the street.

"Excellent."

A man passing me said, "What?"

"Nothing."

Lyssa was home.

I knew before opening the door because light showed at the bottom and I
could hear her moving something inside.

She was sitting on the floor with a basket between her knees.

Her ankle was wrapped again.

I held up one finger.

She said, "No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to."

"Marra said west after second bell."

Lyssa stopped.

"When?"

"This morning."

She stared at me.

"I remembered."

"When?"

"Now."

"Greg."

"I wrote it down."

"Where?"

I pulled the paper from my pocket.

She took it.

"Very useful."

"I had work."

"So did I."

"Did you need the message?"

"No. Jessa found me."

"Good."

"At third bell."

"Less good."

Lyssa put the paper in the basket.

"What did you do?"

"I was four people outside a door."

She looked at me.

"How many were you?"

"One."

"That sounds dishonest."

"It was theatre."

"Same thing."

"Not according to Teren."

"Who were the other three?"

"Pell."

"All three?"

"Some of him."

She nodded as though this clarified it.

I showed her Hessa's note.

"Paper?"

"Day after tomorrow."

"Away?"

"First."

"Then what?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

"You say that too much."

Lyssa returned the note.

"What did they pay you for being four people?"

"One copper."

"Did the other three get paid?"

"I hope not."

We ate bread, onion, and the last piece of cheese.

My coat smelled faintly of dust and lamp oil even though I had not
touched the bad lamp.

There was black on the side of my hand from the wooden block.

My right palm still hurt.

Lyssa's ankle still hurt.

Tomorrow the hall wanted me.

Day after tomorrow Hessa wanted me.

Somewhere in between, Marra would probably want a bowl carried again.

I put Hessa's note under the cup.

DAY AFTER TOMORROW.

AFTER MIDDAY.

AWAY FIRST.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

Nothing on the table moved.

At the theatre, two men had become a crowd.

At home, one onion remained one onion.

I did not ask it to go anywhere.
