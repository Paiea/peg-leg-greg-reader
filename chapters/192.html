PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 192

THE SELLER

The dark cloth was gone in the morning.

So was Lyssa.

There was bread.

This was enough continuity for one household.

I ate, put Hessa's paper back under the cup after checking that it still
did not contain a date, and left for East Market Hall early enough that
Rinna could not accuse me of being late.

She accused me of something else.

"Move those."

I had barely entered.

"Good morning."

"Those."

She pointed at the rear benches.

There were six of them.

"Why?"

"Because nobody bought them."

That answered a different question, but I knew better than to ask for
the first one again.

The hall looked wrong.

Not empty. It was too early for empty to mean anything. But the benches
had been laid for a larger house, and the spaces between them made the
room feel stretched. A whole section near the rear wall had no chalk
marks on the ticket board.

Rinna was erasing seat numbers.

"Bad sales?" I asked.

"Thin."

"How thin?"

"Move the benches."

I moved the benches.

Pell arrived while I was on the second one and took the other end
without being asked.

"Closer," Rinna said.

I was grateful for the word.

We carried the bench forward.

"Closer."

Again.

By the fourth bench, the rear of the house had become open floor. Rinna
had us turn the last two sideways against the wall.

"Now what?" Pell asked.

"Nothing."

"Good."

He left.

I stayed.

Rinna looked at me.

"What?"

"You can also leave."

"I was told work."

"Board."

The board had changed.

KING: MAREK / HARA / SELLEN SHOPKEEPER: VEYA / GREG / HARA LETTER BOX:
VEYA / SELLEN / GREG / NESSA SHORT: EVIN / HARA FRONT: RINNA / GREG
REAR: JORI / PELL

I looked at Shopkeeper.

I had not played it since the road.

Not here, anyway.

The part felt old enough that I knew where the first line lived before I
found the pages.

"Both?" I asked.

Rinna nodded.

"Shopkeeper and Witness?"

"Yes."

"Front too?"

"Until house."

"One copper?"

"If you're lucky."

"I've learned not to negotiate with you."

"No, you haven't."

That was true.

The Shopkeeper rehearsal was barely a rehearsal.

Veya wanted the counter moved six inches because the new bench layout
changed the sightline from the left side.

Hara could not find the narrow brown scarf and used a gray one instead.

Teren came in, watched three minutes, and said, "Again from debt."

We did.

The scene was familiar.

Not memorized like a prayer. Better than that. I knew where Veya would
move when she became angry. I knew Hara liked to take half a beat before
the line about interest. I knew the counter edge caught my sleeve if I
stood too close.

I also knew the joke that usually got a laugh.

That knowledge became a problem later.

For now, it was useful.

"You're less annoying," Veya said after the run.

"Thank you."

"That wasn't praise."

"It was comparative."

She considered that.

"Fine."

Everyone had become infected.

The Letter Box needed less work.

I entered.

I looked at people.

The purse existed without becoming the most important object in the
world.

Teren watched once.

"Fine."

I stared at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

He left to argue with Jori about whether The King needed the second
stool.

It did not.

Then it did.

Then Hara needed it for the short piece, so The King lost it again.

The stool survived all of this without opinion.

By midday, Rinna had sold nineteen seats.

I knew because she made me count.

"Twenty," I said.

She tapped the board.

"Nineteen."

"One man paid for two."

"Two seats."

"Then twenty."

"He hasn't chosen the second."

"That's still a seat."

"Not until someone sits in it."

I looked at her.

"This is a terrible accounting system."

"Sell another."

She handed me the ticket roll.

The market outside was busy enough to make the hall's thin sales
insulting.

People passed with baskets, bolts of cloth, fish wrapped in leaves, hot
food, children, dogs, arguments, and absolutely no urgent need to see
theatre.

I stood at the door.

A woman slowed.

"What's playing?"

"The King, The Shopkeeper, The Letter Box, and a short piece."

"Is The King sad?"

"Sometimes."

She kept walking.

Rinna leaned around the door.

"Sell the seat."

"I answered."

"Poorly."

A man stopped.

"Funny?"

"Some of it."

He kept walking.

Rinna closed her eyes.

"What?"

"Do you want them inside?"

"Yes."

"Then tell them why."

"They asked questions."

"Yes."

Another pair approached.

I tried again.

"The Shopkeeper is funny."

Rinna made a small sound behind me.

I ignored her.

"The Letter Box is also funny."

The older of the pair asked, "The King?"

"Less funny."

"Good."

They bought two seats.

I turned.

"See?"

"You found people who wanted less funny."

"I identified the market."

"Go eat."

I ate bread on the rear step.

Pell had an onion and nothing else.

"That's bleak," I said.

"It's an onion."

"Yes."

He bit it.

I moved farther away from him.

Not magically.

Hara came out carrying one shoe.

"Anyone seen the other?"

"No," I said.

Pell shook his head.

She looked at his feet.

"I don't have it," he said.

"I didn't say you did."

"You looked."

Hara went back inside.

Pell frowned.

"People are becoming unreasonable."

I ate my bread.

A few more seats sold.

Not many.

By house, Rinna had twenty-seven people for a room that could take far
more.

She closed the rear completely.

A family tried to sit there.

"Forward," she said.

"We like the back."

"Not today."

The father looked at the empty benches against the wall.

"Why?"

"Better seats."

He accepted this because Rinna said it like a fact.

They moved forward.

Another woman wanted the aisle.

A boy wanted to sit beside the wall.

A tired-looking man said he did not care where he sat and then rejected
two places.

I helped until Jori called me back.

From behind the hanging, the house sounded strange.

Twenty-seven people made more individual noises than a crowd.

One cough belonged to a specific man.

A child whispered and could be heard.

Someone unwrapped something dry and crackling.

A woman cleared her throat three times.

There was no broad murmur to cover any of it.

The King went first.

Marek entered.

The opening line usually got a small laugh.

Nothing.

He continued.

Hara came in.

Nothing.

Sellen's first complaint landed into a silence so clean I could hear a
cart outside.

I looked at Pell.

Pell was checking a strap.

He did not seem concerned.

Onstage, Marek got smaller.

Not quieter exactly.

Closer.

He stopped throwing the lines at the back wall because there was nobody
at the back wall.

Hara did the same.

They played to each other.

The audience watched.

They were not restless.

They simply did not laugh.

Then a man near the front laughed alone at a line I had never heard get
much before.

Nobody joined him.

Marek continued.

At the end, the applause was modest but real.

"Bad?" I whispered when Hara came off.

"No."

"They didn't laugh."

"It's The King."

"They usually laugh more."

Hara was already taking off the wrong brown scarf.

"Then they usually laugh more."

That was all I got.

The Shopkeeper was next.

I put on the coat.

My right palm was almost normal now, but the leather grip stayed. I had
paid for it. It could continue being useful.

Veya adjusted the counter.

"Don't shout."

"I don't shout."

She looked at me.

"I project."

"Don't project at twenty-seven people like they're seventy."

That seemed like a distinction invented to hurt me.

We went on.

The room was visible.

That was the first difference.

With a full house, faces became a mass beyond the first rows. Tonight I
could see the man who had rejected two seats. I could see the child
leaning against his mother's arm. I could see a woman in a yellow
headcloth who looked tired enough to sleep sitting upright.

I could also see the man who laughed alone during The King.

This became important.

The opening worked.

Veya accused me of cheating her brother.

I denied it.

Hara entered with the account.

I said, "That account is honest."

Veya said, "Your handwriting bends around the truth."

Usually a laugh.

Tonight, two people made small sounds.

Not silence.

Almost worse.

We continued.

I was fine.

Then came the debt line.

It was one of mine.

"If your brother wanted mercy, he should have borrowed from a priest."

The lone-laughing man laughed.

A real laugh.

Sharp.

Immediate.

Mine.

The rest of the house gave me a little more.

I felt it.

That was the problem.

My next line was not a joke.

It was supposed to be irritated.

"Debt does not disappear because you dislike the number."

I gave it more.

More voice.

More face.

More Shopkeeper.

The line died.

Not quietly.

It died in a room where I could see every person who had refused to save
it.

Veya looked at me.

Not Greg.

Shopkeeper.

Her expression said I had become embarrassing.

Which worked for the scene.

This was unfair.

She stepped closer.

"Then perhaps the number should learn to be likable."

Small laugh.

She had not pushed.

I hated her.

We continued.

For the next few lines, I could feel myself wanting the room.

I wanted another laugh to prove the dead line had been an accident.

Sellen was not in this piece, so he could not tell me to stop being
stupid.

Veya could.

She did not.

She just played the scene.

Eventually I had to play it with her.

The room came back.

Not much.

Enough.

At the end, the lone man laughed again.

This time I did not try to marry him.

Backstage, Teren was waiting.

"You chased it."

"I didn't."

"You did."

"I said the line."

"At them."

"It was for them."

"Leave them alone."

Veya passed us.

"That's what I said."

"You said don't shout."

"Same thing."

"It is not."

Teren pointed toward the stage.

"Letter Box."

I had no time to defend myself.

Good.

The Letter Box started with a dropped cup.

Not in the script.

Nessa set it on the table badly. It rolled off during Sellen's entrance
and hit the floor.

One woman in the audience laughed.

Sellen looked down.

"Even the crockery is leaving."

That got the best laugh of the day.

Nessa picked up the cup.

"Rent is due from it too."

Another laugh.

I waited in the wing.

The audience had apparently decided pottery was superior to actors.

Fine.

Then the scene settled.

The purse was on the table.

I did not look at it.

This was easier today.

Not easy.

Easier.

"Then let the town hear it," Nessa said.

I entered.

"I was told there was a dispute."

"There is," Veya said.

"There isn't," Sellen said.

Nessa pointed.

"There."

I opened the ledger.

"Good. A dispute."

Yesterday, laugh.

Today, nothing.

I felt the silence.

I did not fix it.

"Names."

They all spoke.

We continued.

The tired woman in the yellow headcloth was watching now.

The child had stopped leaning against his mother.

The man who laughed alone was quiet.

Sellen tried to hand me the box.

"I have not witnessed transfer."

Nothing.

"I'm transferring it."

"I have witnessed an attempt."

A small laugh from somewhere left.

"That's the same thing."

"It is written differently."

Yesterday, good laugh.

Today, one cough.

I wanted to sharpen it.

I did not.

The scene moved.

Veya reached the turn.

"Ask her where she was when the aunt died."

She went still.

The room went still with her.

That part worked.

Not because anyone laughed.

Nobody was supposed to.

The purse became important when Veya let it become important.

The key came out.

The tired woman leaned forward.

Sellen said, "You had it."

Veya said, "I had her purse."

"With the key."

"I didn't know."

"I have not witnessed consent to open the box," I said.

The lone man laughed.

Wrong place.

I nearly looked at him.

I didn't.

Sellen turned on me.

"You have witnessed the key."

"Yes."

"Then open it."

"No."

"Why?"

"I have no authority."

The child laughed.

One bright laugh.

Then his mother did.

Then three more people.

Not huge.

Enough.

Sellen said, "What do you have?"

I looked at the ledger.

"Excellent notes."

The laugh was smaller than yesterday.

It was still there.

I let it go.

At the end, Veya said, "She left us the argument."

Silence.

For half a breath I thought the room had lost her.

Then the tired woman laughed softly.

Someone else did.

The applause came.

Twenty-seven people could still make applause.

It just sounded like twenty-seven people.

Backstage, Sellen took the box from me.

"You were loud in Shopkeeper."

"I know."

"Good."

"Teren already told me."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"I didn't start this conversation."

"You looked conversational."

I walked away.

The short piece was delayed because Hara's missing shoe had finally been
found inside the costume basket under two aprons.

Nobody knew how.

Hara blamed Pell.

Pell said, "I don't even wear that size."

"That isn't a defense."

"It should be."

EvIn missed his first entrance because he was helping move a bench for a
late-arriving couple.

Jori filled the silence by knocking twice from behind the side wall as
though someone in the scene was at the door.

Hara turned toward it.

"Not now."

The audience laughed.

EvIn came in.

The scene survived.

By the end, the house had warmed a little.

Or we had.

I could not tell.

Afterward, Rinna counted coins at the front table.

There were fewer than yesterday.

I knew that without counting.

Pell came up beside me.

"Bad house."

Rinna did not look up.

"They paid."

Pell considered this.

"Good house."

"Go clean."

"Bad Rinna."

"Go."

He went.

I helped collect cups.

There were fewer cups.

Less dirt.

One child had left a length of string tied around the leg of a bench.

I untied it.

The lone-laughing man stopped at the door.

"You were the shopkeeper."

"Yes."

"Funny."

"Thank you."

He left.

I stood there.

Rinna looked up.

"Don't."

"What?"

"Whatever you're about to become."

"I wasn't becoming anything."

"Good."

She gave me one copper.

"Shopkeeper, Witness, front."

"That sounds like at least three copper."

"One."

"I was also benches."

"Do you want less?"

"No."

"You're learning."

Teren came past carrying the ledger.

"Tomorrow's not set."

"Work?"

"Probably."

"Role?"

"Probably."

I waited.

He looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

He left.

Fine.

Outside, the market was nearly done.

I bought an onion because apparently Pell had damaged me.

Then I went home.

Lyssa was there.

The pale wrapping from yesterday was folded on the chair.

The dark cloth was gone.

I put the onion on the table.

She looked at it.

"Why?"

"Food."

"That's one onion."

"It remains one onion."

She looked at me again.

"What happened?"

"The audience hated me."

"All of them?"

"Twenty-seven."

"You counted?"

"Rinna did."

Lyssa cut the onion.

"They paid to hate you?"

"Apparently."

"Good business."

"They didn't laugh."

"At anything?"

"At a cup."

"Was the cup funny?"

"Very."

She put half the onion in the pan.

I told her about The Shopkeeper.

About getting one laugh and trying to get another one too hard.

About Veya making the next line work by not doing that.

Lyssa stirred the onion.

"So they didn't hate you."

"They were quiet."

"That's different."

"I know that now."

"Did they clap?"

"Yes."

"Then stop insulting your customers."

I sat.

Hessa's paper was still under the cup.

AWAY ONLY.

NEAR / FAR.

No date.

I slid it back where it had been without reading it a second time.

Lyssa saw.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

"Why does everyone say that when nothing happens?"

"Because you keep making nothing difficult."

The onion cooked.

Twenty-seven people had watched us.

Some had laughed.

Some had not.

One man had laughed at nearly everything except the parts I expected.

Tomorrow I would probably work.

At some point Hessa would decide how far away to put the paper.

Tonight the onion stayed in the pan.

I left the audience alone.
