PEG-LEG GREG
LIGHTART
CHAPTER 197

THE PETITIONER

My wrist was stiff in the morning.

Not hurt.

Stiff.

There was an important difference, and I spent several seconds proving
it by rotating my hand until Lyssa looked up from tying her hair.

"Stop."

"I'm checking it."

"You've checked it four times."

"That is how you establish repeatability."

"No."

I stopped.

She had already put bread on the table. There was also a narrow strip of
dark blue cloth beside her elbow.

I pointed at it.

"The narrow thing?"

Lyssa looked down.

"Part of it."

"What is it?"

"Binding."

I waited.

She ate.

"That's all?"

"What did you think it was?"

"I didn't know. That's why I asked."

"It's binding for a sleeve edge."

I stared at the cloth.

Three days of mystery had produced sleeve binding.

Lyssa watched my face.

"Disappointed?"

"No."

"You are."

"I had assigned it no importance."

"Good."

"I was still expecting more object."

She folded the strip and put it in her bag.

"Work?"

"Theatre."

"All day?"

"Probably."

"You know when?"

"Morning."

She nodded.

This was progress.

I ate.

The cup remained on the table.

There was no new paper under it.

I knew because the edge of the old note was still visible in exactly the
same place.

I did not lift the cup.

Lyssa noticed.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because you keep checking."

"I didn't check."

"You looked."

"That is different."

She kissed the side of my head on the way past.

"Go to work."

This made it difficult to continue the argument.

Outside, Carrow had already warmed enough that the stone near the market
smelled dry.

A man was selling fried dough from a pan set over coals. I bought one
piece because bread had not entirely convinced me breakfast had
occurred.

The dough was hot, greasy, and folded around chopped greens.

It cost enough that I regretted it for one bite.

Then I stopped regretting it.

This was becoming financially dangerous.

At the next corner two men were arguing about whether a cart had damaged
a drain cover.

The cart was gone.

The drain cover was still there.

Neither man appeared to own either.

Carrow remained well governed.

I reached the hall with grease on two fingers and soap still faintly on
my wrists.

By the time I went inside, the hall removed both improvements.

Pell was sanding the edge of a painted frame.

Dust settled on my sleeve.

I looked at it.

"Morning," he said.

"You've ruined me."

"Already?"

"I was clean."

He looked at my coat.

"No."

"Cleaner."

"Maybe."

The board was short.

KING: SELLEN / HARA / EVIN

PETITION: VEYA / SELLEN / GREG / EVIN

WIDOW: VEYA / NESSA / HARA

SHORT: HARA / EVIN

GREG: PETITIONER / SET

Marek was still absent.

The company had survived the night.

I found this encouraging.

"Petitioner?" I asked.

Rinna was tying a stack of ticket slips with cord.

"Yes."

"Which petitioner?"

She looked at me.

I pointed at the board.

"There are several people petitioning in that piece."

"You're the fence."

"Third Man."

"No."

I looked again.

"Not Third Man?"

"No."

"Then which fence?"

"First petition. Two lines. Then you come back with the drainage
complaint."

I stared at her.

"That man has a drainage complaint?"

"Today."

"Did he yesterday?"

"No."

"Good."

Rinna stopped tying tickets.

"What?"

"I asked."

"Congratulations."

I found Teren near the rear wall with Davin and a door hinge.

The hinge belonged to a narrow painted door that had been removed from
its frame and laid across two benches.

Davin held the old pin between two fingers.

"It bends."

Teren said, "Then don't use it."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good."

I waited.

Teren looked at me.

"Petitioner."

"Apparently."

"First petition. Fence line. Leave. Back after Sellen's tax line.
Drainage."

"How many lines?"

"Four."

"Entrance?"

"Same side as Third Man."

"Coat?"

"Nessa."

"Version?"

Teren looked at me.

"Today."

"Excellent."

He pointed toward the stage.

"Go ask Veya."

This was the entire meeting.

Nessa had three coats on a rail and one coat over her shoulder.

I knew two of them.

The third was Marek's king coat.

Sellen stood with his arms out while Nessa pulled the coat across his
back.

It did not want to be Sellen's coat.

Marek was broader through the shoulders.

Sellen was not.

The fabric gathered beneath the collar and the sleeves hung too low.

Nessa folded one cuff inward.

Sellen said, "That's uneven."

"So are your arms."

"My arms are not uneven."

Nessa looked at me.

"Are his arms uneven?"

"I don't know."

Sellen turned.

"Don't involve him."

"I was invited."

Nessa pinned the inside fold at the shoulder.

Sellen flinched.

"That is me."

"Then stop moving."

"I was stabbed."

"You were touched."

"With metal."

"Hold this."

She handed me a length of narrow cord.

I held it.

Sellen looked down.

"Why does he get the safe job?"

"Because I don't need him."

"That feels insulting."

"It is."

Nessa tied the back of the coat tighter, folded the second cuff, then
stepped away.

Marek's coat now looked less like Marek had left it behind and more like
Sellen had borrowed something expensive from a larger cousin.

"Fine," Nessa said.

Sellen lowered his arms.

"Can I breathe?"

"No."

"Good."

He walked away.

I still held the cord.

"Nessa."

She was already working on another sleeve.

"Nessa."

"What?"

"The cord."

"Rail."

I put it on the rail.

This was how objects migrated.

My petitioner coat was brown, short, and smelled faintly of old smoke.

I had worn it before in something else.

I could not remember what.

That seemed fair. The coat probably could not remember me either.

Veya was at the petition table with the stack of papers.

"Fence," she said.

"I have one."

"Not yours."

"I know."

"Do you?"

I took my place.

The new petitioner was not complicated.

I entered angry about a neighbor moving a boundary post.

Veya, as the official, did not care.

I left.

Later I returned angry about water running from the public lane into my
yard.

Veya cared even less.

This was apparently the entire man.

We ran it once.

"My neighbor moved the boundary post two feet into my garden."

Veya looked at the petition.

"Then move it back."

"I did."

"Why are you here?"

"He moved it again."

"Move it again."

I paused.

Teren said, "Don't wait."

"I thought she might help."

"She isn't going to."

"That seems bad government."

Veya looked at me.

"You're learning."

We continued.

Later I came back.

"The lane drain is pouring into my yard."

Veya said, "Then your garden is watered."

"It has my chair in it."

"Move the chair."

"Where?"

She looked at me.

"Inside."

Teren said, "Good."

That was rehearsal.

I liked the petitioner.

He had problems.

Nobody respected them.

This was familiar.

The rest of the morning was less theatrical.

Davin needed the door held upright while he fitted the new hinge pin.

I started to take the heavier side.

My wrist reminded me about yesterday.

Not pain.

Just a small tightness.

I shifted.

"Pell."

He looked over.

"Other side."

"Why?"

"Guild."

He came over without asking anything else.

We held the door together while Davin drove the pin.

Pell took more weight.

I did not explain.

Davin did not thank either of us.

The door opened.

He closed it.

Opened it.

Closed it.

"Good."

Pell looked at me.

"He tests everything by doors."

"Only things designed for it."

Davin pointed at us.

"Move."

We moved.

At midday Hara arrived with her hair only half pinned and a paper parcel
under one arm.

Rinna said, "You're late."

"I was working."

"Here is work."

"I know."

Hara put the parcel on a bench.

Pell reached toward it.

She slapped his hand without looking.

"Not yours."

"What is it?"

"Food."

"What kind?"

"Mine."

I looked at Sellen.

He looked at me.

This was spreading.

Hara unwrapped two little meat pies.

Pell looked wounded.

"You have two."

"I know."

"That means one is extra."

"No."

"Mathematically."

"No."

He went away.

I ate bread and the last piece of white cheese.

Sellen had nothing.

I watched him not eat.

"Where's your food?"

"Didn't bring any."

"Why?"

"I forgot."

"You forgot food?"

"Apparently."

This was the first truly disturbing thing I had learned about him.

I tore off a piece of bread and held it out.

He looked at it.

"Why?"

"Food."

"I know what bread is."

"Good."

He took it.

"You want money?"

"No."

"Then why are you looking at me?"

"I wasn't."

"You were."

I looked away.

Sellen ate the bread.

After a while he said, "Marek would have brought dried apples."

"That sounds like criticism."

"It is."

"When is he back?"

Sellen shrugged.

"After the baby."

"That's not a time."

"It is to Marek."

Nobody seemed worried.

I was not worried.

Marek had gone north to see his sister have a baby.

The world contained enough dangerous things without making childbirth
travel into one from a distance.

Rinna came through with a chalk stub.

"House is decent."

"How decent?" Pell asked.

"Decent."

"Numbers."

"No."

"That's not a number."

"Work."

He did.

The hall filled slowly through the afternoon.

Not full.

Not thin.

People came in warm from the street and brought dust with them.

A woman argued with Rinna about whether her boy counted as a full seat
if he spent half the show on her lap.

Rinna said yes.

The woman said he was small.

Rinna said the bench did not charge by weight.

The woman paid.

I loved watching professionals.

Before the first piece, I moved a small wooden writing box from beside
the petition table because it was in the path where I entered.

Nessa saw me.

"Put that back."

I stopped.

"It's in my way."

"It's for Widow."

"That's after Petition."

"Yes."

"Why is it here?"

"Because it needs to be."

"For a later piece?"

"Yes."

"Now?"

"Yes."

I put it back.

Then I entered from six inches farther left.

This solved everything.

The King went first.

Sellen wore Marek's coat.

From backstage I could see the folded cuffs.

From the house, nobody cared.

He did not play Marek's king.

I had expected him to, without realizing I expected it.

Marek's king carried irritation like a personal injury. Sellen's seemed
to assume everyone else had become stupid while he was asleep.

The audience liked him.

Hara got the first big laugh.

Sellen got the second.

EvIn dropped a rolled petition, kicked it by accident, then incorporated
the kick into crossing the room.

Nobody died.

I watched until Pell needed the space.

"Move."

I moved.

The Petition came next.

My coat sat wrong at the neck.

I fixed it.

Nessa fixed what I had fixed.

Then I went on.

"My neighbor moved the boundary post two feet into my garden."

Veya barely looked up.

"Then move it back."

"I did."

"Why are you here?"

"He moved it again."

"Move it again."

I did not wait for help.

The audience laughed.

Not a large laugh.

Enough.

I left.

Backstage, Sellen was waiting for his next entrance.

"Your government is terrible."

"Not mine."

"You're in it."

"That is not how government works."

"That explains a lot."

He went on.

Later I returned.

"The lane drain is pouring into my yard."

Veya said, "Then your garden is watered."

"It has my chair in it."

"Move the chair."

"Where?"

"Inside."

A woman near the middle laughed before anyone else did.

Then several people joined.

I left again.

Four lines.

One coat.

No disaster.

I could have done more.

This was not a complaint.

It was simply true.

Then I had to get the coat off because Nessa wanted it for a different
man in Widow.

"Already?"

"Yes."

"I just became attached."

"Don't."

She took it.

The Widow's Room had the writing box I had tried to move.

It turned out to contain three folded letters and a piece of chalk.

None of them mattered to my entrance.

I found this insulting.

During the scene, Veya got the room quiet without asking it.

Nessa sat beside the painted doorway and said almost nothing for long
enough that I forgot I was waiting to carry a bench afterward.

Then Hara crossed the false room, opened the writing box, and found the
wrong letter.

The audience made one small sound together.

That was the strongest moment of the afternoon.

It had nothing to do with me.

Good.

When the scene ended, Pell said, "Bench."

I remembered my job.

I grabbed one end.

My wrist tightened.

I stopped.

Pell looked at me.

"Other hand?"

"Other person."

He called EvIn.

EvIn took my side.

Nobody gave a speech about responsibility.

I carried the lighter stool instead.

This was irritatingly sensible.

The short piece nearly failed because the spoon was missing again.

This time it was not in the Letter Box cup.

It was in Hara's paper parcel.

She stared at it.

Pell said, "You stole your own spoon."

"I did not."

"It is in your food."

"It wasn't."

"It is now."

Hara looked at Nessa.

Nessa said, "Use it."

Hara wiped meat grease off the spoon with a cloth.

"Disgusting."

Pell said, "The audience won't know."

"I will."

"Then act."

Hara threw the cloth at him.

The short happened.

The audience laughed.

At cleanup I found the wooden writing box on the wrong shelf.

I picked it up.

Then I stopped.

"Nessa."

"What?"

"Where?"

She pointed without looking.

I put it there.

This was safer than memory.

Rinna paid me one copper.

"Petitioner. Set."

"Also restraint."

"No."

"I didn't move a box."

"Congratulations."

"I also didn't lift a bench."

"You're negotiating for things you didn't do now?"

"I am expanding the field."

"One copper."

"Fine."

She handed it over.

I pocketed it.

Outside, the afternoon heat had broken.

Not into coolness.

Carrow did not believe in gifts.

But the sun had moved behind the roofs and the stone no longer reflected
it directly into my face.

I stopped at a food stall and bought two little fried cakes with chopped
onion and something green inside.

The woman wrapped them in paper.

"Two?" she asked.

"Yes."

"For you?"

"One is."

She looked at me.

"Then two."

This was unnecessary.

I carried them home.

Lyssa was not there.

I put the food on the table.

The cup remained where it had been.

Still no new note.

I washed.

The wrist felt better after cool water.

The shoulder was already ordinary again.

I sat for a while.

Not because I was practicing peace.

Because sitting existed.

Lyssa came in after dark enough that the window had become a black
square.

She put her bag down.

"Food?"

"One is yours."

She looked at the two wrapped cakes.

"Which?"

"I don't know."

"Then how do you know?"

"I bought two."

She took the larger one.

"That was mine."

"You didn't know."

"I know now."

We ate.

After a few bites she asked, "Theatre?"

"Petitioner."

"Again?"

"Different one."

"What did you want?"

"A fence."

"What did you get?"

"Water."

She waited.

"I had two complaints."

"Did anyone fix them?"

"No."

"Sounds official."

"It was."

I told her Marek's coat did not fit Sellen.

I told her Nessa made it fit anyway.

I told her Hara had stolen a spoon from herself.

I did not tell her about the writing box until she asked why I was
smiling at nothing.

"I moved a box."

"Why?"

"It was in my way."

"Was it yours?"

"No."

"Did you know where it belonged?"

"No."

"Good system."

"I put it back."

"Excellent."

"Then later I asked where it belonged instead of deciding."

Lyssa nodded solemnly.

"Growth."

"Don't."

She ate.

After a while she looked at my wrist.

"Better?"

"Yes."

"Actually?"

"Yes."

I rotated it once.

She looked at me.

I stopped.

"Still no paper?"

"No."

"From Hessa?"

"No."

"I meant you."

I looked at her.

"No."

"Good."

There it was again.

I ate the last bite of my cake.

Twenty-seven draws.

Twenty-three shapings.

Twenty-three external attempts.

Near had worked twice yesterday.

Far had not worked cleanly.

Today I had moved no paper.

I had moved a stool, a coat, part of a door, one petition, one writing
box incorrectly, and one writing box correctly.

None of those counted.

This was probably healthy.
