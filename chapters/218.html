CHAPTER 218

THE PAYER

I moved one copper into the little cloth fold Lyssa used for buttons.

The fold had no buttons in it.

This made it available for promotion.

"What is that?" Lyssa asked.

"Debt."

"That is a copper."

"It can become debt."

"It is already money."

"You're making this difficult."

"No. Antonius did."

Fair.

The Copper Kettle had paid me two copper. One stayed with Present Greg. One belonged, provisionally, to Future Greg, who had debts and apparently required administration.

I sent Antonius a note asking what I currently owed and whether cash could reduce it. I did not mention Bren. Antonius had asked for the other half of Bren's name. I still had a returned cart pin, a verbal threat, and no surname.

Those were not the same thing.

By third bell, the theatre had acquired another piece.

A woman from the Copper Kettle came to the door. She had served the same thin man in a brown coat twice. This time he had asked whether the players got paid, how much, and whether the money came back to the hall.

That was no longer just curiosity about a cart.

It was also still not Bren.

"He wanted the money," I said after she left.

Rinna looked at me.

"He asked about the money."

"Fine."

"Keep that difference."

I hated when everyone became Hessa.

Rinna's response was boring enough to work. Private-job pay stayed private. We stopped carrying obvious client money where we did not need to carry it. We did not tell strangers what work paid. We did not send me into the market asking thin men in brown coats whether they were extortionists.

"I'm not an actor today," I said.

"Excellent. Then you're even less qualified."

Pell laughed.

I threw a rag at him.

Theatre resumed because theatre was rude that way.

That evening a Vale runner brought Antonius's answer. Cash was accepted. The balance required the actual ledgers. He would give me twenty minutes at the west office the next day.

"No labor credit for reviewing your own debt," the runner said.

"Cruel."

"Accurate."

She also carried a note from Antonius advising me not to send one copper across Carrow every time I felt responsible.

Rinna found a bench suddenly fascinating.

The next morning, the copper was still in the fold.

I checked.

This was unnecessary.

I checked anyway.

Lyssa had one arm inside a dark green sleeve that belonged to a customer.

"You think it escaped?"

"No."

"Then why are you checking?"

"I have an appointment."

"With the copper?"

"Antonius."

"Does Antonius know about the copper?"

"No."

"Then the copper is having a more stressful morning than Antonius."

Probably.

The west Vale office occupied the front corner of a warehouse where six barrels could be physically present and still, according to a clerk outside, administratively absent.

Commerce.

Antonius had three ledgers open when I entered.

I stopped in the doorway.

"You've made my debt plural."

"No."

"Then why are there three books?"

"Because you made your debt inconvenient."

He pointed to the guest chair.

One leg was slightly shorter than the others. I rocked once.

"Don't," Antonius said.

"Fix your chair."

"It's a guest chair."

"That explains your business relationships."

My name was on the first page.

GREGORY.

"Greg."

"The debt is Gregory's."

"I am Gregory."

"Then we've resolved something."

The old account was real in a way memory had not been. Original loan. Later advances. Fees. Gambling. Materials. Credits for work. Terms I remembered making while treating future success like a currency.

I had apparently been extremely optimistic.

"Why isn't there one number?"

"There is."

"Where?"

"At the end."

"Then why are we doing this?"

"Because you asked what you owe."

I looked at him.

He was right in the most annoying possible way.

He showed me the current balance.

Large.

Not impossible.

Those were different things.

The recent work credits were there too. Small lines. Real lines.

I touched one.

"You actually wrote it."

Antonius stared at me.

"What did you think credit meant?"

"I knew conceptually."

"Excellent. My favorite kind of repayment."

I took the copper from the button fold and put it on his table.

He looked at it.

Then at me.

"One."

"Yes."

"This is not a strategy."

"No. It's a copper."

His mouth moved slightly.

Accounting amusement.

He accepted it anyway.

The clerk made the entry and handed me a narrow receipt strip.

One copper.

Date.

Account.

The balance changed by one copper.

Not emotionally.

Mathematically.

That was enough.

"What should I pay?" I asked.

"Whatever does not make you useless."

"Very precise."

"Your income varies. You have household expenses. Theatre may disappear. Private work may increase. Work for me can reduce the account when I have work that suits you. Why would I invent a fixed payment that becomes trivial when you earn more or cripples you when you earn less?"

I stared at him.

"You're refusing to budget for me."

"I would rather die."

Good.

"Pay surplus. Work when useful. Do not starve yourself to improve my ledger."

"Lyssa said that."

"She is protecting you."

"You're not?"

"I'm protecting the ledger."

Distinct.

Useful.

When I left, the debt had not become small.

It had become finite.

Impossible things did not require plans.

Finite things did.

I reached the theatre before third bell with the receipt in my pocket.

Rinna looked up from the board.

"Vale?"

"Still rich."

"You?"

"Less indebted."

"How much?"

"One copper."

She stopped.

"You went there to pay one copper?"

"I went there to learn the account."

"And paid one."

"Yes."

"Did he laugh?"

"Not visibly."

"Strong restraint."

I had a four-line Servant role and a dull brown coat. Normal work. I liked normal.

We were setting the King table when the front door opened.

Jori had not latched it yet.

The close-haired man in the heavy coat stepped inside.

Same spokesman as the first night.

Same left glove.

Bren.

Probably Bren.

One man waited outside.

Bren looked at me first.

"Greg."

Rinna stepped forward.

"You're talking to me."

His eyes moved to her, then back to me.

"Wedding went well?"

Nobody answered.

That landed harder than a threat.

Copper Kettle.

He knew.

Rinna said, "What do you want?"

"The same thing."

"We answered."

"You didn't."

"We did."

Bren looked around the hall as if counting what could be counted.

"The share is still small."

Rinna waited.

"One copper," he said.

I almost laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because I had just put one copper on Antonius Vale's table.

Antonius had loaned me value. Recorded it. Recorded my work. Recorded my payment.

Bren wanted the same coin for showing up twice and stealing a pin.

The coin did not care.

I did.

"No," Rinna said.

No speech.

Bren looked at her.

"One."

"No."

"Cheaper than a wheel."

Davin's face changed.

Rinna's did not.

"You returned the pin."

Bren smiled slightly.

"Did I?"

Not a confession.

Enough to confirm he knew exactly what she meant.

He looked at me.

"You agree?"

Rinna said, "You're talking to me."

I felt the old impulse arrive.

Say something strange.

Use the glove. Use Mason's Cut. Use Guild. Make him invent a larger Greg.

That bluff had bought us time once.

Now it had become part of the problem.

"I don't own the theatre," I said.

Bren studied me.

True.

Boring.

Useful.

"Didn't stop you before."

"No."

Rinna said, "It stops now."

Good.

Bren tried the price again. Rinna gave him the same answer.

His jaw tightened.

"Next time won't be one."

Rinna said, "Then this was the cheap time to say no."

He looked at her.

Then at me.

"Greg."

I said nothing.

He waited.

I still said nothing.

That felt harder than the first bluff.

At the door he stopped.

"Good crowd at the Kettle."

Then he left.

No attack.

No broken lamp.

Just street noise swallowing two men.

Rinna shut the door.

Jori latched it.

Pell said, "I liked him better as a runner."

"He was never the runner," Rinna said.

"You know what I mean."

"I do. That's why I'm correcting you."

Teren came from the rear.

"Are we canceling?"

Rinna looked at the door, then the board.

"No."

"Then set."

That was theatre.

We set.

The audience knew none of what had happened before house. I wore the brown coat, carried the folded cloth, said four lines, and did not improve any of them.

I did the job.

Afterward Rinna paid me one copper.

I looked at it.

She saw.

"Keep that one."

"Why?"

"You paid Vale?"

"Yes."

"This one feeds you."

That was not an order.

It sounded like one.

I put it away.

At home, Lyssa read the Vale receipt first.

"One copper."

"Yes."

"How much do you owe?"

I told her what the ledger had shown me.

Her expression changed.

"That's a lot."

"Yes."

"Payable?"

I thought.

"Yes."

Not quickly. Not cleanly. Not without food and oil and broken things and days when theatre paid nothing.

But yes.

Then I told her Bren had come back.

"Actually Bren?"

"The man from the first night. Same close hair. Same left glove. Still no surname."

"What did he want?"

"One copper."

Lyssa looked at the receipt.

Then at me.

"You paid Vale one."

"Yes."

"And Bren wanted one."

"Yes."

"Different."

"Very."

"Did you do the thing again?"

"What thing?"

She looked at me.

"The stupid impressive thing."

"No."

That hurt.

"Good."

"I told him I don't own the theatre."

"True."

"Painfully."

She unwrapped two small rolls and a piece of smoked meat a customer's mother had given her.

I stared.

"Paid and fed?"

"Apparently."

"Your work is improving."

"My customers are."

We ate.

The Vale receipt stayed on the table.

One copper less debt.

One copper Bren had not received.

Same amount.

Different arithmetic.
