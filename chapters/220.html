CHAPTER 220

THE LANDLORD

The Vale receipt went into Lyssa's needle box because she knew where things were and I apparently did not.

At breakfast I tried not to count money. This lasted almost a full bite.

One copper to Vale. One copper Bren had wanted. One copper Rinna had paid me after the show.

"You're doing the numbers again," Lyssa said.

"I'm eating."

"Poorly."

She left with a customer's sleeve before the woman's shoulder could change overnight.

At third bell, Olin's delivery boy arrived without the two tins of oil and wicks Rinna had ordered.

Rinna stared at his empty hands.

"Where are they?"

"Master Olin said not to bring them."

"Why?"

The boy looked like he wished adulthood had happened to somebody else.

"Someone told him your money was spoken for. That there was trouble over it."

Nobody said Bren.

I thought Bren.

That mattered less.

Jori had enough oil for one piece if we did not believe in long endings. Rinna told the boy she was coming to Olin and pointed at me.

"You're coming."

"Why?"

"You heard him."

Better reason.

Olin's shop smelled like every spark in Carrow had become personally interested. He admitted a thin man in a brown coat had asked whether he supplied us and warned him that other people had a claim on the theatre's money.

"We pay cash," Rinna said.

"I know."

"Have I failed to pay you?"

"No."

"Then sell me my oil."

Olin rubbed his beard.

"I don't need trouble for two tins."

There it was.

He did not need to believe the man. He only needed uncertainty to cost more than our business was worth.

Rinna paid the original price. Then she bought one extra small tin so she would not have to discover tomorrow whether someone else had decided her money belonged to him.

She looked at me.

"How much do you have?"

"This feels like a trap."

"Enough for the small tin?"

"Yes."

"Buy it. Company expense."

"Recorded?"

She stared.

"Vale ruined you in one meeting."

"Record it."

I put one copper on Olin's counter.

Not the Vale copper. That one was already gone.

This one had been theatre pay for less than a day. Now it was lamp oil.

Money became objects very quickly when allowed.

The shows went on. That mattered more than I expected. The audience knew nothing about brown coats or claims on money. They wanted a King, a Letter Box, and enough light to see both.

We gave them that.

The next morning I bought bread and a cake of soap on the way in.

Bread felt defensible.

Soap felt optional until I remembered Lyssa saying, "You work in a theatre full of people."

She had not said I smelled. She had built a structure in which I could reach the intended conclusion.

Theatre was corrupting everyone.

Olin's boy was waiting outside the hall.

"You're the oil man," he said.

"I carried one tin."

"Oil man."

"I have done other things."

He shrugged.

Inside, Rinna was fighting with Davin about handbills. The boy waited until she stopped and then told the story badly, which was fair because he was thirteen and adults kept sending him across Carrow with their problems.

The brown-coat man had returned to Olin.

This time he asked whether Rinna bought the oil anyway. Whether she paid cash. Whether the theatre was still doing shows.

Then he asked, "Does the woman keep the money at the hall?"

The room changed.

Rinna asked only what mattered.

Olin had answered nothing. The man had not threatened him. He had not said Bren. He had not asked about carts or Greg.

The boy looked at me.

"Oil man?"

I closed my eyes.

"Greg," Rinna said.

"No."

She wrote the questions down exactly enough that the boy corrected one word.

I liked him.

Then he added that he had seen the man later, walking toward Mason's Cut.

My hands tightened on the crutch grips.

Mason's Cut.

Grit on Bren's boot had once been nothing more than a detail I used to make a bluff larger.

Now the same place-name had returned from someone who had no reason to care about my theories.

I wanted distance, entrance, direction, companions, boots, hands, everything.

Rinna asked one question.

"Did you see him enter Mason's Cut?"

"No. I turned for South Steps."

She wrote: BOY SAW HIM WALKING TOWARD MASON'S CUT. DID NOT SEE HIM ENTER.

Good evidence was deeply unsatisfying.

I stopped the boy before he left.

Rinna looked at me.

"I'm not following him."

"Excellent."

"I was going to give the boy bread."

She looked at my bag.

"Your bread?"

I reconsidered generosity and tore off a piece.

The boy accepted it.

"Thank you, oil man."

"Leave."

Rinna handed me three pages.

LANDLORD.

Nine lines.

"That's it?" I asked.

"No. I'm talking to two more people we actually do business with. You are being a landlord."

"He asked where you keep the money."

"Yes."

"Mason's Cut."

"He walked toward it."

I hated her accuracy.

The Cousin was a stupid play and knew it. Marek pretended to be his own wealthy cousin to avoid paying people. My job was mostly to want rent.

I had experience.

Teren ran my entrance.

Marek smiled broadly.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

Teren stopped me.

"You're waiting for it to be funny."

"It is one word."

"Exactly. Want the rent."

Again.

Marek smiled.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

Better.

I interrupted the smile instead of presenting the joke.

"Of course. My cousin has it."

"Then I want your cousin."

"He is away."

"Then I want the rent."

Teren pointed at me.

"Keep that."

"It is written."

"I know."

Excellent profession.

We rehearsed. Rinna made her calls. The hall stayed a hall.

By the end of the day nobody had produced a surname, an organization, or proof that the brown-coat man worked for Bren.

We had something smaller and more useful.

He had tried to make a supplier uncertain. Olin sold to us anyway.

He came back looking for payment information. Olin gave him none.

He was seen walking toward Mason's Cut. Not entering it.

That was not winning.

It was work.

For now, work was action.
