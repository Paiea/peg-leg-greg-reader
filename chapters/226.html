CHAPTER 226

THE REFERENCE

The note was not under the cup.

It was pinned to the door.

This was an improvement because Lyssa could not accuse Hessa of learning domestic habits.

It was also worse because I saw it before breakfast.

AFTER MIDDAY.

SAME ROOM.

EAT.

Below that, in smaller writing:

DO NOT ARRIVE TIRED AND CALL IT DATA.

I read the line twice.

Lyssa came up behind me carrying the green garment from yesterday and a small paper packet between her teeth. She took the packet out.

"What did you do?"

"Nothing."

"Then why is she already angry?"

"Preventive medicine."

"She isn't your physician."

"Apparently she is my fatigue accountant."

Lyssa took the note, read it, and handed it back.

"She's right."

"Traitor."

"You came home yesterday with your right shoulder tight."

I looked at her.

"It was not tight."

"You rolled it four times while telling me about cups."

"That was theatrical emphasis."

"You were sitting."

"Subtle theatrical emphasis."

She gave me the expression people used when they had decided I was not worth correcting before breakfast.

The brown sleeve was gone. So were the buttons she had carried home last night. The green garment remained, now opened across the table with chalk marks along one side seam and a piece of pale thread laid over the cloth like a question.

"Brown cuff finished?" I asked.

"Delivered."

"Accepted?"

"She looked at it."

"That is not what I asked."

"She paid."

"Even better."

Lyssa folded one edge of the green cloth inward and pinned it.

"She also said the first cuff had been prettier."

I stared at her.

"Violence is wrong."

"Usually."

"Good boundary."

"I charged her twice. I feel fine."

I ate bread.

Then more bread because two women had apparently established a governing coalition around my food intake.

The morning gave me ordinary work.

That helped.

I went to the theatre early enough to catch Jori carrying a bundle of narrow boards through the side door and late enough that he had already decided I was useful.

"Sit."

"Good morning."

"Sit and hold these."

"Warmer."

He put three boards across my lap while I sat on the low platform near the rear wall. They were light, painted on one side, and smelled faintly of old dust and newer glue.

"What are they?"

"Window trim."

"For the tax play?"

"For three plays."

"Efficient."

"Cheap."

"Same thing if you are Rinna."

Jori looked toward the front of the building.

"Do not say that where she can hear."

"She already knows."

He took the first board, checked the edge against another, and scraped a raised bead of dried glue with a knife.

The repaired cart stood outside the rear door with one wheel visible through the opening. It had already brought in a chest and two folded frames that morning. The new brace remained a brace. Nobody gathered around it. Nobody whispered. Nobody discovered a second crack shaped like conspiracy.

Boring repairs were among civilization's greatest achievements.

Rinna passed the doorway once carrying a ledger.

I raised my eyebrows.

She shook her head before I asked.

No new word.

Good.

I did not ask again.

By late morning I had held boards, sorted four lengths of cord, carried one stack of pages badly enough that Nessa took half of them from me, and spent fifteen minutes sitting in the house while Teren ran a scene I was not in.

No role.

No crisis.

Marek tried to open the STAGE ONLY box.

Nessa said his name from across the room without turning around.

His hand stopped above the lid.

"How?" he asked.

"Practice," I said.

He looked at me.

"You are becoming unpleasant."

"I work here."

At midday I left before anyone could make that statement more binding.

I bought a meat pie from a stall near the Guild because Hessa had written EAT in the handwriting of someone prepared to verify.

I ate the pie on a bench.

Then I drank water.

Then I sat for another few minutes because arriving at a controlled magic session with theatre still in my shoulders seemed exactly like the sort of mistake Hessa would preserve forever in writing.

My right hand felt normal.

Thumb normal.

Wrist normal.

Forearm used, not strained.

Shoulder mildly worked from the previous day and this morning, but not tight now.

Right leg ordinary.

Residual limb comfortable.

Phantom foot present and quiet once I looked for it.

I hated knowing my own inventory this well.

The same room was waiting.

Same narrow table.

Same board.

Same glass shield.

Same paper-fin target.

Same paper-fin control.

Same chair.

Same chalk.

Same intermediate mark.

Same far mark.

Hessa had preserved the entire little kingdom of disappointment.

She was standing beside the table comparing two pages of notes.

"You came."

"You pinned a threat to my door."

"It said eat."

"Exactly."

She looked at my hands.

"Sit."

I put the crutches where she indicated.

The chair went exactly on its floor marks. My right foot went where it had gone before. Hips square. Right forearm near the same edge. Shoulder down.

Hessa checked all of it.

"Work this morning?"

"Theatre."

"Doing what?"

"Holding boards. Pages. Sitting. Being prevented from carrying too much paper."

"Shoulder?"

"Worked yesterday. Fine now."

"That is not the same answer."

I sighed.

"Yesterday after the show I noticed some ordinary shoulder fatigue. No pain. No weakness. No tingling. Fine this morning. Worked lightly today."

She wrote.

"Right hand?"

"Normal."

"Thumb?"

"Normal."

"Wrist?"

"Normal."

"Forearm?"

"Normal."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

"Phantom?"

"Present. Quiet."

"Food?"

"Breakfast. Meat pie."

"Water?"

"Yes."

She slid a cup toward me anyway.

Institutional distrust.

I drank.

When she finished writing, I looked at the two chalk positions.

"Now can I know the question?"

"Yes."

That surprised me enough that I almost asked whether she was feeling well.

Hessa set one page down.

"The current record suggests a difference between intermediate and far under this apparatus."

"Intermediate behaves better."

"More observed response at intermediate."

"That is what I said."

"No."

I waited.

She waited longer.

I surrendered.

"What is the question?"

"Whether the apparent difference repeats in the same session when the order is decided before you begin."

I looked at the board.

"That's it?"

"Yes."

"No new distance?"

"No."

"Far does not move farther?"

"No."

"Near?"

"No."

"Different paper?"

"No."

"Larger paper?"

"No."

"Toward?"

"No."

"Anything interesting?"

"Possibly evidence."

I leaned back.

"Your definition is hostile."

"It survives."

She turned one of the note pages so I could see four lines written before I had entered.

FAR

INTERMEDIATE

FAR

INTERMEDIATE

I read them.

"You picked the order already."

"Yes."

"Why far first?"

"Because intermediate has more often been the easier condition. I do not want it always receiving you before any external attempt."

"Fresh Greg is a resource."

"Unfortunately."

I looked at her.

"That was almost humor."

"No."

"Write it down."

She did not.

The order stayed.

Far. Intermediate. Far. Intermediate.

No choosing the next position because the previous one worked.

No adding an attempt because one failed.

No changing direction.

Away only.

Same target. Same control. Same shield. Same board. Same body position except the marked distance changes.

"How many attempts?" I asked.

"Four if your body and the apparatus remain usable."

"And if attempt forty is contaminated?"

"We record that."

"Repeat it?"

"Not automatically."

I disliked the phrase not automatically because it left hope alive without feeding it.

Hessa continued.

"If target and control move together, the attempt does not support a target-specific response. If the apparatus is disturbed, we stop and assess. If your shoulder, forearm, hand, posture, or residual limb changes enough to affect interpretation, we stop."

"Even if that ruins the beautiful pattern?"

"There is no beautiful pattern."

"You wrote four lines."

"That is an order."

"Very aesthetic."

She ignored me.

The supervised minimal draw came first.

Thirty-one previous successes had made it familiar.

Familiar was not safe.

I knew that because Hessa had trained the sentence into me through repetition and because being bored by danger was apparently another human skill.

I settled.

She gave the instruction.

I drew the smallest amount I could manage.

Clean sensation.

Contained.

No strange spread into the thumb. No catch at the wrist. No involuntary shoulder movement.

"Return."

I returned.

Hessa watched.

Nothing lingered.

"Count."

Thirty-two successful supervised minimal draws.

She waited before shaping.

The deliberate shaping setup did not change.

No new form. No strength experiment. No attempt to become clever because I had been left alone with four chapters of imagination.

I began.

The first few seconds felt clean.

Then I noticed my right shoulder wanting to rise.

Not actually rising yet.

Wanting to.

I lowered it before it moved.

Hessa said, "Continue."

That was worse than praise because it meant she had seen it too.

I completed the sequence, held what she asked for, then returned on instruction.

She checked my arm.

"Any discomfort?"

"No."

"Shoulder?"

"Normal."

She wrote.

"Count it."

Twenty-eight supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Then she put the glass between me and the paper.

The room became serious through furniture.

Attempt thirty-eight.

Far.

Away only.

Hessa checked target and control against their marks.

She checked the glass edge.

She checked the door.

A cart passed outside and she waited until the vibration had faded.

Then she looked at me.

"Begin when ready."

I settled.

Far first meant I had no successful intermediate movement in my head from the same session.

I liked that less than I expected.

I drew under supervision.

Shaped.

Held away.

The paper fin remained still.

I kept my eyes where Hessa wanted them, not chasing the tiny shadows at the edge.

Nothing.

The control remained still.

At the end of the interval Hessa said, "Return."

I returned.

She inspected both fins.

"No observed response."

Attempt thirty-eight.

Far.

Away only.

No observed response.

Control unchanged.

I exhaled.

"Excellent start."

Hessa did not look up.

"Do you want evidence or reassurance?"

"Can I choose reassurance now and evidence later?"

"No."

"Then evidence."

She gave me rest.

Not much, but enough that the first attempt could stop occupying my forearm.

She checked hand, wrist, forearm, shoulder.

All normal.

Then she moved me to the intermediate mark.

Same chair geometry as the established setup.

Same board.

Same glass.

The paper looked larger because distance was rude that way.

Attempt thirty-nine.

Intermediate.

Away only.

Hessa checked the target.

Checked the control.

"Begin when ready."

I drew.

Shaped.

Away.

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the target fin shifted.

Small.

Clean enough that I did not have to negotiate with myself about whether I had imagined it.

The leading edge went away from me. The paper rotated slightly, but less than the far responses had. Net movement remained away.

The control stayed where it was.

"Return."

I returned immediately.

Hessa crouched beside the board.

She inspected the marks.

I tried not to smile like an idiot at paper.

"Observed?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Classification?"

"Small response. Net displacement away. Minor rotation. Control unchanged."

"Intermediate remains charming."

"Intermediate is a marked position."

"Cold."

She wrote.

Attempt thirty-nine.

Intermediate.

Observed small response.

Net away.

Control unchanged.

One far failure.

One intermediate response.

That was already the pattern I wanted.

Which meant it was time to distrust myself.

Hessa made me rest longer.

My forearm had a faint worked warmth now, similar to after the second far attempt in the last session but milder.

She checked it.

"Pain?"

"No."

"Tingling?"

"No."

"Weakness?"

"No."

"Shoulder?"

"Fine."

"Hand?"

"Fine."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

She waited until the warmth had mostly faded.

Then she moved the chair back to far.

Attempt forty.

Far.

Away only.

I knew what I wanted now.

That was dangerous.

I also knew that if far failed again, the comparison would become neat enough to tempt me into saying stupid things.

That was apparently dangerous too.

There were many ways to be scientifically annoying.

Hessa checked both fins.

"Begin."

I drew.

Shaped.

Held.

Nothing.

Several seconds.

Still nothing.

Then the control fin twitched.

I froze.

The target moved almost at the same moment.

Not much.

Both turned.

The target shifted away a fraction.

The control rotated in place enough that I could see its edge change.

Hessa said, "Return."

I returned.

She was already moving around the table.

I opened my mouth.

She raised one finger.

I closed it.

She checked the door.

Closed.

The glass.

Stable.

The board.

Stable.

Then both paper fins.

She looked at the control mark longer than the target.

"Both moved," I said.

"Yes."

"So contaminated."

"Potentially."

"Draft?"

"Possibly."

"Vibration?"

"Possibly."

"Me?"

"Not established."

I watched her measure the tiny changes against the marks.

The temptation was immediate and embarrassing.

The target had moved away.

Far.

Again.

I wanted it.

The control had moved too.

I wanted that less.

Hessa stood.

"Attempt forty is not evidence of a target-specific response."

"Does it count as an external attempt?"

"Yes."

"Observed response?"

"Observed movement in target and control."

"Magic?"

"Not classifiable as target-specific under this apparatus."

I rubbed my jaw.

"So I do not get to keep the far movement."

"You get to keep exactly what happened."

Hessa wrote for a while.

Attempt forty.

Far.

Target movement observed.

Control movement observed.

Contaminated for target-specific interpretation.

Cause not established.

No target-specific response counted.

I stared at the control fin.

"That little bastard."

"It is doing its job."

"I dislike competent controls."

"That is why we use them."

The session had become less pretty.

Good.

Probably.

Hessa checked my forearm before moving me.

The warmth had returned, but not more strongly.

Shoulder normal.

Hand normal.

Thumb normal.

Posture still acceptable.

Right leg had begun to feel stiff from sitting. That mattered mostly because shifting it changed the rest of me.

I told her.

She had me stand.

Not for an extra attempt.

For a break.

I took the crutches, moved around the room once, settled the right knee, adjusted the residual limb, then sat again only when she told me.

She rechecked the chair position from the floor marks.

"Still four?" I asked.

"If baseline is acceptable."

"Attempt forty was contaminated."

"Yes."

"So we could replace it."

"No."

"That sounded immediate."

"It was."

"I had prepared an argument."

"Use it elsewhere."

She checked my shoulder.

"Baseline?"

"Yes."

"Forearm?"

"Worked. Not warm now."

"Right leg?"

"Better."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

"Phantom?"

I checked.

"Still quiet."

She looked at the paper fins, reset them to their marks, then moved me to intermediate.

Fourth line.

Predeclared.

No rescue attempt.

Attempt forty-one.

Intermediate.

Away only.

This was the one that could ruin the neat story.

I found that I wanted it to.

Not because I wanted failure.

Because after the control moved at far, any clean result now felt more useful than a satisfying one.

That was either scientific maturity or Stockholm syndrome.

Hessa said, "Begin when ready."

I drew.

Shaped.

Away.

Nothing at first.

The target fin trembled.

I almost reacted.

Then it stopped.

The control did not move.

I held until the interval ended.

At the last part of the interval, the target slid.

Small.

Straighter than attempt thirty-nine.

Away from me.

No obvious rotation this time.

The control remained still.

"Return."

I returned.

Hessa inspected both.

I waited.

"Observed small response," she said. "Net away. Control unchanged."

I let out a breath.

"Two intermediate."

"Today."

"Both observed."

"Today."

"Far zero."

"No."

I frowned.

She tapped attempt forty.

"Far had movement in target and control. It is not a clean no-response attempt."

"Fine. One far no response, one contaminated."

"Yes."

"Two intermediate responses."

"Yes."

I looked at the board.

That was less simple than two against zero.

It was also better evidence.

Hessa wrote the session summary.

I drank water and tried to read upside down.

She turned the page away from me.

"Hostile."

"You can ask."

"What does it say?"

She looked at the record.

"Under the current apparatus, in today's predeclared alternating comparison, both intermediate attempts produced observed target response with unchanged control."

I waited.

"Both far attempts?"

"One produced no observed response with unchanged control. One produced movement in both target and control and is contaminated for target-specific interpretation."

"So the difference repeated."

"The current apparent difference remains supported."

"That sounds like yes wearing armor."

"It is the answer."

"Intermediate is more reliable."

"Under the current apparatus, intermediate continues to produce observed response more consistently than far on the evidence we have."

I liked that sentence.

Too much.

Hessa continued before I could improve it into something false.

"Far reliability is not established. Attempt forty does not strengthen far-response evidence. It strengthens the reason for keeping a control."

"Rude."

"Useful."

"No range."

"No range."

"Not generalized."

"Not generalized."

"Far still has the two earlier clean responses."

"Yes."

"Across sessions."

"Yes."

"And today does not erase those."

"No."

"Today also does not make far better."

"Correct."

I leaned back.

"So your secret next question was whether the difference survives being asked properly."

"Approximately."

"Why not tell me last time?"

"Because the session was over."

"That is deeply unsatisfying."

"Yes."

There was almost a smile.

Possibly a muscle failure.

Hessa checked my right hand.

Then wrist.

Forearm.

Shoulder.

Nothing concerning.

She checked whether the stiffness in my right leg had returned.

It had not.

The residual limb remained comfortable.

The phantom foot remained present and quiet.

The session ended at thirty-two successful supervised minimal draws, twenty-eight supervised deliberate shaping attempts, and forty-one supervised external-effect attempts.

Four new external attempts.

Thirty-eight: far, no observed response, control unchanged.

Thirty-nine: intermediate, small observed response, net away, control unchanged.

Forty: far, target and control both moved, contaminated for target-specific interpretation.

Forty-one: intermediate, small observed response, net away, control unchanged.

The record was ugly enough to trust.

I reached for my crutches.

"Restrictions?"

"Unchanged."

"Independent draw?"

"No."

"Independent shaping?"

"No."

"Paper?"

"No."

"Coins?"

"No."

"Thread?"

"No."

"Larger objects?"

"No."

"External testing?"

"No."

"Practice?"

"No."

"Barrier?"

"No."

"Theatrical magic?"

Hessa looked at me.

"I enjoy checking."

"No."

"Loopholes?"

"No."

"Very thorough."

"You asked."

I stood.

My shoulders felt like I had spent part of the day using crutches and part of it trying to make paper move without visibly trying to make paper move.

Which was accurate.

At the door I stopped.

"Next question?"

Hessa was already filing the page.

"Not decided."

That was new.

"You don't have one?"

"I have several."

"That sounds worse."

"It means the record changed."

I looked back at the control fin.

"Because forty contaminated."

"Partly."

"Because intermediate repeated twice."

"Partly."

"Are you going to tell me the several?"

"No."

"Professional cruelty."

"Eat."

There she was.

Outside, the afternoon had become colder.

I bought a small onion on the way home because Lyssa had not asked me to, which made the purchase feel suspiciously voluntary.

The theatre was still working when I passed it.

I could hear someone onstage through the open side door.

I did not go in.

One full performance yesterday and half a day of boards today had satisfied my current obligation to become furniture.

At home, Lyssa had moved the green garment off the table and replaced it with two sleeves that did not match.

I stopped inside the door.

"Problem?"

"No."

"They are different."

"Yes."

"That seems like a problem."

"They belong to different garments."

I considered this.

"Your profession is intentionally confusing."

"How was Hessa?"

I put the onion down.

"Paper moved."

Lyssa looked at me.

"Specific paper?"

"Excellent. She has infected you."

"Did you hurt yourself?"

"No."

"Rules changed?"

"No."

"Then good."

I sat across from her.

"One of the controls moved."

She paused with the needle halfway through cloth.

"Isn't that bad?"

"Yes."

"Because something else moved it?"

"Maybe. We don't know."

"And the paper you wanted moved too?"

"At the same time."

"So you don't count it."

I stared at her.

"What?"

"I live with you."

That explained too much.

I told her the rest while she sewed.

Far failed once cleanly.

Far produced target and control movement once, so Hessa refused to treat it as target-specific evidence.

Intermediate moved twice with the control still.

Same room.

Same setup.

Same direction.

Order chosen before I started.

Lyssa listened until I reached the part where I called attempt forty ruined.

Then she shook her head.

"It wasn't ruined."

"Hessa would like you."

"It told you something."

"That controls are little bastards."

"That too."

She tied off the thread.

I looked at the two different sleeves.

"You know, that sounds like a larger lesson."

"I am sewing."

Still excellent boundary.

I got up to cut bread.

My right shoulder complained mildly when I reached too far for the knife.

Ordinary work.

I changed the angle instead of turning it into evidence.

Outside, somebody argued with a cart driver.

Upstairs, a child ran hard enough across a floor to make our hanging cup tremble against its hook.

I looked at it.

Then laughed.

Lyssa glanced over.

"What?"

"Nothing."

That was not true.

It was a control.

Which was worse.
