CHAPTER 229

THE ROAD ONE

Lyssa was still in bed when I woke.

That had happened before. It had simply become less common than waking to a room she had already rearranged and a note telling me to eat something.

For a moment I stayed still.

Not because I thought she was ill. She was breathing normally, one arm folded beneath the pillow, hair spread toward the wall. She had come home tired. Tired people occasionally slept.

I was becoming dangerously reasonable.

The blue-gray cloth remained folded on the table where I had left it alone the night before. Beside it sat half a loaf, a knife, and two onions I had apparently bought under professional supervision.

I ate bread and one of the roasted beans that had escaped its paper twist onto the shelf.

When I stood, my right hand felt normal again. No tenderness where the flower spray had rested against the palm. Shoulder fine. Right leg fine. Residual limb comfortable. Left crutch tip still boring.

Jori expected me in the morning.

I knew when he got there.

That was irritatingly close to having a schedule.

I was pulling on my coat when Lyssa spoke behind me.

"Blue-gray."

I turned.

Her eyes were still closed.

"Good morning."

"Take it to Marra."

I looked at the table.

Then at her.

"Now?"

"After windows."

That opened one eye.

"You remember?"

"Jori remembers."

"Same thing."

She closed it again.

I picked up the folded cloth. "What am I telling Marra?"

"Nothing."

"Strong assignment."

"Give it to her."

"What is it?"

"Cloth."

"We established that yesterday."

"Then you're ready."

I looked for a note.

There was none.

"Does she know it's coming?"

"Yes."

That was apparently the entire system.

Lyssa had already started falling back asleep.

I folded the cloth once more so it fit inside my coat without dragging against a crutch handle. It made one side of me look as though I had stolen a very flat loaf.

"Anything else?"

"No."

"Food?"

"For you?"

"For either of us."

She opened both eyes now.

"There's bread."

"I noticed."

"Then no."

A pause.

"You're going north?"

"Later."

"Marra?"

"No."

That was useful only because I had asked the wrong question.

I accepted the answer.

At the door she said, "Greg."

I looked back.

"Don't lose it."

"Finally, confidence."

She pulled the blanket higher.

I left.

The theatre was open when I arrived, but only through the side door.

I did not try the front.

That would have been how I used to arrive.

The window trim was already on the low platform near Jori's workbench. Three long painted lengths, two shorter pieces, a pot of glue, and a rag stiff enough to qualify as lumber.

Jori was scraping an old edge with the back of a knife.

He looked up once.

"Long ones."

I put my crutches where they normally went beside the wall and sat.

No explanation.

No demonstration.

I picked up the first long piece and turned the painted face away from my knees.

Jori noticed.

He said nothing.

That was better than being told I had done it right.

For the next half hour I held trim, passed pieces when he asked, and rubbed old glue from one edge with the stiff rag. The work had survived several plays and several repairs. One strip carried cream paint on the front, green along one side, and a line of blue near the back where somebody had apparently once decided the audience could see through wood.

Jori needed the second long piece turned twice because one edge had lifted. The first time I shifted it toward him too quickly and the end bumped the wall.

He looked at the mark.

I looked at the mark.

Cream paint had transferred onto older green paint.

"Historic preservation," I said.

"Hold it still."

That was the correction.

I held it still.

He worked the edge, pressed it flat, then gave me the piece back without checking whether I had understood. I had.

My right palm stayed ordinary. When the long board began pulling awkwardly against my wrist, I put its far end on the platform instead of pretending one good morning had changed leverage. Jori adjusted his side without comment.

Davin came back once, saw us, and said, "Still windows."

"Apparently permanent," I said.

Jori said, "Go find the small nails."

Davin left.

I looked after him.

"Does he know which?"

"Yes."

That was more trust than the instruction sounded like.

"Ship?" I asked.

Jori glanced over.

"Magistrate's room."

"Why blue?"

"Ask the person who painted it."

"Dead?"

"Probably not."

Less interesting.

Davin arrived carrying a short strip of trim and a cup.

He put the cup on Jori's bench.

Jori moved it away from the glue without looking.

Davin held up the strip.

"This one?"

"Too short."

"It reaches."

"Hold it there."

Davin held it against the frame leaning by the wall.

It reached from one corner almost to the other.

Almost.

Davin said, "Paint covers some."

Jori looked at him.

"Paint does not extend wood."

"I know that."

"Good."

Davin lowered the strip.

"What do I do with it?"

"Keep it."

"For what?"

"Something shorter."

Davin considered the entire future of theatre.

"Do we have something shorter?"

"We always do eventually."

That satisfied him.

He leaned the strip beside the workbench, drank from the cup, and disappeared toward the stage.

Nobody asked my opinion.

Excellent morning.

We finished the long pieces before first bell had become meaningfully late. Jori stacked them by size. I knew which side of the rear passage they went on and carried the shortest one myself by pinning it lightly between my right forearm and ribs while using both crutches.

The longer ones stayed with Jori.

Competence did not make my hands multiply.

Nessa passed us in the rear passage carrying a folded black cloth and a basket with no flowers in it.

She looked at me.

"No role."

"I did not ask."

"You were going to."

"I was going to ask about the flowers."

"Repaired."

"Good."

She kept walking.

That ended my relationship with yesterday's funeral.

At the side door Jori said, "Tomorrow maybe."

"Windows?"

"Maybe."

"Specific."

He had already turned back toward the bench.

I took that as dismissal.

Outside, Carrow was clear and bright enough to make every wet patch from yesterday look deliberate. A woman shook a rug from an upstairs window. A porter cursed at a crate whose bottom had failed in the street. Two children were drawing squares in chalk between the stones while a dog systematically ruined the game.

I had a destination.

I did not have to think about the route.

That was the strange part.

From the theatre I went south two streets, crossed near the bread ovens, cut through the lane with the cracked water trough, then west past Sen's red door. I did not check whether I had confused it with the green one.

I had once needed that distinction explained.

Now Marra's green door appeared where it was supposed to while I was thinking about lunch.

Time had done something to the city behind my back.

The door stood open.

Inside, the room was already working.

Not sewing.

Working.

That distinction had become visible to me even when I understood almost none of what the work meant.

Cloth lay across one table. A half-finished garment occupied a chair. Somebody I did not know stood near the wall holding one sleeve out while Marra looked at the shoulder. Jessa was near the rear with a bundle under one arm and two pieces of something pale over the other.

Nobody stopped because I entered.

The room had changed since the first times I had come through it.

Not in any way I could have listed usefully.

The green door was the same. The tables were still crowded. Cloth still migrated between chairs and arms in ways that made ownership difficult from three feet away.

But I knew where not to stand.

A narrow passage along the right wall stayed clear because people used it. The first time I had been here I had occupied it while trying to understand who I was supposed to speak to. Now I moved left without thinking, stopped beside a table nobody was cutting on, and kept both crutch tips out of the path between Marra and the woman by the wall.

Jessa passed me with her bundle and did not slow down.

Months ago somebody would have had to tell me I was in the way.

This time nobody did.

I did not congratulate myself.

I had learned enough theatre to know that a route working was not an applause line.

Marra glanced toward me.

"The road one."

I stopped.

It had been long enough that I almost looked behind myself.

"Still?"

"You came by road."

"There are streets."

She held out one hand without leaving the woman by the wall.

I took the blue-gray cloth from inside my coat.

"Lyssa."

"I know."

Of course she did.

I gave it to her.

Marra put it on the corner of the worktable and returned immediately to the shoulder she had been looking at.

That was my delivery.

No receipt.

No speech.

No explanation of what the cloth would become.

I remained standing for a second because I had apparently expected the city to reward successful transport with information.

Jessa looked over from the back.

"Marked?"

I looked at the folded cloth.

"I was told to give it to Marra."

Jessa crossed the room, lifted one corner of the fold, and found something I had not noticed. Two short chalk strokes sat near the edge.

"Fine."

She let it fall.

I looked at her.

"What does that mean?"

"Marked."

"I gathered."

She had already turned away.

Marra said, "Don't teach him."

"I wasn't."

"Good."

I had missed these people.

Possibly because they treated my confusion as a condition of the room rather than an emergency.

The woman by the wall lowered her arm. Marra adjusted the cloth at the shoulder, said something too quiet for me to hear, and the woman nodded.

Then another customer came through the green door.

She did not look at me.

"Lyssa?"

"North side," Marra said.

"Today?"

"Not back here."

The woman shifted a wrapped parcel from one arm to the other.

"Tell her tomorrow afternoon is fine."

Marra said, "She knows."

The customer nodded.

That was it.

No introduction.

No explanation.

She left.

I looked at Marra.

Marra did not look at me.

"Do not ask."

"I wasn't."

Jessa made a sound that suggested neither of us believed me.

She picked up her bundle.

"West?"

Marra said, "Already."

Jessa nodded once and went out through the same door the customer had used.

I watched her leave.

I had no idea what had already gone west.

This seemed healthy.

Months ago I might have followed the sentence until somebody explained it.

Now I had learned that other people's work often survived my ignorance.

Marra finally looked at me.

"Done?"

"Apparently."

"Good."

"Lyssa said you would know."

"I did."

"This is very efficient when nobody tells me anything."

"You carried cloth."

"Successfully."

She looked toward my coat.

"Did you?"

I checked.

The cloth was no longer there.

"Yes."

"Then go eat."

I pointed at her.

"Hessa has spread."

"I don't know Hessa."

"Better life."

Marra had already turned back to the woman with the sleeve.

My part in the room was over.

I left.

Lunch cost money.

This remained rude.

I bought bread from a stall near the west market and a small piece of cheese from a woman who cut it with a wire and weighed it in her hand before naming a price. I paid without thinking about Antonius until after the coin had left me.

Then I thought about him.

The debt still existed.

So did cheese.

Both survived.

I ate sitting on the low wall beside a courtyard where three laundresses were arguing about whose line had been moved. None of them asked me to judge.

Carrow had become generous with these small mercies.

By the time I finished, I had nowhere I was required to be.

The theatre did not expect me back.

Vale had not summoned me.

Hessa had not pinned anything to a door, cup, wall, sleeve, forehead, or other available surface.

Lyssa was somewhere north.

Marra had the blue-gray cloth.

For the first time all day, my labor had failed to produce another labor.

I went home.

The table was empty.

Not completely.

Bread knife.

One onion.

The paper from the roasted beans, flattened now because I had used most of them.

But no cloth.

No pins.

No chalk.

No customer's coat occupying my chair.

I sat in my own chair and discovered that the room looked larger when Lyssa's profession was elsewhere.

Not better.

Larger.

There was an important difference.

I considered repairing my shirt cuff again.

Then I looked at the work I had already done.

The loose thread had become two loose threads.

I chose restraint.

Nobody congratulated me.

I washed, cut the remaining bread, and put the cheese where Lyssa would see it rather than where I would accidentally eat all of it while waiting.

Then I stopped waiting.

I had pages from an older theatre role in my coat. I read them for a while, not because I expected the play, but because I had started carrying pages long enough that old ones appeared in pockets like disease.

The role had five lines.

I remembered four.

That seemed fair.

Late afternoon reached the room without drama.

A cart passed outside.

Somebody argued downstairs.

A child knocked on the wrong door, apologized, then knocked on the next one.

I did not inspect the hanging cup.

Progress maintained.

Lyssa came home before dark.

She carried a loaf under one arm and nothing else.

I looked at the loaf.

She looked at the cheese.

"We both bought food."

"Terrible coordination."

"Could be worse."

"How?"

"We could both have bought onions."

She saw the remaining onion on the table.

"Never mind."

I moved my chair so she could sit across from me.

For a moment neither of us mentioned work.

She cut the new loaf.

I cut the cheese.

We ate the old bread first because civilization had rules.

After several bites she said, "Marra got it."

"I know."

"I know you know."

I looked at her.

"Then why are we doing this?"

"Conversation."

"Unfamiliar structure."

She smiled.

"Jessa found me."

There it was.

I sat back.

"I carried the cloth across Carrow."

"Yes."

"I delivered it successfully."

"Yes."

"And before I could report my triumph, your network closed the loop without me."

Lyssa tore bread.

"I don't have a network."

"Jessa found you."

"Jessa knows where I go."

"Marra knew the cloth was coming."

"Because I told her."

"A customer came in asking for you."

Lyssa looked up.

"What customer?"

"I don't know."

"Useful."

"She said tomorrow afternoon was fine."

Lyssa chewed once.

"Good."

"You already knew."

"Yes."

I pointed at her with the cheese.

"This is exactly what I mean."

"What?"

I tried to find the sentence.

The problem was that every sentence made it sound larger and more organized than the room at Marra's had felt.

Not a system.

Not a business network with arrows and departments.

People.

Routes.

Work.

A woman asking for Lyssa.

Jessa going west for something I did not understand.

Marra knowing why two chalk marks mattered.

My entire contribution fitting inside my coat.

I lowered the cheese.

"Nothing."

Lyssa nodded.

That was probably safer.

She looked at my cuff.

Her expression changed.

I followed her eyes.

"Maintenance."

"You made it worse."

"Temporary repair."

"Temporary damage."

"It held through windows."

"That is not the same thing."

"Jori did not complain."

"Jori works with wood."

"Transferable authority."

"No."

I smiled.

She did not reach for the cuff.

Good.

Instead she ate more cheese.

"Windows?"

"Still windows."

"Important?"

"Apparently permanent."

She nodded as though this matched her understanding of theatre.

I asked, "North side?"

"Work."

That was enough.

We kept eating.

No cloth came out.

No customer knocked.

No one needed the table for fitting, cutting, pinning, writing, sorting, or proving anything.

Halfway through supper I realized we were both using the whole table without negotiating for it.

Lyssa had put her elbows down.

I had the bread near the center instead of guarding one narrow corner from thread.

It felt extravagant.

I did not mention that either.

Tomorrow the table might disappear under cloth again.

Jori might still have windows.

Marra might send something back.

Jessa might already know about it.

Hessa might choose one of her several questions.

Bren might remain absent.

Antonius's ledger would continue being correct whether I looked at it or not.

Tonight Lyssa pushed the cheese toward me.

I pushed the better half back.

She took it.

No argument.

The table held supper.

For once, that was its only job.
