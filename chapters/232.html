CHAPTER 232

THE COUNTERSIGN

The black coat was still on the chair when I woke.

That made it my first obligation.

Not debt.

Not magic.

Not even breakfast, although breakfast argued.

The coat belonged to the theatre, and I had brought it home because apparently being trusted with clothing meant eventually becoming responsible for remembering clothing.

Lyssa was already gone.

The brown cloth remained near the wall.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My cuff had three loose threads.

Same failure.

The room had achieved continuity without me.

I ate bread, drank water, put on my own coat, then folded the theatre coat over my left forearm.

That lasted until I picked up the crutches.

I looked at the coat.

The coat looked expensive enough to resent being dragged through Carrow.

I folded it smaller and tucked it under my right arm instead.

That made the first six steps annoying.

By the bottom of the stairs I had found a better arrangement.

Carrow was cool and damp from night rain. Water sat in the broken places between stones and made every cart wheel choose between mud and argument. A delivery boy ran past me with three covered baskets stacked high enough that he could not see anyone below shoulder level.

I moved.

He did not.

Good system.

The theatre side door was open.

I went in, returned the coat to Nessa, and discovered that my entire theatrical importance for the morning consisted of not losing it.

She checked the hem.

Not me.

The hem.

"Fine."

"I also survived."

"I saw."

"You weren't there when I walked home."

"The coat was."

She hung it.

Behind her, yesterday's magistrate room had already become a merchant's office.

The blue-trimmed window stayed.

Desk turned.

Witness rail gone.

A tall shelf had appeared where Hara had surrendered two feet of drainage responsibility to justice.

Davin was standing on a stool holding the shelf while Jori looked at its feet.

"Left."

Davin moved it left.

"Other left."

Davin looked down.

"You have created too many."

Jori pointed.

Davin moved the shelf.

I watched long enough to see it stop wobbling.

Nobody handed me anything.

That was probably a sign.

Rinna crossed the aisle with a stack of folded notices.

"Working today?"

"Apparently not here."

"Tomorrow maybe."

That was not a call.

It was also not nothing.

I nodded.

She kept moving.

I left before somebody discovered a stool that needed carrying.

Outside, I stood under the theatre awning and considered the day.

I had one copper from yesterday.

I also had a debt that did not become smaller through admiration.

Vale's office was not far.

Outgoing bundles happened most days when needed.

That did not mean there were bundles today.

It did mean I knew where the door was.

Good enough.

I went.

The front room at Antonius Vale's office had acquired two people before I arrived.

One was an older man with a red scarf who was arguing quietly about a charge.

The other was a woman holding a folded receipt between two fingers like it had personally insulted her.

Neither looked happy enough to be staff.

I waited near the wall.

The ink-fingered clerk saw me after finishing with the woman.

"Bundles aren't ready."

"All right."

He looked at me.

I looked at him.

That had been the entire plan.

He glanced toward the rear room.

"Can you read small?"

"Usually."

"That isn't an answer."

"Yes."

"Sit."

Work.

A chair appeared because another clerk pushed one away from a table with his foot.

I moved it myself.

The task was not bundles.

A stack of narrow account slips sat beside a thicker book opened flat under a stone weight. Each slip had a house mark, a name, a date, an amount, and one or more short marks along the lower edge. The book carried the same information in rows, except the rows had more space and worse handwriting.

The ink-fingered clerk put one slip beside the book.

"Returned payment acknowledgments."

I waited.

He tapped the slip.

"Runner brings this back after money is received. We match it to the account line. If name, house, date, and amount agree, you put a small cross here."

He showed me a blank margin beside the row.

"Then?"

"Then I check your cross."

"Efficient."

"It is if you stop talking."

He put another slip down.

"If anything disagrees, no cross. Turn the slip sideways."

"Which disagreement matters?"

"Any."

"Spelling?"

"If you can tell it's the same name, no."

"Date?"

"Yes."

"Amount?"

He looked at me.

"Especially amount."

"House mark?"

"Yes."

"Runner mark?"

"No."

I pointed to one of the lower marks.

"That?"

"Runner."

Another.

"Received by."

Another.

"Late."

"Does late matter?"

"Not for matching."

I nodded.

Four things.

Name.

House.

Date.

Amount.

Everything else could be interesting somewhere else.

I had learned enough recently to distrust interesting.

He gave me six slips.

"Do those."

I started.

The first matched.

Name close enough.

House mark exact.

Date exact.

Amount exact.

Cross.

Second matched.

Third had a date one day later than the book.

I turned it sideways.

Fourth had the right name and date but a different house mark.

Sideways.

Fifth matched.

Sixth had the same amount written two different ways because one number in the book had been corrected over itself.

I stared at it.

The clerk noticed.

"Problem?"

"Maybe."

He came over.

I showed him.

The slip said seven.

The book had once said one thing and now said something that could have been seven if the writer had been attacked during the upper stroke.

The clerk looked at the line above.

Then the line below.

Then the account name.

"Match."

"How?"

"Correction mark."

He pointed to a tiny hook beside the number.

I had thought it was an ink scratch.

"That means the corrected figure is accepted?"

"Here, yes."

"Everywhere?"

"No."

Good.

Of course not.

He took my sideways date slip.

"Right."

Then the house mark.

"Right."

Then my uncertain sixth.

"You should have turned this."

"You just said it matches."

"I know. You didn't."

I looked at him.

He tapped the hook.

"You didn't know the correction mark."

"So uncertainty goes sideways too."

"Yes."

That had not been in the first explanation.

Apparently it had not needed to be until I found some.

He gave me twelve more.

The work was boring in a way I liked.

Not pleasant exactly.

Contained.

The slip either agreed with the line or it did not.

When I knew, cross.

When I did not, sideways.

The difficulty was keeping my eyes from following every mark.

Some accounts had little notes in the book.

Some had lines through old figures.

Some had initials.

One had a dark dot that reminded me of the transferred collection mark from the bundle work.

I did not investigate it.

It was not one of my four things.

Progress.

After twenty slips my right leg wanted a different angle.

I moved my foot.

After thirty, my shoulders wanted me to stop leaning over the book like a man trying to enter it.

I sat back.

The clerk noticed that too.

He noticed everything that cost him time.

"Break if you need one."

"I moved."

"That isn't a break."

"I'm aware."

"Are you?"

I took a break.

I stood with both crutches and walked to the front water jug. The older man in the red scarf was gone. The insulted-receipt woman was also gone. A new man stood at the counter asking whether Antonius was in.

He was told no.

Antonius walked through the rear doorway while the clerk was saying it.

The man at the counter looked at him.

Antonius looked at the clerk.

The clerk did not change expression.

"Not for him," he said.

Antonius kept walking.

The man at the counter reconsidered several things about language.

I drank water.

When I returned, the ink-fingered clerk had added another stack.

"Encouraging."

"You came for work."

"I was hoping for the amount where work becomes decorative."

"Wrong office."

I sat.

The next batch included a slip with the right name, right house, right date, and wrong amount.

Not close.

Not a corrected number.

Wrong.

Sideways.

Another had the right amount and date but the house mark was missing one stroke.

I turned it sideways.

The clerk later checked it and put it back in the match pile.

"Same house."

"Missing stroke."

"Runner drew it badly."

"How do you know?"

He pointed to the name.

Then to the account line.

Then to a second tiny mark beside the house symbol.

"Rear court."

I had seen the tiny mark.

I had not known what it meant.

"That makes the house mark enough?"

"With the name."

"So house marks can be approximate."

"No."

I waited.

He waited.

"That one can."

"Because of the rear court mark."

"Yes."

"Which I don't know."

"Now you do."

I turned the next questionable slip sideways anyway.

He smiled.

Not kindly.

"Learning."

"Slowly."

"Safer."

That sounded like Hessa.

I refused to think about why.

By midday, I had matched enough acknowledgments that the first book closed and a second replaced it.

The second book was older.

The paper had gone soft at the corners. The ink changed color halfway down some pages. House marks were drawn with more confidence and less consistency.

The clerk put his hand on it before I started.

"Older accounts. Don't assume the same mark means the same thing."

I looked at him.

"Why would anyone design that?"

"No one designed it."

There it was.

The explanation for most systems.

He opened to a page.

"This district changed marks after the lane numbers changed."

"When?"

"Before you."

"Useful date."

"It is to me."

He showed me two similar symbols.

"This one is old east court. This one is current east court."

"They're the same."

"No."

He pointed.

One had a short line inside the angle.

I leaned closer.

"That's hostile."

"That's bookkeeping."

"Same field."

He gave me eight slips from the older set.

I went slower.

Two matched.

One had an old mark on the slip and a new mark in the book.

I turned it sideways.

One had a name I could barely read.

Sideways.

One matched everything except the amount was one copper lower.

Sideways.

The clerk checked that one twice.

Then he took it to the experienced clerk at the far table.

They spoke quietly.

The experienced clerk came over, read the slip, read the line, and turned back three pages.

He found an earlier entry.

"Partial."

The ink-fingered clerk frowned.

"Not marked."

"No."

They looked at the returned slip again.

I stayed out of it.

Eventually the experienced clerk took a pencil and made a small notation beside the account line.

"Runner collected the remainder. Earlier payment wasn't carried forward."

The ink-fingered clerk looked at me.

"Sideways was right."

"I enjoy being right through refusal."

"Don't get used to it."

The mistake was theirs.

Not dramatic.

One earlier payment had failed to carry into the current line, so the returned amount looked short when it was actually the remainder.

If I had crossed it, someone would probably have found the problem later.

Probably.

That was the thing about offices.

A great deal of civilization seemed to be one person making a mark so another person could discover whether the first person had made the correct mark.

The experienced clerk fixed the earlier line.

The slip returned to the match pile.

Nobody applauded.

I liked that too.

Lunch was bread with a piece of hard cheese I bought from a woman outside.

I ate on the low stone edge beside the office because the chair had become an enemy through repetition.

A cart rolled past carrying bundled reeds.

Two boys argued over whose turn it was to pull a smaller handcart.

A dog slept under it while they argued.

Neither boy had noticed.

I considered warning them.

The dog opened one eye.

It had made its own decisions.

I ate my cheese.

When I went back inside, Antonius was at the rear table.

He had a stack of papers to his left and nothing to his right.

The nothing was shrinking.

He looked at me once.

"Useful?"

The ink-fingered clerk answered.

"Mostly."

I said, "Cruel."

Antonius looked at the matched stack.

"Mostly is billable."

"To whom?"

"Not you."

Good answer.

I returned to the book.

The afternoon became more complicated because the acknowledgments were not all from the same district.

The clerk separated them before handing them to me.

I learned three marks.

Forgot one.

Asked again.

He told me again without pretending I had not already asked.

That was a kind of mercy.

I made one actual mistake.

A woman's surname on the slip had an extra letter.

The house, date, and amount matched.

I crossed it.

The clerk uncrossed it.

"Wrong account."

"Same house."

"Two sisters."

He turned the book slightly and showed me another line three entries down.

Same house mark.

Almost same name.

Same date.

Different amount.

The returned slip matched the lower line.

I had matched the first familiar-looking name instead of checking the full page.

"Right."

"Don't search until you find something that fits."

"I wasn't."

He looked at my cross.

"I was."

"Yes."

I moved the slip.

The mistake had not gone anywhere.

That was why he checked my crosses.

I appreciated the system more when it was protecting everyone from me.

For the rest of the stack, I read the full name first.

Not the shape of it.

The letters.

Late in the afternoon, Antonius came over.

Not to inspect me.

He wanted the book.

The clerk handed it to him.

Antonius looked at the completed pages.

"How many?"

The clerk gave him a number.

I did not count along.

Antonius looked at the sideways stack.

"Problems?"

"Three real. Two unclear. One ours."

"Resolved?"

"Four. Two waiting."

Antonius nodded.

Then he looked at me.

"You came without being sent."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I considered saying debt.

That was true.

It was also obvious.

"Rinna didn't need me."

"Today."

"Today."

He glanced at my crutches.

"Any trouble with the new tip?"

"No."

"Good."

He took the book.

That was apparently the entire medical examination.

I approved.

"Tomorrow?" I asked.

Antonius looked at the clerk.

The clerk said, "Maybe bundles. Maybe nothing."

Antonius looked back at me.

"Then maybe."

No job.

No schedule.

No promise.

Also no dismissal.

I was getting very good at employment that existed only when the room happened to contain something I could do.

Antonius recorded the day's credit before I left.

I saw the line added.

He turned the book enough for me to read the balance.

I did.

Then he closed it.

Still there.

Smaller than before.

Not small.

Outside, the light had gone yellow against the upper walls.

I had spent most of the day sitting and still felt worked.

Different work.

Eyes.

Back.

Right leg from holding one position too long.

Hands from paper, not weight.

My shoulders were better than yesterday.

My patience had suffered more.

I stopped at the market and bought onions.

The same woman from before looked at me.

"Faster today?"

"I know how many."

"Growth."

"Two."

She gave me three.

I looked at them.

She looked at me.

"That is three."

"You're improving."

"At what?"

"Counting before you stand here."

I paid for three onions.

Apparently recognition had economic consequences.

On the way home I passed the theatre.

I did not go in.

The side door was open.

Someone inside shouted for a rope.

Someone else shouted that the rope was already there.

Then someone shouted, "The other rope."

Theatre continued successfully without judicial supervision.

At home, Lyssa was there.

The brown cloth was still near the wall.

Three pins.

Same fold.

Lyssa sat at the table with a dark sleeve turned inside out across her lap.

Different work.

She looked at the onions.

"Three?"

"I was given an opportunity."

"You bought too many."

"I was manipulated."

"By onions?"

"Their representative."

She held out a hand.

I gave her one.

She inspected it.

"Fine."

"Thank you."

"Not you."

Of course.

I put the other two down.

My cuff still had three loose threads.

Lyssa saw me look at them.

She did not.

Good.

I sat across from her.

"Vale?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Work?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"Matching returned payment slips to account lines."

She waited.

"I was wrong once."

"Only once?"

"One confirmed time."

"Better."

"That is not supportive."

"It is accurate."

She pulled a needle through the dark sleeve.

I told her about the two sisters with nearly the same name.

She laughed at exactly the part where the clerk showed me my own cross.

Not the bookkeeping.

My humiliation.

Reasonable.

"Did you get paid?"

"Credit."

She nodded.

That was enough.

No balance.

No lecture.

No plan.

I picked up one onion and rolled it beneath my palm.

Lyssa looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You look like you're testing it."

I stopped.

"I am not."

"Good."

I moved the onion to the middle of the table.

Farther from me.

She watched that.

Then went back to the sleeve.

I ate the last of the bread while she worked.

After a while she said, "Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

"Theatre?"

"Maybe."

"Vale?"

"Maybe."

She looked up.

"Busy."

"Extremely."

Neither maybe belonged to me yet.

That was all right.

The onion stayed exactly where I put it.

So did I.
