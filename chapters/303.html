CHAPTER 303

THE DRY BENCH

The problem with a good evening was that eventually you had to wash it off.

Not emotionally.

Physically.

I woke with fried oil still faintly in my hair, lampwing dust apparently capable of surviving several hours on my coat, and the unmistakable knowledge that I had walked enough streets in two days to become part of the street.

Cala noticed before I finished breakfast.

She looked at me.

Then at the basin.

Then back at me.

"No."

I put down my bread.

"No what?"

"Not in that basin."

"I have used that basin."

"Hands."

"Face."

"Also hands."

"Strong record."

She pointed toward the door.

"Bath."

I looked down at myself.

I had bathed since losing the leg.

Obviously.

Mostly at home, carefully, with water carried in, cloths, a stool, and the sort of planning that turned cleanliness into a minor military operation.

Carrow had public bathhouses.

I knew this.

I had walked past several.

Knowing a place existed and deciding to put your naked body inside it were different categories of information.

Lyssa was at the table with her hair tied up and the brown cuff nowhere visible.

I did not ask where it had gone.

I had survived almost twenty-four hours without interrogating that garment.

Growth continued.

She looked at me.

"You smell."

"Cruel."

"True."

"More cruel."

Tavi was eating something soft from a bowl and contributed, "Greg bath."

The household had reached consensus.

I finished breakfast.

"Which one?"

Cala said, "North Steps."

I knew the place.

Large tiled building tucked below the old uphill road, two courtyards, public steam, wash rooms, separate hotter pool, laundry side entrance.

Not expensive by the look of it.

Not elegant either.

Good.

"How much?"

"One bit wash. Towel extra if you need."

I had one private bit upstairs.

I stared toward the ceiling.

Cala watched me.

"House pays bath."

That changed the question.

"Why?"

"Because you live here."

Simple.

I disliked how often simple answers survived me.

She took two small bits from a household cup and set them beside my bowl.

"One entry. One cloth."

"I have cloth."

"You have a square."

"It is efficient."

"It is a square."

Lyssa said, "Take cloth."

Betrayal remained communal.

I accepted the two household bits.

My private money stayed upstairs.

Three copper.

One bit.

Untouched.

The shoulder bag stayed home too.

There was nothing in it I wanted near public water.

Charcoal especially had a gift for becoming more interesting when wet.

I took only the two bits, a dry change of shirt, my belt, and a folded length of household cloth Cala considered large enough to meet civilization's minimum standard.

My crutches came because the alternative was ambitious.

The morning was clear.

The lampwing flight had mostly vanished with daylight.

A few pale insects rested high under eaves where the shade stayed cool, but people had uncovered charm lamps again. Blue-white sign beads returned to normal brightness. Nobody gathered in the streets to commemorate the restoration of commercial visibility.

Carrow survived another seasonal inconvenience.

I kept walking.

North Steps bathhouse announced itself with steam escaping from three narrow roof vents.

The building was older than the businesses around it.

Dark stone lower walls.

Pale tile above.

Two arched doors, one marked with a painted red cup, the other with a blue line.

I knew the broad bathhouse conventions.

The blue line led to washing rooms.

The red cup marked hotter communal soak and steam.

A smaller side door had a painted bundle of shirts.

Laundry.

No mystery.

Inside, a woman behind a stone counter looked at my crutches, then at me.

"Wash?"

"Yes."

"Hot room?"

"Probably not."

"One bit wash. Cloth?"

"I brought one."

She pointed at the folded household cloth under my arm.

"That's cloth."

Validation.

Cala would be unbearable.

I set one household bit on the counter.

The woman pushed the second bit back toward me.

I looked down.

"I was given two."

"Then keep one."

"Strong business model."

She did not care.

"Dry bench?"

I paused.

"Yes."

She reached below the counter and handed me a small clay token with a square cut through its center.

Not numbered.

Just shape.

"Third room. Left wall."

That was all.

No speech about my leg.

No examination.

No question about how long it had been gone.

Just dry bench.

I liked her immediately and hoped never to learn her name.

A narrow hall led past changing alcoves.

The floor changed underfoot before the wet rooms.

Not visibly at first.

Same pale tile.

Then my crutch tip landed and held with a faint resistance I recognized.

Grip wash.

Common bathhouse and kitchen treatment.

Not sticky.

Not rough.

The tile simply refused the first fraction of sideways slide under wet rubber and bare feet.

I had known versions of it in military infirmaries, guild wash rooms, alchemist floors, and one monastery where the monks had apparently decided falling naked was an unacceptable spiritual practice.

The local treatment looked old.

Faint green seams appeared between every fourth tile when steam drifted low.

Maintenance lines, probably.

I did not stop to investigate.

I was trying to become less street.

The third wash room had six low stools around a long stone trough fed by three bronze spouts.

Two men were already there.

One older, heavy through the shoulders, washing his hair.

One thin and younger than me, aggressively scrubbing one foot as if it had committed fraud.

Neither looked up for more than a second.

The left wall held the dry bench.

I understood why the counter woman had named it separately.

Most benches were stone.

This one was wood raised on four pale ceramic feet, positioned beyond the direct splash line, with two upright slots built into one end.

Crutch slots.

Or cane slots.

Probably both.

The top was wide enough to sit and change without balancing clothing on a wet floor.

A small brass line ran under the front edge.

No glow.

No obvious charm effect.

The bench was simply dry.

Very dry.

Even with steam in the room.

I set my crutches into the upright slots.

They stood without sliding.

Useful.

I sat.

Right foot planted.

Left knee clear.

Then I discovered the next problem.

Boot.

Taking off one boot while seated was easy.

Taking off one boot while keeping clean clothes off a damp floor and not knocking over two crutches was still easy, but only after I stopped trying to do everything at once.

I placed the folded shirt on the dry bench behind me.

Belt beside it.

Boot off.

Sock off.

Trousers next.

No drama.

The younger man scrubbing his foot glanced over.

"Bench good?"

I looked at it.

"Excellent review so far."

He nodded.

"I hate stone."

"Cold?"

"Steals clothes."

I looked at the wet stone bench opposite us.

A shirt had fallen from it onto the floor.

Evidence supported his case.

The older man rinsed soap from his hair.

"Dry bench used to be two."

The younger man said, "Before crack."

"What crack?"

The older man pointed toward the empty stretch of left wall.

A lighter rectangle in the tile showed where another bench had once stood.

"Gone winter."

"Why?"

"Cracked."

That completed local history at the level I required.

I did not ask who maintained it.

I did not ask whether the ceramic feet carried the drying effect.

I did not ask what the brass line did.

I undressed.

The room remained a room.

That mattered more than I expected.

My left leg ended below the knee.

There was no hiding it once the trousers came off.

Not that I had hidden it exactly.

Clothing and crutches told most of the story.

Still, there was a difference between visible fact and exposed body.

The older man looked once.

Not stared.

Looked.

Then returned to his hair.

The younger man had already lost interest in everything except his enemy foot.

I had spent enough of my first life in communal baths, barracks wash rooms, healer rooms, river camps, and terrible roadside inns to know that nakedness became boring quickly when everyone had their own skin to manage.

This body was newer to me than that habit.

That was the strange part.

The bathhouse did not care.

Good.

I checked the skin at the end of the residual limb before washing.

No redness beyond ordinary.

No rubbed spot.

No issue from yesterday's long walking.

I had learned not to trust "feels fine" as the only inspection.

That lesson had arrived through repetition rather than disaster.

Also good.

A wooden dipper hung from the trough.

I moved one crutch from the slot, stood, and took the three steps to the nearest stool.

The grip tile held.

Right foot.

Crutches.

Turn.

Sit.

Easy enough.

The water from the first bronze spout was warm.

Second hotter.

Third cool.

Not labels.

Position and habit, apparently.

I tested with fingers first.

Warm was enough.

The older man saw me doing it.

"Middle bites."

"I noticed."

"It gets worse noon."

"Why?"

He shrugged.

"Hot."

Excellent.

Bathhouse thermodynamics remained undefeated.

I filled the dipper.

Washed.

There was no revelation.

Soap worked.

Water worked.

My body became cleaner.

I appreciated this.

The stump required more care than the rest of me.

Not delicate.

Careful.

I washed the skin, rinsed fully, dried it before putting any cloth back over it.

No magical salve.

No dramatic scar treatment.

No hidden complication.

Just skin under load that benefited from not being left damp.

The bathhouse made that easier because the dry bench existed.

I could wash at the stool, then return to a genuinely dry place without trying to hop, balance clothing, or trust wet stone.

The route between stool and bench was six steps.

I counted none of them after the second trip.

That was another kind of improvement.

The younger man finally defeated his foot.

He leaned back on his stool.

"Hot room?"

The older man said, "No."

The younger man looked offended.

"You always say no."

"Because you stay too long."

"I don't."

"You slept."

"One time."

"In steam."

"Warm."

The older man looked at me.

"See?"

I raised both hands.

"I have no jurisdiction."

The younger man pointed at my crutches.

"You going?"

"Hot room?"

"Yeah."

I considered.

The red-cup room was optional.

I had paid wash only.

Probably additional fee.

Also more wet floor, more heat, more transfers, and no actual reason beyond being there.

"No."

The older man nodded as if I had joined his political party.

The younger man said, "Cowards."

He left for steam.

The older man called after him, "Don't sleep."

No response.

I finished washing my hair.

That required leaning forward over the trough, which changed my balance enough that I kept one crutch braced under my right arm and the other within reach rather than pretending two free hands were worth bad positioning.

Water ran down my face.

Soap got in one eye.

This proved the gods remained attentive.

I swore.

The older man laughed.

"Middle spout."

"What about it?"

"Rinse."

I used the middle spout.

It was much hotter than the first.

I swore again.

He laughed harder.

"You said it bites."

"Did."

"Then why recommend it?"

"Funny."

Carrow's public institutions remained hostile.

I used the warm spout instead.

By the time I returned to the dry bench, I felt human again.

Wet human.

Progress.

The household cloth proved large enough.

I hated Cala for being correct.

I dried thoroughly before dressing.

Especially the left leg.

No rush.

The bench's front brass line warmed faintly under my forearm while I sat.

Not hot.

Just warmer than the room.

I looked at it.

Then deliberately looked away.

Dry bench.

It dried.

Mechanism could remain employed without my supervision.

The younger man returned from the hot room red-faced and triumphant.

The older man stared at him.

"You sleep?"

"No."

"Lie."

"Didn't."

He sat on the stone bench.

Immediately stood again.

"Cold."

I said, "Dry bench has received excellent reviews."

He glared at me.

I was becoming part of the bathhouse culture too quickly.

The counter woman had given me the square token.

I realized I still had it on the bench beside my belt.

I held it up.

"Do I return this?"

The older man said, "Counter."

"Thank you."

No one asked me to write a report.

I dressed.

The clean shirt felt better than it had any right to.

Boot on.

Sock dry.

Trousers.

Belt.

I checked the crutch tips before leaving the wet room.

Both clean enough.

One had a thread stuck near the edge of the rubber tip.

Blue.

I pulled it free.

Ordinary thread.

It went into the refuse pot by the door.

Not Lyssa thread.

Not magical thread.

Not evidence.

A thread.

I felt almost powerful.

At the counter I returned the square token.

The woman took it.

"You use cloth?"

"Yes."

She pointed at the second household bit still in my hand.

"Keep."

"Cala gave it for cloth."

"Your cloth."

"Ah."

The bathhouse had defended Cala's finances against her intentions.

I approved.

"How long does the dry bench stay dry?"

The question escaped before I could stop it.

The woman looked at me.

"Until it doesn't."

I closed my eyes.

"Of course."

"Then we tell Noll."

There was the maintenance boundary.

One name.

One action.

Enough.

I did not ask who Noll was.

I left.

Outside, the day had warmed.

Clean skin made the air feel cooler.

Probably because I was still slightly damp in places.

No theory required.

I had one household bit in my pocket because the bathhouse had refused to invent a towel expense.

That bit belonged to the house.

I knew this without debate.

A baker had set small seed rolls near the door.

I looked.

One bit for two, according to the chalk board.

I kept walking.

This was not restraint around my private money.

It was not my money.

Different skill.

At the next lane a barber had a chair outside.

The man in it wore half a face of white foam and looked asleep.

I touched my jaw.

Then kept walking because solving every form of grooming in one morning would have been suspiciously efficient.

The household door opened cleanly when I returned.

Cala looked up from the table.

"Better."

"Your standards are abusive."

She held out her hand.

I gave her the unspent household bit.

She looked at it.

"Cloth?"

"Brought one."

She looked at the folded damp cloth under my arm.

"Good."

I hated everyone.

Lyssa came down the stairs.

She stopped halfway.

"Bath."

"Apparently visible."

"Hair."

I touched it.

Still damp.

"That is how water works."

She came the rest of the way down.

Tavi appeared behind her with the timing of a curse.

"Greg wet."

"Thank you."

"Bath."

"Yes."

"Greg pole bath."

"No."

He smiled.

Historical contamination spread.

I hung the damp household cloth where Cala pointed and put the clean shirt's old companion into the wash pile.

My shoulder bag remained exactly where I had left it.

Three copper and one private bit remained upstairs.

No client had arrived.

No theatre paper waited.

No Guild message had appeared.

The morning had cost the household one bit and returned one.

Net bath entry: one household bit.

I did not write that down.

Cala already knew what baths cost.

Lyssa already knew.

Carrow already knew.

I was allowed to know something without preserving it for future civilization.

I sat at the table.

The skin at the end of my left leg was dry and comfortable.

My hair dripped once onto my shoulder.

Lyssa noticed.

"Not dry."

"Mostly."

She handed me a smaller cloth.

I rubbed my hair.

"Now?"

She looked.

"Better."

I leaned back.

The public bath had contained treated grip tile, warm-water routing, a dry bench, ceramic feet, a brass line, and at least one unknown man named Noll who apparently became relevant when dry benches stopped being dry.

A younger version of me, meaning the actual nineteen-year-old whose body I currently occupied, might have noticed none of that beyond whether the water was hot.

The older version might once have tried to understand all of it.

Today I had mostly washed.

That seemed healthier.

Then Cala set a basket of beans in front of me.

"Sort."

I looked at her.

"I have just completed an important civic ritual."

"Sort."

"Clean hands deserve better."

"Sort."

I sorted beans.

One pale.

One split.

Good into the large bowl.

Bad into the small.

Tavi climbed onto the stool opposite me and stole one good bean.

Cala took it from him without looking.

Lyssa returned to whatever work she had upstairs.

The brown cuff did not reappear.

Outside, someone uncovered a charm lamp even though the sun was already strong enough to make it pointless.

A single lampwing drifted under the gallery roof, found nothing interesting, and continued on.

I did not follow it.

By midday I smelled like soap instead of city.

The change was sufficient.
