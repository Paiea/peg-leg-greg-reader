CHAPTER 328

THE WRONG DOOR

The problem with knowing which door was behind you was that sometimes the door behind you was the problem.

I learned this at the theatre.

Of course I did.

The message came just after breakfast.

Not from Teren.

Not from Rinna.

Not even from Nessa.

A boy I recognized from the theatre's front steps knocked on our door, handed Cala a folded scrap, and said, "For Greg. They said if he's here."

Cala looked at me.

"You're here."

"Strong start."

The boy had already left.

The scrap said:

GREG

IF FREE BEFORE SECOND BELL

BACK HALL WALK

NEW STORAGE ROUTE

NESSA

No pay written.

No promise of work.

No emergency.

Just a route.

I smiled anyway.

Lyssa saw it.

"Theatre?"

"Maybe."

"You have theatre face."

"I don't have a theatre face."

"You do."

"What does it look like?"

"Like somebody remembered you."

That was rude enough that I folded the note and put it in my bag.

The last-door oval hung from its short cord near the upstairs doorway.

I looked at it.

Then at the bag.

Then back at it.

I had not planned to carry it every day.

That was how useful objects became habits before you understood their failure modes.

Also how people lost useful objects.

I took it anyway.

Not loose.

I wrapped it, put it in the shallow case, and tucked the case into the shoulder bag where it could not knock against the Kiln chit or my charcoal.

The holdfast stayed home.

Separate problems.

Still a good rule.

The theatre looked exactly like itself.

That was more comforting than I expected.

Front doors open.

Painted board crooked by a degree nobody else cared about.

A coil of rope near the wall that had probably been there for months and would outlive us all.

Someone inside shouted, "Not that bench."

Someone else shouted, "I know."

This was almost certainly false.

I crossed the front door.

Click.

The oval moved inside the case.

I did not stop to inspect it.

That was new too.

Useful things did not require applause every time they worked.

Nessa found me near the side aisle.

"You came."

"The note was persuasive."

"It had six words."

"Efficient persuasion."

She handed me a narrow wooden slat with a chalk mark on one end.

"Walk."

"Good morning."

"Morning. Walk."

There she was.

The theatre had changed its back storage route.

Not dramatically.

A rear property room had been partially emptied so a wall repair could happen next week. Until then, long objects that normally went through the rear hall had to move through a side passage, across the old wardrobe room, then behind the left stage wall.

The problem was the wardrobe-room turn.

It was tight.

The second problem was the new shelving someone had installed in the side passage.

It was also tight.

The third problem was Marek.

He was standing beside a long painted spear.

Nessa pointed at him.

"Do not help."

Marek put a hand to his chest.

"I have not done anything."

"Correct. Continue."

"Cruel management."

"Not management. Survival."

I took the wooden slat.

"What is this?"

"Length."

"Of?"

"The flower frame."

The flower frame was a tall narrow piece of scenery used in the garden sequence. Not heavy, but long enough to become an argument in every doorway.

Nessa had cut the slat to match its longest rigid dimension.

Smart.

"You want the route checked before moving it."

"I want you to walk the slat through without deciding the building should be wider."

"That limits my best solutions."

"Good."

Jori appeared from behind a flat carrying a hammer.

"Shelf corner catches at shoulder height."

"Whose shoulder?" I asked.

He looked at me.

"Not yours."

"Useful."

"Marek's."

Marek said, "My shoulders are being unfairly documented."

Nessa said, "Walk."

I walked.

The route began through the side-stage door.

Click.

Then the narrow service passage.

No threshold for several yards.

New shelving on the right.

Old wall on left.

The slat cleared.

At the wardrobe-room door, I turned sideways.

Click.

The slat cleared the frame but clipped a hanging coat inside.

I stopped.

"Object clears. Cloth doesn't."

Nessa, behind me, moved the coat hook two pegs farther in.

No discussion.

Good workplace.

Across the wardrobe room.

Second door.

Click.

Then left behind the stage wall.

I stopped.

"Clear if the frame stays upright until wardrobe, sideways inside, upright after second door."

Nessa nodded.

Marek said, "That sounds familiar."

"Because you learned it with flowers," Nessa said.

"I discovered it with flowers."

"You got stuck with flowers."

"Discovery often begins in adversity."

Jori handed him the spear.

"Discover somewhere else."

The oval had clicked three times.

Front entrance.

Side-stage door.

Wardrobe-room door.

Then second wardrobe door.

Four, actually.

I had stopped counting automatically.

That should have been good.

Except the new route had just demonstrated the object's weakness better than any alley could.

If I needed to retreat from behind the stage wall, the oval would point me back through the second wardrobe door.

Fine.

Once I crossed it, the first wardrobe door became the remembered threshold.

Fine.

One step at a time.

But if somebody opened another qualifying framed access along the way and I crossed it by mistake, the useful retreat point disappeared.

The theatre was full of doors.

Backstage was basically architecture trying to create mistakes.

Nessa took the slat.

"Again with the actual frame."

The flower frame arrived with Jori on one end and Davin on the other.

I stepped aside.

"Where do you want me?"

Nessa pointed ahead.

"Wardrobe. Call the turn."

That was familiar enough.

I went ahead.

Side-stage door.

Click.

Service passage.

Wardrobe door.

Click.

I positioned myself inside the wardrobe room where I could see both ends of the turn.

"Straight."

The flower frame entered.

"Stop."

Jori stopped.

Davin stopped half a beat later.

"Top right close. Lower left clear. Rotate toward me."

They rotated.

The painted flowers passed the moved coat hook by two inches.

"Good. Keep."

Marek appeared in the other doorway.

Of course.

"Need me to take the front?"

Nessa's voice came from behind the frame.

"No."

"I could."

"Continue not helping."

He leaned against the second doorframe.

The frame came through.

"Upright after threshold," I said.

Jori lifted his end.

Davin matched.

Clear.

Easy.

Then someone shouted from the stage.

Not panic.

Urgency.

"Nessa!"

She answered, "What?"

"Rain side's in."

Nessa swore once.

Specific problem.

The rain side was a painted weather flat stored near the rear wall.

If water had gotten into the damaged back room, the wall repair had apparently started early without permission from weather.

Nessa pointed at Jori and Davin.

"Frame to left storage. Greg, with me. Marek, don't touch anything wet."

"That is too broad."

"Good."

We moved.

Fast for me.

Which meant everybody else moved at a pace that did not make me want to murder them.

That was belonging too.

Nobody announced it.

They just did not leave me behind.

We crossed back through the wardrobe-room door.

Click.

Then the first door.

Click.

Then side-stage.

Click.

The oval was now remembering a door I could see behind me.

Very useful.

We reached the rear hall.

Water had come under the outer service door and spread along one wall.

Not flood water.

A hard wind had driven rain through a gap around temporary boards outside.

The rain-side flat leaned against the wall with its lower edge darkening.

Nessa said, "Lift bottom."

Two workers grabbed it.

I took the nearest loose cloth bundle off the floor and moved it onto a bench.

"Not that one," Nessa said.

I stopped.

"Why?"

"Wet dye."

I put it on a different bench.

"Excellent time to learn."

"That's why I said it."

Jori and Davin arrived without the flower frame.

Good.

Jori looked at the water.

"Board seam."

Davin said, "Outside?"

"Has to be."

Nessa pointed.

"Check."

They went through the rear service door.

Click.

I had not crossed it.

The oval still clicked.

I froze.

That was new.

Nessa was already moving the next object.

I almost ignored the sound.

Then the oval clicked again.

Soft.

Inside my bag.

Nobody had crossed near me.

I opened the case.

The oval was turning.

Toward the rear service door.

I stared.

I had not crossed it.

That broke the rule.

Or improved it.

Or revealed that my rule had been wrong from the beginning.

"Greg."

Nessa.

I closed the case.

"Coming."

Work first.

Mystery later.

We moved two rolled backcloths, one crate of painted cups, and a basket of costume trim away from the wet wall.

The water line stopped growing.

Outside, Jori and Davin shoved something heavy against the temporary board seam.

Someone inside fetched rags.

Marek arrived carrying three dry ones.

Nessa looked at him.

"Those dry?"

"Painfully."

"Good. Floor."

He put them down.

Useful.

No disaster.

Just work.

Then the rear service door slammed shut in the wind.

The oval clicked hard enough that I felt it through the bag.

Not loud.

Sharper.

I opened the case again.

The narrow end pointed toward the rear service door.

I had still not crossed it.

I looked at the door.

Then at the oval.

Then at Nessa.

"Who went through there last?"

"Jori and Davin."

"Before them?"

"Why?"

"Humor me."

She thought.

"Teren earlier. Maybe Venn. Why?"

"Not enough."

"Greg."

"Later."

She accepted that because the floor was wet and unlike me, Nessa had priorities.

Jori opened the rear door from outside.

"Seam's blocked for now. Need someone to tell Rinna the outside boards are wrong."

"I'll go," Nessa said.

She turned.

The oval rotated slightly.

Not toward her.

Toward the door.

Good.

Not person tracking.

Probably.

I looked at the threshold.

I had not crossed it.

But the object behaved as if it had become relevant anyway.

Why?

The memory came back sideways.

Not the shelter.

A ruin.

First life.

Smoke.

Three doors in a stone corridor.

A scout named Pel had tied a black return piece to his belt.

I remembered mocking him because he had walked through the wrong arch and overwritten it.

Then later, when half the corridor collapsed, he had thrown the piece through a doorway before crossing.

No.

Not thrown.

Someone else had carried it.

The memory blurred.

But there had been an argument.

"It marks the crossing," I had said.

Pel had answered something.

Not crossing.

Passage.

Use.

Claim.

I could not recover the word.

I looked at the rear door again.

Jori and Davin had crossed it.

The oval was in my bag.

So why would their crossing matter?

Unless the artifact was not keyed to me.

That possibility was much worse.

I took the oval out by its cord.

Nessa saw.

"Is that the garbage thing?"

"One of them."

"Good garbage?"

"Increasingly rude garbage."

I hung it from one finger so it could turn freely.

It pointed toward the rear service door.

Marek leaned in.

"Compass?"

"No."

"Door compass?"

"Also no."

"It points at a door."

"You point at doors."

He pointed at the rear service door.

"I do."

"Still not a compass."

Nessa said, "What does it do?"

"I thought it remembered the last doorway I crossed."

"Thought?"

"Now it appears interested in a doorway I did not cross."

Marek said, "Maybe it likes Jori."

"Most objects don't."

Jori, from outside, said, "I heard that."

"Evidence," Marek said.

I walked away from the rear door.

The oval kept pointing toward it.

I crossed the open frame into the adjacent prop bay.

Click.

The oval turned.

Now it pointed back toward the prop-bay frame.

There.

My crossing overwrote the rear door.

So the old rule still worked.

But something else had caused the rear door to become current before I crossed anything.

I stepped back through the prop-bay frame.

Click.

It pointed back to the frame.

Nessa watched.

"Useful?"

"Yes."

"Dangerous?"

"Unknown."

"Annoying?"

"Extremely."

"Then it fits here."

Fair.

We finished moving the wet-side property.

Rinna arrived with a carpenter who looked personally offended by weather.

He inspected the rear boards.

Nobody asked me about the oval.

Also good.

I stayed another hour because the new storage route still needed walking after the rain-side interruption.

No one offered pay.

I had not come for pay.

That mattered less than it once would have.

Not because I had money.

Two copper was not wealth.

Because this was a place where a six-word note could bring me in to check a route before somebody moved the actual frame.

That had value too.

Different ledger.

On the second route walk, I paid attention to the oval without turning the chapter of my life into an experiment.

Side-stage door.

Click when I crossed.

Wardrobe door.

Click when I crossed.

Second wardrobe door.

Click when I crossed.

No surprise.

Then Nessa carried the wooden length slat through a narrow framed opening beside me while I remained on my side.

No click.

Good.

Marek crossed the same opening.

No click.

Also good.

So other people's crossings did not generally update it.

That made the rear door stranger.

I wrote only:

REAR SERVICE DOOR UPDATED WITHOUT ME CROSSING

J + D CROSSED / NOT ENOUGH TO CLAIM CAUSE

LATER NESSA + MAREK CROSSINGS NEAR ME -> NO UPDATE

Then I stopped.

Nessa read over my shoulder.

"You write like Rinna."

"That is the meanest thing anyone has said to me today."

From across the room Rinna said, "I can hear you."

"Then it worked."

She did not look up from the carpenter's note.

Home by late afternoon.

The oval had behaved normally the entire walk back.

Every qualifying threshold I crossed replaced the last.

No unexplained updates.

At the apartment, I crossed the front door.

Click.

Building door.

Click.

Upstairs doorway.

Click.

I hung the oval from its short cord.

It pointed back toward the upstairs frame.

Tavi came in.

No click.

Lyssa came in.

No click.

Cala passed the doorway twice.

Nothing.

Good.

I sat on the floor with the narrow scrap.

The rear service door bothered me.

Not because it broke the rule.

Because it suggested a second rule.

And second rules were where useful artifacts became expensive mistakes.

I went through my memory again.

Pel.

Smoke.

Collapsed corridor.

Black piece.

Argument.

He had said...

"You don't mark the door," I whispered.

Lyssa looked over from the table.

"What?"

I held up a finger.

The memory came.

Not all of it.

Enough.

Pel had been angry because I called it a last-door marker.

"It doesn't remember doors," he had said.

"It remembers retreat."

I stared at the oval.

That was different.

Maybe.

A door I crossed was an obvious retreat threshold.

But the rear service door had become the only useful retreat from a wet, blocked work area when Jori and Davin went outside and the wind shut it.

Had the artifact selected it because the situation changed?

That sounded too intelligent.

Too convenient.

Too much like turning one strange observation into a miracle.

I wrote:

FIRST-LIFE MEMORY: `REMEMBERS RETREAT` ?

Then beneath it:

DO NOT TRUST WORDING YET

Lyssa said, "Good rule."

"You weren't invited."

"You said it out loud."

"Household espionage."

The oval turned slowly on its cord.

Not toward the doorway.

Away from it.

I stopped breathing for half a second.

Then it continued turning.

Past the wall.

Past the window.

Until its narrow end pointed toward the stairs.

The upstairs doorway was behind it now.

No one had crossed anything.

I stood.

"Lyssa."

She looked up.

The oval clicked.

Downstairs, somebody screamed.

Not theatrical.

Not playful.

A real scream.

I grabbed both crutches.

The oval pointed toward the stairs.

And for the first time, I did not know whether it was showing me the way back.

Or the way out.
