CHAPTER 337

THE RED PATH

The next morning, Cala sent me to buy onions.

This was probably good for me.

Thirty-six-copper artifacts were dangerous to a man's sense of scale.

Onions corrected that.

"Six," Cala said.

"Large?"

"Normal."

"Define normal."

"The ones that look like onions."

Strong procurement standard.

Tavi was at the table building another impossible machine from the remaining repair-sweep junk.

Today the wooden spoon had acquired two metal loops and a bent strip of brass.

"Carrier," he said.

"Still?"

"Faster one."

"Does it move?"

"No."

"Excellent safety profile."

Cala handed me the market cloth.

"Onions."

"I understand."

"You came home yesterday talking about accounts and repair room."

"I was asked what I learned."

"Today learn onions."

I left.

The oval was in my shoulder bag.

Not because I expected anything.

Because it had become something I carried.

That was different from something I tested.

Front door.

Click.

Normal.

Street.

Morning traffic.

Normal.

The temporary black conduit still ran along the upper front-hall wall, but I no longer looked at it every time I passed.

That was also progress.

Carrow was damp from night rain.

Not wet enough for puddles everywhere.

Wet enough that cart wheels had darkened the grooves between stones and every wooden step looked slightly more suspicious than yesterday.

I took the lower market route.

Not because onions were better there.

Because the upper lane had three stairs in a row outside a baker and I was feeling lazy.

Disability was sometimes philosophy.

Mostly it was route selection.

Halfway down Tanner's Cut, I saw the red stakes.

Four of them.

Thin wood.

Fresh paint.

One at each corner of a section of street where the paving changed from gray stone to older brown blocks.

A red cord ran between them at knee height.

A municipal board leaned against the wall.

CHANNEL WORK

PEDESTRIANS USE EAST WALK

NO CARTS AFTER SECOND BELL

Fine.

Street work.

I crossed to the east side.

So did everyone else.

A woman with two baskets complained.

A man carrying folded hides complained louder.

Neither complaint changed the road.

Useful government.

Then the ground moved.

Not much.

Enough.

The brown paving inside the red cord rose perhaps half an inch in a line from one stake to the next.

A soft grinding sound came from underneath.

People stopped.

I stopped too.

A municipal worker inside the cord shouted, "Keep walking. East side clear."

People resumed.

I did not.

Not because danger.

Because the raised line was moving.

Slowly.

A ridge under stone.

Then another ridge formed behind it.

The first sank.

The second rose.

Something traveled beneath the street.

I knew the category.

Not this implementation.

Buried service crawler.

Ground mole.

Channel worm.

Different regions had different names and different levels of honesty about whether the things were alive.

This one was probably not alive.

Probably.

The municipal worker saw me watching.

"East side."

"I'm on east side."

"Then keep moving."

Fair.

I kept moving.

The ridge followed the cord line under the brown blocks.

At the next stake, a metal cap in the street lifted by itself.

Not dramatically.

It rose on three little arms.

The buried thing stopped beneath it.

A second worker crouched and fitted a black fork into the opening.

The fork vibrated.

The first worker looked at a strip of pale paper clipped to a board.

"Still red?"

"Red."

"How red?"

"Don't be clever."

"I'm asking."

"Third line."

"That's not good."

There.

A problem.

Not mine.

I walked on.

Then heard the first worker say, "West branch or old drain?"

The second said, "Map says west branch."

"Street says old drain."

I stopped again.

Not because I was going to solve it.

Because I had heard that sentence in twelve different forms across two lives.

Map says one thing.

Ground says another.

The ground usually won.

A third person approached from the far end of the work zone.

Woman.

Green coat.

Mud to the knees.

Short iron staff with a brass wheel near the grip.

She ducked under the red cord.

"Where?"

First worker pointed at the lifted cap.

"Third line. Map puts branch west. Crawler keeps pulling south."

"You let it pull?"

"Two lengths."

"Why?"

"Because map puts branch west."

The woman looked at him.

He looked away.

Professional communication.

She crouched by the cap.

Turned the brass wheel on her staff.

The iron shaft hummed.

Not mana I could feel directly.

External tool response.

She pressed the staff against the street.

The hum deepened.

Then shifted pitch.

She moved three feet south.

Again.

Another three.

Again.

"Old drain," she said.

The first worker swore.

"Registered?"

"Not on this sheet."

"Then what is it doing under our channel?"

"Existing."

Excellent answer.

The woman stood.

"Stop crawler. Open south cap. Hand trace before you move anything else."

"That adds half a day."

"Less than breaking a drain."

"Probably."

"Definitely."

I liked her too.

Carrow contained too many competent women. It was terrible for my ego.

I resumed my onion mission.

Then the oval clicked.

I had not crossed anything.

I stopped.

The sound was quiet inside the bag.

Not sharp.

One click.

I did not reach for it.

The municipal woman called, "You. Crutches."

I looked around.

There were no other crutches.

Unfortunate.

"Yes?"

"Don't stop there."

I looked down.

The east walk beneath me was ordinary gray paving.

"Why?"

"Crawler's turning under the walk."

I moved.

Immediately.

Not because oval.

Because professional with vibrating ground stick.

Three steps forward.

The paving behind me gave a low scrape.

One stone shifted perhaps a finger-width.

Nothing exploded.

Nobody screamed.

The worker moved the pedestrian cord farther east.

"Use wall side," he called.

People adjusted.

I adjusted.

The oval had clicked before the warning.

Interesting.

Still not proof.

The crawler had been turning under the walk.

Maybe the object responded to changing retreat geometry.

Maybe it responded to the worker crossing some invisible service boundary.

Maybe it clicked because a cart two streets away offended it spiritually.

I bought onions.

Six.

Normal-looking.

Cala would be proud.

On the way back, Tanner's Cut was more disrupted.

The south cap was open.

A square section of paving had been lifted.

Inside was a narrow brick-lined channel running diagonally beneath the street.

Old.

Wet.

Very real.

The buried crawler was now visible too.

Only part of it.

A segmented brass-and-black body about as thick as my thigh sat inside the municipal service groove.

Not alive.

Probably definitely now.

Each segment had little folding feet pressed against the channel walls.

A cable ran from its rear to a spool cart.

At the front, three flat metal whiskers spread against the brick.

The green-coated woman was kneeling beside it.

The first worker held the black fork.

"Hand trace says drain cuts across channel for eleven feet," he said.

"Depth?"

"Changes."

"Of course it does."

"Old work."

"Old work isn't an excuse for being old."

"I think it is exactly the excuse."

She looked at him.

He smiled.

Ah.

They knew each other.

The world continued without me.

Good.

I slowed because the pedestrian lane narrowed around the opened section.

The woman saw me.

"You came back."

I lifted the market cloth.

"Onions."

She nodded solemnly.

"Critical route."

"Apparently."

"Wall side."

I took wall side.

Then she said, "Wait."

I waited.

She looked at my crutches.

Then at the gap between the wall and the moved cord.

"Too narrow?"

"I can fit."

"Can you turn if someone comes opposite?"

I looked.

No.

"Not well."

She pointed at the cord.

"Drop that stake."

The first worker pulled one red stake and lowered the cord to the ground, widening the path by about two feet.

"Go."

I went.

Simple.

No speech.

No heroic accessibility lesson.

Someone saw geometry and changed it.

I appreciated civilization again.

For nearly six minutes.

Then a cart tried to enter Tanner's Cut despite the board saying no carts after second bell.

The driver argued with the worker.

Civilization reverted.

At home, Cala inspected the onions.

Actually inspected them.

"Good."

"I have achieved produce."

"You took a long time."

"Street was being eaten."

Tavi looked up.

"By what?"

"Government."

Cala threw a cloth at me.

I caught it badly.

Lyssa was near the window working on a sleeve.

"What actually happened?"

I told them.

Buried service crawler.

Old drain not on current sheet.

Hand trace.

Half-day delay.

Pedestrian route moved.

Tavi asked, "Did it have teeth?"

"No."

"Then not eating."

"Strong objection."

Cala started cutting onions.

I sat.

The oval clicked when Tavi crossed the kitchen doorway.

Normal.

I took it out later.

Not immediately.

After lunch.

After the onions had become part of something better than onions.

After the house was quiet enough that I was not turning one click into a family event.

I hung the oval from its cord near the upstairs doorway.

It pointed back toward the threshold.

Normal.

I crossed.

Click.

Normal.

Returned.

Click.

Normal.

I did not recreate Tanner's Cut with chairs.

This was growth.

I did take out the narrow scrap.

For a while I wrote nothing.

The useful observation was simple.

OVAL CLICKED BEFORE MUNICIPAL WARNING / CRAWLER TURNING UNDER EAST WALK

I stared.

Too dramatic.

It implied connection.

I crossed out BEFORE MUNICIPAL WARNING.

Wrote:

TANNER'S CUT: NO-CROSSING CLICK WHILE SERVICE ROUTE / PEDESTRIAN ROUTE CHANGING

Better.

Then:

NO EVENT AFTER MOVING EXCEPT MINOR PAVING SHIFT

Also true.

Then I stopped.

The more interesting thing had not been the oval.

It had been the old drain.

A current map said west branch.

The ground said diagonal drain.

The specialist believed the ground.

That should not have been profound.

It wasn't.

It was just useful.

I thought about the marker field with Antonius.

Filed line versus physical posts.

Mara's housing read.

Auction condition notes.

Hessa separating target distance from effect magnitude.

Different domains.

Same irritating fact.

Descriptions were not objects.

Maps were not streets.

Prices were not value.

A clean result was not a whole ability.

I almost wrote that down.

Then did not.

Too broad.

I was becoming allergic to wisdom.

Healthy.

Someone knocked downstairs.

Front door.

Normal door now.

I heard Cala answer.

A man's voice.

Not urgent.

Then footsteps.

Cala called up.

"Greg."

"What?"

"For you."

That was suspicious.

I went downstairs.

A boy perhaps fifteen stood inside the front hall holding a folded paper.

Not someone I knew.

Courier strap across his chest.

He held out the paper.

"Greg?"

"Yes."

"From East Kiln gate."

I took it.

The paper was folded once and sealed with a square smear of black wax.

No formal crest.

Just a thumb mark pressed through the wax.

The boy said, "No reply paid."

"Good. I wasn't prepared."

He did not laugh.

Professional youth.

Then left.

The oval clicked when the front door closed.

Normal.

I opened the paper.

Four lines.

GREG,

BLACK CHIT STILL GOOD.

PUBLIC COUNTER AFTER FIRST BELL TOMORROW.

JESSA.

I read it twice.

Not because complicated.

Because I had almost forgotten the black yard chit.

Not forgotten forgotten.

Background forgotten.

The best kind of continuity.

Kiln Street.

Counterload frame.

Emergency message.

Jessa.

East gate public counter.

The square black chit was still in my bag's inner fold.

I checked.

There.

No job promised.

No tools.

No training.

No store access.

Just the narrow thing it had always been.

Access.

I looked at the note from the auction.

ACCESS = CASH + ACCOUNT + INSPECTION + TRANSPORT + REPAIR ROOM

Apparently I had missed one.

People remembering you.

I did not add it to the equation.

That would have been unbearable.

I folded Jessa's note and put it beside the chit.

Tomorrow after first bell.

Fine.

I had onions today.

A yard gate tomorrow.

Thirty-six-copper ambitions somewhere above both.

That felt more like a life than a plan.
