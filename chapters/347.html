CHAPTER 347

THE GATE HAND

The North Secondary reopening lasted exactly one morning before somebody found a way to turn it into paid labor.

I respected Carrow.

The notice came through Cala.

Not because she had become my employment office.

Because a woman from two streets over had asked whether the crutch man who could read work tags was home.

I objected to the description.

Cala did not.

"She means you."

"That is a very broad category."

"You are the only crutch man in this house."

Technically fair.

The woman waited outside with a folded yellow strip in one hand and a flour mark on her sleeve.

I had seen her before.

Maybe at the water line.

Maybe buying onions.

Carrow had reached the stage where strangers came preloaded with uncertainty.

"Greg?"

"Yes."

She handed me the strip.

"North Secondary gate needs day hands. My brother took one. They still need two more."

I read.

DAY PASSAGE SUPPORT
NORTH SECONDARY
MARKED ROUTE ONLY
MIDDAY WINDOW
CHECK TAG / CALL LOAD / RECORD CLEAR
NO ROAD ENTRY
TWO COPPER
MEAL INCLUDED
REPORT EAST GATE SHED

I read the important line twice.

NO ROAD ENTRY.

Good.

"What does check tag mean?"

"Don't know."

"Call load?"

"Don't know."

"Record clear?"

"Probably write that it cleared."

Reasonable civilian interpretation.

"Why me?"

She shrugged.

"My brother said they want people who can read fast and don't get clever with carts."

I looked at my crutches.

"I am naturally protected from getting clever with carts."

She looked at them too.

"That was my thought."

Excellent hiring practice.

"When?"

"Before midday bell."

I looked at the house.

Lyssa was at the table with a length of gray cloth across her lap and three pins in her mouth.

She removed the pins.

"Go."

"I haven't decided."

"Two copper."

"Money isn't the only consideration."

Cala said, "Meal."

"Thank you for supporting my strategic process."

Tavi came around the table.

"Can I go?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you would get clever with carts."

He accepted this immediately.

I read the strip again.

No road entry.

Marked-route support.

Two copper.

No standing arrangement.

The long mile had changed yesterday.

Today somebody needed bodies around the change.

That was how cities worked.

Infrastructure moved one inch and employment leaked out.

"I'll go."

The woman nodded.

"East gate shed. Ask for Saret."

There.

A name.

Dangerous.

I put the yellow strip in my shoulder bag.

Then stopped.

The oval was already in there.

I took it out.

No reason.

The rest stone stayed boxed.

The holdfast stayed high.

Three copper stayed upstairs except one I carried by habit.

I was learning to leave magic home.

This was becoming suspiciously mature.

North Secondary looked different under permission.

The last time I had seen the notice, people stood outside reading about closure.

Now the street feeding the gate had become a temporary yard.

Not a real yard.

A borrowed one.

Three freight carts waited nose to tail along the left wall.

Two handbarrows sat under a striped awning.

A city barrier divided pedestrians from freight.

Yellow route cloth ran from the east gate arch toward the road beyond.

Not painted.

Tied.

Every twelve or fifteen paces, a narrow yellow strip had been fixed to a waist-high iron stake.

The strips moved slightly in the breeze.

Each had a dark mark near the top.

Same shape.

Not letters.

Route mark.

People entering on foot were being checked at one side.

Freight at the other.

Nobody looked excited.

That reassured me.

Excited infrastructure was usually bad infrastructure.

The east gate shed was a narrow stone room attached to the wall.

A man stood outside with a board in one hand.

Broad shoulders.

Bald head.

Brown work vest.

"Saret?"

"Yes."

I gave him the yellow hiring strip.

He read it.

Looked at me.

Looked at my crutches.

Then back at the strip.

"Can you stand twenty minutes, sit ten, repeat?"

"Probably longer, but that sounds better."

"Can you write numbers?"

"Usually."

"Can you shout?"

"Professionally?"

"Across six carts."

"Yes."

"Good."

No inspiring speech.

Perfect.

He pointed inside.

There were three stools.

A narrow desk.

Two water jugs.

Stacks of route tags.

A wall board with four columns.

LOAD
TAG
IN
CLEAR

"You're gate hand three."

There was my title.

Apparently.

"What happened to gate hand two?"

"Outside."

Good.

He gave me a thin wooden board with a clip at the top and a paper sheet.

Twenty-four numbered lines.

"Do not invent anything."

"I like you already."

He did not care.

"Freight comes to yellow line. You check tag number against my board. You call number and lane. Inside road caller answers. When they say receive, you mark IN and send cart. When far caller sends clear number, you mark CLEAR."

"Do I inspect cargo?"

"No."

"Check seals?"

"No."

"Open anything?"

"No."

"Ask driver why they have seventeen barrels of suspicious glowing fluid?"

"If tag matches, no."

"Excellent."

"If tag doesn't match, stop and call me."

"Do I enter route?"

"No."

"Touch route stakes?"

"No."

"Move barrier?"

"No."

"Interpret road marks?"

"No."

"Fix anything magical?"

"No."

I smiled.

"Beautiful."

Saret finally looked at me more carefully.

"You ask too many questions."

"I am trying to discover how little my job contains."

"Your job contains numbers and volume."

Even better.

The first cart came fifteen minutes later.

Two wheels.

Canvas top.

Driver with red scarf.

Tag 118.

I checked Saret's board.

118.

Lane freight.

Window midday one.

"One eighteen, freight lane."

A voice from inside the arch answered.

"Hold."

I did nothing.

This was excellent work.

Then:

"Receive one eighteen."

I marked IN.

"Go."

Driver clicked tongue.

Cart crossed gate.

No drama.

Second cart.

Tag 121.

Third.

Tag 119.

Order did not match numbers.

I survived.

Gate hand two was a woman named Eda who handled pedestrian work on the other side of Saret.

She had a round face and the calm irritation of someone who had already explained the same rule thirty times.

"Yellow cloth stays on your right until second marker."

A man asked, "What if I know the old route?"

"Yellow cloth stays on your right until second marker."

"I've walked North Secondary for twelve years."

"Then you have twelve years of experience following the yellow cloth today."

I liked her.

The road beyond the arch looked normal.

This was the problem.

Stone.

Dirt at edges.

Low drainage ditch.

Trees farther north.

Nothing visibly wrong.

No glowing fracture.

No impossible weather.

No road spirit standing with folded arms.

Just route stakes.

Yellow cloth.

Spacing.

City workers.

And farther down, maybe forty paces, a squat object beside the road with three metal legs.

A dark bowl sat on top.

Every cart passed it.

I noticed because every cart slowed.

Not much.

A little.

The bowl gave one dull amber pulse.

Then the far caller sent clear.

I watched three cycles before asking.

Saret was beside me.

"That bowl part of route check?"

"Yes."

"What does it check?"

"Not your job."

Good.

"Who owns it?"

"Road office."

Also good.

"What happens if it doesn't pulse?"

"Nobody sends another cart."

That was enough.

I shut up.

For almost six minutes.

Then a larger wagon arrived.

Four wheels.

Two draft animals.

Tag 126.

The driver stopped at yellow line.

I checked.

126 existed.

But the lane entry beside it had a second mark.

A little hooked symbol.

I had not seen that on the others.

"Saret."

He came over.

I pointed.

"What does hook mean?"

"Stabilized load."

"Different lane?"

"Same lane. Different spacing."

He took my board.

"Call it with hook."

I did.

"One twenty-six, freight, hook."

Inside caller answered, "Hold."

Longer this time.

The wagon waited.

Canvas side shifted slightly.

Something heavy inside settled with a low wooden knock.

Then another sound.

Not from cargo.

A soft three-note chime under the wagon bed.

I looked.

There.

Bolted beneath the rear half of the wagon was a black metal cradle.

Inside it sat a gray stone cylinder with a shallow groove around its middle.

Not my rest stone.

Not same shape exactly.

But same ugly industrial family.

I knew that immediately.

Old memory did not arrive as a clean answer.

It arrived as annoyance.

Warehouses.

Loading frames.

A cracked yard.

Men arguing about recovery intervals because somebody had tried to save eleven minutes and lost a wall.

Rest units.

Not rest for people.

Rest for systems.

The name had always been terrible.

The cylinder under the wagon gave another faint chime.

A tiny brass tongue beside it moved from left to center.

Saret was watching the road.

Not me.

I said, "That stabilizer uses a rest unit."

He glanced under wagon.

"Yes."

"Current model?"

"No idea."

Healthy.

"That one paired to the wagon frame?"

"Ask transport yard if you care. Not during window."

Excellent.

The inside caller shouted.

"Receive one twenty-six."

Saret held up one finger.

"Wait."

The driver did not move.

The black bowl down the road pulsed amber.

Once.

Then again.

Not normal.

Saret's posture changed.

Not panic.

Attention.

Inside caller said, "Hold receive."

Meaning don't send it.

I did not mark IN.

Good.

The driver leaned down.

"Problem?"

Saret said, "Stay."

The draft animals stood.

The wagon's under-frame cylinder chimed again.

That little brass tongue shifted toward the right.

I had seen that behavior before.

Not this unit.

Not this world exactly.

But the principle.

Load buffering.

Release debt.

Stored mismatch.

A system could look stable because the buffer was eating the difference.

Until the buffer reached its own limit.

I kept my mouth shut.

This was not my apparatus.

Farther down the route, two road workers moved to the black bowl.

Neither touched it immediately.

One crouched.

One looked toward the wagon.

Then toward something outside my angle.

The yellow cloth nearest the road edge fluttered.

No wind here.

Interesting.

Not useful.

The road worker raised a red paddle.

Saret said, "Back one wagon length."

Driver obeyed.

Slowly.

The under-frame cylinder's brass tongue moved back toward center.

One chime.

Then stopped.

The black bowl down-road returned to one amber pulse.

Saret looked at me.

"Clear the line."

I called.

"Freight line holding."

Eda repeated to her pedestrians.

"Pedestrian hold."

Nobody entered.

Two minutes.

Maybe three.

Then inside caller shouted.

"One twenty-six return yard. No passage."

Saret relayed it.

Driver swore once.

Not at us.

At economics.

"Reason?"

"Route response under stabilized load."

"Road or wagon?"

"Not established here."

Good.

The driver looked under wagon.

"Unit's green."

Saret said, "Then transport yard can argue with road office."

Institutional romance again.

The wagon backed out.

I did not mark IN.

Saret wrote something on his own board.

I asked, "Does the failed passage count against driver?"

"No."

"Against tag?"

"Tag gets incident note."

"Can they try again?"

"Not this window."

"Different load?"

"Not my decision."

Good.

The four-wheel wagon rolled away.

The line restarted with a smaller handcart.

Normal amber pulse.

Clear.

Then another.

Normal.

City did not collapse because one load failed.

I liked that.

At first rest I sat on stool.

Eda came in.

"First window?"

"Yes."

"You look disappointed."

"I was promised numbers and volume."

"You got numbers and volume."

"Then the road behaved."

"It does that."

"Rude."

She drank water.

I asked, "How long have you done this?"

"Today?"

"That answers enough."

She snorted.

Saret came in with three wrapped meal packets.

Bread.

Cold sliced meat.

Pickled green thing.

No price because included.

Civilization.

We ate in shifts.

While I ate, I kept thinking about the wagon unit.

Not because it was magical.

Because I owned something in the same broad family.

K-14-6.

Retired industrial component.

Known release delay outside yard limit.

Boxed at home.

Untested.

I had been thinking of it as an object.

Maybe that was already wrong.

A rest unit could be less like a tool and more like a system organ.

Useful only because the rest of the machine knew what to do with what it stored.

A lung was not bad because it failed to become a bird when placed on a table.

This analogy was disgusting.

I kept it.

Saret saw me looking toward road.

"Don't."

"I am eating."

"You're thinking."

"Hostile workplace."

"Whatever happened with one twenty-six is not yours."

"I know."

"Good."

I bit bread.

Then asked, "Can I ask one general question?"

He sighed.

"One."

"Do stabilized loads have different passage spacing because the road responds to the stabilization equipment, or because those loads are usually heavier?"

Saret stared at me.

"That's two questions."

Cruel.

"Pick whichever answer costs less."

"I don't know."

Excellent.

He continued.

"Hook means spacing. Road office set it. We follow it."

There.

Procedure without theory.

Useful anyway.

I finished eating.

Seven freight loads.

Twelve pedestrians.

One old woman who argued that yellow cloth was ugly.

Eda agreed and still made her follow it.

A boy tried to touch a route stake.

His father caught him first.

I respected the father.

One cart tag did not match Saret's board.

I stopped it.

The driver insisted it had been approved.

Saret checked.

Wrong day window.

Driver cursed the clerk who issued it.

Not us.

Good.

I did not ask.

This was growth.

The black route bowl pulsed amber for each load.

One pulse.

Clear.

One pulse.

Clear.

The magical world had become most magical when everyone treated it like traffic.

Smaller than 126.

Hook mark.

Tag 133.

Different unit.

Flat black plate with two pale stones locked into side channels.

No chime.

The cart passed.

Route bowl pulsed once.

Clear.

No problem.

Good.

Also good.

The first failed wagon had taught almost nothing about cause.

But it had taught something about category.

These things lived in systems.

Mounted.

Paired.

Read by other indicators.

Bounded by procedures.

Not sitting on bedroom floors while idiots poked them.

I felt personally attacked by engineering.

Twenty-three lines used.

One mismatch.

One failed passage not marked IN.

All clear marks matched his board.

He nodded.

"Fine."

Highest praise.

"Yes."

No speech.

Meal included.

Five now.
