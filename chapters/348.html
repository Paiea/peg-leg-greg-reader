CHAPTER 348

THE CLEARER

The morning after North Secondary, I woke to a table leg blocking the hallway.

Not metaphorically.

A real table leg.

Dark wood.

Square.

Possibly hostile.

I stopped at the top of the stairs and looked down.

The leg belonged to something folded against the wall below.

Four legs.

Two hinged braces.

A broad top wrapped in old canvas.

Lyssa stood beside it with both hands on her hips.

Cala stood on the other side with the expression she usually reserved for weather entering the house without permission.

Tavi was underneath it.

Of course.

"Why is there a child in the furniture?" I asked.

Tavi looked up.

"I'm checking."

"For what?"

"If I fit."

"Result?"

"Yes."

Useful research.

Lyssa pointed at him.

"Out."

He crawled out.

I came down carefully.

One crutch.

Second crutch.

Right foot.

No magical effect.

Progress remained appropriately unimpressive.

The folded thing was not exactly a table.

Too narrow when folded.

Too many braces.

The canvas wrapped around a top wider than our dining table but thinner.

A pale strip of stone showed along one edge.

I stopped.

"What is it?"

Lyssa smiled.

That was dangerous.

"Mine."

Not the answer I had requested.

Cala said, "Work table."

There.

"You bought a work table?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

I stared at her.

"While I was shouting numbers at carts?"

"You were busy."

"Apparently everyone was."

Lyssa touched the canvas.

"It came this morning."

"I noticed the invasion."

"Two boys brought it."

"Paid?"

"Yes."

"By you?"

Her eyebrows went up.

Right.

Bad question.

"Your money," I said.

"Correct."

"Your table."

"Also correct."

Cala folded her arms.

"Her table currently lives where people walk."

There was the actual problem.

I looked at hallway.

Then main room.

Then the table again.

"Where is it going?"

Lyssa pointed toward the front window.

Cala said, "No."

Immediately.

Lyssa looked at her.

"That corner."

"That corner has a chair."

"The chair can move."

"To where?"

Lyssa looked around.

So did I.

Our house had reached a mature stage of civilization where every empty patch of floor had acquired a purpose.

Chair.

Basket.

Hooks.

Small shelf.

Water sack.

Tavi.

Tavi was not technically furniture but occupied similar space.

I asked, "How wide when open?"

Lyssa lifted the canvas enough to show the top.

"This wide."

That was not measurement.

It was also large.

The top had two hinged leaves that folded inward.

A narrow central plank stayed fixed between them.

Along one long edge ran the pale stone strip I had noticed.

Not decorative.

Inset.

Three finger-widths wide.

Tiny brass points at both ends.

"Light rail?" I asked.

Lyssa looked pleased that I knew one thing.

"Flat line."

"For hems?"

"For anything I need straight."

"Does it heat?"

"No."

"Cut?"

"No."

"Move thread?"

"No."

"Good."

"It makes a line, Greg."

"Lines have become dangerous around me."

Fair caution.

She pulled a little brass tab at the end.

Nothing happened.

Then looked at Cala.

"Not plugged in."

I looked under the frame.

A braided white cord had been tied around one brace.

At its end sat a flat two-pronged house contact.

Common enough.

Not a mysterious artifact.

Our main room wall had one household feed plate near the window for a lamp and small heating stone.

Cala pointed at it.

"Lamp uses that."

Lyssa said, "Not during day."

"And at night?"

"I don't work with it at night if you need the lamp."

Cala did not answer.

Negotiation.

Good.

I asked, "Why this one?"

Lyssa looked at the table instead of me.

"Because I am tired of moving everything every time we eat."

There.

Simple.

Not simple.

Our dining table had become hers whenever cloth work got large enough.

Which meant pins moved.

Bowls moved.

Tavi moved badly.

Cala moved everything back for meals.

Then Lyssa moved it again.

I had watched that happen without naming it as a problem because it was not my problem.

Apparently it had become expensive enough to buy furniture.

"How much?" I asked.

Lyssa looked at me.

"Mine."

Right.

Again.

"You do not need to tell me."

"Good."

I smiled.

"Was it a good price?"

"Yes."

"That I am allowed to know?"

"Apparently."

Cala pointed toward the front corner.

"Price does not make the chair disappear."

The chair had become politically important.

I went over to it.

Plain wood.

One repaired rung.

Usually where Cala sat when mending or shelling something or simply watching the room.

Not decorative.

Not spare.

I looked at opposite wall.

No.

Too close to stair base.

Near door?

Terrible.

Beside dining table?

Would narrow the route.

My route.

I moved through the main room with two crutches every day.

Furniture geometry had consequences.

Lyssa said, "I can fold the table when I'm not using it."

"How flat?"

"This flat."

She slapped the folded top.

Still thick.

"Against wall?"

"Yes."

"Legs stay out?"

"No. They fold."

"Then why is one attacking the hallway?"

"Because the boys left it there."

Fair.

I walked the room.

Not performing.

Actually walking.

Crutch tips.

Right foot.

Turn near table.

Back toward stairs.

The front-window corner gave enough width if the chair moved.

The chair could go near the side wall if one basket moved.

Basket could go beneath hooks if the little stool moved.

Little stool could go upstairs.

I stopped.

This was how empires formed.

One work table and suddenly three territories required resettlement.

"Chair to side wall," I said.

Cala looked.

"Basket?"

"Under hooks."

"Stool?"

"Upstairs."

"Whose stool?"

I looked at stool.

Mine.

Of course.

A low one I used sometimes to sit while sorting notes near the stair wall.

I did not need it there.

I liked it there.

Different thing.

"Mine," I said.

Lyssa looked at me.

Not smiling.

"You don't have to."

That made it worse.

I looked at the room again.

Chair mattered more.

Her work table mattered more than my convenience stool.

Annoying hierarchy.

"Stool upstairs."

Cala said, "That works."

Decision achieved through sacrifice.

Tavi raised his hand.

"Can I have the stool?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I am sacrificing it nobly, not dying."

He lowered his hand.

We started moving things.

Not the work table.

I was not carrying a broad folded frame while using two crutches.

This did not need experimentation.

Cala and Lyssa shifted it together after clearing the route.

I took the small stool upstairs.

One hand occupied by crutch.

The other also occupied by crutch.

The stool became a problem.

I stared at it.

Tavi appeared beside me.

"I can carry it."

I looked at him.

Small child.

Small stool.

Appropriate scale.

"Both hands."

"I know."

"Do not wear it."

"I wasn't."

He had absolutely considered wearing it.

He carried it upstairs.

I followed.

Delegation.

S-class technique.

Back downstairs, Cala had moved her chair.

Basket beneath hooks.

The new route was narrow at one point but not bad.

I walked it twice.

Lyssa watched.

"Enough?"

"For me."

"For everyone?"

I looked at Tavi.

"He has no route discipline. Nothing is enough for him."

"True."

Cala said, "Door still clears."

It did.

Window still opened.

Dining chairs could pull out.

Good.

Then came the table itself.

Lyssa unwrapped canvas.

The wood underneath had old cuts and needle scratches.

Not pretty.

Good work furniture.

One corner had been patched with a darker triangle of wood.

The pale flat-line rail ran nearly the full long edge.

Stamped into one brass end piece were tiny maker characters.

I did not read them.

Not because I couldn't.

Because this was Lyssa's table.

Growth hurt.

She folded the left leaf down.

Brace locked.

Then right.

The table became broad and flat.

Bigger than dining table by enough to matter.

Cala put one hand on it and pushed.

"Moves."

Lyssa frowned.

One rear foot rocked.

The floor was uneven.

Of course.

Old house.

Old boards.

Nothing level except opinions.

Lyssa crouched.

I looked under.

Each leg ended in a threaded wooden foot with a brass collar.

Adjustable.

"Leveling feet," I said.

"I know."

"I was helping."

"You were naming."

Cruel accuracy.

She turned rear foot half a turn.

Cala pushed again.

Still slight rock.

Another quarter.

Stable.

Then Lyssa plugged the white cord into wall feed.

The flat-line rail stayed dark.

She pressed brass tab.

A thin pale line appeared across the tabletop.

Not bright.

Not beam.

A clean white-blue line exactly parallel to the stone rail.

Tavi gasped.

I also wanted to.

Mine stayed internal.

Lyssa laid gray cloth across table.

The line showed through weave as a faint straight reference.

She shifted cloth.

Pulled one edge.

Pinned it.

The line stayed.

No heat.

No cutting.

No moving thread.

Just geometry made visible.

I loved it.

This was unfortunate.

"That is excellent," I said.

Lyssa looked at me.

"It's a line."

"A reliable line is civilization."

Cala touched table edge.

"How much feed?"

"Less than lamp."

"How do you know?"

"Seller showed the mark."

She pointed under brass tab.

Three tiny dots and one open circle.

Cala recognized it before I did.

"Low draw."

"Yes."

There.

Household magical literacy that did not belong to me.

Good.

Cala nodded.

Not approval exactly.

Acceptance.

"Day use. Lamp gets plate after dark."

Lyssa said, "Fine."

"And fold it if we need floor."

"Fine."

"No pins left on it if Tavi is awake."

"That one was already true."

Tavi said, "I don't eat pins."

Nobody had accused him of eating pins.

Concerning defense.

Lyssa spent the next hour actually using the table.

That mattered more than setup.

Gray cloth became two pieces.

Then three.

She used the light line to square one edge.

Chalked another.

Folded a narrow hem.

Nothing magical happened beyond the line.

Which made the magic better.

It was not a miracle.

It was a tool.

A good one.

I sat at dining table and tried not to watch.

Failed.

"What are you making?"

"Work cover."

"For what?"

"Work."

"Excellent detail."

"Customer wants covers for two instrument cases."

"Musical?"

"Maybe."

"Magical?"

"Maybe."

"Do you care?"

"Only dimensions and closures."

Healthy.

She marked another edge.

I said, "So you bought table because case covers?"

"No."

"Because green vest?"

"No."

"Because mysterious future sewing empire?"

She stopped.

Looked at me.

"Because I'm tired of asking Cala whether she needs the table every time I spread something out."

There.

Again.

I shut up.

Cala was in the kitchen side, close enough to hear.

She said nothing.

That was probably wise.

Lyssa continued.

"I have enough work that moving it all twice a day is stupid. Not enough work for a room. Table is enough."

That was a very good sentence.

Not dramatic.

A threshold.

Enough work for furniture.

Not enough for a room.

I wrote it mentally and hated myself.

"Makes sense," I said.

"I know."

She was enjoying this.

Then somebody knocked.

Not Vale.

Not Hessa.

Already a good day.

Cala opened door.

A woman stood outside holding a long narrow parcel wrapped in brown cloth.

Maybe forty.

Maybe less.

Hard to tell under practical hair.

"Lyssa?"

Lyssa stood.

"Yes."

The woman stepped inside only after Cala moved aside.

Good house.

She looked at work table.

Then at Lyssa.

"You got one."

Lyssa smiled.

"This morning."

"Good."

No introduction to me.

Also good.

The woman unwrapped parcel enough to show a long dark sleeve or tube of fabric with a stiffened mouth.

Not clothing.

Maybe equipment cover.

Along one seam ran little copper-colored knots.

Decorative?

Functional?

Not mine.

The woman said, "This is the other one. Same width as first, but mouth sits wrong."

Lyssa took it.

Looked at seam.

Then laid it on new table.

The pale line crossed underneath.

She aligned the stiff mouth.

"Not same width."

The woman frowned.

"It is."

Lyssa measured with cord.

Once.

Then again.

"Body is. Mouth isn't."

She pointed.

"This side turns in sooner."

The woman leaned.

"Can you fix it?"

"Probably."

"Without touching knots?"

Lyssa looked at copper-colored seam knots.

"Yes. I think."

"Think?"

"I haven't opened it."

Good.

The woman nodded.

"I need both by fourth day."

"I can do this one by third if first fit is still right."

"It is."

"Then third day."

They discussed price quietly enough that I could have heard if I tried.

I did not try.

This was harder than not asking Mara about artifacts.

The woman paid something.

Lyssa put coin away without showing me.

Correct.

Then the woman left.

I waited until door closed.

"You have customers coming here now."

Lyssa froze for half a second.

Not because it was wrong.

Because she was deciding whether I had said something stupid.

"Sometimes."

"Since when?"

"A while."

I looked at Cala.

Cala looked innocent.

Terrible liar.

"A while?"

Lyssa said, "You are not here all day every day."

Right.

Important fact.

The house continued existing while I left it.

Again.

I had learned this theoretically.

Practice remained difficult.

"Do they all come here?"

"No."

"How many?"

"Greg."

"Right."

I stopped.

Her work did not need to become my dataset because I had noticed furniture.

The table had arrived because the work already existed.

Not the other way around.

That was the part I had missed.

I stood.

My shoulder bag hung by usual hook.

Five copper upstairs.

Twenty-six Vale.

Holdfast high.

Rest stone boxed.

Oval home.

All my little systems had become very visible to me lately.

Counts.

Artifacts.

Debt.

Work offers.

Routes.

And Lyssa had quietly crossed a threshold measured in floor space.

Enough work for furniture.

I walked the new room route again.

Past dining table.

Turn by basket.

Chair now side wall.

Lyssa's work table open under front window.

Enough clearance.

When folded, more.

"What?" she asked.

"Checking route."

"Again?"

"The room changed."

"It changed six feet."

"That is a lot when your turning radius includes wood."

She looked at my crutches.

"Okay."

Not apology.

Not pity.

Just recalibration.

She moved one scrap basket two inches farther under table.

"Better?"

I walked.

"Yes."

That was enough.

Cala sat in relocated chair.

She tested it by sitting harder.

Apparently satisfied.

"Window draft?" she asked.

Lyssa looked at table.

"Good for chalk. Bad for loose paper."

"You'll learn."

"Yes."

Tavi crawled under table again.

Lyssa looked down.

"Out."

"I'm checking if I still fit."

"You do."

"How do you know?"

"Because you are under it."

Strong evidence.

He crawled out.

By midday the room looked almost normal.

That was the trick.

New things became normal quickly when they worked.

The chair belonged on side wall now.

The basket belonged under hooks.

My stool belonged upstairs.

Lyssa's table belonged by front window.

A thin pale line crossed gray cloth when she pressed the brass tab.

Then disappeared when she released it.

No explosion.

No appraisal.

No mysterious provenance.

No hidden legendary maker that I recognized from sixty years in the future.

Just a used professional tool bought by somebody who needed it.

I found that more interesting than I expected.

That evening she folded both leaves up.

Braces released.

Legs folded inward.

The table became narrow against wall.

Not invisible.

Still hers.

Cala brought chair slightly forward.

Dinner happened.

No one moved a pile of cloth first.

There.

Benefit.

Lyssa looked at clear dining table.

Then at me.

"Worth it?"

I smiled.

"I don't know what it cost."

"Exactly."

Cruel.

Cala laughed.

Tavi asked, "Can the line cut bread?"

"No," all three of us said.

He looked disappointed.

Good.

After dinner I went upstairs.

The little stool sat near my bed now.

Actually better location.

I sat on it.

This was irritating.

Five copper.

Twenty-six Vale.

Magic fifty-three, fifty-three, one hundred fifteen.

I did not add anything.

Nothing had changed in my numbers.

The house had changed anyway.

I opened my narrow scrap.

Almost wrote:

LYSSA WORK TABLE / FLAT-LINE RAIL

Stopped.

Not mine.

I closed the scrap.

That felt strangely important.

Some things could become part of my life without becoming part of my inventory.

Downstairs, the faint click of Lyssa folding the table brace carried up through the floor.

Then Cala said something I couldn't hear.

Lyssa laughed.

The house continued.

I sat on my relocated stool.

Apparently that was my contribution.

Clear space.

Then get out of the way.
