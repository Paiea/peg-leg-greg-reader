CHAPTER 353

THE BAD LANTERN

Eight copper was enough money to become annoying.

Not rich.

Not comfortable.

Annoying.

At five copper, most interesting things solved themselves by being impossible.

At eight, I had to make decisions.

I discovered this while standing in the Lower Steps with a lantern in my hand that did not produce light.

"Six," the woman said.

I looked at the lantern.

"It is bad at being a lantern."

"Five, then."

That was not how I expected bargaining to work.

I looked at her.

She was perhaps forty, broad through the shoulders, with gray beginning at both temples and three stacked wooden trays at her feet.

Not a stall.

Not a shop.

A building clearance.

The narrow house behind her had its front door open and half its life on the street.

Chairs.

Two bed frames.

A cracked wash basin.

Cook pots.

A bundle of curtain rods.

Three lamps.

One small household warming plate with the core removed.

And the lantern.

I had not come to buy anything.

I had come for onions.

This was why planning was useless.

"Why five?" I asked.

"Because you insulted it."

"That usually raises the price."

"I want it gone."

Good answer.

The lantern was squat and ugly.

Dark brass body.

Square glass on four sides.

Handle wrapped in old black cord.

No flame cup.

No wick.

A thumb-sized cloudy stone sat behind the rear plate.

The front shutter had three positions marked by shallow cuts.

Open.

Half.

Closed.

Except open did nothing.

The woman had demonstrated that already.

She pressed the rear stud.

Stone warmed faintly.

Glass stayed dark.

Then she turned the front shutter to half.

Still dark.

Closed.

Still dark.

"Registered?"

"No current registration. Household light."

"Old paper?"

She pointed to one tray.

"Somewhere in that if my brother didn't throw it away."

"Maker mark?"

She turned lantern over.

A shallow stamp under base.

Three reeds inside a circle.

I knew it.

Not the maker.

The family.

Or I thought I did.

That was worse.

First life.

A long time later.

Camp equipment.

Cheap field shelters.

People complaining because the newer ones were lighter and the old ones made everything look sick.

Not light.

That was the wrong memory.

I looked through one dark pane.

"Can I see it active again?"

"You saw it."

"I saw the stone warm."

"That's active."

Maybe.

"Can we go inside?"

She narrowed her eyes.

"Why?"

There.

Do not tell seller the question.

"Shade. Hard to see weak light out here."

Reasonable.

True enough.

She shrugged.

"Front room. Don't touch anything else."

I followed her in.

The room was nearly empty.

One table.

Dust rectangles where furniture had been.

Curtains already down.

Bright daylight came through bare window.

Not enough.

"Darker room?"

"You buying a lantern or renting my house?"

"Depends on lantern."

She sighed.

Led me down a short hall.

Back room had no window.

Door open gave a stripe of daylight.

She held lantern.

Pressed rear stud.

Cloudy stone warmed.

No light.

I watched the room.

Not lantern.

Nothing.

"Close door?"

"No."

Fair.

"Mostly?"

She pulled it until only a hand-width gap remained.

Room dimmed.

Lantern remained dark.

But the stripe of light from hall changed.

Not much.

I moved my head.

The wall opposite the gap looked flatter.

Wrong word.

Less bright around the stripe.

I looked at my right hand.

Then lantern.

Then the floor.

Memory arrived properly.

Oh.

Oh, you ugly little bastard.

Three Reeds was not a lamp family.

Not originally.

They made old glare-cutters.

Field signal control.

You put one near a map table or shuttered shelter and it ate spill.

Later models paired with actual light sources.

People called the whole combined unit a lantern because people were lazy and history was cruel.

This one might be only the cutter half.

Or broken.

Or both.

I did not know exact model.

I knew category.

Maybe.

Important word.

Maybe.

"Stud again?"

She pressed it off.

The hallway stripe seemed brighter at its edges.

Subtle.

On.

Edges dulled.

Not darkness.

Not concealment.

Not light removal from whole room.

Something around contrast.

Spill.

Glare.

Maybe.

I kept my face boring.

This was difficult because my face had just become economically interested.

"Still doesn't light," she said.

"No."

True.

"Five."

"You said six outside."

"Then you insulted it."

Right.

"Why are you clearing house?"

She looked at me.

Wrong question.

"Never mind."

"Brother's moving north. House sold. Half this was our mother's."

I did not ask more.

Good.

"Did she use this?"

"Cupboard."

"Ever work?"

"When I was little I remember it making light. Might be remembering another lamp."

Useful uncertainty.

"Any repairs?"

"Not by me."

"Dropped?"

"Probably. It's old."

"Stone replaced?"

"Don't know."

"Can I look for paper?"

"If you put everything back."

I sat on the floor because balancing over a tray while using crutches was stupid.

The paper tray contained receipts, household marks, two dead warranty slips, a folded recipe for something involving pears, and one narrow brown card with Three Reeds circle at top.

Not for this exact lantern.

General household instruction card.

I read only what was there.

THREE REEDS SHADE UNIT

FOR TABLE / WALL / CARRIED SERVICE

KEEP VENTS CLEAR

DO NOT ENCLOSE ACTIVE CORE

PAIR ONLY WITH RATED LAMP ASSEMBLY

There.

Shade unit.

Not lantern.

Below that, half the writing was water-stained.

One surviving line:

...REDUCES STRAY THROW BEYOND SET FACE...

My pulse moved.

Set face.

Front shutter positions.

Not illumination.

Directional spill control.

Maybe.

The card did not identify model.

Did not prove condition.

Did not prove this unit matched card beyond maker family.

But the market problem was now obvious.

The woman was not stupid.

She had an old household object remembered as a lantern, with no current registration, no exact paper, no visible light output, and a core that merely warmed.

Its useful effect was subtle in daylight.

Five copper was not nothing for a thing that appeared broken.

And if it really was an old shade unit, five might be excellent.

Not because it secretly shot lightning.

Because I knew a use.

Theatre.

No.

Not theatre specifically.

Any place where controlling where light did not go mattered.

Camp.

Night travel.

Windows.

Signals.

Observation.

Reading without advertising the reader.

Maybe paired with another lamp.

Maybe carried.

Maybe mounted.

And the downside was already on the card.

Pair only with rated lamp assembly.

Meaning this thing might be useless alone.

Worse, active core produced heat.

Vents clear.

Do not enclose.

So the obvious temptation to wrap it and make a perfect dark box could cook the core or me.

Excellent.

A useful object with instructions against the first stupid thing I wanted to do.

I folded the card exactly where it had been.

"This card goes with it?"

"If you buy it."

"Five?"

"Five."

I had eight.

Three after.

Exactly where I had been before yesterday's work.

That annoyed me.

But cash was not the only thing that counted anymore.

If I bought it, I would have three copper, twenty-six Vale, a holdfast I did not understand, a retired rest stone I had not tested, an oval with bad boundaries, and now a possibly functional shade unit that required compatible lighting hardware I did not own.

This was beginning to resemble a collection assembled by a man who hated convenience.

I looked at the woman.

"Four."

"Five."

"No exact paper."

"Card."

"Family card. Not model paper."

She looked at card.

Then me.

"You know what it is?"

Danger.

"I know the card says shade unit."

"So it works?"

"I don't know."

True.

"You wanted darker room."

"Because weak effects are easier to see in dark rooms."

Also true.

She stared at me.

I waited.

"Five."

She was not moving.

Good seller.

I could walk.

That mattered.

Five copper was sixty-two and a half percent of my cash.

I hated percentages when they were mine.

I looked at the unit again.

The old Three Reeds family had survived into my first life mostly as jokes about ugly military lighting.

That did not mean this specific object worked.

It did mean the family was real enough in my memory that I was not inventing value from one stained card.

I wanted it.

There.

Not curiosity.

Not maybe.

I wanted it.

Because if it worked, even narrowly, it changed night.

Not by making night brighter.

By letting me decide where brightness leaked.

That was a different operating rule.

"Five," I said.

She held out hand.

I paid.

Three copper remained.

Again.

This time it felt different.

Not poorer.

Converted.

Cash into a question with handles.

She gave me the brown family card.

Then found a cloth sack.

"Don't put it in sack active," I said.

She paused.

Looked at card.

"Vents."

"Yes."

"You did know something."

"I can read."

"Saret was right."

I froze.

"You know Saret?"

"Who?"

Right.

My brain had become socially diseased.

"Nobody."

She handed me sack.

I put inactive shade unit inside.

It fit shoulder bag badly but better than holdfast.

Handle protruded.

I wrapped it in cloth so brass did not beat against my hip.

The onions were forgotten.

I remembered them halfway home.

Turned around.

Bought onions.

I did not count the bit-level price because civilization had progressed.

At home, Lyssa saw the handle sticking from my bag.

"No."

"Hello."

"You bought something."

"Technically."

"How much?"

Cala looked over from kitchen.

I considered lying.

Not seriously.

"Five copper."

Silence.

Tavi said, "That's a lot."

Betrayed by youth.

Lyssa put both hands flat on work table.

"Yesterday you made three."

"Correct."

"Today you spent five."

"Also correct."

"On what?"

I took out the shade unit.

Set it on table inactive.

"A bad lantern."

Lyssa closed her eyes.

Cala said, "Take it upstairs."

"It is inactive."

"Still upstairs."

Reasonable artifact policy.

I showed them card first.

Lyssa read.

"Shade unit."

"Yes."

"So not a lantern."

"Probably."

"Does it work?"

"Possibly."

"Five copper."

"You keep returning to that."

"Because it is five copper."

Fair.

I explained only what I had observed.

Core warmed.

No emitted light.

In darker room, hallway spill seemed to flatten at edges when active.

Family card said reduces stray throw beyond set face.

No exact model paper.

No proof of rated lamp compatibility.

No test beyond seller demonstration.

Lyssa listened.

Then pointed at front shutter.

"Set face."

"Maybe."

"And this turns it?"

"Maybe."

"You don't know anything."

"I know more than when I bought the holdfast."

"That is not comforting."

Cala asked, "Danger?"

"Heat if active. Vents must stay clear. Card says don't enclose active core. Pair only with rated lamp assembly."

"Do you have rated lamp assembly?"

"No."

Cala pointed upstairs.

"Wonderful."

I took it upstairs.

Did not activate it.

That was important.

I had already seen enough to justify the purchase decision.

Home was not test bench.

Also I had no compatible lamp.

I put the shade unit on a different shelf from holdfast.

Not above Tavi reach merely in theory.

Actually above it.

Family card beneath.

Then I sat.

Three copper.

Again.

But now I owned something I had wanted strongly enough to spend five.

That was new.

The load lock had taught me I could want and lose.

This taught me I could want and choose.

I took out scrap.

Wrote:

THREE REEDS SHADE UNIT / 5 C

NO EXACT MODEL PAPER

FAMILY CARD ONLY

CORE WARMS / NO LIGHT OUTPUT OBSERVED

DARK ROOM: POSSIBLE REDUCTION OF STRAY LIGHT EDGE / NOT MEASURED

CARD: REDUCES STRAY THROW BEYOND SET FACE

KEEP VENTS CLEAR

DO NOT ENCLOSE ACTIVE CORE

PAIR ONLY WITH RATED LAMP ASSEMBLY

NO HOME TEST

Then:

3 C CASH

26 C VALE

I looked at those numbers.

Did not write KEEP IT.

That instruction had lasted one day.

Good instructions were allowed to expire when the world changed.

I added:

FIND COMPATIBLE LIGHT CONTEXT BEFORE TEST

Then stopped.

No price target.

No immediate shopping list.

No plan to drag it to Mara.

I knew the broad family better than that.

What I needed was not appraisal.

I needed a lamp.

Not to own.

To borrow, rent, observe, or access safely.

That was a different problem.

Downstairs, Lyssa called, "Did you at least get the onions?"

I looked at the sack beside my chair.

"Yes."

"Good."

A pause.

"Then you're only mostly stupid."

I smiled.

Five copper for a bad lantern.

Three left.

Twenty-six owed.

And for the first time in a while, the thing I wanted next was not more money.

It was the right kind of darkness.
