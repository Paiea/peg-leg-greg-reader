CHAPTER 356

THE BLUE HOUR

The fog came in speaking with other people's voices.

This was less dramatic than it sounds.

Carrow had procedures.

Most frightening things became tolerable once somebody painted instructions on a board.

I was with Lyssa when the first bell changed.

Not rang.

Changed.

The afternoon street bell at East Cloth normally gave one clean bronze note before the late-market shift.

This one struck and answered itself from half a street away.

Same pitch.

Same bell.

Wrong direction.

Lyssa stopped.

So did three other people nearby.

A woman carrying folded baskets looked toward an alley.

The actual bell tower stood behind us.

"Blue hour," she said.

Then kept walking.

I looked at Lyssa.

"Is that local terminology or are we about to become weather?"

"You don't know?"

"I know echo fog. I don't know what Carrow calls everything because Carrow enjoys branding."

That was true.

Old life had included several kinds of mana-heavy fog that bent sound.

Usually near rivers.

Sometimes old battlefields.

One terrible marsh outside Vellen where every frog sounded like it was sitting inside your left ear.

That one had not been dangerous.

Just persuasive.

This fog was already visible at the end of the street.

Not gray.

Blue.

Very faint blue.

More like daylight had forgotten how to leave properly.

It rolled low first.

Ankles.

Cart wheels.

Thresholds.

Then climbed.

Shopkeepers reacted before anyone panicked.

One pulled a bright yellow rope across the mouth of a side lane.

Another turned a hanging sign sideways.

A boy in a red vest started walking down the street ringing a small handbell twice, pausing, twice again.

Unlike the tower bell, his bell did not seem to duplicate.

Interesting.

I did not ask why.

Growth.

Lyssa grabbed my sleeve.

"Marra's first."

"We were already going to Marra's."

"I'm reminding you before you decide to study weather."

"I was observing public procedure."

"With your whole face."

Again.

My face needed regulation.

We had been heading to Marra's green door because Lyssa had cloth to return and a message to deliver.

Not my cloth.

Not my message.

I was there because we had left the house at the same time and because walking with somebody was sometimes simply walking with somebody.

This concept remained underdeveloped in my professional life.

The blue fog reached us at knee height.

Cold.

Not freezing.

Wet without making cloth wet immediately.

Then someone said my name behind me.

"Greg."

I turned.

Nobody.

Lyssa did not.

"Don't."

"That sounded like Cala."

"Cala is home."

"I know."

"Then don't."

Reasonable.

The same voice came from ahead now.

"Greg."

Not exactly Cala.

Close enough to catch attention.

Wrong enough once I listened.

Echo fog did not create voices from nothing.

Usually.

It carried existing sound along mana gradients and returned it late, bent, or displaced.

A name spoken somewhere could arrive from somewhere else.

A laugh could move streets.

Sometimes your own steps followed you.

The danger was not supernatural deception.

The danger was human reflex.

We followed voices.

Especially familiar ones.

The red-vest boy kept his paired handbell rhythm.

Two.

Pause.

Two.

People started favoring main streets.

Side lanes emptied.

A cart driver stopped before turning and physically checked both directions rather than calling around the corner.

A woman with two children tied a strip of cloth from her wrist to the older child's wrist.

Local adaptation.

Good.

"Do we need rope?" I asked.

Lyssa looked at me.

"I am not tying myself to you."

"You say that now."

"I will say it in worse weather too."

We kept walking.

The fog rose to waist height.

Then chest.

Visibility remained decent.

Thirty feet, maybe more.

Buildings softened.

People became darker shapes before becoming people again.

Sound got stupid.

A vendor shouted, "Apples!"

The word came from the roof.

A baby cried from somewhere under the street.

Someone laughed directly beside my right shoulder.

There was nobody there.

I did not turn.

Progress.

Lyssa's hand remained on my sleeve.

Not gripping tightly.

Contact.

Useful.

"You do this often?" I asked.

"Once or twice most wet seasons. Sometimes none."

"Bad?"

"Depends."

"Useful local answer."

"If it stays light blue, mostly annoying. If it goes dark blue, people close more streets."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

Good.

She was allowed not to know things about the city she lived in.

People used systems without studying them.

I knew this.

I was trying to become one of them occasionally.

At the next crossing, a city worker had already hung a white board from a post.

BLUE HOUR

MAIN ROUTES OPEN

SIDE CUTS CLOSED WHERE ROPED

DO NOT FOLLOW VOICES INTO CLOSED LANES

VISUAL CONFIRM BEFORE CROSSING CART ROUTES

CHILDREN / CONFUSED PERSONS TO MARKED DOORS

Below that, someone had added in chalk:

IF YOU HEAR YOURSELF, IGNORE HIM

I liked Carrow.

"Marked doors?" I asked.

Lyssa pointed.

Several shops had blue-white squares hung outside.

Temporary refuge points.

Not shelters from fog itself.

Places for children, lost people, frightened people, maybe drunk people, to wait until someone came.

A bakery had one.

So did a cooper.

A small shrine door farther down.

Distributed city competence again.

No grand emergency center required.

Then Lyssa's voice said, "Greg, left," from behind me.

At the same time the actual Lyssa tugged my sleeve right.

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

"That was rude," I said.

"What?"

"You told me left from behind."

"I did not."

"I know."

Her expression changed slightly.

Not fear.

Attention.

The fog had climbed to face height.

Still pale blue.

Still visually manageable.

But voices were becoming more convincing because they were closer to current speech.

The echo had less delay now.

Maybe.

I was not timing it.

Not my job.

We reached Marra's street.

Rope across the shortest cut.

Closed.

Lyssa stopped.

"Long way."

"Yes."

She did not complain.

The green door was only two streets away by normal route.

Now four.

This was why magic mattered even when nobody cast spells at anyone.

A weather event could rearrange a city.

We took the long way.

Halfway around, somebody shouted Lyssa's name.

"Lyssa!"

She turned.

I caught her elbow.

She froze.

Not because I had authority.

Because she realized at the same moment.

The voice came again from a closed alley.

"Lyssa! Wait!"

A woman's voice.

Could have been Marra.

Could have been Jessa.

Could have been anyone who had said Lyssa's name within the wrong patch of fog.

Lyssa stared toward the yellow rope.

Then at me.

"That sounded like Jessa."

"Do you see Jessa?"

"No."

"Then not enough."

She exhaled.

"I know."

Important.

No lecture.

No Greg saves competent woman from weather.

She knew.

Her body had moved before the rest of her did.

Mine had done the same with Cala's voice.

We kept walking.

Then the problem got more ordinary.

We lost contact.

Not dramatically.

No scream.

No magical wall.

A handcart came through the crossing.

People shifted.

I moved to the edge to give wheels room.

Lyssa stepped around a woman carrying a basket.

Someone between us stopped suddenly because a voice called their name from a doorway.

Three bodies changed lanes.

The fog swallowed half a step.

And Lyssa was gone.

Not gone.

I could see six people.

Then eight.

Then a tall woman in dark cloth who was not Lyssa.

"Lyssa?"

Mistake.

Her name returned from two directions.

"Lyssa?"

My own voice, displaced.

Excellent.

I shut up.

The paired handbell sounded somewhere ahead.

Two.

Pause.

Two.

Main route.

Stay put or move to marked door.

Those were likely local rules.

I looked for blue-white square.

Cooper's doorway twenty feet away.

I moved there.

Not because I thought Lyssa would magically know.

Because if both of us used public procedure, we had a chance of behaving predictably.

The cooper had become a temporary collection of people who were not in crisis but had lost somebody within thirty seconds.

An old man.

Two girls maybe ten and twelve.

A young delivery worker holding three wrapped parcels.

The cooper himself stood at door with a chalk board.

"Name?" he asked me.

"Greg. Looking for Lyssa. Tall. Dark skin. Dark cloth bundle."

He wrote:

GREG / LYSSA

"Stay here unless you see her."

"Good."

One of the girls said, "My brother sounds like he's in the wall."

The other said, "He always sounds like that."

Sibling support.

I leaned on my crutches and waited.

This was difficult.

I wanted to move.

Of course I wanted to move.

Moving felt like doing.

Doing felt safer than waiting.

This instinct had caused several problems across two lives.

I stayed.

A minute passed.

Maybe two.

Then Lyssa's voice said, very close, "Greg?"

I did not answer.

The cooper looked at me.

"You waiting for Lyssa?"

"Yes."

"That sounded near."

"Yes."

"You going?"

"No."

"Good."

Professional validation.

Then someone touched my shoulder.

I almost hit them with a crutch.

Not because I am violent.

Because I am experienced.

I stopped before doing it.

Lyssa stood behind me.

Actual Lyssa.

Visible.

Annoyed.

"Why didn't you answer?"

"Because your voice had already been lying to me."

"I was standing right there."

"Now you are."

She looked at cooper board.

Saw our names.

"You went to a marked door."

"I followed procedure."

"You?"

"I am capable of growth under supervision."

The cooper said, "Take reunion elsewhere."

Reasonable.

We moved aside.

Lyssa explained she had done almost the same thing.

When she lost me, she went to the nearest marked door on the other side of crossing.

They had a runner going between marked doors every few minutes carrying written names.

Her door had sent:

LYSSA LOOKING FOR GREG / CRUTCHES

My cooper had received it before she walked over.

I had not noticed because the runner came from behind me.

Again.

Distributed procedure.

No magic solution.

Just writing.

"Crutches," I said.

"Useful description."

"Tall and devastating would have worked."

"Too many men would answer."

"Cruel."

She touched my sleeve again.

This time deliberately.

"New rule."

"Which?"

"If we lose each other, marked door."

"Nearest?"

"Nearest open marked door. Don't chase voices."

"And if no marked door?"

She thought.

"Last place we both saw each other."

"Good."

"And don't shout my name unless you can see me."

"Also good."

"Because then your stupid voice multiplies."

"My voice is excellent."

"There were four of you."

"Nightmare."

"Exactly."

That became shorthand.

Not romantic.

Not symbolic.

Useful.

We finally reached Marra's green door.

It was marked blue-white.

Of course it was.

Jessa opened it before Lyssa knocked twice.

"You took forever."

Lyssa said, "Fog."

Jessa said, "Yes, I have windows."

Different speech register.

Good.

Marra was inside with two customers and a boy I did not know sitting near wall because he had gotten separated from his aunt.

The boy held a wooden token with an address scratched into it.

Marra had already sent someone to the nearest marked door with his name.

No one made a speech.

Lyssa delivered cloth.

Delivered message.

Marra answered with work shorthand I did not understand.

Lyssa understood.

I stayed out of it.

Progress continued irresponsibly.

The fog deepened outside while we were there.

Not dark blue.

Still pale.

But enough that Marra said, "Wait for second clearing bell."

"How long?" I asked.

"Could be ten minutes. Could be an hour."

"Excellent scheduling."

She looked at me.

"You can leave."

"I enjoy waiting."

"Nobody believes that."

We waited.

The separated boy's aunt arrived first.

She came in angry enough to survive any weather.

"You left the spice stall."

The boy said, "You told me go to marked door if I lost you."

She stopped.

Closed her mouth.

Then hugged him.

"Yes. Good. Still angry."

Excellent parenting.

Ten minutes later, the city bell struck once.

Then struck again from three different streets.

Nobody moved.

Marra said, "Not clearing."

Good.

Five minutes later, paired handbells started outside.

Then a different bell pattern.

Three slow notes.

One pause.

Three slow notes.

Jessa opened door and looked at street.

A red-vest runner shouted while remaining visible:

"MAIN ROUTES CLEARING. SIDE CUTS STAY CLOSED UNTIL WHITE ROPES."

His voice returned from somewhere above us after he finished.

Nobody followed the echo.

We left by main route.

The blue fog was thinning from the top down.

Faces returned before feet.

Street sounds still arrived wrong, but less convincingly.

At one crossing, I heard myself say "Greg" from an alley.

I ignored him.

He had terrible judgment.

By the time we reached home, the fog had dropped back to knee height.

Cala opened door.

"You took forever."

"Fog."

"I have windows."

I looked at Lyssa.

Lyssa looked at me.

Apparently this was a local sentence.

Tavi ran in from the other room.

"Did you hear yourself?"

"Yes."

"What did you say?"

"Mostly my name."

"Did you answer?"

"Once."

He looked disappointed.

"You're not supposed to."

"I learned."

"I knew already."

"Congratulations."

He began explaining blue-hour rules in the way children explain weather they have survived twice.

"If it sounds like somebody behind you but they're not behind you then you don't go behind you and if you get lost you go to the blue-white door but not the door if the square is covered because that means full and you don't follow dogs because dogs can hear the wrong thing too and last year Kalo followed his own whistle all the way to the fish street and his mom got so mad she made him hold her bag for like a week."

I believed approximately half of this.

Possibly all.

Cala asked, "You two stayed together?"

Lyssa said, "No."

I said, "Briefly."

Cala looked at us.

Lyssa explained marked doors.

No drama.

No blame.

Then she said, "We have a rule now."

"Marked door," I said.

"Nearest open marked door," she corrected.

"And no shouting names unless visible."

Cala nodded.

"Good."

That was all.

No artifact came out.

No money moved.

No job appeared.

No one asked me to solve the fog.

The city had managed before I arrived and would continue after I died.

This was comforting.

Upstairs, I wrote nothing about mana density.

Nothing about echo delay.

Nothing about why paired handbells seemed easier to localize.

Those belonged to people who actually studied blue hour.

I wrote only:

BLUE HOUR

DO NOT FOLLOW FAMILIAR VOICE WITHOUT VISUAL CONFIRM

IF SEPARATED: NEAREST OPEN MARKED DOOR

NO SHOUTING NAMES UNLESS VISIBLE

Then:

LYSSA KNOWS SAME RULE

I stared at that.

Too formal.

Crossed it out.

Wrote:

MARKED DOOR.

That was enough.

Some relationships accumulated through declarations.

Some through fights.

Some through promises.

Ours had apparently acquired emergency weather shorthand.

I liked that better.
