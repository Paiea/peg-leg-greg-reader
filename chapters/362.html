CHAPTER 362

THE LOOKOUT

I became a lookout because Tavi wanted to see a fish fly.

This was not a metaphor.

Carrow had actual flying fish.

Apparently nobody had considered telling me.

"They're not fish," Lyssa said.

Tavi was standing on a chair at breakfast, which meant Cala had already told him to sit down at least once.

"They are," he said.

"They're river kites."

"Kites aren't alive."

"Those are."

"Then fish."

"That is not how categories work."

I looked at Cala.

"What are river kites?"

She looked surprised.

"You don't know?"

"I have successfully not known for nineteen years."

"They come through lower spill after first warm rain."

"Doing what?"

Tavi answered before she could.

"Flying."

Useful.

Cala said, "Gliding. Mostly."

Lyssa tore bread.

"Jump from water, catch the lift off spill channels, land upriver."

I stopped eating.

"Why?"

Three people looked at me.

Not because question was stupid.

Because apparently this was childhood knowledge.

Tavi said, "Because they're fish."

That explained nothing and satisfied him completely.

"Breeding run," Cala said. "Or feeding. Depends who you ask."

Better.

"Magical?"

Lyssa shrugged.

"A little."

Carrow's relationship with magic remained offensive.

A little.

Like seasoning.

"How little?"

Cala pointed spoon at me.

"You can go look without making it work."

I had been known too long.

Tavi bounced on chair.

"We have to go before second bell because Naro said they were already at lower gates and if we miss them then they go past mill turn and you can't see them good because roofs and also his brother said one hit a woman in face last year but he lies sometimes, except maybe not about fish because why would you lie about fish when real fish already fly?"

There.

Tavi speech had become a cart rolling downhill.

I liked it.

"Who is Naro?" I asked.

"Naro."

"Excellent."

Cala said, "Boy two doors east."

"I know his mother," Lyssa said.

"Do I?"

"No."

Good.

I finished breakfast.

Eight copper stayed in purse.

No work scheduled.

No reason not to go watch magical fish make poor decisions.

Tavi was already at door.

"Shoes," Cala said.

He looked down.

Bare feet.

"I knew."

"Then do it."

He ran upstairs.

Lyssa said, "You taking him?"

"Apparently."

"I was asking Cala."

Cala said, "Greg can."

I looked between them.

"Interesting transfer of authority."

"Bring him back," Cala said.

"Alive?"

She looked at me.

"Greg."

"Alive."

Tavi returned with shoes on wrong feet.

I pointed.

He looked.

"They still fit."

"That is not the standard."

He changed them while standing.

Nearly fell.

Caught wall.

I waited.

"Sit," I said.

"I can do it."

"You demonstrated. Sit."

He sat.

Shoes corrected.

We left.

I had taken Tavi places before.

Not often enough to have system.

Enough to know children did not move through city like adults.

Adults selected destination and route.

Children selected every object between destination and route.

A blue bottle cap.

A dog with one white ear.

A chalk circle somebody had drawn around a drain.

A man carrying six chairs.

"Why six?"

"Maybe he needs six chairs."

"But why all now?"

"Because carrying one six times is worse."

"What if he drops them?"

"Then carrying one six times becomes competitive."

Tavi watched man until corner hid him.

Then ran three steps to catch up.

"Walking," I said.

"I am."

"That was running."

"Only little."

"Still running."

"You can't run."

I looked at him.

He looked at my crutches.

Not insult.

Observation.

"Correct."

"Can you run with those?"

"Badly."

"Show me."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I enjoy having teeth."

He considered.

"You could run without falling."

"Probably."

"Then show me."

"No."

"Why?"

This was going well.

Lower spill sat beyond dye lanes where two old water channels came down from east cut and split around a wedge of warehouses.

I had crossed upper bridges.

Never stood at lower gates during fish season.

Apparently season was three mornings long some years and ten days others.

Nobody agreed.

Crowd told me we had found it before water did.

Families lined stone rail.

Children on shoulders.

Vendors with hot buns and paper cones of salted seeds.

Two city workers in green vests had roped off the lowest ledge.

Not because fish were dangerous.

Because people were idiots near moving water.

Comforting.

Tavi stopped.

"There."

I saw nothing.

Then water exploded.

A silver shape came out of spill channel at an angle no fish had business attempting.

Longer than my hand.

Flat fins snapped wide.

Not wings.

Close enough to offend taxonomy.

Blue-white lines lit along its sides.

It climbed.

Actually climbed.

Not much.

Maybe ten feet above water.

Then glided across lower basin, curved in air, and hit upper channel with a splash.

Crowd cheered.

I stared.

Tavi looked at me with enormous satisfaction.

"Fish."

"Fine."

"Say it."

"Fish."

"Flying fish."

"River kite."

"Greg."

"You sound like Lyssa."

"Flying fish."

Another launched.

Then three.

One failed badly and slapped sideways into stone lip before flopping back into current.

Nobody gasped.

It recovered.

Tried again twenty seconds later.

Successful.

I loved it immediately.

Not because artifact.

Not because useful.

Because world had apparently hidden ridiculous fish from me and expected me to accept this.

Blue-white side lines brightened only during air phase.

Maybe field interaction.

Maybe body-generated lift.

Maybe something in water.

I stopped.

Cala's spoon pointed at me in memory.

You can go look without making it work.

Fine.

I looked.

Tavi squeezed between two adults toward rail.

I caught back of his shirt.

"No."

"I can see."

"From here."

"People are tall."

"Cruel world."

"I can't see."

That was true.

Crowd had thickened.

I looked around.

There was a low freight step beside warehouse wall, maybe a foot high.

Bad for me.

Good for him.

Also crowded.

A stack of empty produce crates sat farther back.

Absolutely not.

Tavi saw them same moment.

"I can stand on those."

"No."

"They're strong."

"No."

"You didn't check."

"I checked spiritually."

"That's not checking."

Damn child.

A woman beside us laughed.

She had a baby tied against chest and a girl maybe eight holding sleeve.

"He's right," she said.

"Please don't strengthen him."

"Sorry."

She was not sorry.

Tavi pointed at her.

"She says."

"She is not in charge of you."

Woman said, "Definitely not."

Good.

A city worker moved along rope.

"Keep children behind yellow stones. No crates, no rail sitting. If you need a viewing step, west wall has public blocks."

There.

Infrastructure defeated improvisation.

"Public blocks," I said.

Tavi groaned like government had ruined childhood.

We moved west.

Public blocks were three broad stone risers built into wall.

Not seating exactly.

Viewing steps.

Children occupied most.

Parents behind.

I found Tavi space on lowest block.

"Stay on this one."

"Second is better."

"Lowest."

"Why?"

"Because I can reach you without climbing."

He looked at my leg.

Then at step.

"Okay."

No argument.

That landed strangely.

Not pity.

Geometry.

He climbed lowest block and immediately forgot me because six fish launched together.

For ten minutes I was not Greg the collector or model or reader or whatever else city had temporarily named me.

I was an adult standing behind a child at water.

This turned out to involve more work than expected.

"Don't lean."

"I'm not."

"Your entire body is past your feet."

"But I'm not falling."

"That is a temporary success condition."

"Look!"

I looked.

A fish came high.

Higher than others.

Blue-white side light bright enough to see in daylight.

It crossed over rope line.

Over crowd.

People ducked.

The fish did not.

It hit canvas awning over seed vendor.

THUMP.

Canvas sagged.

Fish flopped once.

Vendor shouted something impolite.

Then fish slid down canvas, launched off edge somehow, and smacked a man in shoulder.

Crowd erupted.

Man caught fish reflexively.

This was wrong move.

The side lines flashed.

His hair lifted straight up.

He yelled and dropped it.

Fish hit street, flopped twice, then skitter-glided under rope and back into water.

Tavi was vibrating.

"Naro didn't lie!"

"About woman in face?"

"Fish hit man. Same."

"Those are different people and body parts."

"But fish hit."

Fair.

Green-vest worker shouted, "Do not catch kites! Let them fall!"

The vendor was inspecting awning.

Man's hair slowly lowered.

No injury beyond dignity.

Good.

Tavi turned to me.

"If one hits us, can I touch it?"

"No."

"What if it can't get back?"

"Worker."

"What if worker isn't there?"

"We find one."

"What if all workers leave?"

"Why would all workers leave during fish?"

"Maybe lunch."

"Then fish have won."

He accepted this.

Crowd shifted.

Somebody behind us said, "Big run coming."

I looked toward lower channel.

Water had changed.

Not level.

Texture.

Silver flashes beneath surface.

Dozens.

Maybe more.

Blue-white light flickered under water like broken reflection.

Workers started moving.

Not panic.

Procedure.

"Children down one step," one called. "Keep west lane open. Big run."

Parents repeated it.

Children complained.

Tavi stayed lowest already.

Good decision paid dividends.

I moved closer behind him.

Not touching.

Ready.

First wave launched.

Eight.

Ten.

Hard to count.

Then second.

The air over basin filled with silver bodies.

Some low.

Some high.

Blue-white lines made arcs.

Not organized.

Not school formation.

Chaos with direction.

Every fish wanted upstream and possessed a different opinion about route.

People shouted.

Laughed.

Duck calls moved through crowd.

One fish clipped rope post.

Another landed in empty bun basket and immediately left.

Tavi screamed with joy.

Then he stepped down.

Not toward water.

Sideways.

Following one fish that had landed behind viewing blocks.

I caught shoulder.

"Stay."

"It can't get up."

Fish flopped against wall.

Blue lines flashed.

No clear path to water because crowd feet.

Tavi pulled against my hand.

"Greg."

"Stay."

"It'll die."

Maybe.

I looked for worker.

Nearest green vest was twenty feet away managing rope.

I raised voice.

"Kite behind west blocks!"

Worker heard.

Pointed at another worker farther back.

That one came carrying long shallow net with wooden handle.

Good.

Tavi watched.

Worker did not touch fish.

Slid net under it.

Fish flashed hard enough that net rim glowed pale for a second.

Worker angled it over wall gap.

Fish launched before reaching water and vanished into basin.

Tavi exhaled.

"See?"

I said.

"We helped."

"You stayed. I yelled. Worker helped."

"That's helping."

I considered.

"Yes."

He looked pleased.

Not because he rescued fish.

Because he had been part of correct chain.

Good.

Big run lasted maybe five minutes.

Felt longer.

Then water settled.

Crowd loosened.

People began leaving.

Tavi did not.

"More."

"Probably."

"We wait."

"How long?"

"Until more."

Children understood scheduling differently.

We waited.

I bought nothing because I had eaten breakfast and did not need bun merely because bun existed.

Economic heroism.

Tavi stared at water.

Then asked, "Why do they fly?"

There it was.

I could give broad first-life category.

Mana-assisted migration behavior existed.

Aquatic creatures using field pressure, thermal gradients, air currents, buoyancy changes, temporary fin stiffening, ridiculous evolutionary solutions to geography.

I had seen worse.

I had eaten worse.

Probably.

But I did not know these.

"I don't know," I said.

Then corrected because old habit of sounding ignorant when I knew broad family was also stupid.

"I know ways animals can use mana to move. I don't know which way these do it."

Tavi nodded.

"They jump."

"Yes."

"Then fly."

"Glide."

"Fly."

"Fine."

"So that's how."

He had solved ecology.

I envied him.

A man on upper viewing step leaned down.

Older, white beard, river boots.

"They ride spill lift."

I looked at him.

"Air?"

"Partly. Water throws cold field off lower gate. Warm air comes over stone. Kites catch both."

Maybe.

"You work river?"

"Thirty-two years."

Better source.

"Do side lines create lift?"

He shrugged.

"Lines stiffen fins. Maybe more. Cut one open, lines go dark."

"People cut them open?"

"People eat them."

Tavi looked horrified.

Old man looked at him.

"Not during run."

Tavi remained horrified.

"Ever see them without warm rain?" I asked.

"Small jumps. Not this."

"Why upstream?"

"Egg beds above mill turn."

There.

Local implementation.

Enough.

Not full mechanism.

I stopped asking before observation became interview.

Old man returned to water.

Tavi whispered, "He eats them."

"Probably not these exact ones."

"Fish family."

"You eat chickens."

"Not chickens I know."

Reasonable ethical framework.

Another run came smaller.

Tavi watched every fish.

I watched Tavi half the time.

That was role.

Not expert.

Not investigator.

Lookout.

I was there to notice when his feet moved where they should not.

This became relevant again when Naro appeared.

I identified him by process of elimination.

A boy about Tavi's age came sprinting through west lane yelling, "TAVI!"

Tavi yelled back.

"NARO!"

Science.

Naro had older sister behind him saying, "Stop running," with exhausted precision of someone who had said it since birth.

He stopped only because Tavi existed.

"You saw face fish?"

Tavi said, "Man shoulder."

"I told you."

"You said woman face."

"Last year."

"Greg says different."

Naro looked at me.

"Are you Greg?"

"Unfortunately."

"Tavi says you have magic things."

Tavi froze.

I looked at him.

He looked at water.

Interesting.

"Does he?"

Naro nodded.

"A stone that knows doors."

That was not ideal.

Tavi said quickly, "It doesn't know doors."

"You said."

"I said it clicks."

"At doors."

"Sometimes not doors."

This was becoming peer-reviewed badly.

I crouched as much as crutches allowed, which was not much.

"Tavi."

He looked at me.

"House rule isn't secret magic treasure. But we also don't tell people what is in locked chest."

His face changed.

Worry.

"I didn't tell chest."

"I know."

"Only oval."

"Oval travels with me, so people can see it. Still better not to explain our stuff to everybody."

"Why?"

Naro listened with professional interest.

"Because people get curious. Curious people ask questions, touch things, decide they know what things do, or decide they want them."

Naro said, "I don't want it."

"Good."

"I want to see it."

Exactly.

Tavi looked vindicated and guilty simultaneously.

"Can he?"

"No."

Naro accepted this faster than Tavi.

"Okay."

Children were strange.

Naro's sister arrived.

Maybe thirteen.

Breathing hard.

"Sorry. He runs."

"Observed."

She looked at Tavi.

"Your grown-up?"

I opened mouth.

Tavi said, "Greg."

Not answer.

Apparently enough.

She nodded.

"I'm Mevi."

"Greg."

"I know."

Of course.

Senna's portrait continued doing social damage.

Or Tavi.

Hard to know.

Mevi said, "They're saying last big run before midday."

"Source?"

She pointed at old river man.

Good source.

We stayed.

Naro and Tavi argued taxonomy.

Naro called them spillwings.

Tavi called them flying fish.

Mevi called them river kites.

Old man called them kites.

I refused vote.

Last big run came with less warning.

Water flashed.

Workers called lane clear.

Children down.

This time Tavi moved back before I told him.

Good.

Naro did not.

Mevi caught his shirt.

Shared civilization.

Fish launched.

Dozens again.

One came directly toward viewing blocks.

I saw line before body.

Blue-white streak.

"Down," I said.

Tavi ducked.

Not because city worker.

Because me.

Fish passed where his head had been and struck wall behind us.

THWACK.

It dropped between my right crutch and stone.

Blue lines flared.

I did not move crutch.

Did not touch fish.

Did not panic.

Tavi stayed crouched.

Excellent.

Right boot.

Not painful.

Enough.

"Back," I said.

Tavi backed.

Worker was already coming with net.

Fish flashed again.

Field discharge.

Small.

Gone.

"It hit you."

"Boot."

"A little."

"Yes."

"Any numbness?"

"No. Tingling through boot. Gone."

"I will."

Just fish.

"I ducked."

"You did."

"Before it hit."

"Yes."

"Because you said."

"Also yes."

That mattered to him more than fish hitting me.

Maybe because being competent inside exciting thing felt good.

No tingling returned.

"No."

"Small one."

"No."

"What if biggest one comes after we leave?"

"Then it will live without us."

Still came.

Good luck to her.

"I didn't."

"Correct."
