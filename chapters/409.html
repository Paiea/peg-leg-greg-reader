CHAPTER 384

THE COMPANION

Sleeping on packet deck was less romantic than traveling stories implied.

Mostly because traveling stories cut the benches.

And the snoring.

And the child who had been kicking the same wall for twenty minutes.

Not hard.

Just enough.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Sori opened one eye.

"I will throw him into river."

"You don't mean that."

"I mean adjacent to river."

"Better."

Maro had somehow folded himself into corner bench and looked asleep.

Dask was not asleep.

Of course.

He sat under covered middle deck with guild pamphlet open against one knee and lantern hooded low.

I said, "If you blind yourself before Duskport, I'm not reading signs for you."

"This is not bright."

"It's brighter than closed eyes."

"Most things are."

There.

Packet wisdom.

Bright Gull moved through dark water with steady engine thrum under floorboards and occasional chain-rattle from cargo restraint.

Banks had become shapes.

A few lamps.

Black reeds.

Farmhouses rare now.

More docks.

More industrial lights.

Lower Lume smelled different.

Wider water.

Mud flatter.

Smoke farther away but more constant.

I lay on bench with shoulder bag under head because trusting thirty strangers with twenty-two copper and several weird tools felt optimistic.

Crutches rested against chest and rail.

Not comfortable.

Secure.

Good enough.

Tessa's seat was empty.

That was annoying.

Not because I wanted her back.

Because empty seats made absences visible.

Her medicine case had gone with her.

Good.

Oren maybe got it.

Maybe didn't.

No way to know tonight.

I hated not knowing less than I hated pretending.

Progress.

Still hated it.

Somewhere near first night bell, packet slowed.

Not stop.

Just changed rhythm.

Passengers noticed because everyone notices when thing carrying them changes mind.

Rell Koss came through covered deck.

"Gate Nine queue. Twenty minutes if honest. Forty if not. Stay aboard. Galley open."

Gray-coat father stood immediately.

The one with daughter's hearing.

He had name now because Sori had asked earlier.

**Harven Coil.**

Mid-fifties.

Gray coat too warm for weather.

Hands that kept folding and unfolding same handkerchief.

"Forty?"

Rell looked at him.

"Could be less."

"Could be more."

"Yes."

Harven's jaw worked.

"You said Duskport morning."

"Captain said morning or midday if river and traffic cooperate."

"My daughter's hearing is second bell."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Rell did not take bait.

"Yes."

Harven looked past him toward forward deck.

"Can we pass?"

"Not without slot."

"Can you buy priority?"

There.

Interesting.

Rell's face changed one degree.

"Sometimes."

"Then buy it."

"Packet doesn't carry discretionary gate-priority budget."

Harven laughed once.

Not humor.

"Of course."

Then bird trader stood.

Her name was **Mira Sol**.

She had three reed cages under cargo awning, each holding long-necked gray birds that looked personally offended by existence.

"Do not buy priority," she said.

Harven turned.

"Excuse me?"

"If packet pays emergency rate, captain will pass cost to manifest surcharge. I have twelve birds under commercial ticket and quarantine fee already paid."

"My daughter may be sentenced tomorrow."

"And my birds may be refused if inspection closes before arrival."

"They're birds."

Mira went still.

Bad choice.

Harven knew it after saying it.

Too late.

"They are six months of breeding," Mira said. "And contract deposit. And my sister's farm debt."

"I didn't mean..."

"You meant exactly enough."

There.

Human problem again.

Apparently river had curriculum.

Sori sat up fully.

Dask closed pamphlet.

Maro opened eyes without moving head.

Rell said, "No gate priority purchase tonight. Captain's decision."

Harven looked toward forward stairs.

"Then I want to speak to her."

"She is navigating queue."

"I don't care."

"She does."

Rell moved on.

Harven followed three steps, stopped because crewman blocked forward access politely and completely.

Then he turned back.

Looked at me.

Of course.

Why did everyone keep deciding I had face for this?

"You helped that woman."

I stayed on bench.

"A little."

"Help me."

Sori looked at ceiling.

I said, "With what?"

"Tell captain to buy priority."

There it was.

Again.

Same shape.

Different people.

I could say I didn't know enough.

True.

Useful maybe.

Also starting to sound like shield.

"How much is priority?" I asked.

Harven blinked.

"I don't know."

"Then first question."

Mira said, "Gate Nine after-hours priority isn't fixed. Depends freight queue."

Harven turned on her.

"You know?"

"I ship birds."

"Then help."

She laughed without warmth.

"You called them birds."

"They are birds."

"And she is your daughter. Both can be true while you remain an ass."

Maro coughed into sleeve.

Not hiding laugh well.

Harven rubbed face.

"Fine. I'm sorry."

Mira looked surprised.

So was I.

"Fine," she said.

Not forgiven.

Enough.

Sori said, "What actually happens if you miss second bell?"

Harven sat opposite us.

That was first time he looked old instead of angry.

"Hearing starts second bell. Appeals clerk said family witness statement can be accepted before decision if I am present. If I'm not, counsel can read written statement."

"You have written statement?"

"Yes."

"Then your daughter isn't unrepresented."

"No."

"But you want to be there."

He looked at her.

"She's nineteen."

There.

Not answer.

All answer.

Sori nodded.

Mira's face softened despite herself.

Harven continued.

"She hit a dock foreman."

Dask said, "Why?"

Sori elbowed him.

Harven didn't mind.

"Foreman grabbed her apprentice by throat. She broke his nose. He fell. Hit edge. Lost two teeth. Foreman says assault. Yard says insubordination. Apprentice says foreman started it. Three workers say they didn't see."

There.

Mess.

"What is she appealing?" I asked.

"Work-ban order and six months labor levy."

"Not prison?"

"No."

Some tension left room.

Harven saw it.

"That still destroys her trade."

"I know."

"Do you?"

He stopped himself.

"Sorry."

Mira said, "Learning."

He ignored her.

Sori asked, "What does your being there change?"

Harven looked down at handkerchief.

"Maybe nothing."

There.

He laughed once.

"That's the worst part. Maybe nothing. But if she looks up and I'm not there..."

He did not finish.

That was wanting something badly.

Not legal leverage.

Presence.

Hard to price.

Hard to procedure.

Mira said, quieter, "My inspection closes third bell, not second."

Harven looked at her.

"So?"

"So if priority costs too much, don't buy it for me."

That was not same as consent.

Not quite.

She stared at cages.

"But if delay goes past first bell, I need to know. I can send runner from first dock if we arrive south quay. Quarantine office might hold clerk if warned. Maybe."

There.

Another maybe.

But hers.

Harven said, "Thank you."

Mira shrugged.

"Still birds."

One bird hissed.

"See?" she said. "Assholes."

Humanity restored.

I got up.

Shoulder fine.

Right hand fine.

Crutches planted against moving deck without much thought now.

"I'm going to ask gate cost."

Sori looked at me.

"Captain said no priority."

"I'm not asking her to buy."

"Good."

There.

Different problem.

Facts still useful.

Just not verdict.

Rell was near aft rail watching signal lamps.

I asked, "Priority cost?"

"Why?"

"Because people are arguing about thing with no number."

He considered.

"Fair."

Forward signal tower showed three red lamps and one amber.

Gate structure ahead looked like black wall with lit slots.

Rell said, "Current after-hours pass for packet our class likely two to four copper. Could spike six if stone convoy keeps slot."

"Per packet?"

"Yes."

"Who pays if captain chooses?"

"Company account if operational need. Passenger-originated discretionary priority gets surcharge by manifest class."

"Meaning?"

"Split across eligible tickets. Freight heavier share. Passenger lighter."

"How light?"

"Depends final cost. Maybe bit or two each passenger. Freight account takes most."

There.

Small individually.

Not nothing commercially.

"Why captain refused?"

Rell looked at me.

"Because if every personal deadline buys gate slot, packet timetable becomes auction. Also company reviews discretionary passes. Too many and captain loses route."

There.

Another person with something to lose.

Captain's job.

Not just budget.

I nodded.

"Can passengers voluntarily cover priority without forcing others?"

Rell's mustache moved.

"Technically? Maybe."

"Allowed?"

"Private payment to gate office can be accepted if packet master approves and payment doesn't alter freight contract."

"Would Iven approve?"

"Ask her."

"She's navigating."

"Yes."

Fair.

Rell looked forward.

"Queue just moved. Might be irrelevant in five minutes."

Of course.

We waited.

Five minutes became eight.

No move.

Harven came aft.

"Well?"

"Two to four copper likely, maybe six. Passenger surcharge usually small because freight carries most. Captain also risks route review if she uses discretionary priority too often."

Harven stared.

"I'll pay six."

Mira had followed.

"You have six?"

"Yes."

"Then pay it yourself."

"I said I would."

"You said buy priority."

"Fine. I will pay."

Sori arrived behind them.

"Hold."

Harven's face tightened.

"What now?"

"Can you pay six and still afford Duskport transport, hearing fees, food?"

He looked angry again.

Then tired.

"Barely."

"Barely is answer."

"I don't care."

"Your daughter might."

"She can yell at me after."

There.

Decision.

Maybe bad.

His.

I said, "Captain still has to approve."

Harven looked at me.

"Then ask her."

I did.

Not because I knew right answer.

Because he had one.

Iven met us at stair after another ten minutes when queue stabilized enough to spare attention.

Harven spoke first.

"I will personally pay up to six copper for discretionary priority if Gate Nine accepts it. No passenger surcharge. No freight surcharge."

Iven looked at him.

Then me.

"This your idea?"

"Question was mine. Choice is his."

Good sentence.

Keep.

She looked back at Harven.

"You understand priority does not guarantee hearing arrival. We can still be delayed downstream."

"Yes."

"You understand payment may be nonrefundable once gate slot changes."

"Yes."

"You understand I can still refuse if passage unsafe or traffic command says no."

"Yes."

Mira said, "And if he pays, my birds don't become reason."

Iven looked at her.

"Correct."

Harven said, "Fine."

Iven considered longer than comfortable.

Then shook head.

"No."

Harven went white.

"Why?"

"Stone convoy is emergency bank repair material. Priority would move us ahead of it if accepted now. Gate office already flagged convoy. I'm not asking them."

There.

Fuck.

Correct procedure had found another person not even aboard.

Somewhere downstream bank needed stone.

Delay that and who knew what happened.

Harven laughed.

"Of course."

Not at her.

At world.

Iven said, "Queue estimate now seventeen minutes. We hold."

"And if it becomes forty?"

"Then we reassess."

"My daughter..."

"I know."

Harven snapped.

"Stop saying that."

Iven did.

Good.

Nobody won.

Seventeen minutes became nineteen.

Then gate lamp went green.

Bright Gull moved.

No bought priority.

No heroic exception.

Harven sat with face in hands until gate doors closed behind us.

Mira put cup of tea beside him without speaking.

He drank it.

People were irritating.

Also good.

Night deepened after Gate Nine.

Some passengers finally slept.

I didn't immediately.

Sori sat beside me on rail bench.

"You asked different question."

"Apparently still didn't fix it."

"That's point."

"Annoying point."

"Yes."

Harven's decision had been clear.

Pay six.

Captain's decision had been clear.

Don't delay emergency bank stone.

Both could be right enough.

One still lost.

Maybe only nineteen minutes.

Maybe those nineteen mattered.

Maybe downstream delays erased everything anyway.

Could not know.

Sori said, "You keep trying to find option where nobody has to be villain."

"Usually exists."

"Does it?"

I thought of first life.

War.

Triage.

Retreats.

Contracts.

People left behind.

Orders that saved hundred and killed ten.

Of course it didn't always exist.

I had known that.

Maybe second life had let me forget because smaller problems usually had smaller exits.

"No," I said.

"Good."

"Why is admitting misery good?"

"Because otherwise you'll keep treating it like missing information."

There.

She was annoying when correct.

"Sometimes it is missing information."

"Yes."

"Sometimes better question changes options."

"Yes."

"And sometimes every option still sucks."

"Congratulations."

"I hate this lesson."

"Good lessons usually rude."

Maro spoke from bench across aisle.

Eyes closed.

"Bad lessons too."

Sori looked over.

"Were you awake?"

"Unfortunately."

Dask said from floor near cargo wall, "I was also awake."

"Nobody asked."

"I know."

Progress.

Eventually I slept.

Not well.

Packet sleeping came in pieces.

Engine vibration.

Cold rail against shoulder.

Someone stepping around crutch tip.

A baby crying once then stopping.

Bird hissing in dream.

At some point river widened enough that packet movement changed.

Less bank echo.

More open-water roll.

I woke before dawn.

Maro was already awake.

Of course.

He stood at rail with cup.

"Duskport?" I asked.

"Not yet."

East ahead was gray.

Lower Lume had broadened into something almost lake-like between low banks.

Freight silhouettes moved in distance.

Tall masts.

Long barges.

A line of lights far downstream that could have been town or industrial docks.

Maybe both.

Harven stood forward near passenger boundary.

Still awake too.

I joined him.

He didn't look at me.

"How late are we?"

"Rell says maybe twelve minutes off schedule now."

"Could make it."

"Yes."

He nodded.

Then:

"I shouldn't have said birds."

"Probably not."

"I don't care about birds."

"I know."

He looked at me sharply.

I almost apologized for phrase.

Then didn't.

"That's problem," he said. "Everyone cares about one thing most. Then boat acts like all things weigh same."

There.

"Boat has to carry all of them," I said.

"I know."

He smiled without humor.

"See?"

Fair.

We stood quiet.

After a while he said, "If I miss hearing, she'll know I tried."

"Probably."

"You people love probably too?"

"Deeply."

That got tiny laugh.

Good.

Not forgiveness.

Not needed.

When physical sun began lifting east, river reflected it correctly.

No wrong dawn.

Nobody cared.

Good.

For once light could just be light.

Then packet horn sounded twice.

Rell walked deck.

"Duskport outer basin in one bell. South quay first. North quay after. Passengers with hearings, inspections, and other reasons to ruin my morning should gather bags now."

Harven moved immediately.

Mira too.

They looked at each other.

Harven said, "Quarantine third bell?"

"Yes."

"If we dock south first, your office north?"

"East quarantine pier."

He thought.

"I know court runner near south gate. If I make hearing, I can send boy east with arrival notice."

Mira stared.

"Why?"

"Because they're birds."

She looked offended.

Then laughed.

"Asshole."

"Apparently."

There.

People disagreeing did not mean people stayed enemies.

Useful.

Bright Gull rounded long mud point.

Duskport appeared.

Not as city all at once.

First cranes.

Then towers.

Then roofs stacked behind water.

Then ships.

Actual ships.

Not river packets.

Deep-hulled coastal vessels moored beyond lower quays.

Masts like stripped forest.

Warehouses five stories high.

Smoke stacks.

Temple roof with green tiles.

A market canopy running nearly quarter mile along water.

And farther east, beyond basin mouth, something gray-blue and enormous.

Sea.

I stopped breathing for one second.

Not because never seen sea.

First life had.

Because second life had not.

Carrow felt impossibly inland suddenly.

Sori came beside me.

"Your face."

"Shut up."

"That's sea."

"I know."

"You look surprised."

"I am allowed."

Dask pushed between us.

"Duskport mirror district is near east quay."

We both looked at him.

"What?"

Maro laughed.

Bright Gull sounded horn again.

South quay workers waved signal flags.

Harven held handkerchief so tightly knuckles went white.

Mira checked bird cage straps.

Everyone wanted something.

Duskport didn't care.

Good city already.

I had twenty-two copper.

A debt.

A bag of tools.

Three companions who had chosen same boat for different reasons.

A home west.

An unknown boy behind us with fever.

A father beside me trying to reach daughter before second bell.

A bird trader trying not to lose farm contract.

And sea ahead.

Adventure, apparently, was not one big problem.

It was thirty small lives all insisting theirs mattered most.

Hard to argue with any of them.
