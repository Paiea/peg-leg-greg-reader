CHAPTER 453

THE CLIMBER

High Shelf coach left on time.

I distrusted it immediately.

Grey Post had spent enough time teaching me weather possessed administrative authority.

But eighth-day second ring arrived cold, clear, and rude.

Coach board still said:

**HIGH SHELF / OPEN / FROST POCKETS ABOVE STONE MOUTH / SLOW M22-M31**

Paid ticket.

Fourteen copper.

Twenty-three Vale.

Ten-copper don't-be-stupid floor.

Four copper currently available for stupidity.

Not much adventure budget.

I boarded by lower step on crutches.

Trial leg wrapped.

Coach smaller than Survey six-wheel.

Four wheels, low roof, iron drag shoe mounted under rear.

Driver was woman named **Rell Tann**.

No relation questions.

I had matured.

Maybe.

"Pack inside?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Anything breakable?"

"One timing demonstrator. One borrowed leg. My dignity."

"Last one's not insured."

Good.

Passengers:

older quarry cutter with wrapped hands;

young man carrying two cages of gray moths;

woman with rolled maps;

me.

No party.

We left Grey Post north.

Road climbed.

Not metaphorically.

Actual sustained grade.

Shelf country rose in steps, each broad enough road and scrub, then another dark stone lip above.

Sea disappeared behind lower ridges within first bell.

That felt important.

First time since coast expansion I couldn't find water by turning head.

Land had swallowed horizon.

Cold came next.

Not snow.

Thin frost in shaded cracks.

Scrub silver at edges.

Breath visible when coach slowed.

Moth cages clicked.

"What are those?" I asked young man.

"Dust moths."

"Quarry thing?"

"Crack thing."

He elaborated after deciding I wasn't mocking.

Dust moths hated certain charged stone dust. Release one in sealed test hood with sample; wing pattern changed if dust carried active resonance.

"Precise?"

"No."

Excellent region.

"Useful?"

"Enough to not breathe bad dust."

Better.

Name **Toma Senn**.

He was delivering moths to High Shelf cutting sheds.

At marker nineteen road narrowed between black walls.

Stone Mouth.

Literal enough.

Rell stopped before shaded bend.

Got down.

Checked road with boot and short hooked tool.

Frost.

Not much.

Enough.

She fitted drag shoe lower.

Quarry cutter said, "Could walk faster."

Rell said, "Do."

He did not.

We continued slow.

At marker twenty-two frost became patches across full road.

Coach wheels had iron rims.

Bad combination.

Rell kept one side on rough gravel shoulder where possible.

No one chatted.

At marker twenty-five, uphill freight cart ahead stopped.

Two draft animals steamed.

Load covered with gray canvas.

Road too narrow to pass safely.

Rell stopped well back.

Driver ahead waved.

Something wrong with rear wheel.

Not broken.

Chocked.

Cart had slid backward half a wagon length on frost and driver had jammed wheel chocks.

Now couldn't restart without risking another slide.

High Shelf road problem.

We waited.

Rell walked up.

Quarry cutter followed because apparently knew freight driver.

I stayed.

Driver owned movement.

Good lesson retained.

Map woman got out and stretched.

Toma checked moth cages.

Five minutes? Don't use local minutes. Short while.

Rell returned.

"They're unloading two blocks."

"To where?" map woman asked.

"Shoulder. Then cart takes grade lighter. Yard wagon comes back for blocks."

"How long?"

"Longer if everybody asks."

Fair.

Workers unlashed canvas.

Under it were two pale stone blocks with black seams.

Each maybe person-sized but flatter.

As first block shifted, air made low tone.

Not block itself exactly.

My teeth felt it.

Tiny vibration.

Toma's moths all turned same direction in cages.

Interesting.

"Resonance stone?" I asked.

He nodded.

"Low grade."

"That is low?"

"You can stand near it."

Excellent scale.

Workers used screw jack and rollers to lower first block onto stone shoulder.

Second block caught.

One roller skated on frost.

Block tilted.

Everybody moved fast.

Not panic.

Training.

Quarry cutter shoved chock under lower corner with long bar.

Rell grabbed rope already tied uphill.

Freight driver yelled clear.

Block settled against chock.

No injury.

I had stood up instinctively.

Crutches under me.

Then stopped.

What exactly would I contribute?

Nothing useful in icy resonance-block rigging.

Sat back down.

Growth again.

Old me could have done more physically.

Current me knew that didn't grant certification.

Map woman said, "You looked ready."

"I was preparing to become obstacle."

She laughed.

Workers reset roller with sand and rough mat.

Second attempt clean.

Cart unloaded.

Moved uphill.

Our coach followed after road cleared.

Two blocks stayed shoulder under red marker flags.

Temporary problem waiting for yard wagon.

No Greg solution.

At marker twenty-nine, terrain opened.

High Shelf appeared.

Not one town.

A cut valley in gray rock with buildings stacked along terraces.

Quarry faces west.

Smoke low because wind tore it sideways.

Tall wooden frames crossed some cuts, hung with bronze bells and glass strips.

Stone carts moved on narrow rail grooves.

I stared.

Not trains.

Probably gravity haul lines or guided carts.

Ask later.

At entry gate, sign:

**HIGH SHELF CUTTING DISTRICT**

**PUBLIC ROAD / LOWER TERRACE**

**ACTIVE CUTS / PERMIT**

**RESONANCE HOURS POSTED DAILY**

**HEARING WRAPS REQUIRED BEYOND BLUE LINE**

Good.

Town had rules because stone could apparently hum your teeth.

Coach stopped lower terrace.

I got off.

Dry stone but uneven.

Crutches.

Trial leg stayed wrapped.

First concern money.

Lodging board.

**CUTTER HOUSE FLOOR 2C**

**OLD SCALE SHED BUNK 1C / NO HEAT AFTER SECOND BELL**

One copper.

Excellent.

I asked whether available.

Three spaces.

Paid.

Fourteen to thirteen.

Three above floor.

Very thin.

It was exactly what name promised.

Long stone room behind retired weighing platform.

Six bunks.

One iron stove that apparently became decorative after second bell.

Floor dry.

Good.

Work board next.

I needed money before anything else.

Listings:

**CUT THREE / SPOIL CART / 5C / STRONG BACK**

No.

**LOWER SCALE / TICKET SORT / 2C HALF / SEATED**

Possible.

But another paper sort.

**MOTH HOUSE / CAGE WASH / 2C / WATER + BRUSH / SEATED BENCH**

Different.

Wet but indoor.

Could do on crutches seated.

**RESONANCE BELL WALK / 4C / BLUE LINE / HEARING WRAPS / UNEVEN CUT ROAD**

No.

Not today.

Toma Senn was there.

Of course.

Not because destiny.

Because he delivered moths to moth house.

He looked at tab.

"You followed me."

"I followed two copper."

"More believable."

Supervisor **Della Orr** showed work.

Cages were palm-sized wooden frames with fine wire mesh and removable clay trays.

Dust moths moved to clean cages.

Dirty empty cages washed with warm water and stiff brush.

No charged solvent.

No touching moths.

No opening occupied cages.

"Can sit?" I asked.

"That's why bench."

Good.

"Leg?"

"What about it?"

"I have trial work leg. Dry floor. Could use for standing transfers if okay."

"Don't kick cages."

"Strong policy."

I could wear.

Rested since Grey Post previous day? Ch452 wore 27 min, Ch453 next day travel. Not full rest day. Road sheet allowed non-consecutive wear, so consecutive days not ideal. Original max morning/evening non-consecutive means wear sessions same day non-consecutive? But progression has rested. Better don't wear. Preserve recovery.

"Actually no," I said. "Crutches."

Della shrugged.

Work.

I sat.

Washed cages.

Simple.

But moth house itself interesting.

Occupied cages lined wall by stone sample tags.

Some moths gray.

Some wings developed dark spots.

One cage moths pressed flat against mesh away from sample tray.

"That bad?" I asked.

Della looked.

"Active dust."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning cutter shouldn't breathe it until washed or bound."

"Bound?"

"Clay mist. Makes dust heavy and quiet."

Magic occupational safety.

Nice.

Low.

Then second bell different pitch.

Workers in room paused.

Della counted.

Third bell.

Everyone resumed.

"Posted hours?"

"They ring before charged cutting. Blue line closes during cycle."

"How long?"

"Depends seam."

"Timing fixed?"

"Start fixed. End when bell reader clears."

Interesting.

Not Lot 67 today.

Actual system.

At midpoint? Don't know. Later.

Man rushed in carrying sealed sample tin.

"Six east face."

Della took.

"Why here?"

"Moths went wall-side."

Della's face changed.

Not panic.

Attention.

She put tin into empty test hood, transferred three moths with sleeve tool.

Moths immediately climbed upward and pressed against far mesh.

"Shit," Toma said.

Della rang small handbell twice.

Outside big resonance bells stopped.

Cycle aborted.

That fast.

Workers knew signal.

Della wrote tag.

"East face dust active above expected. Nobody resumes until wash crew and reader clear."

Man said, "Foreman says seam map had it low."

"Moths don't read maps."

Good line.

"Could be sample contamination."

"Then second sample will tell us."

Procedure.

No Greg involvement.

I washed cage.

Then Toma said, "You like instruments."

"Unfortunately."

He nodded at my pack.

"You have one?"

"Timing demonstrator."

"Can it time resonance?"

"No."

Good immediate answer.

"Why?"

"Because I have not established what its marks mean in this environment, it isn't certified, and timing cycle length doesn't tell dust safety anyway."

Della looked over.

"You can stay."

High praise.

Second sample arrived.

Same moth response.

Cut Six remained closed.

Workers outside grumbled because closure cost production.

Nobody argued with moths exactly.

One foreman did come in.

Big man, red ears.

"We lose half shift if wash crew takes east face."

Della said, "Then lose half shift."

"Map says low."

"Two fresh samples say active."

"Moths can react to iron binder."

"These are dust moths from new batch. Iron control passed this morning."

Foreman looked at Toma.

Toma nodded.

"Passed."

Foreman hated facts.

Not because stupid.

Because facts cost him.

"Can we cut west side?"

Della thought.

"If reader clears separate airflow and blue line stays east. Ask yard. Not me."

He left.

Again system not clean but functional.

Work resumed around interruption.

I finished cages.

Two copper.

Thirteen to fifteen.

Five above floor again.

Good.

"Tomorrow if you want another half, maybe."

"Maybe."

No automatic job.
