CHAPTER 436

THE FIXER

The legal way to Cinder Cross cost five copper.

The illegal way paid enough silver to make the risk an actual decision.

That was not how the morning started.

The morning started with me standing at Lakeward east freight board pretending I was a respectable person.

Fifteen copper.

Vale still open.

Ten-copper floor.

One reed-glass specification folded inside notebook.

One possible inland source near Cinder Cross.

One tool-house buyer there asking for retired route hardware.

And no interest in spending a third of my liquid cash just to discover East Fen Glassyard had become somebody's goat shed.

The public transport board offered exactly what I already knew.

**CINDER CROSS / 5C / MIDDAY CART**

The freight board was messier.

**BACKHAUL SPACE EAST / HALF RATE / BULK ONLY**

**DRIVER WANTED / TWO DAYS / OWN TEAM**

**CINDER TOOL FREIGHT / HANDLER RIDES FREE / LOADING REQUIRED**

Loading eliminated that one.

My shoulder was better, not forgiven.

Then a woman beside the board said, "You looking east?"

"Depends what east costs."

"You have two hands?"

I looked at crutches.

She looked at crutches.

"Fair."

Good start.

She was maybe forty, maybe fifty, with a wool vest full of chalk marks and a ledger tied shut with cord.

No name yet.

"What do you do?" she asked.

A week ago I might have said whatever was paying.

"Route notes. Field witness. Some artifact screening. Brokerage badly, so far."

"Brokerage badly?"

"No profit yet. Very clean record."

That got half a laugh.

She pointed at a covered freight wagon beyond the shed.

"I'm taking surplus repair stock to Cinder Cross Tool House. Buyer there keeps changing what he calls acceptable route metal. You know road hardware?"

"Some. Enough to know I don't know most of it."

"Better than collectors."

"Low standard."

"Can you tell Three Rays from ordinary bridge brackets?"

"Usually if stamped. Not necessarily from shape alone."

Her eyes sharpened.

"Good. Come look."

I did not move.

"Why?"

"Because I might pay you."

"Better."

The wagon carried four narrow crates and two bundled iron frames.

Everything looked old.

Nothing looked glamorous.

One crate had a Lakeward municipal surplus tag.

The second had a warehouse sale seal.

Third and fourth had both.

The iron frames were generic shutter or gate fittings.

Maybe.

She opened no crate.

Instead she handed me the sale inventory.

That mattered.

Ownership first.

All six lots came from a district warehouse closure on the south quay.

Declared categories:

retired lamp-house shutter fittings;

obsolete road-maintenance clamps;

miscellaneous iron and brass repair stock;

two lots marked **PRE-MERGER ROUTE HARDWARE / RELEASE STAMP REQUIRED BEFORE DISTRICT EXPORT**.

There.

"Those two stamped?" I asked.

"No."

"Then they can't legally leave Lakeward."

"They can if I wait nine days for heritage desk to inspect them and pay seven copper for release processing."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because Cinder buyer closes his repair lot in four days."

"Why does he care?"

"He melts ugly pieces, keeps useful fittings, and resells anything functional. He pays function. Not history."

"Sale price?"

"Forty-eight silver for the whole mixed load if he takes it as listed."

There it was.

Silver.

Not mine.

Still real.

"And you want me to tell you if the route hardware is secretly collectible enough that smuggling it is stupid."

She did not flinch at word.

"Partly."

"Other part?"

"I need someone on the east end who can tell him what is and isn't Three Rays nonsense before he discounts the whole load."

"And the release stamps?"

"I was hoping you'd be less interested in those."

"Bad luck."

I crouched by crate labels.

No opening because seals intact.

Inventory gave piece counts and old warehouse categories.

Two route-hardware lots included six clamp bodies, three toothed arcs, four shade-glass frames without glass, and assorted anchor plates.

Three Rays?

Could be.

Could also be later compatible roadwork.

The old route system had broader infrastructure than one maker.

"Do you know where these were removed from?"

"Warehouse records say recovered stock."

"That's not answer."

She sighed.

"I bought them at municipal surplus auction. I have sale receipts. I did not strip a road."

"Could whoever supplied warehouse have stripped road?"

"Probably twenty years ago, if they did. Half this stock predates me."

"Any current road claims?"

"None on sale notice."

"Any safety reason the heritage release exists?"

She leaned on wagon.

"Originally? Yes. People were pulling marked hardware off active roads and selling it to collectors. District made all pre-merger road hardware export-controlled until somebody stamped provenance. Now it also catches warehouse junk the district itself sold me."

That sounded plausible.

Not proven.

"Show me receipts."

She did.

Municipal sale.

Lot numbers matched.

Buyer name matched her ledger initials, which I did not need to repeat.

Paid.

No obvious theft.

No active-road removal in sale description.

No hazardous material.

No person being coerced.

The law's purpose made sense.

The application here looked stupid.

That did not make violation safe.

"What happens if east gate checks?"

"Confiscation hold. Fine. Maybe charge if they think I concealed marked stock deliberately."

"Which you would be doing."

"Yes."

Good.

No euphemism.

"Driver know?"

She looked toward wagon.

"He knows there's export-controlled road stock."

"Does he get paid extra for that risk?"

Pause.

There.

"He gets normal day rate."

"Then no."

"No what?"

"No deal with me. You want me attached to something illegal while the person holding reins eats the same risk for ordinary pay. Fix that first."

She stared.

"You're worried about his morals?"

"His incentives."

"He's driven gray freight before."

"Then he knows what extra risk costs. Ask him."

She did not like being managed by a man she had met three minutes ago.

Reasonable.

But she called the driver over.

He was older, broad, missing two front teeth.

Also unnamed.

"You know the two route lots don't have export release?" I asked.

"Yep."

"You know load can be held?"

"Yep."

"You getting risk premium?"

He looked at freight woman.

"Apparently we're discussing that now."

I liked him.

She swore.

Then said, "Two silver extra if it clears."

He said, "Four."

"Three."

"Then take your own wagon."

She looked at me as if this were my fault.

It was a little.

"Four silver."

He considered.

"Fine."

Good.

Now my turn.

"What are you offering me?"

"Ride east. Six silver if buyer takes full load."

"No."

"You haven't done anything."

"I'm already improving payroll."

Driver laughed.

She did not.

"Eight."

"Twelve."

"Absolutely not."

"Your sale is forty-eight silver and misses window if you wait legal release. Twelve silver plus ride if buyer accepts full load. Eight silver plus ride if he rejects the marked route lot but takes the remainder because my screening helps preserve the sale. Zero if I materially misrepresent what I know."

"Ten full. Six partial."

"Twelve full. Eight partial."

She looked at driver.

He said, "Pay him twelve so we can leave."

"Nobody asked you."

"I'm paid by day."

Support network forming beautifully.

"Twelve full. Eight partial," she said. "Ride included."

"And I do not lie about provenance to buyer."

"Fine."

"I do not open sealed crates."

"Fine."

"If gate asks me directly whether marked road hardware is aboard, I answer."

That one bothered her.

"Then what's the point?"

"I'm willing to participate in moving legitimately owned surplus without the release stamp. I'm not agreeing to invent a false story if questioned. Different risk profile."

"You have strange criminal standards."

"They're new. Give them time."

She rubbed face.

"Fine."

There.

Choice.

Knowingly illegal.

Morally acceptable enough by my current information.

Twelve silver upside if the whole load cleared.

Ride included.

Potential confiscation, fine, charge, damaged relationship with Lakeward road offices.

And if seller had lied about ownership, much worse.

I checked receipts again.

Still matched.

I checked load seals against inventory.

Still matched.

I wrote the relevant lot numbers in notebook.

Evidence discipline did not become less useful just because law was now obstacle instead of shield.

We left before midday.

No dramatic hidden compartment.

No night road.

No masks.

The freight woman simply listed the wagon at departure desk under the sale inventory's broad category:

**retired repair stock**.

True enough to be incomplete.

The clerk stamped road departure.

Did not inspect every crate.

I stood beside wagon and knowingly let the incomplete category pass.

That was the crime.

Smaller than stories made crime sound.

Still crime.

My heart did almost nothing.

That bothered me less than it probably should have.

Old life had contained worse.

Much worse.

I had not been a monster.

I had also not been clean.

Apparently some parts survived reincarnation better than mana.

Road east climbed out of Lakeward basin.

Cinder Cross lay beyond a long saddle where dark soil gave way to pale stone and scrub.

The driver let me ride front bench because climbing in and out of freight bed with shoulder was stupid.

We talked route.

Not crime.

He had run east road for eleven years.

Cinder Cross was not named for ash.

It was named for old black cinderstone mile blocks at the intersection of three freight roads.

People still argued about whether the blocks were volcanic, fired slag, or something magical.

Driver's opinion:

"They're rocks."

Strong scholarship.

Halfway, we passed a roadside inspection shelter.

Closed.

Nobody cheered.

Nobody said lucky.

We kept moving.

I noticed how quickly success could become ordinary before success had technically happened.

Dangerous.

At east district line, there was a gate.

Open.

One clerk.

One bored guard.

The wagon papers were checked.

Retired repair stock.

Destination Cinder Cross Tool House.

Municipal surplus receipt.

The clerk looked at total crate count.

Looked at seals.

Looked at us.

"Any charged glass?"

"No," freight woman said.

True.

"Active signal parts?"

"No," she said.

Also likely true.

The clerk stamped entry.

No one asked about pre-merger export release.

We rolled through.

Five copper fare avoided.

Twelve silver potentially earned.

No lightning bolt from ethics.

Interesting.

Cinder Cross appeared by late afternoon.

Three roads met around a broad stone square with black rectangular blocks set at each corner.

Rocks.

Probably.

Tool House occupied an old mill building with half its windows replaced by shutters.

Functional people.

Good.

The buyer at loading yard was a short man with magnifying lens hanging on cord and a habit of tapping every piece twice before speaking.

He remained unnamed for now.

He checked sale inventory.

Then route lots.

Seals were opened by owner and buyer together.

Not me.

Inside:

clamp bodies.

anchor plates.

three toothed arcs.

four empty glass frames.

No black disks.

No shade glass.

No obvious collector-grade intact unit.

Buyer picked up first toothed arc.

"Sunroad?"

Freight woman looked at me.

There was my fee.

"Maybe compatible with that family," I said. "Not enough to claim Sunroad. No location provenance in sale record. Three Rays infrastructure used related toothed adjustment hardware, but these pieces aren't a complete system and I don't see a maker stamp on this one."

Buyer grunted.

Second arc had stamp.

Three lines over circle.

There.

"That one is Three Rays marked," I said. "Still no site provenance."

"Collector premium?" buyer asked.

"Not from me."

He smiled.

"Good."

He did not care about history.

He cared whether tooth spacing matched repair jigs.

He measured.

First arc wrong.

Second close.

Third exact to one jig.

The empty glass frames fit two standard shutter widths after minor redrill.

Buyer wanted function.

Freight woman had been right.

Then he found municipal sale tag wired under one clamp.

"Release stamps?"

Silence.

There it was.

He knew.

Freight woman said, "Municipal sale."

"I can read. Release?"

"No."

He looked at me.

"Broker?"

"Today."

"You know that's controlled export."

"Yes."

"And you brought it."

"I rode with it."

"Difference?"

"Probably not to a magistrate."

He laughed.

Not kindly.

Not cruelly.

Professionally.

Then he looked at seller.

"Forty-eight silver was for clean paper."

Ah.

Risk repricing.

Good.

She said, "You posted no provenance premium."

"I posted no provenance premium. I did not post `bring me district trouble.`"

"They're legally purchased."

"Lakeward legally sold you half the problem."

Excellent line.

Negotiation began.

Not mine at first.

He offered lower terms.

Not denomination I could cleanly compare because mixed lot and no silver-copper conversion established.

Freight woman rejected.

They argued over whether release defect followed ownership or only export.

I did not know law enough.

So I asked what actually changed his value.

Buyer looked at me.

"If Lakeward comes asking, I need to prove I didn't strip road stock. Municipal sale receipt helps. Missing release means I may have to hold pieces instead of resell until paper is cleaned. Capital sits."

"How long?"

"Could be days. Could be season if offices fight."

"Which pieces create that problem?"

"Only marked route stock. Rest clean."

There.

Split transaction.

"Then price clean lots at full functional value and hold back route lots under conditional purchase."

Both looked at me.

"Meaning?" freight woman asked.

"He buys clean stock now. He takes custody of route lots only if he wants, but price/payment for those depends on whether municipal receipt plus later release can clear them. Or you keep them and solve paper yourself. Don't discount whole wagon for two dirty-paper lots."

Buyer said, "I'm not warehousing her problem for free."

"Then charge holding against route lots only."

Seller said, "And lose sale window."

"You already moved clean goods into his yard. Save what can be saved."

They hated it.

Then started calculating.

Good.

That was management too.

Not commanding.

Separating problems so one failure did not contaminate everything.

Eventually:

clean lamp/shutter repair lots sold now.

One unmarked route lot also sold as generic repair stock after buyer verified fit.

The marked Three Rays lot stayed with seller, still sealed after inspection? It had been opened. Buyer refused current purchase without release or deeper discount she would not accept.

So not full-load acceptance.

My contract said eight silver if buyer rejected the marked route lot but took the remainder because my screening helped preserve the sale.

That was what happened.

Eight silver.

Plus the ride.

Not the full twelve.

Not nothing.

Freight woman paid eight silver.

My copper purse stayed fifteen.

Then buyer stopped me before I left.

"You know other marked route stock?"

"Some locations. Not ownership."

"I don't pay collector stories."

"I know."

"I pay matching tooth spacing and usable frames."

"I know now."

"Bring me clean paper and functional measurements before you drag metal here."

That was almost a relationship.

Not trust.

A specification.

Useful.

Freight woman was less happy.

"You cost me the route lot."

"Missing release cost you route lot."

"You could've called it Sunroad collector junk and pushed him."

"He doesn't pay history."

"Could've tried."

"Then next time he assumes I'm selling stories."

She looked at me.

"You care about next time already?"

Yes.

That was the point.

"Unfortunately."

She shook head.

But before leaving she said, "If I clean the release, you want another cut?"

"If I find buyer or improve terms."

"Three Rays lot is mine."

"I know."

"Don't broker around me."

"Then don't broker around me either."

Pause.

She smiled once.

"Maybe you're not terrible at this."

Dangerous sentence.

I went to Cinder Cross lodging board.

One copper lodging.

Fifteen to fourteen copper.

Eight silver stayed eight silver.

If I had taken normal cart, I would have arrived with ten.

Instead I arrived with sixteen.

Illegal choice had improved my position by six copper relative to the legal baseline.

That was material.

No punishment.

No immediate consequence.

No lesson that rules existed for secret reasons I'd failed to respect.

Just a better purse and a new buyer relationship.

Temptation worked better when rewarded.

I wrote that more carefully.

Not those words.

**GRAY FREIGHT RESULT:**

Municipal ownership supported by receipt.

Export release knowingly absent.

Driver informed and paid extra risk premium.

No active/safety hardware identified.

East gate did not ask controlled-export question.

Buyer discovered missing release independently.

Clean lots sold.

Marked route lot rejected pending paper or discount.

My fee: 8s + transport.

Cash: 8s + 14c after lodging.

Then:

**Important: illegality did not create profit. Mispriced delay created profit. Law was part of spread.**

That felt precise.

The law made clean surplus slower to move.

That delay had value.

So did willingness to carry enforcement risk.

So did knowing when not to lie further.

Could have gone worse.

Could go worse next time.

Still worked.

I was not sorry.

That answer came too quickly.

Old me would have recognized it.

Maybe approved.

Maybe laughed at how long it took.

Tomorrow: East Fen Glassyard.

Reed glass.

Actual first sourcing hypothesis.

And now Cinder Cross Tool House had given me functional measurements for route hardware.

One road.

Two markets.

Five copper of fare avoided and eight silver earned on a legally exposed transaction.

The scale had changed.

This felt less like climbing a ladder.

More like finding the side door.

I had always been good at side doors.
