CHAPTER 445

THE BIDDER

Thirteen silver was enough to buy something stupid.

That was different from being enough to buy anything I wanted.

Important distinction.

I had twelve copper and thirteen silver in my purse, Vale still open, a two-silver reed-glass commission somewhere west of me in the dangerous metaphysical state called probably, and one Tool House reference proving I could tell people not to stand under eleven hundred units of iron.

Progress.

Also, apparently, enough confidence to walk into an artifact sale.

Cinder Cross held it in the back room of a cooper's hall because the actual auction house had a roof leak.

That was reassuring.

Nothing said rare magical equipment like barrels and damp plaster.

The notice had been on the private board under three ordinary freight postings.

**SURVEY / ROAD / WORKSHOP LOTS**

**DEACCESSION + ESTATE GOODS**

**VIEWING SECOND BELL**

**BIDS AFTER THIRD**

**NO FIELD GUARANTEE UNLESS WRITTEN**

That last line was why I came.

Not because I expected a miracle.

Because people wrote warnings where money had taught them to.

The viewing cost one bit.

Fine.

Bits could remain ordinary.

I paid and went in.

Nine tables.

Maybe thirty people.

Enough that nobody needed Greg specifically.

Better.

Most of the goods were tools with magic somewhere in their family but not necessarily enough to matter.

Two fog lamps.

A cracked line-reader.

Three heat stones in a fitted box.

A road compass that apparently pointed toward the last registered marker instead of north, which was useful if you were on the right road and deeply confusing if you were not.

A pair of vibration wedges.

A sealed brass thing nobody was allowed to activate indoors.

Healthy rule.

I looked slowly.

Not because I was pretending not to care.

Because old me had learned that the easiest way to buy the wrong artifact was to fall in love with the first one that did something.

Then I reached table seven.

Black iron half-ring.

Three pale ceramic plates set around the inside.

Leather hand strap.

Short chain.

Flat hook.

Brass thumb stud.

One ceramic plate cracked cleanly through the center.

The tag read:

**SURVEYOR STILLHOOK**

**ROAD OFFICE RETIREMENT LOT**

**TWO PLATES RESPOND / THIRD FAILED**

**ANCHOR-DEPENDENT LATERAL HOLD**

**NO CURRENT BODY-RATED CERTIFICATE**

**OPEN 4S**

I stopped.

Not visibly, I hoped.

Internally, very much.

Stillhook.

I knew the class.

Probably.

That was dangerous word number one.

I knew later stillhooks.

Old-life support crews used them around unstable casting platforms, cliff work, breach teams, rescue lines, and sometimes people who were too important to let a shockwave move six feet to the left.

Not because the hook made you immovable.

It did not.

Nothing useful was ever that simple.

You anchored it.

You held activation.

The field resisted displacement relative to the anchor.

Different generations handled angle, load, and rebound differently.

Some were brilliant.

Some broke wrists.

One famous design had killed three surveyors because the anchor held and the cliff did not.

This one was older.

Much older.

Maybe.

Pattern familiarity.

Not exact recognition.

Good.

Do not become an encyclopedia because object is interesting.

A man beside me picked it up.

The table attendant immediately said, "Stud only over test rail."

The man rolled eyes.

"Wasn't pressing."

"Everyone says that with thumb on stud."

He moved thumb.

Good attendant.

The test rail ran along back edge of table.

Heavy iron bolted into floor.

The man clipped hook to rail.

"Leather strap?"

"Wrist only for display," attendant said. "Old body certification expired. If you wear it, that's your problem."

"Useful."

"Accurate."

I liked her.

He put hand through strap anyway, not tightening.

Thumb on stud.

Ceramic plates dulled from pale cream to gray.

Two of them.

The cracked third flickered once and stayed pale.

The man pulled sideways.

His arm stopped.

Not frozen.

Resisted.

He leaned farther.

The hook held him relative to rail strongly enough that his shoulder moved before his hand did.

Then he changed angle.

Resistance fell off.

He nearly overbalanced.

He released stud.

"That's narrow."

"Yes."

"How narrow?"

"Written test says strongest inside thirty degrees either side of plate axis. Falls fast outside. Third axis unavailable."

There.

Visible utility.

Visible failure.

No mystery discount from stupidity.

A surveyor standing on unstable platform could brace against sway from two useful directions if anchor geometry cooperated.

A rigger might use it.

A person with one free hand and a fixed anchor could hold position against lateral force.

A person needing to move could not.

A person with both hands occupied by crutches had a more immediate design problem.

Me.

Excellent.

I wanted it.

Obviously.

That was irritating.

I read the attached sheet.

Cinder road office retirement.

Used equipment.

Service mark scratched near hook.

Last certified body inspection six years ago.

Third plate failure noted three years ago.

No repair record after that.

No documented catastrophic failure.

No proof the two working plates still met original load rating.

Seller made no claim beyond demonstration.

Four silver opening.

I could afford four.

I could afford eight without crossing my five-silver operating floor.

Eight silver was not gold.

Eight silver was also not nothing.

More importantly, the object did not deserve eight merely because I had eight available.

I asked attendant, "Any maker mark?"

She pointed to underside.

Three tiny stamped squares around a diagonal line.

My stomach did a small unpleasant thing.

Not because I knew exact artifact.

Because I knew mark.

Later.

Much later.

Third-Line Survey Works.

I thought.

Maybe Second-Line before rename.

The diagonal changed by generation.

I could not remember which.

Wonderful.

Old memories had chosen now to become decorative.

I asked, "Road office know manufacturer?"

"Retirement sheet says Third-Line pattern. No workshop authentication."

"Why no repair?"

"Office replaced class."

"Meaning?"

"Newer braces have wider hold and automatic release."

Of course they did.

"Repair quote?"

"None current."

"Nobody checked?"

"Two local shops refused ceramic replacement without original pattern gauge."

There.

Repair bottleneck.

Price rational.

The man who had demonstrated it asked, "You bidding?"

I looked at him.

"Maybe."

He looked at my crutches.

Then the stillhook.

"You need a hand to use it."

"Excellent assessment."

He shrugged.

"Just saying."

"So was I."

He moved away.

Not hostile.

Not stupid.

He was right.

Current me had two crutches.

Stillhook needed one hand on brass stud.

That meant if I used it standing, one crutch had to go somewhere.

Unless work limb.

There.

The unfinished leg changed the artifact.

The artifact changed the value of the leg.

That was what I wanted from tools.

Not another object that sat in bag waiting for plot.

A thing that made other infrastructure more valuable.

I kept moving.

Table eight had a genuine better object.

A compact road barrier plate.

Demonstrator threw a wooden bolt at it.

The bolt stopped two feet away and fell.

Everyone made appropriate noises.

Tag opened at one gold.

Of course it did.

Visible miracle.

Visible price.

I did not waste time being offended by economics.

The barrier also required a charged base stone replaced every fifty activations.

Seller had two.

Good artifact.

Expensive.

No hidden bargain apparent.

I moved on.

Table nine held a copper warming bowl and a ridiculous self-leveling cup.

The cup got more attention than half the magical tools.

People understood priorities.

I went back to stillhook only after looking at everything.

The attendant noticed.

"Questions?"

"Can you demonstrate load from both working axes separately?"

"Yes."

She did.

Test rail.

Hook.

Strap loose.

Thumb stud.

First plate axis.

Strong.

Second.

Strong, maybe slightly weaker.

Diagonal between them.

Still useful but uneven.

Toward cracked plate axis.

Almost nothing.

"Any rebound?"

"Release under tension and you'll feel it."

"How much?"

"Depends how stupid you were before release."

Good answer.

"Can you show without body?"

She clipped strap around a weighted leather block instead.

Pulled it with rope against field.

Released stud remotely using a wooden lever arrangement.

Block jumped a handspan toward rope.

There.

Stored tension.

Not huge at test load.

Could be ugly at higher load.

Known downside.

"Does it resist magical displacement?"

Her eyes narrowed.

"Tag says lateral hold."

"That wasn't my question."

"No certified magic-class test in lot papers."

"What kind of force did road office certify?"

"Platform sway. Wind rig. Cart-bed vibration. Manual pull."

"Nothing else?"

"Nothing written."

Good.

Do not tell her why I asked.

Old Greg memory supplied fragments.

A breach team.

A healer anchored to masonry.

A shockwave hitting.

A stillhook holding long enough for a cast to finish.

Was that this generation?

No idea.

Could have been a later military derivative that looked similar.

Could have been a completely different maker.

Could have been story inflation after twenty years.

But the class had potential beyond survey comfort.

Potential.

Not fact.

I checked the crack.

Clean through ceramic.

No burn discoloration.

No melted metal.

No obvious overload scar.

Could be age.

Impact.

Internal failure.

Unknown.

I did not touch it.

"Can plate be isolated?" I asked.

"Current two already operate without third."

"I mean physically removed."

"Seller doesn't authorize disassembly before sale."

Correct.

"Fair."

I left table again.

Then found a woman selling tea in hallway and bought a cup with a bit.

Artifact analysis improved with tea.

Most things did.

I sat against wall.

Thirteen silver plus ordinary copper.

Five-silver operating floor.

Two silver pending.

Vale still open.

Work-limb deposit already sunk.

Stillhook opening four silver.

Unknown repair.

Unknown magical interaction.

Known two-axis function.

Known one-hand activation.

Known anchor dependence.

Known rebound.

No body certificate.

No reason to believe anybody else was missing obvious value.

Exactly what I wanted.

A real artifact problem.

Not free treasure.

Third bell.

People gathered.

Auctioneer stood on old keg.

The cooper's hall had committed to theme.

Lots moved quickly.

Heat stones sold.

Fog lamp did not meet reserve.

Self-leveling cup sold for embarrassing amount.

I respected buyer more than I should have.

Stillhook came seventh.

Auctioneer read sheet.

No poetry.

"Road survey stillhook. Two live plates. One failed. No current body certification. Demonstrated only to posted test. Opening four silver."

Silence for half breath.

Then:

"Four."

The man from viewing.

Of course.

"Five," said a woman near back.

I did not know either.

Good.

"Six," man said.

Now price had information.

At six silver, two people wanted current visible utility.

Not just me.

Auctioneer looked around.

I had planned ceiling.

Eight silver.

Not because eight was value.

Because eight silver was maximum I could deploy without crossing floor, and artifact was not worth violating reserve on uncertain memory.

Those were different ceilings.

My private value might be higher than eight.

My available risk was not.

I raised hand.

"Seven silver."

The woman in back looked at me.

Not surprised.

Evaluating.

Auctioneer pointed.

"Seven silver."

Man from viewing said, "Eight silver."

There went ceiling.

Good.

Walk away.

That was a skill too.

I did nothing.

Auctioneer called eight once.

The woman in back said, "Nine silver."

Interesting.

Man frowned.

"Ten silver."

No.

Definitely no.

My artifact remained not mine.

Mildly painful.

Healthy.

Auctioneer called ten.

Woman hesitated.

Then: "Eleven silver."

The man stopped.

Eleven silver.

More than I could spend while preserving floor.

Also still below the obvious one-gold barrier-plate tier.

Damaged survey gear with two useful axes had a market.

As it should.

Auctioneer called eleven twice.

Then the woman said, "Condition on sale sheet includes test rail hook?"

Attendant answered, "Hook and wrist strap included. Rail not included."

She nodded.

Auctioneer called.

No higher bid.

Sold eleven silver.

I felt stupid.

Not because I had lost.

Because I had spent half an hour mentally redesigning my life around an object I did not own.

Useful correction.

The woman came to table after sale.

Older than me by maybe fifteen years.

Brown coat.

Short hair.

Hands with pale pressure scars around thumb and index finger.

Surveyor, probably.

Or someone who used tools like one.

She inspected hook before paying.

Not gloating.

I asked, "You know the class?"

She looked at me.

"Enough to buy broken."

"Repair?"

"Maybe."

"Third-Line?"

"Maybe."

Good.

I liked her immediately and distrusted that.

"Why eleven silver?"

"Because two working plates are worth eight silver to me, hook assembly two, and I can gamble one on ceramic."

That was clean enough to hurt.

No secret.

No idiot.

She had her own use.

"What use?"

"No."

Fair.

I smiled.

"Had to try."

She looked at crutches again.

Everybody did eventually.

"You were bidding for yourself?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"No."

She laughed.

Fair again.

Then she said, "You asked magic displacement."

I had not realized she heard.

There.

Visible interest as information.

Bad poker.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Old story."

"What story?"

"A later stillhook holding under something that wasn't wind or platform sway."

"Later where?"

"Different place. Different time. I don't know this unit does it."

That was more information than ideal.

Also not enough to create a lie.

Her expression changed from amused to professional.

"You know who told story?"

"No one useful to you."

Technically true in a reincarnation way.

"Documented?"

"Not here."

She looked at the hook.

Price already paid.

My information could not hurt my purchase because I had no purchase.

Could still alter future market.

Annoying.

She said, "If I test that, where do I find you?"

I should have said nowhere.

Instead I thought about information networks.

If she owned object and had reason to test a layer I cared about, her result had value.

"Tool House knows Greg."

"Greg what?"

"Just Greg has worked surprisingly often."

She waited.

"Carrow when I'm home. Cinder right now."

"You're not from here."

"No."

"You're very tall."

"Excellent field work."

She ignored that.

"Name's Elian."

I stored it.

She lifted stillhook.

"I test anything strange, information costs."

"Obviously."

"What do you pay?"

"Depends whether strange is useful."

"Broker."

"Recent diagnosis."

She left.

I did not own artifact.

But I had learned something almost as important.

People with enough expertise to buy broken artifacts also created information.

And they charged.

The market was not only objects.

It was people who could reduce uncertainty.

I hated how every road returned to networks.

At Tool House, the buyer looked up from crate slate.

"Buy anything?"

"No."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"You have the face of someone who would buy a cursed spoon if it had an interesting hinge."

"That is slander."

"You bought that old line thing."

"Quietener."

"Same problem."

I leaned against counter.

"Stillhook sold eleven silver."

He grunted.

"Too much broken."

"Maybe."

"What did you bid?"

"Seven silver."

He looked at me.

"How much cash you have?"

"Enough that seven wouldn't kill me."

"That's not answer."

"Then stop asking accounting questions."

He smiled.

I asked, "You know anyone who works Third-Line ceramic?"

"Old survey gear?"

"Yes."

"Not here. Maybe South Road maintenance. Why?"

"Curiosity."

"Expensive disease."

"Starting to notice."

He went back to slate.

I did not ask about vane bracket.

Growth continued.

At the post counter, still no Lakeward commission.

Also reasonable.

No Hessa.

No Hark & Venn.

No home reply.

Distance remained annoyingly consistent.

I checked private board.

South-road organizer notice still there.

So was a new line:

**ARTIFACT TEST ASSIST / CONTROLLED SHOP / STRONG MANA REQUIRED**

Not me.

Good.

I left it.

Outside, Cinder afternoon had gone windy.

Not dangerous.

Enough to make hanging shop signs complain.

I found a bench near black corner block and opened notebook.

Wrote:

**STILLHOOK**

Then stopped.

Not mine.

Changed heading.

**STILLHOOK MARKET / CLASS NOTE**

Better.

**Visible:** two-axis lateral hold relative fixed anchor. thumb-held activation. third plate dead. narrow angle. rebound if released under tension. no current body cert.

**Market:** opened 4s, sold 11s. Multiple bidders. Broken does not mean worthless.

**Recognition:** class familiar from later-life use. Third-Line mark maybe. Exact generation unknown. Possible later nonphysical-displacement use remembered, NOT established for this unit/class.

**Buyer:** Elian. likely experienced user from hand marks / behavior, profession unestablished. May test. Info will cost.

Then:

**I lost because my reserve mattered more than my theory. Correct result.**

I stared at that.

It did not feel like losing anymore.

Not much.

I had wanted artifact acquisition to become real.

Real meant somebody else could buy the thing.

Real meant price could exceed my safe capital.

Real meant knowledge was not automatically ownership.

Real meant I needed money before opportunities appeared, not after.

There.

Economic pull without another manifesto.

I still wrote one more line.

**Need deployable capital above floor. Not just purse total.**

Eighteen copper sounded better than it was if ten had a job.

Eight was my actual flexible copper.

That was the number artifacts cared about.

And maybe that five coming from Lakeward.

Maybe.

Still not money.

I closed notebook.

Wind shoved at my coat.

For one stupid second I imagined the stillhook clipped to black cinderstone block, one hand on stud, body held against a force nobody else expected it to resist.

Then I corrected myself.

Two crutches.

One occupied hand.

One unfinished work limb.

One unverified memory.

One artifact now owned by somebody else.

Good.

Problems.

Problems were shape.

Back at bunkhouse, I checked shoulder.

Tired from yesterday.

Better today.

No new pain.

Hands fine.

Right leg fine.

No trial-limb wear.

No magic.

Fifty-nine.

Fifty-three.

One hundred thirty-nine.

Eighteen copper.

Twenty-three Vale.

Five pending.

Same as morning.

Financially, I had accomplished nothing.

That was okay.

Not every chapter of a life needed income.

I had found a category of tool I wanted.

Not a trophy.

A support tool.

Something that could let position become a resource.

Something whose limitations were as important as its effect.

And I had watched somebody else pay more than I safely could.

That part mattered most.

Old Greg had known powerful artifacts existed.

Current Greg had finally started meeting them at prices that hurt.

Good.

Pain was information.

Not injury.

Different thing.

I lay down with the bunkhouse roof creaking above me and thought about the work limb.

One free hand.

Not independence as slogan.

Not walking like everyone else.

One free hand meant a stillhook became usable while standing.

A lamp became usable.

A line became usable.

A shield tool.

A latch.

A person.

A weapon, if needed.

The leg was not merely mobility.

It was hand economy.

I had written that before.

Now an artifact had priced the idea.

One silver and eighteen copper for the finished work limb.

Twenty already down.

I did not know when the remainder came due.

Do not invent terms.

But for the first time, the quote did not feel like an obligation hanging over me.

It felt like infrastructure between me and a class of things I actually wanted to use.

That was different.

I slept badly because the man above me snored like structural damage.

Magic remained unsolved.

Money remained insufficient.

The artifact remained somebody else's.

Still.

For the first time in a while, insufficient felt directional.
