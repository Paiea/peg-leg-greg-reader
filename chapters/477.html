CHAPTER 407

THE WAYFARER

I paid five copper to go west.

Nothing clever happened first.

That was the clever part.

Cinder Cross public road board still had the same Lakeward fare I had refused three chapters ago.

**LAKEWARD / 5C / MIDDAY CART**

No freight work attached.

No gray-market repair stock.

No broker fee.

No driver who needed somebody to classify old route metal.

Just five copper.

A seat.

A road.

I stood in front of the board with eighteen copper and experienced the unfamiliar sensation of not needing to invent a second job before traveling.

Ten stayed floor.

Five bought the seat.

That left thirteen.

Not comfortable enough to become stupid.

Comfortable enough that time could have a price.

I bought the seat.

The clerk did not ring a bell.

No one congratulated me for economic development.

He gave me a stamped card and said, "West cart. Third bay."

"That's it?"

He looked at me.

"What else were you expecting?"

"Honestly? A character lesson."

"Third bay."

Good institution.

I went to third bay.

The cart was larger than Daro Venn's Bell Ford freight wagon and less interesting.

Four wheels.

Canvas roof.

Bench seats.

Two chestnut road striders with dull copper braid worked through their manes.

The braid caught my attention.

Not decoration.

Thin metal threads ran from collar rings into little ceramic beads tied near shoulder.

Grounding.

Probably.

I knew enough not to announce that.

Driver was a woman with a red scarf and one sleeve rolled above elbow.

No name needed.

She took my fare card, looked at crutches, and pointed to front-side bench.

"Least climbing."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me until we get there."

Strong salesmanship.

Other passengers accumulated.

A merchant with two square cases chained together.

A young man carrying a rolled sleeping mat and talking before anyone asked him anything.

An older woman with a basket full of sealed clay jars.

Two road workers with yellow cord at wrists.

A mother with a girl maybe eight.

And me.

The talking young man sat opposite.

He had not yet finished describing why he was traveling.

"I wasn't supposed to be going Lakeward today, see, except my brother says if I wait until tomorrow the room is gone, and I told him that's ridiculous because rooms don't go anywhere, people go somewhere, but apparently the landlord has a cousin coming in from Doss Hill and the cousin has a wife and maybe a baby, he wasn't clear about the baby, which I think matters because babies are small but somehow require more room than adults, so now I'm paying five copper because nobody in my family understands planning."

He inhaled.

I said, "Sounds difficult."

"It is."

He continued.

Good.

Different speech.

The driver climbed up.

"Road rule."

Talking man stopped.

Miracle.

She pointed along the cart roof.

"Metal stays down through High Cinder stretch if weather bells sound. Nobody touches ground braid while beads are lit. If I tell you off, you get off. If I tell you stay seated, stay seated. If road wardens stop us, don't make their day interesting."

The merchant said, "Weather's clear."

Driver looked west.

"So is your face. Neither tells me what the ridge is doing."

He shut up.

I liked her.

We rolled out.

Cinder Cross shrank behind us in black blocks, tool roofs, and the square where three roads disagreed about what direction counted as important.

I had thirteen copper.

Twenty-three with Vale.

Five still potentially coming from Lakeward.

Protocol fifty-nine, fifty-three, one hundred thirty-nine.

No new artifact.

No stillhook.

No reason to reopen that wound every half hour.

I failed at that immediately.

The first time the cart leaned through a turn, I imagined clipping a stillhook to the bench frame and letting the road move under me.

Then remembered Elian owned it.

Useful discipline.

The road west climbed for an hour before leveling across pale scrub.

Cinderstone markers stood every so often.

Not the big black corner blocks from town.

Smaller.

Dark rectangular posts sunk waist-high beside road.

Some had copper caps.

Some did not.

Most had shallow grooves down one face.

The little girl beside her mother asked, "Are those magic?"

Her mother said, "Some."

"Which?"

"The ones that work."

Excellent taxonomy.

One of the yellow-cord road workers laughed.

"They all work. Just not all for same thing."

The girl looked at him.

"What thing?"

He opened mouth.

His partner said, "Don't."

"What?"

"You'll explain until Lakeward."

"I can explain short."

"No."

The girl waited.

He failed to resist.

"Old ones mark distance. New caps bleed ridge charge. Side grooves show flood level. Some hold lamp hooks. Three east of here used to carry fog line but that's gone. One near split road has funeral names scratched on back but that isn't official."

His partner stared.

"Short."

"That was short."

The talking young man leaned in.

"My uncle says one of them hums before somebody dies."

Road worker said, "Your uncle needs hobbies."

"I told him that."

He did not seem like somebody who told anyone less than six paragraphs at a time.

The world moved.

I let it.

That was harder than it sounded.

For several chapters I'd been turning places into problems and problems into leverage.

Source.

Coordinate.

Bid.

Price.

Move.

Useful.

Also dangerous if it became the only way I knew how to look.

Sometimes a road was a road.

Sometimes five copper bought the right not to improve it.

We reached the first rise.

Wind changed.

Not stronger.

Different.

Dry air from west became cold across my right cheek.

The road striders' ears turned at the same time.

Driver sat straighter.

The copper braid in their manes gave a tiny clicking sound.

One bead near left animal's shoulder flashed pale blue.

Then went dark.

Nobody else reacted.

I did because I was watching.

The driver looked up at canvas edge.

There were four tiny brass bells tied under roof lip.

All still.

We continued.

Half a mile later the first bell rang.

Not from cart motion.

A single clean note.

The talking man stopped mid-sentence.

That was how I knew it mattered.

Second bell answered.

Then third.

The road workers swore at same time.

Driver said, "Metal down."

Merchant already had one square case upright between knees.

Driver pointed.

"Chain flat."

He lowered it.

One road worker took a narrow iron measuring staff from his pack and laid it on floorboards.

The older woman pulled the wire handle down against her basket.

I had crutches.

Metal tips.

Wood shafts.

Not fully metal.

Still.

I laid both as flat as space allowed.

Driver looked back.

"Good."

The little girl whispered, "Is it lightning?"

Her mother said, "Not exactly."

That was usually a worse answer.

The sky remained mostly clear.

No storm wall.

No thunder.

Only thin high cloud over ridge and that cold crosswind.

Then grass beside road began to shine.

Not glow.

Catch light wrong.

Silver threads moved across seed heads in waves that did not match wind.

The road worker who liked explanations pointed but kept voice low.

"Charge grass."

His partner said, "She can see it."

"I know."

"Then stop naming things."

"It has a name."

Everything did.

That didn't make it useful.

Ahead, one cinder post lit along cap.

Blue line ran down copper seam into ground.

Then next.

Then another.

The third post did not.

Driver saw it.

So did both road workers.

She slowed.

No panic.

No heroics.

She pulled cart toward a widened gravel pocket on left.

A black iron hoop stood there over packed earth.

Ground layby.

Probably.

One of the road workers stood.

Driver said, "Sit."

"I'm route crew."

"Not on my cart."

He sat.

Authority clarity remained beautiful.

We rolled into layby.

The black hoop had three chains dropping to iron plates buried in earth.

No visible glow.

Driver set brake.

"Stay seated."

Talking man whispered, "I don't like that."

Nobody had asked.

Fair.

The cart roof bells rang again.

All four now.

A faint blue crawl appeared across the copper braid in the road striders' manes.

They stamped but did not bolt.

Driver kept reins loose.

Interesting.

Not restraining harder.

Letting animals move their heads.

The merchant reached toward chain case without thinking.

The older road worker slapped his wrist.

"Don't."

He jerked back.

"Why?"

"Because she said metal down."

"I was just moving it."

"Move yourself later."

Driver said, "Thank you."

No sarcasm academy.

Just irritation.

I liked that too.

Across road, silver threads in grass intensified.

Then I saw why metal mattered.

A tall weed stalk leaned toward one cinder post.

No contact.

Blue-white thread jumped from seed head to copper cap with a snap.

Tiny.

Sharp.

Another jumped lower.

Then a dozen faint threads flickered between grasses.

Charge moving toward post.

The post that had not lit sat thirty paces farther west.

Around it, silvered grass moved more violently.

Not because wind.

Field.

The failed post was creating a gap.

The road workers both leaned forward.

Younger one said, "Cap's dead."

Older said, "Or ground break."

"Same result."

"Not same repair."

Good.

Driver said, "Can we pass?"

Older worker watched.

"No."

Simple.

No explanation.

Driver nodded.

We waited.

There was something deeply satisfying about competent people saying no before disaster.

Old life had contained too many people whose definition of bravery was refusing to let information alter a plan.

This road had built a layby for exactly that species.

Maybe civilization was learning.

The girl whispered, "What happens if we go?"

Her mother started to answer.

Road worker said, "Don't scare her."

Mother said, "I was going to say we wait because waiting is easier."

"Good answer."

The girl looked unconvinced.

So was I.

The younger road worker scratched jaw.

"If cap's dead, nearest field cut is next live post. That leaves whole gap riding raw."

Older said, "Unless break's only marker lead."

"Grass says not."

"Grass says charge isn't draining here. Doesn't tell you why."

I knew that argument.

Observation versus mechanism.

Hessa would have approved in a deeply unpleasant way.

I kept mouth shut.

Protocol stayed fifty-nine, fifty-three, one hundred thirty-nine.

Not my test.

Not my road.

Not my instrument.

Good.

Five minutes.

Ten.

The bells rang less often but grass remained silver.

Another cart approached from west.

Driver stood on brake and raised red paddle from seat side.

Other cart stopped before failed post.

Its driver saw layby, saw our bells, and backed into smaller shoulder pocket.

Shared system.

No messenger.

No Greg.

Nice.

Then from east came two wardens on low two-wheeled road skiff pulled by one narrow gray animal.

The skiff had a copper cage mounted over rear axle.

Inside, something blue pulsed.

Not a lantern.

Too dim.

More like trapped heartbeat.

The girl said, "Magic."

Her mother said, "Yes."

Finally.

Wardens wore yellow cord like road workers but with black caps.

One got down carrying a hooked wooden pole.

The other stayed by skiff.

Driver called, "Third cap west of layby didn't take."

Warden said, "We see."

No gratitude.

No insult.

Work.

He approached failed post from ditch side, not road.

Used wooden pole to touch copper cap.

Nothing.

Then lowered pole toward groove.

A blue thread crawled from grass to pole hook, ran down to a little ceramic bulb near handle, and died.

He did it twice.

Then yelled to partner.

"Ground's open."

Younger road worker muttered, "Told you."

Older one said, "You said cap."

"I said cap or ground."

"You said cap's dead."

"Spiritually."

Driver shushed them.

Warden opened small hatch at base of post with another wood tool.

Inside I saw braided copper dropping into dark ceramic sleeve.

He did not touch it.

Instead he connected a temporary chain from post base to a spike carried from skiff.

Not ordinary chain.

Every third link was ceramic.

He drove spike into ditch earth using wooden maul.

Then signaled partner.

The skiff's blue cage brightened.

For one second the whole roadside changed.

Silver grass flattened away from failed post.

Blue line snapped from dozens of seed heads toward temporary spike.

The bells under our roof screamed.

Not metaphor.

All four rang so hard the brass blurred.

The talking man yelled.

The little girl laughed.

Children were insane.

Driver covered one road strider's eye with hand and kept other rein loose.

I gripped bench.

Not because danger reached us.

Because the field changed pressure in my teeth.

Just enough.

A deep metallic taste.

Then gone.

The failed post lit blue.

Not bright.

Alive.

Warden watched for half minute.

Raised green paddle.

Driver waited.

Older road worker said, "She'll wait second cycle."

Driver glanced back.

"Correct."

One minute later the post lit again when silver grass moved.

Temporary ground held.

Driver lifted brake.

We rolled out only after wardens waved.

No applause.

No chapter-ending insight.

Just road opening.

I looked back.

The warden was already writing tag for post.

The other cart crossed after us.

Infrastructure.

That was what magic looked like when people got tired of dying from it.

Not spells.

Procedures.

Laybys.

Wooden poles.

Ceramic links.

A driver who knew when not to be brave.

I liked it more than I expected.

The talking man recovered first.

"That happens often?"

Road worker said, "No."

His partner said, "Often enough."

"Which is it?"

"Both."

"That's not possible."

"It absolutely is."

Then the younger worker started explaining seasonal ridge charge, and his partner gave up on stopping him.

I listened for a while.

Not because I needed to turn it into profit.

Because it was interesting.

Important distinction.

High Cinder stretch lasted another hour.

The bells rang twice more.

No stop.

Then copper beads in the road striders' manes went dull.

Driver said, "Metal up if you want."

Merchant lifted chain case.

Talking man resumed family history mid-thought like he had only been briefly paused by magic.

"My brother also thinks five copper is nothing, which is easy for him because he has a room already..."

I looked out road.

Five copper.

Yesterday I had refused to spend eleven on an artifact because eleven crossed reserve.

Today I had spent five on travel because five bought time, rest, and return to places where unresolved useful things actually existed.

Same purse.

Different decision.

That was the point.

No deprivation performance.

No rich-guy performance.

Price depended on what it bought.

By late afternoon the road descended toward Lakeward basin.

Water appeared first as light.

Then roofs.

Then docks.

My shoulder appreciated every mile I had not crutched.

My right leg appreciated it too.

Public cart was not laziness.

It was body infrastructure rented by the day.

That thought sounded irritatingly like something I'd write down.

I did not.

Growth.

We entered Lakeward before dusk.

Driver handed bags down.

I climbed carefully.

She passed crutches after me.

"Still thanking me?"

"Yes."

"Then trip went well."

"Your standards are inspiring."

"My standards are arrival."

Fair.

I paid nothing else.

The five was already gone.

Thirteen copper remained.

Above floor by three.

Selective constraint.

Not crisis.

I could have found cheapest floor and eaten.

Instead I went to post counter first.

Because timing was now plausible.

The clerk checked name slate.

"Greg?"

"Unfortunately."

"Two slips."

There.

My stomach did stupid little thing again.

First was Bracken reference.

Not money.

I opened that first because I had apparently learned restraint in one domain.

**BRACKEN RIDGE ROAD OFFICE**

**PRIOR HAZARD REPORT RECEIVED BEFORE WEST-ACCESS TEMPORARY CLOSURE. SUBSEQUENT RIDGE INSPECTION CONFIRMED CONTINUED CLOSURE PENDING REPAIR. THIS NOTICE CERTIFIES REPORT TIMING AND RECEIPT ONLY.**

Perfect.

Not "Greg was right."

Not "Greg diagnosed hazard."

Timing and receipt.

Useful.

Narrow.

Second slip had merchant hall mark.

Lakeward reed-glass buyer.

I opened it.

**SIX BUNDLES RECEIVED. SIX ACCEPTED TO CONTRACT SPECIFICATION. INTRODUCTION FEE 5C PAYABLE AT HALL DESK.**

There.

Actual money.

Not pending.

Not hoped.

Not probably.

I read it twice.

Then walked to merchant hall before closing.

Buyer in dark green vest was still there.

Ink on three fingers again.

She looked up.

"You came back."

"Eventually."

"The glass passed."

"I heard."

"Four were clean. Two had ugly color."

"Color was irrelevant."

"Yes."

She reached under desk and counted five copper.

No ceremony.

I took it.

Thirteen became eighteen again.

Interesting.

I had paid five copper to return to Lakeward and then received five copper because earlier work had matured while I was traveling.

Net purse unchanged from morning.

Position changed.

Cinder behind me.

Lakeward here.

Bracken reference in notebook.

Glass commission closed.

East Fen buyer relationship now real.

Stillhook still gone.

South Road notice still copied.

Work limb still unfinished.

Vale still twenty-three.

No debt paid.

No victory montage.

Just motion.

Buyer said, "East Fen can make more?"

"Probably."

"Probably is expensive word."

"Orin can tell you better."

"You have contact?"

"Yes."

"Good. I'm buying direct next time."

There.

Brokerage success reducing need for broker.

Also real.

I smiled.

"Then I did my job too well."

"You got paid."

"Once."

She shrugged.

"If I need another source, I'll ask."

Not recurring income.

Not guaranteed lane.

A completed introduction.

Good.

I asked, "Any complaint from carrier?"

"None. Rack terms were right."

"Good."

That was enough.

I left.

Outside merchant hall, Lakeward evening smelled like water, hot oil, wet rope, and food I could not identify.

Home was still not here.

Hark & Venn still not here.

Hessa still not here.

The work limb was somewhere west beyond more road and timing.

But Lakeward was closer to those lanes than Cinder.

I could decide tomorrow.

Maybe home.

Maybe wait one day for mail.

Maybe inspect a better road route.

Maybe pay again.

That last thought no longer felt like failure.

Useful.

I bought dinner with bits.

Ordinary.

Then a bunk.

Ordinary.

No philosophical crisis.

I sat on cot and updated notebook.

**18c start.**

**-5c Cinder -> Lakeward public cart.**

**13c arrival.**

**+5c East Fen / Lakeward introduction fee after 6/6 acceptance.**

**18c end.**

Then:

**Bracken reference received. Certifies timing + later closure only.**

Then:

**High Cinder charge-weather infrastructure observed. Failed ground at third capped marker west of layby. Wardens installed temporary ground. Do not generalize mechanism beyond observation.**

I paused.

That sounded like Hessa.

Terrible.

Then:

**Travel can be purchased when time/body/location value exceeds fare. Stop forcing every road into a job.**

That one I underlined once.

Not twice.

Protocol fifty-nine, fifty-three, one hundred thirty-nine.

No magic test.

No limb wear.

Shoulder better after sitting most of day.

Cash eighteen.

Vale twenty-three.

Bits unspecified.

Ten floor.

No debt closure.

No artifact.

No new employer.

No new friend.

I had spent a chapter going somewhere.

That was enough.

For once, the road had done the interesting part.
