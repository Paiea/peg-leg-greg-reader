CHAPTER 434

THE WEARER

The Hark & Venn note was still on the table in the morning.

Lyssa had put a cup beside it.

Not the cracked cup.

Apparently there were limits to symbolism before breakfast.

I sat down, looked at the fold, and did not immediately open it.

Lyssa was already dressed for work, hair tied back badly enough that one side had escaped. She was sorting dark thread into three piles and muttering at one of them.

I said, "You know the letter is watching me."

"It is paper."

"Hostile paper."

"Open it or eat."

"Those are not mutually exclusive."

"They are if you drip lentils on it."

Good point.

I opened it.

No dramatic seal.

No bill folded inside.

No tiny drawing of a leg with a knife through it.

Strong start.

The note was short.

**HARK & VENN WORK ORDER**

**RETURN FOR FIT REVIEW BEFORE FINAL SOCKET WORK.**

**BRING CURRENT TRIAL COMPONENTS, LINER, AND WEAR NOTES.**

**EXISTING QUOTE UNCHANGED. NO ADDITIONAL PAYMENT REQUESTED FOR THIS REVIEW.**

**DO NOT INCREASE UNSUPERVISED WEAR ONLY BECAUSE TRAVEL SESSIONS WERE TOLERATED.**

I read the last line twice.

Lyssa looked over.

"Did they write specifically to insult you?"

"Apparently."

"Good shop."

"Everyone is joining Hessa."

"Maybe because you make the same face before doing something stupid."

"I have several faces."

"You have one face and too much confidence in it."

I folded the note.

That was the entire financial event.

No surprise balance.

No due date.

No threat.

Existing quote unchanged.

Twenty copper deposit still paid.

One silver and eighteen copper remained the quoted total for the work order, but the note did not tell me what portion was due when, and neither did I.

Excellent.

I had twelve copper.

I kept twelve copper.

Progress could occur without money leaving my hand.

That felt suspicious after the last month.

Lyssa held up two spools.

"Which is darker?"

"The left."

She swapped hands.

"Now?"

"The right."

"Wrong."

"You cheated."

"I moved them."

"Exactly."

She stared at me.

I stared back.

Then she said, "Take your leg and leave."

Romance.

The trial components were still stored where I had left them.

Socket.

Liner.

Temporary ash shaft.

Medium test foot.

Nothing glamorous.

The whole assembly looked less like a replacement body part than a workshop argument that had learned to stand upright.

I checked everything before packing it.

No split in temporary shaft.

No obvious socket crack.

Foot attachment tight by hand check.

Liner dry.

Straps intact.

I did not improve anything.

I put the pieces in the carrying cage, tied it closed, and discovered again that carrying a leg while using two crutches was an excellent advertisement for owning a leg.

The route to Hark & Venn was familiar enough that I stopped thinking about turns.

That was another kind of progress.

Not important enough to write down.

The workshop smelled the same.

Wood dust.

Oil.

Hot leather.

Something sharp and mineral from the rear room.

A man at the front bench was filing a metal brace while talking to a customer who kept answering questions he had not been asked.

"No, because I can walk stairs. I told the other fellow I can walk stairs, and he said that does not mean the hinge is correct, which I understand, obviously, but I can walk stairs, so the hinge cannot be completely wrong, can it? Because if it were completely wrong, stairs would become more difficult, and they are difficult, but stairs are always difficult, especially the narrow kind, which is why I think the hinge is mostly correct."

The bench man kept filing.

"Sit."

"I can sit."

"Good."

The customer sat.

Different speech.

Excellent.

I waited.

Nobody rushed because Greg had returned from the east with exciting stories about capitalism.

Also excellent.

A woman from the rear room came out, saw the carrying cage, and pointed at the fitting bench.

"Work order?"

I handed her the note.

She read my name.

"Trial set?"

I lifted cage slightly.

"Unless somebody replaced it with bread."

She did not laugh.

Better.

"Bring notes."

I gave her the notebook.

That hurt more.

She read while I sat.

Not everything.

Only wear entries.

Short sessions.

Skin response.

Surface.

Crutch use.

Shoulder state.

Travel rest.

She stopped at Lakeward courtyard entry.

"Twelve minutes."

"Yes."

"One crutch brief."

"Yes."

"Two-hand basin carry."

"Empty."

"Good."

She turned page.

"Why no wear after that?"

"Road travel. Then Cinder. Then I did not want to increase while shoulder was recovering."

She nodded once.

Not praise.

Just information fitting into place.

"Socket off when you stopped?"

"Yes."

"Pink where?"

I pointed.

Upper contact, slightly front and outside.

She pressed the liner there.

"Any heat?"

"No."

"Deep ache?"

"No."

"Skin open?"

"No."

"Phantom change?"

"No."

"Residual pain after?"

"No."

She looked at me.

"You rehearsed."

"I have had a lot of people ask me questions."

"Good."

That one might have been praise.

Dangerous.

She took the test socket from cage and ran two fingers along inside.

Then she compared it to my residual limb without fitting, eyes moving between shape and old pencil marks on socket edge.

"You changed."

I looked down.

"Usually."

"Volume."

"Ah."

She pressed lightly around upper tissue.

Not painful.

"Travel?"

"Several weeks."

"More walking on crutches. Some trial wear. Lots of sitting. Bad food."

"Bad food does not help me."

"It helped me."

"No."

Fair.

She brought a narrow cloth tape.

Measurements.

Three points.

Then again.

Then checked old workshop card pulled from a drawer.

No announcement.

No dramatic "your body has transformed."

Numbers.

That was better.

She said, "Old front pad is too much now."

"Too much pressure?"

"Too much correction for current shape."

"Difference?"

"If I leave it, you may tolerate ten minutes and hate thirty."

That was concrete enough.

She removed a thin temporary pad from inside test socket.

Replaced it with a narrower wedge from a tray.

Then added a small soft strip higher on the inside wall.

I watched.

Did not ask why every six seconds.

Growth.

She handed liner over.

"Fit."

I did.

Socket on.

Temporary shaft.

Foot.

Both crutches.

Stand.

The first load felt wrong.

Not painful.

Wrong.

My left side arrived half a beat earlier than expected.

I shifted.

She said, "Don't fix it."

I stopped.

Annoying.

She crouched and watched.

"Again. Weight off. Then on."

I did.

"Where?"

"Upper inside catches first."

"Pressure or contact?"

"Contact."

"Then?"

"Lower front."

She marked socket.

"Again."

Three cycles.

Then she took it off.

Adjusted strip.

Back on.

Standing again.

Better.

Not perfect.

Better enough that my body stopped trying to solve it before I asked.

She said, "Walk rail."

Parallel rails ran along one side of fitting room.

I had used them before.

Crutches stayed with me first.

Ten steps.

Turn.

Ten back.

She watched foot.

Then hip.

Then shoulder.

Not face.

Specialists had priorities.

"Again slower."

I did.

At step six she said, "You're shortening right."

I stopped.

"Because left is long?"

"Maybe."

That answer pleased me.

She checked height.

No change.

"Not long."

"Then why?"

"You learned to protect it."

I frowned.

"The left?"

"Whole pattern."

She gestured.

"Crutch habit. Right leg takes certainty. Left gets permission after."

That sounded true enough to be dangerous.

"Can I correct it?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"Not by thinking harder."

Hostile profession.

She put one finger against rail.

"Stand. Both feet where I put them."

I did.

"Weight right."

Done.

"Center."

Done.

"Left enough to feel socket."

Done.

"Center."

Again.

No walking.

Just transfers.

After six, she said, "Now step."

I stepped.

Right side still wanted to hurry.

Less.

"Again."

We did that for several minutes.

Not training montage.

Nobody became beautiful.

The test foot made a small dull knock every time I placed it badly.

Useful feedback.

Annoying feedback.

At one point the customer from front room leaned around partition.

"I also shorten one side."

The fitter said, "You are not in this fitting."

"I know, I was only saying."

"Don't."

He disappeared.

Good.

After about eight minutes total standing and walking, she stopped me.

"Off."

"I feel fine."

"Wonderful."

"That sounded hostile."

"It was eight minutes."

Right.

Socket off.

Skin check.

She looked before I did.

Mild pink at upper inside contact.

Nothing sharp.

No lower front hotspot.

She pressed.

"Pain?"

"No."

"Heat?"

"No."

We waited.

Not exciting.

That was the point.

Five minutes.

Pink already fading.

She wrote on workshop card.

Then looked at my notes again.

"You want hands."

I looked up.

"Yes."

"Not distance."

"Distance too, eventually."

"But work order priority."

That had apparently survived.

"Hands."

She nodded.

"Good. Then stop treating one-crutch walking as the test."

I thought about stillhook.

One hand on stud.

One support hand free if leg worked.

Or two hands briefly if standing geometry allowed.

"Standing tasks?"

"Yes."

"Carrying?"

"Light, controlled."

"How light?"

She looked around.

There was a wooden tray on shelf.

Empty.

She put it in my hands.

"That."

Scientific units remained difficult.

I stood with trial limb and both crutches.

Then she took left crutch away.

One crutch right.

Tray left.

Fine.

Then she took right crutch too.

I looked at her.

She said, "Rails."

Both rails within reach.

Right.

Not free standing in middle of room.

I held tray with both hands for maybe five seconds.

My body stiffened immediately.

Not falling.

Preparing too hard.

She tapped tray.

"Put down."

I did.

"Again. Don't perform standing."

"I was standing."

"You were auditioning."

Theatre had infected medicine.

I tried again.

This time knees softer.

Weight mostly right, some left.

Tray in both hands.

Three breaths.

Four.

I reached one hand to rail.

No drama.

She said, "Better."

I said, "Could carry a lamp."

"Not yet."

"A very light lamp."

"No."

"An empty lamp."

"No."

"Hostile."

"Alive."

We repeated with tray from one shelf to another two steps away.

Both hands only during the actual lift.

One hand to rail before moving feet.

Then place.

Then reset.

The movement was absurdly small.

It was also more useful than another hundred feet of hallway walking.

I could imagine work.

Not imaginary heroic work.

Real work.

Carry folded cloth.

Move a plate.

Hold a ledger while standing.

Open a door with one hand while the other steadied.

Use an artifact that demanded continuous contact.

That last one stayed in my head.

I did not tell the fitter about a stillhook I did not own.

Strong discipline.

She stopped the session at fifteen minutes total wear.

I had forgotten to count.

Excellent.

Socket off again.

Skin.

Upper inside pink.

Fading.

No heat.

No pain.

She wrote.

Then she took the socket back to bench.

"Leave trial socket."

I looked at it.

"Why?"

"Final work needs new shape."

There.

Progress.

"New mold?"

"Not full."

She held up the old work card.

"Measurements changed enough I don't want final socket cut from old front correction. We adjust model from today and keep this trial here so shop can compare."

"Final socket work starts now?"

"Prep."

Specific.

Good.

"Does that change quote?"

"No."

"Payment?"

"Not today."

Also specific.

Excellent.

"When do I come back?"

"When shop sends note, or if we ask you to test another temporary fit first."

No invented date.

No fantasy scheduling certainty.

"What do I wear until then?"

"Crutches."

I looked at empty left trouser.

"I had guessed."

"Good."

"Any home trial?"

"Not with this socket. It's staying."

Ah.

That changed something.

The trial limb had been mine in the practical sense for weeks.

Not ownership.

Availability.

Now it was not coming home.

I felt oddly disappointed.

She saw.

"That a problem?"

"No."

"Good."

"I liked having it."

"Then finish it."

Simple.

I almost said I was trying.

Did not.

The work existed whether I narrated effort or not.

She packed liner separately and handed it back.

"Keep liner clean. Bring it next time."

"Anything else?"

"Do not make your crutch gait worse because you miss the test leg."

I stared.

"Was that likely?"

"Yes."

Rude.

Probably correct.

I asked one more question.

"Today changes what?"

She answered immediately.

"Fit model. Current load pattern. Work priority."

"Not final clearance."

"No."

"Not finished limb."

"No."

"Not payment."

"No."

"Good."

She looked at me.

"You need every sentence boxed?"

"Sometimes."

"Then box that."

I did.

Outside, I had the carrying cage without the socket, shaft, and foot.

Much lighter.

Mostly liner and straps.

That should have felt good.

Instead it felt like walking home after leaving part of a future body on somebody else's bench.

Not sad.

Not exactly.

Just strange.

I stopped at a food stall and bought lunch with bits.

No copper.

Twelve remained twelve.

Vale twenty-three.

Ten floor.

Two above.

Economic position narrow.

Earning capacity better than the purse suggested.

Same story.

I did not need to repeat it into submission.

At home, Lyssa was not there.

Good.

She had a life.

The room was quieter without her work noise.

I put liner where it stayed clean.

The high shelf cup watched me.

"Don't."

It said nothing.

Strong relationship.

I opened notebook.

**H&V REVIEW**

Then exact note terms.

Existing quote unchanged.

No additional payment requested for review.

Trial socket retained by shop.

Today's measurements changed enough that final socket prep should not use old front correction unchanged.

No final clearance.

No pickup date.

No new payment schedule.

Bring liner next time.

Wait for shop note or requested temporary refit.

Then body.

Fifteen minutes total supervised test wear.

Adjusted upper inside contact.

No sharp pressure.

Pink fading after removal.

Right-side shortening appears protective habit, not established length issue.

Standing-task priority remains hands, not distance.

Two-hand tray hold only within rail reach.

No lamp.

I underlined that last one because apparently adults required rules.

Then I wrote:

**Trial equipment leaving home is progress, not loss.**

I stared at it.

Too neat.

I crossed out **progress, not loss**.

Wrote:

**Shop kept trial socket because final prep needs current fit. I miss having it. Both can be true.**

Better.

The door opened.

Lyssa came in carrying cloth under one arm and a paper sack in the other.

She looked at empty cage.

"Where's the leg?"

"Shop kept it."

She stopped.

"Bad?"

"No."

"Then why?"

"Final socket prep."

Her face changed slightly.

Interested.

Not celebration.

"Good?"

"Probably."

She pointed at me.

"That was a question."

"Yes. Good."

"Then say good."

"Good."

She put cloth down.

"Did you pay?"

"No."

That got more reaction.

"Excellent."

"Existing quote unchanged."

"Even better."

"You're very romantic about money."

"I live with you."

Fair.

She opened paper sack.

Bread.

Onions.

I looked at onions.

Then at her.

"Road house."

"What?"

"Nothing."

She began putting food away.

I told her fitter said my right side was shortening because I had learned to protect the left.

Lyssa said, "You do that."

I frowned.

"You've noticed?"

"You limp differently when tired."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because you already know you limp."

"Different pattern."

She shrugged.

"I am not Hessa."

"Everyone keeps saying that while acting like Hessa."

"I said you move differently. I did not write a report."

Fair.

She looked at me again.

"Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Then good."

That was her evidence ceiling.

Simple.

Human.

Enough.

I did not turn dinner into a biomechanics conference.

We ate.

The sealed Hark & Venn note was gone now because it had become an opened Hark & Venn note folded inside my notebook.

One old uncertainty converted into three narrower ones.

Final socket prep had begun.

No date.

No payment due today.

No guarantee.

Good.

The limb was not finished.

Also good, in a more irritating way.

I had twelve copper.

Vale had twenty-three.

Protocol stayed fifty-nine, fifty-three, one hundred thirty-nine.

No magic.

No artifact.

No income.

No debt payment.

But the body lane had moved.

Not because I spent more.

Because I returned to the specialist, let them tell me the old fit was no longer the right fit, and left part of the equipment behind so they could make the next version.

That was less satisfying than buying something.

Probably healthier.

Lyssa finished eating and pointed at my coat.

"Tomorrow I fix that seam."

"I can do it."

"No."

"You told me to fix my own seam."

"That was before I saw how you bought thread."

"I have been professionally insulted all day."

"Good."

I looked at her.

At the room.

At the liner drying clean.

At the empty place where the trial cage had been heavier yesterday.

Tomorrow still existed.

So did the leg.

Just not here.

For once, that was exactly where it needed to be.
