CHAPTER 391

THE RETURNER

Carrow had replaced the awning.

That was the first thing I noticed.

Not Lyssa.

Not home.

Awnings.

The market awning that had torn while I was in Duskport was now striped brown and cream instead of green.

Apparently civilization had survived.

I got down from passenger coach on two crutches.

The trial leg rode in my bag.

Masta had allowed travel in it.

She had not required stupidity.

Three hours sitting had made the upper edge noticeable, so I took it off before the last road stop and finished the trip on equipment that had already proven itself.

Crutches.

Excellent.

The driver set my bag on curb.

"That all?"

"Yes."

He looked at tied prosthesis bag.

Then at me.

Then decided not to ask.

Professional restraint.

I tipped one bit because he had moved the heavier bag without making me turn it into a negotiation.

Carrow smelled like wet stone, frying onions, horse, dye, and one bakery overcommitting to cinnamon.

Home smell.

Not poetic.

Specific.

I crossed market slowly.

The green awning was gone.

Three onion baskets remained.

Different baskets probably.

I hoped.

Theatre notice board had a new sheet over the harbor comedy Lyssa had mentioned.

**TWO EXTRA NIGHTS.**

Good for them.

I had missed first one.

Also good.

Life did not hold seats.

At the corner near our street, Jessa came out of Marra's work room carrying folded gray cloth.

She saw me.

Looked at bag.

Looked at crutches.

"Back?"

"Apparently."

"Lyssa's working late."

There.

No surprise party.

No one had been waiting at window measuring absence.

"Thanks."

Jessa adjusted cloth.

"Blue cord got there."

"I paid postage. It had better."

"Wrong blue."

I stopped.

She smiled.

"Mostly."

Hostile workplace ecology.

"How wrong?"

"Lyssa used it."

That was not answer.

Jessa kept walking.

Good.

I went home.

Door stuck at bottom in wet weather.

Still.

I put one crutch against wall.

Pushed with shoulder.

Door opened.

Home.

The room looked almost exactly like the room I had left.

Almost mattered.

Dark cloth on table.

One cup I did not own.

Tavi's slate near window.

A stack of folded work pieces where my notebook had previously occupied more territory.

My chair had moved six inches.

Not insult.

Work.

I closed door.

The blue cord was on table.

Twelve feet had become maybe four.

A narrow strip had already been sewn along inside edge of dark garment.

Wrong blue apparently compatible with commerce.

Good.

My upstairs room was still mine.

That sentence remained strange.

I went up.

Bed.

Desk.

Window.

One boot under chair.

One empty space beside it where another boot used to live before leg stopped needing one.

The space had been empty long enough that it no longer surprised me every time.

Today it did a little.

Because I had brought replacement equipment home in a bag.

I set the trial leg there.

Not in empty shoe space.

Beside desk.

Too obvious otherwise.

Then moved it to wall.

Then back beside desk because wall caught window draft.

I was becoming domestic about prosthetic storage.

Excellent.

My Duskport receipt went in notebook.

Resident rate still had unused nights.

No refund.

I had left anyway.

This felt good in way I refused to make important.

I unpacked spare liner.

Wrench.

Clothes.

No artifact.

No Veyr compass.

No shelf object.

The bag became lighter.

Then I went downstairs and discovered home had not paused.

There was a note under cup.

**Tavi at Rena's until fourth bell.**

Another note beside it in Lyssa's handwriting.

**Soup pot is for tonight. Do not let him claim this was arranged for you.**

There.

Conspiracy anticipated.

I opened lid.

Soup.

Thick.

Beans.

Greens.

Something smoked.

Consequences.

I put lid back.

Too early.

Masta's wear schedule said short use after travel if skin was clean.

Skin clean.

No heat.

No irritation.

I could put leg on.

I did not.

Not yet.

Because I had just traveled.

Because room existed.

Because crutches worked.

This was apparently decision now instead of defeat.

Annoying.

I made tea.

Sat.

Carrow sounds through window.

Cart wheel hitting same bad stone.

Someone selling pears too aggressively.

Two children arguing about chalk.

A hammer from somewhere behind work room.

Normal.

I had been gone long enough to miss things.

Not long enough for city to care.

At fourth bell Tavi arrived before Lyssa.

Door opened.

He came in carrying school slate and one half-eaten thing wrapped in paper.

He stopped.

Looked at me.

Looked at crutches.

Looked toward stairs.

"Where is it?"

No greeting.

"Hello, Tavi."

"Leg."

"Duskport kept it."

His face fell.

I waited.

Three.

Two.

One.

"Greg."

"I brought it."

He dropped slate on table.

"Show."

"Careful. That slate has future."

"Show."

I went upstairs on crutches.

He followed halfway.

Stopped when I looked back.

Good.

Some household laws had survived.

I brought the trial leg down.

Tavi stared.

Not reverent.

Interested.

"That is ugly."

"Thank you."

"It looks like boot got promoted."

I looked at it.

Leather shell.

Straps.

Foot.

He was not wrong.

"Can you run?"

"No."

"Can you kick?"

"Probably. Not you."

"Can you jump?"

"No reason."

"Can you carry soup?"

There.

We had arrived.

"Maybe."

"You said water."

"Water was test."

"Soup is test."

"No."

"Lyssa said soup science."

"Lyssa made tactical error."

Tavi grinned.

I set leg on chair.

"Not now."

"Why?"

"Because I traveled."

"So?"

"So body is tired."

"You sit on coach."

"Still body."

He considered this.

Did not like it.

Accepted anyway.

"After dinner?"

"Maybe."

"Depends?"

I stared at him.

"Who taught you that word?"

"Masta."

"You have never met Masta."

"Your letter."

Right.

Information leak.

At fifth bell Lyssa came home with pins in cuff and a folded parcel under one arm.

She opened door with hip.

Saw me.

Stopped.

Not long.

One beat.

Then parcel went on table.

"You're back."

"I found route."

She came over.

Kissed me.

Ordinary enough that it hurt more than ceremony would have.

She leaned back.

Looked at my face.

Then crutches.

Then trial leg on chair.

"You brought it."

"Trial."

"Still."

"Still."

Tavi pointed.

"He won't carry soup."

Lyssa took coat off.

"He just got here."

"I got here hours ago."

Wrong alliance.

Lyssa looked at me.

"Skin?"

"Clean."

"Heat?"

"No."

"Travel?"

"Took it off before final road stretch."

She nodded.

Not Masta.

Not therapist.

Just person who knew enough now to ask better questions.

Then she unwrapped parcel.

Customer garment.

Work continued.

"I have to finish hem before morning."

"Do that."

"I was going to."

Correct.

No reunion cancellation of work.

I liked that.

Tavi opened soup pot.

Lyssa said, "Not yet."

He closed it.

Household authority superior to mine.

I put trial leg on before dinner.

Not for Tavi.

Mostly.

Skin still clean.

Liner.

Shell.

Straps.

Stand.

Home floor was flatter than Duskport street.

Also more dangerous because I knew it.

Familiarity encouraged speed.

I did not give it speed.

Three passes across room.

Turn.

Return.

Fine.

Lyssa stitched at table.

Tavi watched without pretending not to.

"Walk normal," he said.

"I am."

"No. Like normal normal."

"Excellent clinical language."

Lyssa did not look up.

"Tavi."

"What?"

"Let him walk."

Good.

I walked.

Not normal.

Not old normal.

Current normal maybe.

That word could wait.

After fifteen minutes upper outside remained quiet.

I sat.

Checked skin.

Slight pink.

Expected.

No bad spot.

Leg back on.

Dinner.

Lyssa ladled soup into three bowls.

Then looked at me.

I looked at her.

Tavi looked between us.

Absolutely unbearable.

"No," I said.

Tavi said, "Coward."

Lyssa covered mouth.

She was laughing.

Betrayal.

"I crossed mountains."

"Soup."

"I have been stabbed."

"Soup."

"I lost a leg."

Tavi pointed at replacement.

"Found one."

That was good.

I hated him.

Lyssa put two bowls on counter.

Not three.

"Flat route," she said.

I looked at her.

"Whose side are you on?"

"My floor."

There.

Masta would approve.

Probably.

Counter to table was six steps.

Flat.

No threshold.

No turn if I approached right.

I tested first without bowls.

Tavi groaned.

"Testing."

"Soup gets cold."

"Consequences."

He had created monster.

I returned counter.

One bowl each hand.

Not tray.

Soup heavier than water cup and less forgiving than pride.

Steam.

Smelled good.

I lifted.

Two hands occupied.

No crutches.

There.

First step.

Second.

Foot landed clean.

Third.

Upper body wanted to lean because right bowl was fuller.

I corrected bowl, not body.

Fourth.

Fifth.

Sixth.

Table.

I set Lyssa's bowl down.

Then Tavi's.

No spill.

Not one drop.

Tavi inspected rim.

Then table.

Then floor.

"Again."

Absolutely not.

"No."

"You need yours."

Damn.

Lyssa laughed openly now.

I went back.

One bowl.

Two hands anyway.

Because I could.

Carried mine.

Sat.

Tavi looked disappointed.

"You should carry bread too."

"Certification ended."

"Advanced level."

"Closed program."

Lyssa said, "Eat."

Finally.

Authority.

We ate.

Soup was good.

Tavi asked whether Duskport had sea monsters.

I said no.

Then remembered windrams were from another road and not this life.

Wrong story.

Nothing.

Duskport had gulls big enough to count as organized crime.

I told him about auction instead.

Not the Veyr theory.

Just losing by one copper.

"Why didn't you pay more?" he asked.

"Because I decided before bidding what it was worth to me."

"Then you decided wrong."

Lyssa said, "He has a point."

"No."

Tavi ate bread.

"Did winner get it?"

"Yes."

"Then wrong."

Economics defeated.

After dinner Lyssa finished hem.

Tavi did schoolwork badly and then less badly.

I wore trial leg another twelve minutes before warmth started.

Small.

I stopped.

Took it off.

Crutches returned.

Nobody sighed.

Nobody called it setback.

Tavi said, "Tomorrow soup again?"

"No."

"Water?"

"No."

"Plate?"

"Maybe."

There.

Future negotiator.

When Tavi went to wash, Lyssa looked at the trial leg beside chair.

"How does it feel?"

That question had several answers.

Pressure.

Weight.

Heat.

Balance.

Hands.

Loss.

Return.

I chose one.

"Useful."

She nodded.

Then, after a second:

"And?"

Cruel.

I looked at her.

"It felt good bringing soup."

There.

Same sentence shape as letter.

Harder in person.

Lyssa reached across table and touched my hand.

Not leg.

Hand.

"Good."

We sat there while Tavi splashed water somewhere he had been told not to.

Then Lyssa pulled her hand back because she still had work.

I carried my empty bowl to wash basin on crutches.

One hand.

Awkward.

Still.

The trial leg rested by chair.

Tomorrow I could wear it again.

Carrow had continued.

So had I.

Neither one had waited.

That seemed fair.
