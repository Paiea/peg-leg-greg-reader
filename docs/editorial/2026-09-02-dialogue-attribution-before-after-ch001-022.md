# Peg-Leg Greg Dialogue + Attribution Pass

## Before / After Debrief, Chapters 1-22

Status: **REVIEW ARTIFACT ONLY**

Source editorial branch: `editor/voice-compression-pass`

Verified dialogue-pass edge represented here: **Chapters 1-22**

This document is for evaluating the editorial direction before any dialogue-pass material is integrated into canonical manuscript authority.

It is not canon. It is not a structural compression proposal. It does not change chapter numbering, events, outcomes, magic facts, money facts, relationships, or body continuity.

The question is narrower:

**Does the book become easier to hear, easier to follow, and more character-specific when attribution handles speaker identity instead of forcing dialogue voice to do all of that work?**

---

# 1. What the manuscript felt like before this pass

The early manuscript already has a lot of good dialogue.

The problem is not that everyone sounds identical.

The problem is that several important characters are naturally dry, competent, quick, and comfortable giving short answers. In two-person scenes, that works extremely well. In scenes with three or more active speakers, the prose occasionally asks the reader to count turns or reconstruct the speaker from context.

That is a small visual-reading cost.

It is a much larger audio cost.

A reader can glance backward. A listener cannot.

This becomes especially noticeable in scenes involving combinations such as:

- Greg + Jorren + Alden;
- Greg + clerk + Jorren + Alden;
- Greg + Antonius + another specialist;
- Greg + a classroom or work group;
- Greg + multiple competent local workers who all speak concisely.

The result is not usually confusion for an entire scene. It is a repeated half-second of friction:

**Wait. Who said that?**

That half-second matters because the dialogue itself is often doing character work.

---

# 2. What the pass is actually changing

The pass is deliberately conservative.

Primary intervention:

- add plain `said` and `asked` where a speaker handoff is not instantly legible;
- re-establish speakers after interior thought, movement, interruption, or a long beat;
- tag more aggressively when three or more speakers are active;
- let action beats remain when they reveal behavior, not merely because the prose is avoiding `said`;
- preserve compact dialogue, shared local dryness, and relationship-driven conversational convergence.

The pass is **not** trying to make every character maximally different.

People who work together, live in the same city, or become friends can sound more alike over time.

The goal is that important recurring characters retain a persistent underlying fingerprint in what they notice, what they explain, what they refuse to explain, how they disagree, and what kind of problem they believe they are solving.

### Effect

Nothing about the joke changes.

The listener simply receives the joke immediately instead of decoding it first.

That is the core philosophy of this pass.

# 4. The bigger voice change emerging underneath the attribution work

The most useful finding so far is that PLG does **not** need bigger character voices.

It needs more clearly protected **domains of thought**.

Several characters can be terse, dry, competent, or skeptical without becoming the same person.

## Greg

Greg expands problems.

He negotiates definitions, keeps looking for the larger system, turns events into models, and has to learn when not to spend every new piece of information.

His humor often appears while reasoning.

## Jorren

Jorren sees physical truth.

He notices feet, stance, rhythm, fatigue, overcommitment, bad ground, and what the body is actually doing.

He often reaches the useful answer before Greg finishes building the theory.

He does not need to explain the theory.

## Alden

Alden seeks usable initiative.

He is fast, ambitious, willing to learn, and naturally assumes that an advantage he has earned is now his to spend.

As he improves, his voice should not become more academic. His questions should become better targeted.

## Hessa

Hessa's precision is instructional and bodily.

She cares what Greg actually did, what his current body can reproduce, how many times he cast, whether the variable changed, and when he needs to stop.

## Edrin

Edrin can sound similarly controlled around magic, but her authority is custody and ward responsibility.

She decides what may be touched, opened, tested, or released because she is responsible for the sealed object and the consequences.

## Antonius

Antonius's restraint is financial and operational.

He cares what is owned, what pays, what information is actually available, and what Greg is about to turn into a plan before the evidence exists.

## Octavia

Octavia's precision comes from freight.

A route is never just a route. Food, nails, medicine, furniture, passengers, cows, couriers, wagons, drivers, and horses fail differently.

`Free` is not the same as `temporarily unused` because the shed still belongs to an operating system.

This is a very strong voice because it grows directly from work.

## Dema Rusk

Dema Rusk's clarity comes from party recovery.

`STOP. COUNT. MOVE. DECIDE.`

She is teaching Bronze adventurers what happens after a plan fails.

Her preference for a followable imperfect decision belongs to that training context. It should not become a universal authorial philosophy placed into everyone else's mouth.

## One-chapter workers

The quartermaster in Chapter 20 may be one of the best models for the whole voice project.

He does not explain a safety framework.

He hears the roof crack and says:

> "Out."

When somebody grabs flour:

> "Leave it."

When the worker protests that it is thirty silver of flour:

> "It's not ten people."

That is character voice through responsibility.

No catchphrase required.

# 5. What feels better after the pass

## A. Audio clarity

This is the clearest improvement.

The prose becomes much easier to imagine in Read Aloud or audiobook form because the reader no longer has to maintain invisible turn counters during crowded scenes.

## B. Jokes land faster

A lot of PLG humor is dry and conversational.

A joke loses force if the reader spends the first half-second determining who made it.

The added tags mostly disappear when doing their job correctly.

## C. Character differences become subtler, not louder

This is important.

The pass gives us permission to let Greg and Alden sound socially closer because speaker identity no longer depends on them sounding maximally different.

It also lets multiple competent adults use short, dry answers while remaining distinct through domain and behavior.

## D. Greg is less gravitational

When another character's line is explicitly anchored, their competence feels more independent.

Jorren's answer belongs to Jorren.

Octavia's system belongs to Octavia.

The quartermaster's safety call belongs to the quartermaster.

The scene is less likely to feel as if everybody exists mainly to feed Greg observations.

---

# 6. What could go wrong if we overdo it

There is a real failure mode here.

Too many tags can turn sharp dialogue into a transcript.

The current pass should therefore keep the following rule:

**Two-person scenes remain light unless the speaker has been lost after a long beat. Three-plus-person scenes earn more explicit attribution.**

Another failure mode is confusing voice work with differentiation theater.

Do not give everybody:

- a signature phrase;
- a unique dialect;
- a verbal tic;
- a different joke density merely to make them identifiable;
- Hessa-style calibrated uncertainty because the author likes Hessa's method.

Voice should grow from person, relationship, work, education, confidence, and social context.

# 7. What the first 22 chapters tell us about the whole-manuscript pass

The original diagnosis appears correct:

**PLG does not primarily have a bad-dialogue problem. It has a speaker-clarity problem in specific crowded scenes, plus a long-serial risk that competent characters gradually converge toward Greg's analytical operating system.**

That distinction matters because the solution can stay surgical.

We do not need to rewrite every conversation.

In fact, a large number of chapters in the first 22 were deliberately marked **no change required** because their two-person dialogue already works.

Verified no-change chapters through this review edge:

**1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 18, 19**

Hard or targeted attribution work was identified in:

**2, 3, 8, 9, 10, 15, 16, 17, 20, 21, 22**

That is a healthy ratio.

The editor is not manufacturing edits merely because a chapter was opened.

# 8. My current read of the before / after

The "after" is stronger when it behaves like invisible infrastructure.

The best changes do not make me think:

**This dialogue is more stylish now.**

They make me stop thinking about attribution entirely.

That is exactly what we want.

The more interesting secondary effect is that the pass is revealing where the manuscript's character voice is already good.

Jorren, Alden, Hessa, Antonius, Octavia, Edrin, Arlo, and various one-scene workers do not need theatrical reinvention.

They need protection from long-serial drift.

The voice-library work is useful when it records **how they approach reality**, not a bag of phrases they must keep repeating.

If that principle holds across 301 chapters, the book should feel less like Greg speaking through a city full of avatars and more like Greg living among people who have their own methods, jobs, blind spots, humor, and authority.

That is the real prize of this pass.

# 9. Debrief questions for the next review

When comparing the before and after aloud, ask:

1. Do the added tags disappear, or do I notice them?
2. Do crowded conversations require less mental bookkeeping?
3. Do jokes land faster?
4. Can Greg and Alden become conversationally closer without becoming indistinguishable?
5. Does Jorren still feel more physical and immediate than Greg?
6. Do Hessa, Edrin, Antonius, Octavia, and other competent adults feel like different kinds of competence?
7. Are there places where a line itself should change rather than merely receive attribution?
8. Are we protecting local/shared Carrow speech rather than over-designing everybody?
9. Does the prose feel better in audio, not merely cleaner on a page?
10. Most importantly: does the scene become easier to inhabit because the reader is no longer solving speaker identity in the background?
