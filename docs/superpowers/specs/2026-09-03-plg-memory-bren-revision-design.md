# Peg-Leg Greg Memory and Bren Revision Design

## Status

Design approved in principle by the author on 2026-09-03. This file defines the revision architecture only. It does not authorize manuscript integration until the author reviews this written spec.

Source branch at design split: `editor/voice-compression-pass` at `810032cebf360e5b858c32d79a71feb19b4c773b`, where the dialogue/attribution pass had advanced through Chapter 196.

Design branch: `editor/bren-memory-repair-design`.

## Goal

Repair two linked manuscript weaknesses without flattening Peg-Leg Greg into a conventional action fantasy:

1. make Greg's first-life memory behavior feel psychologically consistent rather than conveniently selective;
2. give the Bren/extortion thread a legible escalation and payoff that fulfills the existing threat while preserving the novel's municipal, workplace, theatre, and evidence-driven identity.

The two repairs should reinforce each other. The Bren payoff should become an early major demonstration of the clarified memory rule and of the long-range convergence between Old Greg, second-life Greg, and Theatre Greg.

## Non-goals

Do not:

- explain Greg's memory with a new Chronoclast damage rule;
- create a hard taxonomy that the prose names or teaches;
- claim that Greg retains only technical memory and loses all personal memory;
- make Greg omniscient about his first life;
- turn Bren into the head of a newly invented criminal empire;
- invent a Vale/Bren connection without evidence;
- make Greg combat-capable through sudden magic escalation;
- allow independent personal illusion casting that violates current Hessa restrictions;
- transform theatre into retroactive secret combat training;
- replace Rinna's practical authority with Greg taking over the theatre's defense;
- solve the thread through a large fight merely because the buildup contains danger;
- renumber the entire post-220 manuscript while the active dialogue pass is still moving.

## Part I: Greg's memory rule

### Governing principle

Greg remembers what Old Greg repeatedly used more reliably than what Old Greg merely experienced.

Memory strength should correlate primarily with repeated retrieval, repeated action, embodied use, professional necessity, survival relevance, and durable habit, not with whether a detail appears narratively important to the current plot.

This is not a rigid neurological chart. It is a storytelling calibration rule that should create a recognizable pattern across the manuscript.

### Practical memory behaviors

#### 1. Embodied and procedural memory: strongest

Examples:

- weapon handling and support habits;
- threat assessment;
- spell structures repeatedly practiced over decades;
- fieldcraft and creature behavior personally encountered many times;
- equipment use;
- movement habits;
- professional routines;
- social or command behaviors repeatedly used under pressure.

These memories may arrive before Greg can consciously explain them. His body, attention, or judgment can sometimes know first.

This does not mean his nineteen-year-old body can execute what his old body could do. Knowledge transfer remains distinct from physical or magical capacity.

#### 2. Operational and semantic memory: generally strong, but historically vulnerable

Greg may remember what a common old-life thing did, how a known class of system worked, or what a particular danger normally meant.

He may still be wrong about:

- current terminology;
- present manufacture;
- local implementation;
- market value;
- who currently controls a system;
- whether forty years of history changed the context.

This preserves future knowledge as a real advantage without converting it into divination.

#### 3. Autobiographical scene memory: cue-dependent and uneven

Personal memories should often arrive through cues rather than direct search.

Useful cues include:

- smell;
- posture;
- an object;
- a phrase;
- a route;
- clothing;
- a scar;
- a physical habit;
- a type of room;
- a piece of work;
- a dangerous situation whose structure resembles an older one.

Greg may fail to retrieve a person or scene when deliberately searching for it, then remember a fragment later when something in the present activates the old pattern.

#### 4. Names, chronology, decorative details, and incidental appearance: especially slippery

Weak recall is most believable when the information was not repeatedly useful to Old Greg.

Examples:

- whether a bridge was painted;
- exact decorative color;
- the date of an event he did not personally organize;
- the name of a person whose role mattered more often than the name itself;
- what someone wore on a specific ordinary day;
- exact order among similar events decades apart.

Do not use this category to conveniently erase any fact that would complicate the plot. The test remains: would Old Greg have repeatedly retrieved or acted on this information?

### Emotional residue is independent from factual retrieval

Greg may retain emotional certainty even when factual retrieval is poor.

He can:

- know he loved someone while failing to retrieve the name;
- remember that a person made him feel safe while losing the exact conversation;
- remember the way someone laughed, stood, argued, or fought while losing chronology;
- remember a professional habit learned beside someone while losing who first taught it;
- carry guilt, affection, anger, grief, or familiarity without a clean autobiographical file attached.

This is a feature, not an inconsistency.

A particularly strong recurring shape is:

> Greg remembers exactly how to survive beside someone and cannot remember their name.

Use sparingly enough that it hurts when it appears.

### How to repair the existing manuscript

Do not add an explanatory chapter or lore speech.

Use a distributed surgical pass. Target roughly 6 to 10 locations where the existing prose currently creates the impression of arbitrary recall.

At each location, prefer one of four small repairs:

1. connect an unusually precise technical memory to repeated old-life use;
2. connect a failed incidental memory to lack of old-life attention or retrieval value;
3. let an autobiographical fragment arrive through a present cue rather than on demand;
4. separate emotional residue from missing factual detail.

These are normally paragraph-scale or sentence-scale edits. Do not add hundreds of words to prove the rule.

### Memory pass validation

After the pass, a fresh reader should be able to infer all of the following without being told them as rules:

- Greg's memory is not random;
- repeated-use knowledge is unusually durable;
- personal memory is not simply deleted;
- names and chronology can fail even when emotional or procedural residue survives;
- precise recall does not guarantee current-world accuracy;
- Greg's knowledge is still genuinely useful and sometimes unfairly strong.

## Chapter 220A: the threat becomes materially expensive

### Function

Fulfill the promise that Bren's next move is not merely another one-copper conversation.

The escalation should produce a concrete cost or constraint without requiring serious bodily violence.

### Desired pressure shape

Bren's side stops merely asking questions and makes refusal materially inconvenient.

Possible mechanisms that fit existing continuity include one strong primary event plus supporting evidence, such as:

- a supplier refusing or delaying delivery because someone followed or pressured them;
- a regular route becoming unsafe or unavailable after a direct warning;
- a theatre worker receiving a message with a larger demand or a more specific consequence;
- a small but costly piece of interference that demonstrates the earlier threat can reach ordinary business operations;
- Olin or another existing business node changing behavior because the threat reached them personally.

Do not stack all examples. Choose the one that creates the most life per word and uses already-established people or routes when possible.

### Required reader effect

By the end, the reader should understand:

- Bren's pressure is real;
- Rinna's refusal now costs something;
- the threat is larger than one weird man at the front door;
- nobody yet knows a complete criminal hierarchy;
- the theatre is not helpless, but the problem cannot be solved by pretending it went away.

### Ordinary-life requirement

The chapter must still contain normal work, money, theatre, food, delivery, repair, or route material. Danger collides with the ordinary rather than replacing the book's operating texture.

## Chapter 220B: Current Greg performs Old Greg

### Function

Deliver the confrontation/payoff the earlier buildup has earned.

Greg should deliberately use Old Greg's real knowledge and habits through second-life theatrical/perceptual skill to make Bren and several accompanying men uncertain enough to withdraw.

### Central synthesis

> Old Greg provides the truth. Second-life Greg provides the performance.

The scene should visibly depend on things Greg learned or practiced in the current life:

- timing;
- controlled eyelines;
- withholding explanation;
- silence;
- stage geometry;
- audience attention;
- costume or silhouette;
- prepared lighting or magical stage effects;
- ordinary misdirection;
- deciding what the target must notice and what Greg must not look at.

The reader reaction should be:

> Oh shit. He learned that doing plays.

Do not have the prose announce this thesis.

### Magic constraint

Greg does not independently cast a new personal illusion spell.

At this point, the safest and strongest mechanism is a hybrid of:

- prepared theatrical magical effects already allowed by continuity;
- mundane stagecraft;
- silhouette, cloth, light, sound, blocking, costume, or prop manipulation;
- genuine old-life knowledge;
- Greg's performance choices.

One real magical effect inside several mundane or staged effects is preferable to a new power unlock.

This lets Bren know that some part of what he is seeing may be theatrical while remaining unable to price which part is real.

### Old Greg performance rule

Greg is not possessed and does not resurrect a second personality.

He consciously or semi-consciously performs a role built from old habits:

- less explanation;
- fewer defensive answers;
- stillness where Current Greg normally fidgets or jokes;
- controlled cadence;
- specific professional threat-reading;
- truthful statements that invite frightening inference;
- the behavior of someone evaluating danger rather than pleading to be evaluated correctly.

This should feel slightly alien because Current Greg is nineteen and usually more verbally restless, but the material comes from his own life.

### Memory-rule demonstration inside the scene

Use at least one moment where the clarified memory rule matters materially.

Recommended shape:

Greg effortlessly retrieves a deeply used operational habit or technical fact from Old Greg's dangerous life, then cannot retrieve an incidental autobiographical detail associated with the same memory.

For example, he may remember exactly where an experienced attacker looks before committing to violence, or exactly how a room feels when a group has decided who moves first, while the name of the person beside whom he learned that lesson remains unavailable.

Do not stop the confrontation for a memory essay. The contradiction should flash through and sharpen the scene.

### Bren and companions

Use more than one threat actor so the scene has social pressure rather than becoming another two-man verbal duel.

Recommended behavioral spread:

- one man is genuinely rattled;
- one accepts a wrong explanation or becomes visibly uncertain;
- Bren is the most perceptive and recognizes that some part of the situation is staged.

Bren seeing the performance must not make the bluff fail automatically.

The stronger result is:

> He knows Greg is manipulating perception, but he cannot determine which impossible-seeming pieces are fake and which are true.

### Success condition

Greg does not dominate Bren.

Greg wins a pause by making certainty expensive.

Bren's group should leave because immediate escalation no longer looks like the cheapest or safest option.

Valid success states include:

- withdrawal;
- delay;
- reassessment;
- violence turning back into negotiation;
- Bren deciding the theatre is currently more complicated than its expected return justifies.

### Cost and aftermath

The scene must restore scale immediately after the performance.

Possible costs include:

- shaking;
- adrenaline crash;
- physical fatigue;
- shoulder/hand pain from staging geometry;
- embarrassment at how close one cue came to failing;
- recognition that the bluff bought space, not power;
- another theatre worker revealing how mundane one of the supposedly frightening effects actually was.

Do not let Greg walk away believing he has become Old Greg again.

## Chapter 220C: the quiet resolution becomes legible

### Function

Convert the existing municipal/business defense from apparent plot dissipation into the actual second half of the victory.

The confrontation wins one night. The network makes returning unattractive.

### Systemic mechanism

Rinna's existing behavior and social network should matter more than Greg's performance in making the outcome durable.

Useful existing shapes include:

- suppliers share questions instead of answering them;
- cart-yard people compare approaches;
- descriptions and messages are recorded carefully;
- deliveries adjust routes or timing;
- businesses stop treating each demand as an isolated private problem;
- Bren's people receive less useful information and face more witnesses, delay, coordination, or friction.

Do not create a formal anti-Bren alliance unless the prose naturally earns one. Informal coordination is enough.

### Required thematic equation

The reader should be able to feel this without a thesis statement:

> Greg scared them off once. The network made coming back not worth it.

That is the resolution.

### Quiet payoff image

End or near-end with an ordinary thing functioning normally again because the threat has lost leverage.

Strong possible shapes include:

- a delivery arrives on time;
- the theatre cart is used without special ceremony;
- Olin or another supplier resumes ordinary business;
- a routine payment or handoff occurs with no lookout;
- Rinna removes or folds away part of the threat notation only after enough evidence supports the change.

The ordinary event becomes satisfying because the reader now understands what had to happen for ordinary life to become ordinary again.

### Keep uncertainty

Do not necessarily establish:

- Bren's surname;
- his employer;
- a boss;
- a gang name;
- a complete organization;
- whether he permanently leaves Carrow;
- whether every brown-coat questioner worked for him;
- a Vale connection.

The local extortion pressure can be resolved without solving the entire criminal ecology of the city.

## Relationship and authority constraints

### Rinna

Rinna remains the theatre's business authority.

Greg does not seize command of the theatre's response.

If Greg proposes or performs the dangerous bluff, Rinna must retain meaningful agency around whether, when, where, or under what practical limits it happens. Her network work is what makes the outcome durable.

### Theatre ensemble

The bluff should use distributed competence. Other workers can contribute stagecraft, materials, timing, access, or practical correction without becoming faceless Greg assistants.

At least one important part should work because another theatre worker is better than Greg at their own domain.

### Lyssa

Lyssa does not become mission control. She may react to risk, argue, notice the cost, or deal with the domestic aftermath, but the plot should not turn her into Greg's analyst or tactical planner.

### Hessa

Hessa does not authorize independent combat magic after the fact. If the bluff uses only prepared theatrical effects and no new personal spellcasting, do not drag Hessa into the scene merely to certify it.

## Numbering and integration strategy

### Draft phase

Draft the three new chapters as `220A`, `220B`, and `220C` in dedicated WIP files on the repair branch.

Do not alter the authoritative running manuscript or chapter index during first-draft exploration.

### Acceptance phase

After the author accepts exact prose for all three chapters:

1. verify the active dialogue/attribution pass has cleared the affected neighborhood and identify the newest authority;
2. reconcile any dialogue edits made to Chapters 189-220 after this design branch split;
3. choose final chapter numbering once, not incrementally;
4. integrate the three accepted chapters into the permanent manuscript in one controlled structural transaction;
5. update chapter index, state, reader data, illustration references, and any chapter-number-dependent links or metadata mechanically;
6. verify no chapter prose was lost or duplicated during renumbering.

### Final numbering preference

If the reader architecture requires integer chapter numbers, the likely final result is that existing Chapter 221 and later chapters shift by +3.

Do not perform that renumbering until the inserted prose is final. Ninety-plus mechanical number changes are cheaper and safer once than three times during revision.

If the reader supports stable non-integer insertion identifiers without harming reading order, evaluate that option during implementation, but do not force it merely to avoid mechanical renumbering.

Story order outranks preserving an arbitrary round chapter count.

## Interaction with the current dialogue/attribution pass

The dialogue pass remains the active broad revision lane.

This repair branch must not overwrite newer dialogue work.

Implementation should either:

- draft inserted chapters entirely outside the running manuscript until voice work clears the neighborhood; or
- rebase/recreate the repair branch from the newest dialogue authority before integration.

The memory calibration pass can be mapped while dialogue work continues, but exact prose edits to already-touched chapters must be made against the newest edited authority, never the older manuscript snapshot used for design analysis.

## Verification criteria

The revision is successful only if a fresh whole-book read supports all of the following.

### Memory

- precise old-life recall no longer feels arbitrarily convenient;
- the reader can infer that repeated-use knowledge is more durable;
- personal/emotional memory still exists and remains messy;
- missing names/details do not read like selective plot amnesia;
- Greg retains genuine future/old-life advantages.

### Bren

- `Next time won't be one` receives a real payoff;
- the threat creates a material cost;
- Greg gets the deliberate Old Greg/Theatre Greg bluff moment the story has been building toward;
- illusion/perception enters through earned theatre craft and prepared effects rather than a magic power jump;
- multiple threat actors are meaningfully affected;
- Bren remains intelligent;
- Greg wins uncertainty, not superiority;
- Rinna and the business network make the resolution durable;
- the later ordinary deliveries/business rhythm now read as payoff rather than abandonment;
- no new criminal hierarchy or Vale link is invented without evidence.

### Project integrity

- no active dialogue-pass edits are lost;
- exact magic counts remain correct;
- no independent magic violation is introduced;
- no em dash appears in manuscript prose;
- chapter order/index/state/reader agree after final integration;
- no later chapter is accidentally duplicated, omitted, or paired with the wrong title during renumbering.

## Final artistic test

The repair should make the manuscript feel more like Peg-Leg Greg, not less.

The desired combined effect is:

- Greg's old life feels lived rather than queried from a database;
- theatre finally pays off under real pressure without becoming combat school;
- the threat becomes frightening enough to justify its buildup;
- the resolution remains social, economic, local, and materially grounded;
- the strongest dramatic scene depends on accumulated mundane competence;
- ordinary life returning afterward feels earned.

The repair succeeds if the reader can believe both of these statements at once:

> Greg scared dangerous people with a performance built from real experience.

and

> The theatre survived because a bunch of ordinary people made extortion inconvenient.
