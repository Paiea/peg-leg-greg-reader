# Economic Value / Pay Calibration Design

## Purpose

Repair Peg-Leg Greg's economic slope without rewriting story structure.

The core continuity problem is not that cheap copper work exists. Cheap labor can remain cheap. The problem is that later Greg sometimes performs scarce, commercially valuable, dangerous, or legally risky work and receives compensation that reads like interchangeable Bronze labor despite early authority already establishing silver-scale earning, spending, borrowing, and specialist value.

## Design principles

1. **Pay follows value, risk, scarcity, and leverage.** Greg's purse does not determine what a job is worth.
2. **Cheap work remains cheap when the work itself is cheap.** Carrying flour, simple escorting, ordinary theatre support, and low-skill labor may still pay copper.
3. **Scarce judgment can pay far more than labor time.** Antonius's early 6s appraisal payment is a canonical anchor: Greg can earn silver when his contribution materially changes a valuable decision.
4. **Crime has a risk premium, not a magical wage multiplier.** Petty crime may pay little. Meaningful smuggling, illicit freight, contraband, institutional exposure, or dangerous gray work should pay enough to justify arrest, confiscation, violence, reputation damage, or lost legal opportunity. Tens of silver can be normal for serious work; gold-scale compensation becomes plausible when the underlying value/risk is genuinely large.
5. **Earning capacity must ratchet upward.** Greg's available opportunities should progress from cheap labor and volatile silver into specialist fees, larger contracts, commissions, management, brokerage, capital deployment, and eventually ownership.
6. **Reaction must scale with earning capacity.** Small pay can remain socially meaningful without remaining financially impressive.
7. **Do not establish an unstated copper/silver/gold conversion.** Normalize relative scale from manuscript anchors.
8. **Greg may remain financially wasteful.** Higher earnings permit higher-quality mistakes. Later bad spending should usually cost opportunity rather than reset him to survival poverty.

## Target economic shape

### Early reset
Greg is silver-active but liquidity-poor. He borrows, gambles, invests, trains, overspends, makes partial payments, and mistakes expected income for available capital.

### Rebuilding
Cheap copper work still exists, but Greg increasingly earns multi-copper and silver-scale money for scarce competence. He starts judging jobs against time and opportunity cost.

### Current / later
Silver is ordinary operating money for meaningful work. Serious commercial, specialist, gray, and criminal opportunities may reach tens of silver. Gold appears when transaction value, risk, artifacts, businesses, or ownership justify it. Small copper gains become incidental unless the real reward is social, artistic, relational, or strategic.

## Normalization method

Prioritize scenes where money drives behavior. For each paid scene classify:
- labor intensity;
- scarcity of Greg's contribution;
- commercial value moved/protected;
- urgency;
- physical/legal/reputational risk;
- capital required;
- replaceability;
- strategic/social non-cash value.

Then classify the nominal pay as KEEP / RAISE / LOWER / REFRAME / NEEDS CONTEXT.

Change numbers first. Change arithmetic second. Change reaction language only where the original emotional framing no longer fits. Preserve story beats unless arithmetic makes them impossible.

## Crime calibration

Do not set a universal crime price. Use risk/value bands:
- petty illegal favor or low-value contraband: can remain small;
- real smuggling / gray freight with meaningful exposure: silver-scale and often materially above legal day labor;
- specialist illicit logistics / high-value contraband / serious institutional risk: tens of silver can be a reasonable floor;
- major cargo, rare artifacts, dangerous route, or substantial organized operation: gold-scale compensation may be appropriate.

If Greg is risking arrest or serious retaliation for a payment comparable to routine flour-moving, the scene requires an explicit non-cash reason or a higher price.

## Acceptance criteria

The economic engine should be able to answer:
- why this job pays what it pays;
- why Greg accepts it;
- what alternative use of his time/capital exists;
- whether the amount fits the value/risk rather than merely his current purse;
- whether his reaction reflects his present earning tier;
- whether the scene preserves the manuscript's upward economic slope.
