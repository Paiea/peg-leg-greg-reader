# CHAPTER 321

## THE REFEREE

Tavi accused a six-year-old girl of corruption before breakfast.

This was early even for Carrow.

I heard the argument from upstairs.

"That was gate."

"No."

"Gate."

"No."

"Greg!"

I stopped tying the shoulder bag.

This was the danger of becoming locally available.

The bag no longer smelled like leather grease. Narrow scrap inside. Wrapped charcoal inside. Stay bead on first notch when worn. One copper remained upstairs because I had no reason to bring my entire liquid fortune to a courtyard dispute involving children.

I went down.

Tavi stood near the drain with both fists on his hips.

Across from him stood a girl in a yellow shirt holding a flat wooden disk about the width of her palm.

Three other children watched.

Cala sat on the bench shelling peas and making no visible effort to preserve justice.

I stopped beside her.

"You called a court?"

Tavi pointed at the girl.

"She moved gate."

The girl held up the disk.

"He moved line."

"I did not."

"You kicked it."

"Accident."

"Still moved."

Tavi looked at me.

"Tell."

I looked at Cala.

She put another pea in the bowl.

"You know game?"

"No."

"Good."

That seemed like the opposite of qualification.

The game occupied most of the dry half of the courtyard.

Three chalk lines crossed the stone at uneven angles. Each line had a small gap in the middle, making three gates. Four flat wooden disks lay on the ground. Two had blue-painted edges. Two red.

A fifth disk sat near Tavi's foot.

I knew the broad family.

Sliding games. Tossing games. Street games built around whatever flat objects survived being thrown at walls.

First life had contained dozens.

Barracks versions used coins when people were stupid.

Children's versions used wood when adults had learned from soldiers.

This exact game meant nothing to me.

"Rules," I said.

Everyone began talking.

I raised one hand.

"That is not rules. That is weather."

The girl in yellow pointed at the first chalk gap.

"Three gates. You send through all three."

"With one throw?"

"No."

Tavi said, "Yes if good."

She glared at him.

"Not one throw."

A boy with a missing front tooth said, "Can."

"Does it count?"

"No."

"Then why did you say can?"

"Because can."

Strong legal tradition.

I crouched with care, then changed my mind and sat on the low step instead. Better angle. Better body choice. Right foot flat. Left knee clear. Crutches beside me where nobody could turn them into sporting equipment.

"Start again. Who goes first?"

The children looked at each other.

Nobody knew.

Excellent.

The girl in yellow said, "Red."

Tavi said, "Blue."

"How decided?"

"Red starts."

"Why?"

"Because."

Tavi said, "Blue started yesterday."

"Yesterday Niko had red."

I looked at the boy with the missing tooth.

"Niko?"

He nodded.

"Did red start yesterday?"

"Yes."

"Because you had red?"

He thought.

"Maybe."

I looked at Cala.

She was smiling into the peas.

"You are enjoying this."

"Very."

I returned to the court.

"Fine. Today red starts because apparently history is written by whoever remembers yesterday loudest."

The girl in yellow handed one red disk to a smaller boy.

He set it behind a chalk scratch near the wall.

Not one of the gates.

A start line.

Good.

He flicked the disk with two fingers.

It slid over the stone, turned slightly, passed through the first gate, and stopped.

Everyone accepted this.

I learned one rule.

Tavi went next with blue.

He set his disk at the same line.

Then he moved it half a handspan forward.

Yellow Shirt shouted.

"See!"

Tavi looked at me.

"Stone bad there."

I looked.

The original spot had a shallow pit.

That was true.

"Where do people start if the stone is bad?"

"Side."

"How far side?"

He moved the disk farther forward.

Yellow Shirt made a sound of outrage.

I pointed to the chalk scratch.

"Same distance from the gate. Move sideways, not forward."

Tavi frowned.

"Why?"

"Because I have invented geometry."

Cala laughed.

The children accepted it only because none had a better adult available.

Tavi flicked.

His disk clipped the chalk edge of the first gate.

"Gate!" he shouted.

"No!" Yellow Shirt shouted.

We had arrived at the original crime.

I looked at the disk.

Half across the line.

Maybe more.

"What's the rule?"

"Whole disk," Yellow Shirt said.

"Touch counts," Tavi said.

Niko said, "Depends."

I pointed at him.

"On?"

"Which gate."

Of course.

The first gate required whole disk.

The second allowed touch.

The third required the disk to stop beyond it without touching the far chalk line.

I stared at them.

"Why?"

Four children shrugged.

Tavi said, "Game."

That was the best answer.

We reset his disk.

"No gate," I ruled.

He looked betrayed.

"You don't know game."

"Correct."

"Then why you tell?"

I looked at Cala.

She did not rescue me.

"Because you called me."

Tavi considered this structural failure.

Then accepted it.

The game continued.

I learned more.

You did not retrieve a disk after a successful gate until your next turn.

If another disk struck yours, both stayed where they ended.

If your disk stopped on a chalk line, it was "sleeping" and could not score the next gate until another turn moved it clear.

If it went into the drain channel, you lost that throw but not the disk unless the water was running.

The water was not running.

Good.

A girl with two braids joined halfway through.

Nobody objected.

She took the second blue disk.

"Team?" I asked.

"No."

"Then why colors?"

The children looked at me as if I had asked why stone was hard.

Niko said, "So you know."

"Know what?"

"Color."

I stopped asking.

The flat disks were ordinary wood except for a small dark dot set into the underside.

I noticed only when Braids flipped hers after a bad throw.

The dot gave one faint amber blink.

Then nothing.

I knew cheap tally beads.

Broad family.

Marks that recorded a contact, turn, threshold, sometimes ownership.

Children's toys used versions too.

I did not know what this one recorded.

I did not ask immediately.

Growth.

Then Tavi slapped his disk against the stone and shouted, "Wake."

The dark dot blinked amber.

Yellow Shirt said, "Cheat."

I sighed.

"What does wake mean?"

Everyone talked again.

Eventually I learned that when a disk slept on chalk, the little bead could be reset by tapping the disk flat against bare stone before the next throw.

Not magic to move it.

Not magic to aim.

Just a cheap state marker so children could not pretend a sleeping disk had not been sleeping.

This was brilliant.

Not because the enchantment was sophisticated.

Because adults had apparently spent money to prevent children from lying about chalk.

Civilization.

"Who checks the bead?" I asked.

Yellow Shirt pointed at whoever was nearest.

"No official?"

Blank faces.

"Then why am I here?"

Tavi said, "She cheats."

Yellow Shirt said, "He cheats."

There it was.

I had not been called because the game needed a referee.

I had been called because two people wanted a larger person to agree with them.

This was familiar from carts, posts, theatre cues, and most governments.

"New rule," I said.

Yellow Shirt narrowed her eyes.

"You can't make rule."

"Excellent. Then old rule. You check each other's bead before the throw."

"We do."

"Then why am I here?"

Tavi opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Cala dropped a pea into the bowl.

I could have left.

I stayed.

Not as judge.

As audience.

This changed the game immediately.

Without me ruling every gate, the children argued, appealed to each other, replayed throws when nobody had seen clearly, and occasionally accepted outcomes that made them unhappy.

Yellow Shirt's name turned out to be Pali because Braids shouted it when Pali nearly stepped on a red disk.

Braids was Sela.

The smaller red player was Omi.

Names arrived through danger.

Reliable.

Tavi reached the second gate first.

Touch counted there.

He flicked too hard.

The disk passed through the gap, struck Omi's red disk, and sent both spinning.

Tavi's stopped clear.

Omi's landed on chalk.

The underside bead flashed twice.

Everyone screamed.

Not fear.

Law.

"Sleep!"

"Hit!"

"Two!"

"Red sleep!"

Omi looked close to tears.

Not because the disk had been damaged.

Because Tavi was celebrating like he had conquered a province.

I said, "Did the hit make Omi sleep?"

Pali said, "Yes."

"Is that allowed?"

"Yes."

"Then why is everyone yelling?"

She stared at me.

"Because it happened."

Fair.

Omi retrieved nothing.

His disk remained sleeping until his turn.

He tapped it flat on bare stone.

The bead gave one amber blink.

Reset.

Then he flicked carefully out of the chalk line instead of trying for the next gate.

Good choice.

Nobody praised him.

The game moved.

I watched hands.

That became more interesting than rules.

Pali used two fingers and almost no wrist.

Tavi used his whole hand and too much force.

Niko sighted along the disk edge before every throw, then missed half of them.

Sela changed technique every time something failed, which meant she never learned whether the change helped.

Omi became steadily better because he kept making the same small motion.

I knew enough from a lifetime of weapons, tools, games, and bad apprentices to have opinions.

I kept most of them.

Then Tavi asked.

"How she do that?"

He meant Pali.

Pali had just sent red through the second gate with a light flick that barely disturbed the disk.

"Ask her."

Tavi looked offended.

"You know."

"I know what I saw. She knows what she did."

Pali looked at her hand.

"Don't push down."

Tavi looked at his.

"Why?"

"Stops."

He tried.

His next throw improved immediately.

Not perfect.

Better.

He looked at me.

I pointed at Pali.

"Her."

He did not thank her.

Children had standards.

But he stopped accusing her of corruption for almost five minutes.

A shadow crossed the courtyard.

I looked up automatically.

Maris leaned over the gallery rail with a wet cloth.

"Move."

Every child moved at once.

She dropped the cloth over the line.

Not on purpose.

It landed across the third gate.

The game stopped.

Pali stared upward.

"Maris!"

"What?"

"Gate!"

Maris looked down.

Saw the cloth.

"Oh."

She pulled it up by one corner.

The chalk beneath had smeared.

Silence.

A constitutional crisis.

Tavi looked at me.

I raised both hands.

"No."

"You referee."

"I have resigned."

"Can't."

"I was never appointed."

Pali crouched over the smeared line.

"We know where."

Niko said, "No, farther."

Sela said, "Here."

Omi said nothing.

He took a small chalk piece from beside the wall.

Then he placed his red disk where the old line had been, using the unsmeared ends on both sides as reference.

He drew a new line between them.

Everyone looked.

It matched.

Approximately.

Good enough for children and several municipal projects I had seen.

"Done," Omi said.

The game resumed.

I smiled.

No adult geometry required.

Cala had finished the peas.

She stood.

"You staying?"

"I have apparently become decorative."

"Good."

She took the bowl inside.

I remained on the step.

My hands were fine.

Right leg fine.

Residual limb comfortable.

Sitting cost less than standing.

The crutches rested safely against the wall until Tavi leaned toward one.

I looked at him.

He leaned away.

Some rules did not require speech.

By the time anyone reached the third gate, two more children had joined.

One brought a disk with a green edge.

Nobody explained green.

I refused to ask.

This was difficult.

The third gate was narrowest.

And the rule was cruel.

Disk had to pass through and stop beyond the gate without touching the far chalk line.

Too hard and you crossed the line.

Too soft and you stayed in the gate.

Pali failed.

Tavi failed harder.

Niko failed after a long sighting ritual.

Sela changed grip and failed creatively.

Omi came closest.

His disk cleared the gap, slowed, and stopped with its front edge almost on the far line.

Everyone bent down.

"Touch."

"No."

"Touch."

"Air."

I stayed seated.

They looked at me.

"No."

"Greg."

"No. Use the bead."

Pali flipped the disk.

The dark dot stayed dull.

Apparently the bead did not judge distance from the far line.

Of course not.

It only tracked chalk contact when the disk had actually crossed a line hard enough to trigger.

Maybe.

That was more mechanism than I had evidence for.

I stopped.

Sela tore a hair from her own head.

I stared.

She laid it between the disk and chalk.

The hair slid through.

"No touch."

Everyone accepted this instantly.

Omi had passed.

He smiled.

Small.

Then he picked up his disk.

"Winner?" I asked.

"No."

Of course not.

"What now?"

"Back."

I closed my eyes.

The game required all three gates outward.

Then all three in reverse.

I had been watching the first half.

Tavi laughed at my face.

"You don't know game."

"Correct."

This time he sounded pleased rather than accusatory.

I considered leaving.

Then Pali held out a red disk.

"Play."

I looked at it.

Then at my crutches.

Then at the start line.

"From here?"

"Sit throw."

"Allowed?"

She shrugged.

"Now."

I had seen this legal system before.

I took the disk.

Flat wood.

Worn edge.

Dark tally dot underneath.

No stored force that I could feel.

Not that I tested with mana.

No mana.

Bad angle.

Too far sideways.

I shifted from the step to the bench instead.

Right foot planted.

Left knee clear.

Crutches laid beside me.

The start line sat almost directly ahead.

"One throw," I said.

Tavi said, "All gates."

"One throw."

Pali nodded.

I flicked.

Too hard.

The disk shot through the first gate, skipped once on rough stone, missed the second gate by a full foot, struck the courtyard wall, bounced, and disappeared under Cala's bench.

Silence.

Then seven children laughed at me.

I bowed from the bench.

"Professional."

Tavi crawled under the bench and retrieved the disk.

The tally bead blinked amber once.

Pali looked at it.

"Gate one."

"You went through."

"Then wall."

"Still gate."

Tavi handed it back.

"Again."

"No."

"Two more."

"No."

"You started."

This was dangerous logic.

Second throw failed the second gate.

Third throw clipped the second gate and slept.

Amber blink.

Reset.

"Not thumb."

"I have used a thumb for fifty-nine years."

"Wrong."

"Strong claim."

Two fingers.

Less wrist.

Like hers.

The disk touched the second gate.

Touch counted.

Children shouted.

I felt absurdly pleased.

"No."

"Third."

Then at the far line.

"Fine."

Not continuously.

Barely.

I raised both hands.

"Done."

"No."

Not injury.

Better.

# CHAPTER 322

## THE BIDDER

The first expensive thing I wanted in Carrow belonged to a dead mason.

That was not why I wanted it.

It did improve the price.

I found the sale because half of Cooper Street had decided to stand in my way.

There were carts at both ends of the block, a chalk board tied to a post, and enough people under the awnings to make the middle of the street useless to anyone with crutches and standards.

I stopped before entering the crowd.

My shoulder bag sat high and close. Narrow scrap inside. Wrapped charcoal inside. One copper upstairs at home because carrying every coin I owned through a crowd was unnecessary, even when every coin I owned was currently one coin.

That fact had become less dramatic once I stopped narrating it to myself.

I could afford lunch.

I could not afford a building.

Civilization contained many intermediate states.

A man beside the chalk board said, "Viewing until second bell. First lots after."

I looked at the board.

HOUSE CLEARANCE.

TOOLS. FIXTURES. STONE GEAR. HOUSE GOODS.

Then, lower down in different chalk:

ONE REGISTERED FIELD PIECE.

That was why the street was crowded.

I had been heading nowhere important.

This was dangerous.

I moved closer.

The sale occupied the ground floor of a narrow stone-front house with its shutters propped open. Ordinary things filled the front room. Three planes. Two saws. A box of chisels. Lamp brackets. A folding work table. Kitchen pots. A cracked wash basin somebody had still bothered to number.

Nothing about the room suggested treasure.

That helped.

The people suggested treasure.

Two men in work coats stood near the back without touching anything. A woman with a leather account case kept checking the same folded paper. A broad man wearing a guild-blue cord had positioned himself where he could see both the rear table and everyone approaching it.

He looked at me once.

Then at my crutches.

Then away.

Good.

I preferred being underestimated for reasons that did not require effort.

The rear table held one object.

It was not impressive.

A dark metal sleeve lay on gray felt, about the length of my forearm and open along one side. Four narrow ribs ran from wrist end to elbow end. The inner surface was lined with old brown leather. A dull red stone sat in a shallow socket near the outside edge.

Beside it lay a paper tag.

REGISTERED IMPACT BRACE.

ESTATE OF ORREN VALEK, MASON.

FUNCTION DEMONSTRATED.

NO CURRENT SERVICE MARK.

SOLD AS SEEN.

I stopped three steps away.

Not because I recognized it.

Because I almost did.

That was worse.

The rib spacing bothered me.

Three narrow. One wide.

The red stone was too far toward the elbow for most common catch-braces I remembered.

And there was a tiny stamped mark under the second rib.

I could not read it from where I stood.

The guild-cord man noticed me looking.

"Don't touch."

"Wasn't going to."

"Everyone says that."

"Then your sign is working."

There was no sign.

He looked at the table.

Then at me again.

"Viewing only until demonstration."

"What does it do?"

He gave me the patient expression of a man answering the same question for the fiftieth time.

"Takes impact through the brace. Mason used it for bad stone and overhead break."

"How much impact?"

"Demonstration at second bell."

"That's not a number."

"Correct."

I liked him slightly more.

I shifted left so I was not blocking anyone.

A woman behind me stepped forward, read the tag, and said, "No service mark?"

The guild man said, "Last shop closed. Estate couldn't produce later papers. Registration is clean."

"Recharge?"

"Unknown."

"Repair?"

"Unknown."

She made a face and moved away.

Useful.

The market was not blind.

A field piece that did something valuable could still be a terrible purchase if nobody could keep it doing that valuable thing.

I looked again at the ribs.

Three narrow.

One wide.

Something in my first life moved.

Not memory exactly.

Recognition before memory.

I had seen that spacing on a wall.

No.

On armor.

No.

A broken door frame in Red Fen, maybe.

I hated this part.

People imagined living twice would make memory clean.

It mostly gave me twice as many places to misfile things.

"Valek piece?"

"His?"

"Estate says."

Lyssa said, "What?"

"Nothing."

"You have artifact face."

"I don't have an artifact face."

"You do."

"What does it look like?"

"Like you bought trouble and are happy it came with instructions."

I looked at the oval.

Then at the red chalk line visible through the stair opening below.

"Instructions would be generous."

"Warnings, then."

That was closer.

For one copper, I had bought a thing that sometimes remembered the last way back and sometimes, maybe, suggested a way out.

The difference between those two functions was large enough to get somebody killed.

USEFUL ENOUGH TO CARRY

NOT TRUSTED ENOUGH TO OBEY

For now.

# CHAPTER 330

## THE BACK DOOR

For three days, our front door became decorative.

This improved it.

No one knocked on it.

No one stood in it talking while everyone else tried to pass.

No one opened it and discovered Tavi directly behind them for reasons known only to Tavi.

The municipal workers had boarded the damaged section of the front hall, chalked a red line across the floor, and declared the entire front route closed until the linewright finished deciding whose fault the wall was.

Cala had asked when that would be.

The worker had said, "When he knows."

She had stared at him.

He had added, "Probably tomorrow."

It was now the day after tomorrow.

Civilization.

We used the rear service route instead.

Kitchen passage.

Storage turn.

Rear stair.

Courtyard.

Then around the building to the street.

It added perhaps forty seconds for a person with two good legs.

For me it added more.

Not much more.

Enough that I noticed every single time.

The rear stair was narrower than the front one and had a turn halfway down where the inside crutch had to land closer to the wall than I liked.

The first morning I hated it.

The second morning I had a pattern.

Right crutch.

Right foot.

Left crutch down one.

Turn hip.

Do not catch shoulder bag on the peg beside the landing.

The third morning, Tavi moved the peg.

"Why?"

He looked at the peg in his hand.

"You hit it."

"Once."

"Twice."

"That is slander."

He put it on a lower hole where it no longer caught the bag.

Then continued downstairs.

I stood there for a moment.

Belonging was apparently people altering architecture because you were annoying.

I liked it.

The oval clicked when I crossed the rear service door into the courtyard.

I barely looked.

That was progress too.

It lived in the shoulder bag now.

Not because I trusted it.

Because an object that had been useful twice earned pocket space.

Case space.

Whatever.

I still kept it wrapped.

Still did not press the mark.

Still did not channel mana into it.

Still did not ask it what I should have for breakfast.

High standards.

The building changed socially before it changed physically.

People who had used the front stair for years suddenly met in the courtyard.

Neris met the old man from the upper rear unit and discovered they had both been living ten feet apart without knowing each other's names.

Cala learned that the woman across the courtyard had been borrowing our rear wall to judge whether her own kitchen was warm enough in winter.

The woman refused to explain.

Tavi found three children who normally entered from the street side and immediately turned the rear route into a race.

Cala ended that within four minutes.

"No running on the rear stair."

"We weren't running," Tavi said.

"Then whatever faster thing you invented is also banned."

Strong law.

The linewright finally arrived near noon.

I knew because Cala called upstairs.

"Greg."

"What?"

"The wall man is here."

"Wall man."

"You are going to offend a profession."

"Good. Come listen so I don't have to remember it."

Different danger.

Cala did not apologize.

She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with gray hair braided close to her head and a dark red leather roll under one arm.

No uniform.

Formal meaning unknown.

Two municipal workers were with her.

Good level of fame.

Written down.

Artifact-adjacent equipment.

Specialist tools.

Definitely.

Useful sentence.

Moved right.

Straightened.

Good answer.

Nothing happened.

Improvement.

Mentally.

Not useful yet.
