I set the buckets down.

"Flue?"

"Clay collar split last winter," the man said. "We patched the plaster. Should've pulled the whole section."

He knew exactly where to put his hand on the wall without touching the hot part.

I did not.

Later construction codes ran clay liners all the way through walls like this.

Later Carrow required clear stone around commercial stove flues.

Later was currently useless.

"How far does it run?"

He pointed.

"Up there, then left under the landing."

The landing above us had six people on it.

That seemed worth changing.

I looked toward them.

"Everyone off the front stairs. Rear courtyard only."

The man beside me snapped his fingers at the same time.

"You heard him. Move."

People moved.

I did not ask whether that made the order mine or his.

The answer did not matter enough.

He grabbed the iron poker from beside the stove and drove the point into the browned plaster.

I caught his wrist.

"You open that, it gets air."

"It already has air."

"Not that much."

"And if we leave it, it eats the landing."

He was right faster than I was.

Annoying.

I released him.

"Small opening first."

"That was the plan."

Apparently I had improved it by repeating it.

He punched through.

The plaster broke inward.

Orange appeared behind it.

Not a wall of flame. Worse in a different way. Blackened wood and packed lath glowing around the flue, fire crawling upward through material that had been waiting years for somebody to heat it correctly.

He stepped back.

"Water."

I threw half a bucket through the hole.

Steam hit us both.

He swore.

I approved of the wording.

The fire hissed, dimmed, and continued above the opening.

"Higher," he said.

We broke more plaster.

This part was slower.

Open too much and we fed it. Open too little and we chased smoke while the fire climbed around us.

I had spent years supporting people through monster nests where one bad decision could collapse a tunnel.

The skill transferred less elegantly to a rented building with a stove.

The principle did not.

Find the thing actually killing you.

Do not fight the visible part because it is visible.

The heat line climbed left beneath the landing.

"There," I said.

"I know."

Of course he did.

He had lived here while I spent forty years elsewhere.

He struck the plaster again.

A chunk larger than his head broke loose from above the opening.

His eyes went up.

Too late.

My hand moved before I finished thinking.

Mah-nah gathered badly, then enough.

A support plane snapped into place between the falling plaster and the side of his face.

Five inches.

Maybe six.

The slab hit at an angle.

The plane did not stop it.

It changed the angle.

That was enough.

The plaster broke against his shoulder instead of his temple and exploded across the floor.
