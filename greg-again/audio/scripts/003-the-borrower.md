# Greg, Again — Chapter 3 Audio Performance Script

## The Borrower

**Lens:** GREG-DOMINANT  
**Narrator:** male / masculine, deep, restrained  
**Status:** experimental audio surface derived from the R2 Chapter 3 control

## Performance rules

**CONTINUOUS GREG THOUGHT-LINE**  
Greg's consciousness is the continuity track. Thoughts may associate, revise, and collide with immediate material reality instead of resolving as tidy explanations.

**ATTENTION GRAVITY**  
When something more salient happens, Greg's current thought can be interrupted, redirected, or abandoned. The bucket hurting his hand, Arlo saying no, money landing in his palm, the cart blocking the alley, failed samples, bodily fatigue, and the debt clock are allowed to seize the narration.

**VOCAL SURFACE**  
Use short stops, resets, fragments, self-corrections, repeated words, and breath-sized units where they sound like Greg thinking. Do not add stage-direction markup to the spoken text.

**EMBODIED OUTSIDE CHARACTERS**  
Remove transcript, not embodiment. Arlo, Antonius, the stall owner, Rusk, the cooper, and passersby still act independently through hands, posture, timing, objects, money, silence, refusal, and consequence.

**VOICE BREACH**  
Direct outside speech is scarce. Preserve it when another person's exact wording is the event, carries irreplaceable character information, punctures Greg's model, or lands harder than paraphrase.

**LANGUAGE AS ACTION**  
Prices, loan terms, refusals, deadlines, rates, work conditions, and technical challenges alter story state. Do not blur who proposed, accepted, refused, priced, or committed to what. Greg may own the linguistic surface. He may not steal another character's thought, expertise, leverage, or decision.

## TAKE 01 — Kestrin

Future knowledge was much easier to spend before I tried to buy anything with it.

The east market gave me three possibilities in the first hour.

One was wrong.

One was real but useless for another twenty years.

The third was a pile of gray stone chips beside a masonry stall.

Kestrin shale.

I stopped.

Cheap. Brittle. Mildly mana-reactive. Mostly used where nobody cared if it cracked.

Later, it mattered.

I knew that.

Probably.

There was that word again.

I remembered pale filtration housings stamped with the Arwick mark. Old Kestrin waste binding alchemical residue under heat. Finished filters cheap enough that nobody important bothered to think about them.

That was all.

No formula. No inventor. No exact year.

I had the ending of a story and none of the useful middle.

Still better than two copper.

The stall owner wanted two silver for the cart.

I had two copper.

Right.

A bucket was one copper.

I bought the bucket.

Growth.

The old me would have bought the cart because scale felt like confidence.

Current me had just been hit in the ribs by Jorren and nearly walked into vegetables while practicing footwork.

A bucket was enough to be wrong with.

## TAKE 02 — Arwick

I carried the shale through the market.

Six minutes later the handle started biting into my fingers.

Kestrin shale was cheap partly because stone was heavy.

I had known this in principle.

Principles had excellent posture.

Reality made you switch hands.

Arwick came back slowly.

Not the man.

The stamp.

Three lines inside a square.

Arwick filtration. Arwick regulators. Arwick... something else.

I remembered seeing the mark on expedition gear decades later.

So I asked around.

Current Arwick Works was not works.

It was a rented room behind a cooper.

The man inside was younger than I expected, which was becoming a useless observation. Everyone was younger than I expected.

Dark hair tied back. Burn scar across two fingers. Lamp regulator opened across his bench.

He looked at the bucket.

Then me.

“No.”

I hadn't asked anything.

He pointed out that I was carrying rocks into his workshop.

Shale.

Kestrin shale.

That changed his face.

Not recognition.

Technical interest.

Good.

Now I had his attention.

## TAKE 03 — You’re Nineteen

I asked whether he worked with mana-reactive ceramic mixes.

Sometimes.

Powdered Kestrin in a porous body?

“Why would I?”

There it was.

The dangerous moment.

My memory wanted to become authority because I knew the finished world.

I made myself stay with what I actually had.

“Trace mana. Cheap structure. High surface area if powdered. I think heat changes how it binds residue.”

He looked at the bucket again.

“Think?”

“Yes.”

“Based on?”

Something I had seen a long time ago.

He looked at my face.

“You're nineteen.”

Right.

“It was a very formative childhood.”

Nothing.

No laugh.

I liked him more.

His name was Arlo.

Good.

Not proof.

But good.

“Arlo Arwick?”

He went still.

Ah.

Mistake.

“Who told you my family name?”

No sign on the door.

No maker's stamp visible from where I stood.

Excellent work, Greg.

I blamed the cooper.

Arlo told me the cooper didn't know his family name.

Of course he didn't.

I looked at the lamp regulator.

Could he test the shale?

His expression said the subject had not changed.

Mine said I hoped persistence counted as strategy.

## TAKE 04 — Six Silver

Arlo finally took one chip from the bucket.

Turned it over.

“Six silver.”

I laughed.

He put it back.

“Then no.”

For a test?

“For my time. Materials extra.”

Right.

Six silver for his time.

I called it robbery.

He suggested a cheaper artificer.

I did not know a cheaper artificer.

Worse, I did not know whether the Arwick name mattered because of Arlo, his children, a buyer who kept the name, or a coincidence large enough to make me look stupid.

Future knowledge had given me a door.

The door charged six silver.

I took the bucket outside and sat on the cooper's back step.

Two copper.

Training would cost.

The test cost six silver before materials.

Food mattered if I intended to train every day.

Mana conditioning would cost once I found a teacher.

Rent would eventually become interested in me.

My second life had lasted less than two days and already required financing.

That part felt familiar.

There were several lenders in Carrow.

I knew one.

That was not the same thing as trusting him.

## TAKE 05 — The Ham

Antonius Vale's future reputation had accumulated so many stories that I no longer knew which ones were true.

Merchant houses. Private wars. Guild expansions. Political campaigns.

At least one marriage.

Possibly two.

The second story was better, so probably less true.

I remembered the man.

Enough to be cautious.

Not enough to stop me.

His current office was behind a gambling room near slaughter lane.

I went to the wrong building first.

That improved my mood.

The right place had no polished stone. No private guards. No quiet room designed to make wealthy people feel observed.

There was a ham hanging from the ceiling.

I stared at it.

Antonius came through the back curtain.

Black hair. Cheap coat. Lean.

Same eyes.

I had seen those eyes across negotiating tables when cities were involved.

Now there was a ham over his head.

He asked if he knew me.

Not yet.

His eyes sharpened.

Too much.

“I need a loan.”

That fixed it.

Everyone became ordinary around money.

“How much?”

I had done the math.

Then added margin.

Then doubled it because plans lied.

“Forty silver.”

The man beside him laughed.

Antonius did not.

## TAKE 06 — Rock

Collateral?

No.

Property?

No.

Family?

Dead.

Guarantor?

No.

Rank?

I put the Bronze token on the table.

The other man laughed again.

I was beginning to dislike him.

Antonius picked up the token.

“Forty silver.”

“Yes.”

He wanted to know what for.

Training. Food. An artificer test.

I put one Kestrin chip on the table.

He stared at it.

“Rock.”

“Shale.”

“That improves things.”

I told him I thought it had a useful filtration property nobody was pricing yet.

“Think.”

“Yes.”

And the artificer?

Interested enough to charge me six silver.

“That means he is interested in six silver.”

I liked present Antonius too.

Annoying day.

He pushed the Bronze token back.

“No.”

Of course.

My forty-year reputation did not exist.

S-class did not exist.

I was a nineteen-year-old Bronze asking for forty silver to test rocks.

Present proof.

Fine.

“Ten.”

Antonius looked at me.

“You changed quickly.”

I had asked for what I wanted.

Now I was asking for what he might actually give me.

## TAKE 07 — Terms

Then the negotiation became real.

“Term?”

“Ten days. Twenty percent.”

“Eight days. Thirty-five.”

“Twenty-five.”

“Thirty-five.”

“Thirty and ten days.”

“Thirty-five and eight.”

I looked at him.

He looked back.

Old Greg had negotiated with people who wanted borders.

Current Greg needed ten silver.

Different leverage.

If I failed, I worked the balance.

The work rate he named was bad.

Not impossible.

Type of work?

The man beside him smiled.

I looked at Antonius.

“Legal.”

“Mostly.”

“No.”

That surprised him.

Good.

Legal work. Labor. Collections. Delivery. Bookkeeping if he trusted me near numbers.

Nothing that required making a problem disappear.

The other man laughed again.

“Rusk, stop encouraging him.”

Rusk.

I knew the name now.

Future?

Maybe.

Present first.

Antonius counted ten silver onto the table.

“Eight days. Thirty-five percent. Legal labor if you fail. You miss a day, the work rate drops.”

“That's predatory.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

His fingers paused.

Good?

“Clear terms.”

He pushed the money across.

## TAKE 08 — Possibility

The weight of ten silver hit my palm.

My brain changed immediately.

Two copper had been survival.

Ten silver was possibility.

Possibility had always been expensive for me.

I went straight back to Arlo.

Sensible.

Paid six silver.

Also sensible, if the test mattered.

Better clay. Reagents.

Still defensible.

Then I returned to the masonry stall.

The woman saw me coming and smiled.

“Bucket?”

I looked at the pile.

A bucket was enough to test.

We had established that.

The whole cart was two silver.

If the tests worked, I would need more.

If they did not, the shale remained cheap.

The rational decision was obvious.

So naturally I said:

“Cart.”

She smiled wider.

There was a brief, beautiful period while this still felt efficient.

Then the cart arrived behind Arlo's workshop.

It blocked the alley.

Arlo came outside.

Looked at the cart.

Looked at me.

Looked back at the cart.

“No.”

We might need volume.

“We need a bowl.”

Eventually.

“Greg.”

Storage?

“Not here.”

Right.

Reality had entered the negotiation.

## TAKE 09 — A Bucket

The carter wanted three copper to wait.

Four more to take the shale somewhere else.

I owned no somewhere else.

The cooper objected to the alley.

A woman with a basket objected to me personally.

Arlo stood in his doorway with both arms folded.

Ten silver had lasted less than an hour.

I asked how much shale he actually needed.

“I told you.”

Tell me again.

“A bucket.”

I looked at the cart.

The cart appeared larger now.

“Excellent.”

Arlo closed his eyes.

I paid the carter to move the shale to a cheap storage corner behind the masonry stall I had just bought it from.

The stall owner charged storage.

Of course she did.

By the time I got back, my investment in nearly worthless rock had generated transportation fees, storage fees, and enough public humiliation to qualify as education.

Arlo was already grinding one of the original chips.

“You rich?”

Temporarily.

“That's not usually how rich works.”

You'd be surprised.

He measured powder into a ceramic mix.

I watched his hands.

He knew what he was doing.

I knew what the finished technology looked like decades later.

Those were not competing facts.

Different jobs.

That helped.

## TAKE 10 — Different Jobs

The first sample cracked in the kiln.

The second warped.

The third came out intact and did nothing useful.

Arlo wrote each result down.

I started suggesting changes.

He ignored the first two.

The third made him pause.

“Why lower the heat?”

Memory gave me fragments.

Burned fingertips.

A white filter housing.

Someone swearing that heat ruined the binding.

Or activated it.

Right.

Useful.

“I don't know.”

Arlo looked at me.

“You said lower it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I remember heat mattering and no longer trust which direction.”

“That sentence is useless.”

“It is more honest than the useful-sounding version.”

He stared at me.

Then split the next batch.

One hotter.

One lower.

Both failed.

Good.

Not financially.

Epistemically.

That word did absolutely nothing for my financial condition.

Still.

The failure belonged to the experiment now, not to my memory.

That mattered.

Arlo owned the test.

I owned the suspicious future-shaped clue.

Different jobs.

Again.

## TAKE 11 — Two Things

By dusk, Arlo had one result worth repeating and nothing worth selling.

I had borrowed money, spent most of it, acquired an inconvenient amount of shale, and learned that remembering the future did not include remembering how the future had been made.

Eight days had become seven.

I should have gone home.

Instead I went to the strip of dirt behind the Guild where nobody charged for trees.

My shoulder hurt from carrying the bucket.

My hands hurt from the handle.

Good conditions for sword practice if the goal was stupidity.

I raised the cheap sword.

Overhead cut.

The blade dragged forward.

Again.

Side step.

Too wide.

Again.

Overhead.

Again.

Side step.

My feet crossed.

Stop.

Reset.

Again.

There were at least fifteen things I wanted to fix.

Grip. Recovery. Hip turn. Breath. Weight. Range. Timing. The stop. The landing. How quickly I could move again after both.

Fifteen answers.

No.

Two.

Cut straight.

Step small.

Everything else could wait until tomorrow.

Again.

## TAKE 12 — Reasonable

I practiced until the light went bad.

Cut straight.

Step small.

Again.

The strange thing was that two things were enough to occupy an entire body.

My mind kept offering improvements.

My shoulder kept reminding me about the shale.

My hands kept reminding me about the bucket.

Antonius had seven days left on my clock.

Arlo had one result worth repeating.

The cart existed somewhere behind a masonry stall, charging me rent for being a pile of rocks.

Cut straight.

Step small.

Again.

Eventually darkness won.

On the walk home, without asking permission, my brain began calculating how much gambling capital I would need to clear Antonius in three nights.

I noticed the thought.

That was something.

I knew enough to recognize it as dangerous.

Also something.

Then the more dangerous part arrived.

I knew enough to make it sound reasonable.

Past Greg had decades of experience.

Current Greg had ten silver worth of debt, a storage bill, bad footwork, and an idea.

Right.

That was going to be a problem.
