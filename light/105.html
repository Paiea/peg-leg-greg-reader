LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 105

THE CONTROL

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Hessa made me put my right hand on a towel.

This was worse than the curved copper.

The towel was folded twice on the table beside the bad chair.

Clean.

White.

Ordinary.

I looked at it.

Then at Hessa.

“No.”

“I did not say anything.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to ask why there is a towel.”

“Exactly.”

I sat.

My thumb had behaved since yesterday.

No tingling after I left Hessa.

None while Jorren found a piece of roast pork large enough to justify his new knife.

None during dinner.

None upstairs.

None overnight.

None dressing.

None at breakfast.

I had written this in paragraphs because civilization had rules.

Hessa took the notebook.

She read.

“You wrote ‘thumb remained a thumb.’”

“It did.”

“That is not a symptom report.”

“It establishes continuity.”

“It establishes that you are annoying.”

She handed the notebook back.

“Hand.”

I gave her the right one.

Temperature.

Pulse.

Pressure along the thumb.

Wrist.

Forearm.

Grip.

Fingertips.

Nothing.

“Any recurrence?”

“No.”

“Any numbness?”

“No.”

“Weakness?”

“No.”

“Cold?”

“No.”

“Pain?”

“No.”

“Grip trouble?”

“No.”

“Anything you have not told me because you decided it was unrelated?”

I considered.

“My right calf is no longer sore from Pessa.”

“That was not what I meant.”

“I know.”

“Hessa.”

“Greg.”

I smiled.

She did not.

“Nothing.”

“Good.”

She put my hand back on the towel.

Palm up.

“Leave it there.”

“Why?”

“Because today I want it quiet.”

My pulse changed.

Not much.

Enough.

“We are drawing.”

“Yes.”

“And shaping?”

“No.”

There it was.

I had expected disappointment.

It arrived.

Clean.

Immediate.

Annoying.

“Because of the tingling.”

“Because I have a new observation and I don't want to add another one before I understand the first as well as I reasonably can.”

“You cannot understand it from waiting.”

“Correct.”

“Then.”

“Do not turn that into permission.”

I leaned back.

“What does draw-only tell you?”

“One thing, if we're lucky.”

“What?”

“Whether a minimal draw, without deliberate alteration, immediately reproduces the hand symptom today.”

“That is very narrow.”

“Yes.”

“If it does not?”

“Then it does not.”

“That is not useful.”

“It is exactly as useful as it is.”

“If it does?”

“Then it does.”

“Hessa.”

She looked at me.

“You want me to promise interpretation before the observation.”

“I want to know what the observation would mean.”

“It would mean what happened.”

“That is infuriating.”

“Yes.”

The straight copper strip was on the table.

No curved one.

Interesting.

I looked at my right hand on the towel.

“Why the towel?”

“So I can see whether you move your hand.”

“You could see it on the table.”

“Your hand rests differently on wood.”

“Does that matter?”

“I don't know.”

“You are changing a variable.”

“I am making it easier for you to relax your hand.”

“That is changing a variable.”

“Yes.”

“So this is not a clean comparison.”

“There is no clean comparison.”

Pessa would like her.

I did not say this either.

Hessa moved the copper strip close to my forearm.

Not thumb.

“Minimal draw.”

I closed my eyes.

“Keep the right hand quiet.”

“It is quiet.”

“Keep it that way.”

I drew.

Mana arrived.

Familiar now.

That word required caution.

Familiar did not mean normal.

Chest.

Shoulders.

Arms.

Right side.

Left thigh.

Knee.

Ordinary phantom foot.

Nothing unusual below the knee.

The right hand remained warm against the towel.

No tingling.

No pressure.

No urge to turn the wrist.

No spreading into the palm beyond whatever ordinary draw sensation belonged there.

I noticed myself looking for Barrier.

Not magically.

Mentally.

I stopped.

Hessa said, “Report.”

“Draw stable.”

“Continue.”

“Right hand unchanged.”

“Continue.”

“No thumb tingling.”

“Continue.”

“No unusual below-knee sensation.”

She did not react.

Good.

“Release.”

I released.

Mana went.

Right hand still there.

Thumb still a thumb.

“Do not move.”

“I wasn't.”

“Good.”

She set the copper down.

Then we waited.

I opened my eyes.

“How long?”

“Until I stop.”

“That is not a unit.”

“You do not need one.”

“I could estimate.”

“Don't.”

I stared at the wall.

The wall was aggressively uninformative.

A crack ran from near the window frame toward the ceiling.

I had seen it before.

Or thought I had.

Maybe it had grown.

This was not my jurisdiction either.

I looked at my hand.

“Don't.”

“I am looking.”

“Look somewhere else.”

“This is medically authoritarian.”

“Yes.”

I looked at Hessa instead.

She was writing.

“What are you writing?”

“Things.”

“Specific.”

“No.”

“Did the copper respond normally?”

“Yes.”

“Normally compared to prior draws?”

“Yes.”

“How normally?”

She put the pen down.

“Greg.”

“Fine.”

I looked at the wall again.

My thumb did not tingle.

The longer it did not tingle, the more I wanted it to.

Not because I wanted injury.

Because no symptom was not an answer.

A symptom was also not an answer.

This was a poor system.

“How long now?”

Hessa ignored me.

I counted breaths.

Then stopped counting because that was functionally timing.

Personal record.

Someone knocked at the outer door.

Nerin answered.

Voices.

A child coughed.

Not dramatically.

Twice.

Then there was the sound of a chair scraping.

Life continued outside the experiment.

Good.

I hated that too.

Hessa looked at my hand.

“Anything?”

“No.”

“Move the fingers normally.”

I did.

Once.

Thumb to nothing.

Just open.

Close.

“No tingling.”

“Grip.”

I squeezed her fingers.

Released.

“Nothing.”

“Wrist.”

She had me bend it once each direction.

No pain.

No tingling.

“Stand.”

“What about observation?”

“Stand.”

Both crutches.

Right hand left the towel and took the right crutch grip.

Immediate pressure through palm.

Thumb around handle.

Normal.

I stood.

No tingling.

“Walk to the door.”

I stared.

“That is a test.”

“Yes.”

“You forbade me from changing grip to test.”

“I forbade you from testing yourself.”

“Different because you are here.”

“Yes.”

“Authority is convenient.”

“Walk.”

I walked to the inner door and back.

Ordinary pace.

Two crutches.

Right foot.

Turn.

Not Pessa's turn.

Just a turn.

I did not analyze it.

Mostly.

“Again.”

I went.

Back.

No tingling.

“Again.”

“Hessa.”

“Last.”

Third.

No tingling.

She had me sit.

Right hand back on towel.

“You just tested crutch use.”

“I observed a small amount of ordinary crutch use immediately after a draw.”

“That is testing crutch use.”

“Yes.”

“Could have said that.”

“I did.”

“You said walk.”

“That was the instruction.”

I stared.

She wrote.

“What does it mean?”

“That you completed a minimal draw and a few minutes of ordinary crutch use without immediate thumb tingling.”

“And?”

“And nothing else yet.”

“Nothing?”

“Nothing else.”

“You are cruel.”

“I am careful.”

“Same texture.”

She smiled.

Tiny.

Then gone.

“Can we shape?”

“No.”

“Why not? The thumb is fine.”

“The thumb is fine right now.”

“Which is what we wanted.”

“It is one thing we wanted.”

“What else?”

“Time.”

“We have time.”

“Not in this room.”

I leaned back.

Bad chair.

Same enemy.

“You want another day.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the second tingling episode happened later during ordinary life, not during the immediate observation after shaping.”

“So immediate draw-only plus crutch use not reproducing it tells us nothing about delayed recurrence.”

“It tells us it did not happen immediately.”

“You are impossible.”

“I am repetitive.”

“That is worse.”

She folded the towel.

Experiment over.

I watched her put it aside.

“Tomorrow?”

“No magic tomorrow.”

“Day after?”

“Come back.”

“For shaping?”

“For assessment.”

“Hessa.”

“I know what you want.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“To try the thumb again.”

That was too small.

I shook my head.

Her expression changed.

“What?”

“I want to know if I can still learn magic.”

The room got quiet.

Not theatrically.

The child in the outer room coughed again.

Hessa's face did not soften.

Good.

I did not want soft.

“You are learning,” she said.

“I failed.”

“Yes.”

“That is not what I mean.”

“I know.”

“I mean casting.”

“I know.”

“Barrier.”

“I know.”

“Other spells.”

“I know.”

“Being able to use mana without someone sitting six feet away with copper.”

“I know.”

I hated how calm she was.

“I was good at this.”

“Yes.”

“Not the best.”

“No.”

“I know.”

“But good.”

“Yes.”

“And now I put mana in the wrong part of my hand and everyone is pleased.”

“I was not pleased.”

“I was.”

“I noticed.”

“That matters.”

“Yes.”

There.

Not enough.

Enough to continue.

I looked at my right hand.

“Do you think I can?”

Hessa took time.

This was worse than an immediate no.

“I think you have already shown that you can draw mana repeatedly.”

“Yes.”

“You deliberately altered it once.”

“Wrong.”

“Yes.”

“You stopped when the alteration began to move somewhere you did not intend.”

I looked up.

“That matters?”

“Yes.”

More than I had given it.

Interesting.

“You recognized something familiar and stopped before it became more.”

“Yes.”

“You reported the hand symptom.”

“Yes.”

“You did not reproduce it yourself.”

“I considered several excellent methods.”

“I know.”

“You don't know.”

“I know you.”

Fair.

She continued.

“These are all things I need before I let you do more.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No.”

“Do you think I can cast again?”

“I don't know.”

There.

Clean.

I nodded.

It hurt less than I expected.

Or differently.

“Do you think I cannot?”

“I don't know.”

“Excellent.”

“You asked.”

“I did.”

“Would you rather I lie?”

“No.”

“Then stop looking offended.”

“I can be offended by reality.”

“Yes.”

We sat.

After a moment I said, “If the old pattern keeps taking over, how do you tell whether I am learning anything new?”

“I don't, from one attempt.”

“I mean eventually.”

“Eventually I would want you to make a deliberate change that is not simply the first movement of something you already know.”

“That was the thumb.”

“That was the question.”

“And I failed it.”

“Yes.”

“So we repeat until I don't.”

“Maybe.”

I glared.

She ignored it.

“Repetition can teach the thing we are trying to observe,” she said.

I stopped.

There was the ugly part.

If I practiced the thumb enough under supervision, then eventually succeeding would not necessarily show that I could make an unfamiliar deliberate change. It could show that I had trained a new tiny form.

“That contaminates the test.”

“It changes the question.”

“Same problem.”

“No. Problems are allowed to change.”

I looked at the towel.

“So you might not repeat the same instruction.”

“I might.”

“Or you might change the target.”

“I might.”

“Different finger.”

“Maybe.”

“Left hand.”

“Not yet.”

That answer was immediate.

I noticed.

“Because of the missing-side question?”

“Because I do not need to add that question yet.”

Good.

Separate.

I liked her more when she refused to make the interesting connection.

“So the goal isn't to teach me thumb magic.”

“No.”

“That would be a terrible specialty.”

“It would.”

“Very limited market.”

“Greg.”

“Fine.”

We sat.

The bad chair supported me badly.

The towel sat folded beside the copper.

No magic.

No answer.

Still.

Something had changed.

Not in the thumb.

In the question.

Before the accident, magic competence had been a ladder.

Draw.

Shape.

Spell.

Better spell.

More efficient.

More power.

Different conditions.

Failure meant find the error and train it.

Now the uncertainty lived lower.

Could I even perform the thing at all?

My brain immediately tried to build probabilities from four draws and one failed localization.

I stopped.

Not because Hessa told me.

Because the numbers were garbage.

That was growth.

Or exhaustion.

“Can I train with Pessa today?”

Hessa looked at me.

“Do you want to?”

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

That was not the medical answer.

“You are becoming annoying.”

“I learned from you.”

“I asked whether you want to.”

I checked.

Body.

Right calf fine.

Hip fine.

Hands fine.

Residual limb baseline.

Thumb normal.

I could.

That was not want.

“No.”

“Then don't.”

“What if I want to later?”

“Ask Pessa.”

“Medically?”

“I have no objection based on what I see today. If the thumb tingles again before then, reconsider.”

“Reconsider means?”

“Use judgment.”

“That is dangerously vague.”

“Yes.”

“Are you sure?”

“No.”

I laughed.

Hessa stood.

Appointment over.

At the door I stopped.

“Why did you ask if I wanted to train?”

She was cleaning the copper strip.

“Because you asked if you could.”

“That is not usually how medicine works.”

“It was not a medical question anymore.”

I thought about that.

Dangerous.

Useful.

Probably both.

“Day after tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“Assessment.”

“Yes.”

“Possible shaping?”

“Maybe.”

I groaned.

Nerin laughed from the outer room.

I left.

Outside, Carrow was wet.

Not raining.

It had rained while I was inside.

The stones shone dark.

Roof edges dripped.

A wagon had cut brown tracks through the street.

I stood under the awning for a moment.

No destination.

Pessa was possible.

Guild possible.

Jorren possible.

Lyssa possible if she was not working, which she probably was.

Home possible.

Food definitely possible.

I chose food first because uncertainty consumed calories.

This was medically established by hunger.

I bought a meat roll from a stall and ate it walking slowly toward the Guild.

My thumb did not tingle.

I refused to care.

This lasted until the second bite.

Then I cared.

Still no tingling.

Fine.

At the Guild, Merrin was at the front desk.

He looked up.

“Work?”

I almost said yes.

Not because I wanted work.

Because he had offered a category.

Work was easy to become.

Useful.

Requested.

Paid.

Contained.

“What kind?”

“Receipt comparison. Ground floor. Sitting. Maybe an hour.”

Perfect.

Exactly the kind of thing I could do.

I looked toward the side room.

Then at the meat roll.

Then back at Merrin.

“No.”

He blinked once.

“All right.”

That was it.

No inquiry into my recovery.

No disappointment.

He turned to the next person.

I stood there holding food.

This had happened before.

Still weird.

Jorren was not there.

Alden was not there.

Pessa was somewhere beyond the yard, maybe.

No one required me.

I finished the meat roll.

Then I went upstairs.

Not lodging upstairs.

Guild upstairs.

Stopped.

No.

That was stupid.

I did not need to climb stairs to prove I could make an unproductive choice.

I turned around.

Ugly turn.

Worked.

Outside again.

A boy was floating folded paper boats down the gutter left by the rain.

Not a child I knew.

One boat caught under a cart rut.

He poked it with a stick.

Missed.

Poked again.

Freed it.

The boat spun once and continued.

I watched until it vanished under a bridge stone.

Then I walked home.

The keeper was repairing a loose hinge on the pantry door.

She had the pin out and was tapping it straight against the floor with a small hammer.

“Need help?”

“No.”

Good.

I sat at the bottom table.

She hammered.

I watched.

After a while she said, “You have somewhere to be?”

“No.”

She nodded.

Hammer.

Turn pin.

Hammer.

Check.

“Could be better.”

“Probably.”

“Door still opens.”

“Yes.”

She put the pin back.

The hinge squeaked.

She frowned.

Removed it again.

I laughed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Useful nothing?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She went back to work.

My thumb remained quiet.

I did not write it down immediately.

That was perhaps the most impressive thing I did all day.

Eventually, I went upstairs.

Fourteen steps.

Slow.

Rail.

Crutches.

Right leg.

Pause at the landing.

No drama.

In my room, the repaired tavern chair sat by the window.

I sat.

Carrow outside.

Wet roofs.

People moving.

Someone shouted about onions in the distance.

Possibly the same seller.

Probably not.

The world contained many onion problems.

I opened the notebook.

Wrote:

Minimal supervised draw. No shaping. No immediate thumb tingling during draw or brief supervised ordinary crutch use. No recurrence by late afternoon.

Then:

This does not explain anything.

I stared at that sentence.

Crossed it out.

Wrote:

No conclusion yet.

Better.

I closed the notebook.

My thumb did not tingle.

I let it not.
