LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 112

THE AUDIENCE

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa finished with the customer before she looked at me.

Not because she had not seen me.

Because she was working.

I approved.

Privately.

She tied the last bit of wrapping, took payment, said something I could not hear, and only then came outside.

“You're smiling.”

“No.”

“You are.”

“Hessa let me shape.”

Lyssa stopped.

“Today?”

“Yes.”

“Are you allowed to be doing magic today?”

“Yes.”

“That was fast.”

“Thank you for asking the important question first.”

“Did it hurt?”

“No.”

“Thumb?”

“Nothing.”

“What did you do?”

I explained.

Badly.

Because explaining mana shaping to someone who did not shape mana required either metaphors or lying.

I chose metaphors.

“So,” Lyssa said, “she told you to stop doing the thing without stopping the other thing.”

“Yes.”

“And you sort of did.”

“Yes.”

“And your wrist moved.”

“Yes.”

“And now you're happy.”

“Reasonably.”

She laughed.

I hated that word now.

“Tomorrow no magic.”

“Good.”

“That is not the correct response.”

“It is if I want you for tomorrow.”

I looked at her.

“For what?”

She smiled.

“Are you busy?”

I considered my schedule.

No Hessa.

No required work.

Pessa not promised.

No Sevren route.

No appointment.

“I can become busy.”

“Don't.”

“What are we doing?”

“I'm not telling you.”

“That is manipulative.”

“Yes.”

“Expensive?”

“Not very.”

“Food?”

“Eventually.”

“Dangerous?”

“Greg.”

“Relevant question.”

“Not intentionally.”

That answer would age poorly.

The next evening I wore the dark green shirt.

Obviously.

I also spent too long deciding whether the blue shirt might look better because wearing the green one every time Lyssa saw me risked making it look like I owned one acceptable shirt.

I owned at least two.

Probably.

The gray-white one did not count.

I chose green.

Vanity was a form of evidence.

The keeper watched me come downstairs.

“Date?”

“No.”

She looked at the shirt.

“Date.”

I left.

Lyssa met me near the Guild.

She had changed after work.

I noticed.

I did not catalogue.

Growth.

She looked me over.

“Green.”

“You said you liked it.”

“I did.”

“That influenced the decision.”

“I know.”

She kissed my cheek.

Good start.

“Where?”

She pointed west.

We walked.

Not fast.

Lyssa had learned my current speed without turning it into ceremony.

That was nice.

She also did not walk artificially slowly.

She moved, stopped at stalls, doubled back once because she wanted sugared nuts, and generally behaved like the city belonged to both of us at different speeds.

I bought the nuts.

She ate most of them.

The building appeared after a turn between a chandlery and a narrow brick shop.

Three stories.

Old stone first floor.

Timber above.

Wide doors.

Painted sign.

People entering.

I looked at Lyssa.

“No.”

“What?”

“You are not inspecting the building.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You looked at the upper windows.”

“They're narrow.”

“Greg.”

“Fine.”

Theatre.

I had seen performances.

Small ones.

Street performers.

Guild celebrations.

Bad music in taverns.

This was different.

Tickets.

Ushers.

Printed playbill.

Actual seating.

A stage.

Lyssa had bought the cheaper seats, which I approved because I was not paying for architectural prestige.

Our seats were on the aisle.

That mattered physically.

She had thought about it.

She did not mention that she had thought about it.

Also good.

I sat.

Crutches angled where they would not trip anyone.

Residual limb comfortable enough.

Right foot planted.

Lyssa beside me.

People filled the room.

A lot of people.

More than I liked being responsible for.

Fortunately I was responsible for none.

“What is it?” I asked.

“You didn't read the bill?”

“I was evaluating the exits.”

“You promised.”

“I promised not to inspect the building.”

“That is the building.”

“Exits are safety.”

She took the playbill from me.

“You are impossible.”

“What is it?”

“A comedy.”

“About?”

“You'll see.”

“I dislike preventable uncertainty.”

“You love preventable uncertainty.”

That was slander.

The lamps lowered.

Conversation softened.

Curtain.

I forgot the exits.

Not immediately.

But eventually.

The play was stupid.

This was not criticism.

A merchant had disguised himself as his own dead uncle to avoid paying a debt.

His daughter knew.

His wife did not.

A magistrate was sleeping with someone he should not have been sleeping with.

A servant understood everything and was paid by everyone to understand nothing.

It was excellent.

The merchant's dead-uncle voice was terrible.

Intentionally.

I laughed.

Lyssa looked at me.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You looked.”

“You laughed.”

“It was funny.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you looking at me?”

“Because you look surprised.”

“I am not surprised.”

The dead uncle tripped over his own cane.

I laughed harder.

Fuck.

At some point I stopped thinking about how the scenery changed.

Then I noticed how the scenery changed and became angry at myself.

Two people moved a wall while everyone watched an actor argue with a hat.

The wall was gone before the hat lost.

Good.

There was a musical bit.

I disliked it.

Then liked the end.

Intermission came.

I needed to stand.

Not because of pain.

Because sitting that long had made everything stiff.

We moved into the aisle after the crowd thinned.

Lyssa stretched.

“You like it.”

“It is competent.”

“You like it.”

“The servant is good.”

“You like it.”

“Yes.”

She smiled like she had won something.

“I knew it.”

“You did not know.”

“I did.”

“You suspected.”

“I knew.”

“No.”

We bought watered wine.

Mine mostly water.

Food later.

Second half.

The merchant's wife now believed she was being haunted by two different dead relatives, only one of whom was her husband.

This was becoming structurally ambitious.

Then I smelled smoke.

Not stage smoke.

I did not know why I knew that.

Maybe because the stage smoke earlier had smelled sweet.

This smelled like hot cloth.

I looked toward the left side of the stage.

Nothing.

Actor talking.

Audience listening.

Lyssa noticed me stop laughing.

“What?”

“Smoke.”

She sniffed.

Her expression changed.

Onstage, the servant entered too early.

I knew because the magistrate looked at him.

One heartbeat.

Then smiled.

“Ah,” the magistrate said, “and here comes the man who arrives precisely when he should not.”

Audience laughed.

The servant bowed.

Behind him, at the edge of the curtain, someone moved fast.

Not actor-fast.

Work-fast.

I looked at Lyssa.

She was already looking.

“You see it.”

“Yes.”

A thin line of smoke appeared high near the side wall.

People in the first rows did not notice.

Some farther back did.

Murmur.

The actors continued.

Why?

The servant crossed downstage.

Too far.

Blocked the audience's view of the left wing.

Deliberate.

The magistrate began a story that had not been set up.

Or maybe I had missed it.

No.

Lyssa grabbed my sleeve.

“We're going.”

“Yes.”

We stood.

An usher was already moving down the aisle.

Not shouting.

“Side doors, please. Slowly. We're clearing the hall.”

That was worse than shouting.

Because it meant there was a plan.

People started standing.

The actors kept going.

The magistrate's story became louder.

The servant interrupted him repeatedly.

Audience laughter turned uneven.

More people noticed smoke.

An usher opened another door.

“Come on,” Lyssa said.

“I am.”

We moved.

She was ahead.

Of course she was.

Everyone was ahead.

My crutches found floor between shoes.

Right foot.

Swing.

Right foot.

People pressed from behind.

Not crushing.

Yet.

I hated crowds.

A man tried to step around my left side, saw the missing lower leg too late, nearly collided with the crutch, apologized, kept moving.

Lyssa turned.

“You good?”

“Yes. Go.”

Wrong word.

Not because she abandoned me.

Because the crowd took permission literally.

People flowed between us.

Lyssa slowed.

Someone behind her said, “Keep moving.”

She looked back.

I lifted a crutch slightly.

Go.

She went.

I followed.

Increasingly behind.

The aisle narrowed near the door.

Smoke thickened.

Not black.

Gray.

Enough.

Somebody coughed.

A woman started asking whether there was a fire.

The usher said, “Keep moving, please.”

Onstage:

“You cannot arrest a dead man!”

Huge laugh.

Perfect line.

Terrible timing.

Or perfect timing.

I could not tell.

Then something crashed backstage.

Not stage crash.

Real crash.

Audience changed.

You could feel it.

The room stopped being entertained and started becoming a crowd.

Ushers raised voices.

“Side doors. Keep moving.”

Lyssa disappeared through the doorway.

Good.

I reached maybe six people from it.

Then another usher grabbed the rope divider and redirected us.

“Not this door. Back passage.”

“What?”

“Back passage. This way.”

“I need that door.”

“Blocked outside. Move.”

I moved.

Slowly.

He saw the crutches.

His face did a calculation.

“Can you manage three steps?”

“How high?”

“What?”

“The steps.”

“Normal steps.”

Useless unit.

“Rail?”

“No.”

“No.”

He swore.

“Backstage.”

“That seems worse.”

“Backstage is level.”

He pointed.

Smoke.

People.

A narrow opening beside the stage.

“Go.”

I went.

This was how I entered the theatre.

Not the building.

The machine.

The first thing backstage did was try to kill me with a painted tree.

Two men came around a corner carrying it sideways.

“MOVE!”

“I AM.”

“MOVE!”

“I AM CURRENTLY MOVING.”

They swerved.

The tree passed over one crutch.

Someone behind them shouted, “Not there!”

I had no idea whether that was for them or me.

A woman in a soot-streaked shirt grabbed my upper arm.

Not hard.

“Where are you going?”

“Outside.”

“Wrong direction.”

“I was redirected.”

“Of course you were.”

She looked at the crutches.

Looked toward the passage.

Looked toward smoke.

“Can you do stairs?”

“Poorly.”

“No stairs.”

“Excellent.”

“Stand there.”

She pointed beside a red curtain.

I went there.

Someone immediately shouted:

“Not there!”

I looked at the woman.

She swore.

“Fine. Chair.”

There was a chair.

Prop?

Real?

Did not matter.

“Sit.”

“I can stand.”

“Congratulations. Sit.”

I sat.

People ran around me.

Not random.

Mostly.

Buckets moved one direction.

Empty buckets returned another.

A man carried a lamp tray with no lamps.

Someone dragged a rolled carpet.

An actor in a ridiculous white wig came through, coughing.

“Contained?”

“Not yet,” the soot-shirt woman said.

“Do I go?”

“Yes.”

He walked onto the stage.

The cough vanished.

His body changed.

Voice changed.

“Wife!” he boomed.

Audience laughed.

I stared.

The soot-shirt woman pointed at me.

“You. Can you read?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She shoved a folded page into my hand.

“What is this?”

“Page.”

“I know.”

“Then we're both doing wonderfully.”

She disappeared.

I opened it.

Dialogue.

Names.

Lines.

A mark beside one sentence.

Someone ran past.

“Where's Teren?”

“Bucket line!”

“Fuck.”

Another actor appeared at the curtain.

Young man.

Blue coat.

Fake mustache.

He looked at me.

“Are you Teren?”

“No.”

“Can you shout?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“What?”

He pointed at the page.

“When she says, ‘Who walks beyond the grave?’ you say the marked line.”

“I am not in this play.”

“You are now.”

He vanished onto the stage.

I looked at the page.

This was unreasonable.

Smoke curled near the ceiling.

Bucket line.

People moving.

Somebody yelled for sand.

The audience was still there.

Not all of them.

Maybe half.

Maybe less.

The actors were still acting.

Why were they still acting?

Then I understood enough.

Not theatre.

Crowd.

If the stage stopped, everyone looked at the smoke.

If everyone looked at the smoke, everyone decided at once.

The performance was buying exits time.

Not perfectly.

But time.

A woman onstage said:

“Who walks beyond the grave?”

Oh.

Fuck.

I looked at the marked line.

“No man who intends to return.”

My voice went out through the gap.

Too flat.

Probably.

The audience went quiet.

The woman onstage turned toward where I sat.

I could see only part of her face.

She waited.

Why was she waiting?

Then she smiled.

“Then my husband is safe.”

The audience exploded laughing.

I stared at the page.

That was not funny.

Backstage someone hissed:

“Good.”

“What?”

“Again if she calls it.”

“There is another?”

No answer.

The scene continued.

I heard feet behind me.

A stagehand stopped.

“You're blocking the bucket return.”

“I was put here.”

“Move.”

“Where?”

“Anywhere.”

“That has already failed twice.”

He grabbed the chair.

Not me.

The chair.

He dragged it three feet with me still sitting.

“What the fuck?”

“Better.”

It was not better.

A bucket nearly hit my right crutch.

I pulled it in.

“Keep those close.”

“I require them.”

“Then require them smaller.”

Impossible profession.

The soot-shirt woman returned.

“Fire's down.”

“Down?”

“Contained.”

“Is the building safe?”

“Safer.”

“That is not the same word.”

“No.”

“Why are they still performing?”

She looked at me like I was stupid.

“Because they're performing.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is tonight.”

Then:

“Can you do the line again?”

“I already did it.”

“She may call it.”

“Why?”

“Because we're missing Teren.”

“Who is Teren?”

“Bucket.”

Right.

The woman disappeared.

I looked toward the audience.

Some were still leaving.

Some were sitting.

Some were laughing.

Some were looking at the smoke.

The actors kept pulling them back.

Not all.

Enough.

The woman onstage turned again.

Different setup.

Improvised.

I knew because the line did not match the page.

She said:

“And if the dead return?”

Silence.

Was that mine?

No marked cue.

She looked toward me.

Oh, fuck her.

I had one line.

I used it.

“No man who intends to return.”

This time I changed nothing.

The audience laughed before she answered.

Why?

The woman covered her face.

“Then I married an idiot.”

Bigger laugh.

Someone backstage slapped the curtain.

I had no idea whether that was approval.

A bell rang.

Not theatrical.

Three quick.

The soot-shirt woman shouted, “Clear!”

Actors changed.

Fast.

The servant crossed stage.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he said, still in character, “my master has finally misplaced the entire house.”

Laugh.

“Fortunately, we have found several better ones outside.”

More laugh.

Then his voice shifted.

Barely.

“Please follow our ushers. Slowly. Take your cups if you must. Leave the chairs. They are terrible chairs.”

People laughed and stood.

That was the evacuation.

Inside the joke.

Holy shit.

The remaining audience moved.

No stampede.

Not calm exactly.

Directed.

The actors stayed visible until the room emptied.

Then the servant dropped character like a coat.

“Where's the west bucket?”

Someone answered.

He ran.

I sat in the dragged chair holding a page from a play I had not been in.

My hands smelled like charcoal from yesterday and smoke from today.

No.

That was metaphor.

My hands smelled like smoke.

Someone said:

“Who the fuck is he?”

The soot-shirt woman pointed.

“Audience.”

The fake-mustache actor said, “Not anymore.”

“I am absolutely audience.”

“You had two lines.”

“One line twice.”

“Still counts.”

“It does not.”

Nobody cared.

I remembered Lyssa.

Fuck.

I stood too fast.

Right foot planted.

Crutches.

“Outside.”

The soot-shirt woman blocked me.

“Front is still closed.”

“Lyssa is outside.”

“Then she is safer than you.”

“That is not comforting.”

“Back exit in a minute.”

“I need to go.”

“I know.”

Her tone changed.

Not soft.

Just real.

“We'll get you out.”

I believed her.

Annoying.

The fire had been in a backstage storage area.

I learned this accidentally from people shouting.

A lamp.

Maybe.

Curtain cloth.

Maybe.

Nobody agreed yet.

Contained.

Smoke still bad.

One person had burned a sleeve.

No serious injury that I could see.

I was not qualified to know.

The back exit opened.

Level until the last threshold.

Good.

Two stagehands moved a trunk.

Someone told me to wait.

I waited.

Then moved.

Slow.

Painfully slow now because my hands had been gripping too hard from adrenaline and the floor was cluttered.

Outside air hit.

Cold.

Lyssa was there.

Not calmly.

She saw me.

Her whole body changed.

Then she crossed the distance fast.

“Where the fuck were you?”

“Behind you.”

“You were not behind me.”

“I was behind you. I was simply becoming increasingly behind you.”

She stared.

Bad time.

“Sorry.”

Better.

She grabbed my shirt.

Not the crutches.

Not my arm.

Shirt.

“I thought you were still inside.”

“I was.”

“That is the problem.”

“Yes.”

She looked at my face.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Burned?”

“No.”

“Hand?”

“Fine.”

“Leg?”

“Fine.”

“Why do you have paper?”

I looked down.

Still holding the page.

“I was in the play.”

Lyssa blinked.

“What?”

“No.”

That was wrong.

“I was not in the play.”

She looked at the paper.

Then at the theatre.

Then at me.

“Greg.”

“I read one line.”

Her mouth opened.

Closed.

“You acted?”

“No.”

“Did the audience hear you?”

“Yes.”

“Did someone answer you?”

“Yes.”

“That's acting.”

“That is not a sufficient definition.”

She laughed.

Angry laugh.

Relieved laugh.

Then she hugged me.

Careful of crutches only because she had learned where they were.

I leaned into her.

For a while neither of us said anything.

Behind us the troupe was still working.

Buckets.

Doors.

Arguments.

Someone shouted about a missing wig.

The building had almost caught fire and someone cared about a wig.

I liked them immediately.

This was concerning.

Lyssa pulled back.

“Our date caught fire.”

“Yes.”

“You said not intentionally dangerous.”

“You said that.”

“I did.”

“Your prediction failed.”

She shoved my shoulder.

“Asshole.”

We found food very late.

Not good food.

Everything good was closing.

We sat on a low wall again.

Apparently this was becoming ours.

Lyssa stole half my bread.

I told her about the painted tree.

The chair.

The bucket line.

The actor who became someone else the instant he stepped through the curtain.

The evacuation hidden inside a joke.

I did not tell her what I thought it meant.

I did not know.

At the end I showed her the page.

She read the marked line.

“No man who intends to return.”

“Yes.”

“That got a laugh?”

“The second time.”

“Why?”

“I don't know.”

She smiled.

“That bothers you.”

“Deeply.”

“Good.”

We walked home.

At the lodging door she kissed me.

Long enough that I forgot the theatre for several seconds.

Useful.

Then she said:

“You liked the play.”

“Before the fire.”

“And during.”

“I did not like the fire.”

“You liked the weird people.”

I looked back toward the distant street.

Smoke was no longer visible.

“I do not know them.”

“Mm.”

“That is not an argument.”

“Goodnight, Greg.”

She left.

I went inside.

The keeper looked at the soot on my shirt.

“Date?”

I looked down.

Dark green.

Still dark green.

Mostly.

“Technically.”

She nodded as if this explained everything.

Upstairs, I found the folded play page still in my pocket.

I put it on the table.

Not the notebook.

The table.

Tomorrow no magic.

The day after, Hessa.

The theatre had not asked me to come back.

Good.

I was not going back.

Probably.
