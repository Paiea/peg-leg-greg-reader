TEXT READER · CHAPTER 115

THE RETURNER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I went west.

Not immediately.

That would have been embarrassing.

I ate breakfast.

Checked my hand.

Normal.

Checked residual limb.

Baseline.

Checked right calf because I might ask Pessa to train.

Fine.

Then I spent twenty minutes pretending the order mattered.

Pessa first would be reasonable.

Physical competence mattered.

I wanted it.

Hessa had no medical objection if Pessa agreed.

Theatre had no appointment.

No invitation.

No page.

No emergency.

No reason.

I went west.

This was worse than returning the page.

The page had belonged there.

I did not.

Probably.

The theatre doors were open again.

No ladder.

The black smear above one door remained, lighter at the edges.

Inside, the vinegar smell had mostly lost to sawdust.

Several audience seats were still covered.

A man was kneeling in the aisle fastening something under a bench.

He looked up at my crutches.

Then at me.

“Careful. Wet left side.”

“Thank you.”

He returned to the bench.

No recognition.

Good.

I moved right.

The stage curtain was up.

The darkened section had been replaced by a lighter patch that did not match anything.

Someone had decided matching could happen later.

A painted wall leaned sideways near the wings.

Three people were arguing about whether a window should exist in it.

“It existed yesterday.”

“It was burned yesterday.”

“The room still needs a window.”

“The room is imaginary.”

“So is the fire in the second act and we still need the fucking lamp.”

Professional.

I stood near the back.

Nobody came for me.

Excellent.

I could leave.

I stayed near the back.

Less excellent.

Onstage, two actors were rehearsing something I had not seen during the date.

Different scene.

Different play?

No.

Same clothes nearby.

Maybe replacement scene.

Maybe earlier scene I had missed while looking at Lyssa.

Possible.

The servant actor I had liked was onstage.

I recognized him immediately.

He was carrying a tray with three wooden cups.

He crossed toward a seated man, stopped, turned, and said:

“My lord, the physician has arrived.”

The seated man said:

“Then tell him I have died.”

The servant looked at him.

Nothing.

Someone in the audience seats said:

“Again.”

The servant went back.

Tray.

Cross.

Stop.

“My lord, the physician has arrived.”

“Then tell him I have died.”

The servant looked at him differently.

I did not know how.

The person in the seats said:

“Better.”

Fuck.

I had arrived at exactly the wrong profession.

I moved toward the side aisle.

Quietly.

The soot-shirted woman from the fire was carrying a coil of rope.

She saw me.

Stopped.

“Oh.”

Again.

Recognition without ceremony.

“Hello.”

“You came back.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

There was no page.

I looked at the stage.

She followed my eyes.

“Bad reason.”

“Yes.”

“Fine.”

She kept walking.

That was all.

I hated this place.

I moved closer.

The unnamed woman from yesterday was sitting three rows from the stage.

She had not seen me.

Good.

She was the one saying again.

The servant actor reset.

Same line.

Same response.

This time he set the tray down before the seated man's answer.

“No,” she said.

He picked it back up.

“Why?”

“You know he's going to say it.”

“I do know.”

“The servant doesn't.”

“I am the servant.”

“No. You're a man who knows the next line.”

He sighed.

“Again.”

I understood that problem.

No.

I understood the words.

Different.

He reset.

Tray.

Cross.

“My lord, the physician has arrived.”

“Then tell him I have died.”

This time the tray stayed in his hands.

He looked at the seated man.

A beat.

Then:

“Shall I ask him to confirm?”

The seated man broke.

Laughed.

The woman in the seats said:

“Keep that.”

The servant smiled.

“Written?”

“No.”

“Then you'll forget.”

“I won't.”

“You forgot the door yesterday.”

“The door was on fire.”

“Excuses.”

I smiled.

Mistake.

The woman turned.

Saw me.

No dramatic recognition.

Just:

“You said no.”

“I did.”

“And yet.”

“I know.”

She pointed to an empty seat beside the aisle.

Not beside her.

Just available.

I sat.

She turned back to the stage.

No welcome.

No question.

No page.

For several minutes I thought she might have forgotten I existed.

That should have been uncomfortable.

It was not.

I had spent years in rooms where being noticed meant someone wanted an answer.

Guild rooms.

Work rooms.

Vale rooms.

Hessa's room, where being noticed usually meant she wanted a symptom I had failed to report.

Here, nobody asked anything.

A worker carried a bundle of cloth down the aisle.

Someone tested a pulley.

The rope squealed.

The soot-shirted woman shouted, “Oil it.”

A voice from above said, “It is oiled.”

“Then apologize to it.”

The rope squealed again.

“Not accepted.”

Nobody looked at me.

The servant actor dropped one wooden cup.

It hit the stage.

He caught it on the second bounce with his foot.

The woman in the seats said, “Do that in the show and I'll kill you.”

“Audience would like it.”

“They like blood too. We're not giving them yours.”

He put the cup back.

The scene reset.

I realized I was comfortable.

Not physically.

The seat was still bad.

But I was not waiting for someone to justify why I was there.

I had come.

I was sitting.

Apparently that could be enough for at least five minutes.

Then the servant reset again.

I watched.

For a while, that was all.

This was different from yesterday.

Yesterday I had been used.

A little.

Today nobody needed me.

The scene ran.

Stopped.

Ran from halfway.

Stopped.

Someone moved the chair three inches.

Ran again.

The servant entered too early.

Again.

The seated actor missed a word and swore.

Again.

The tray lost a cup.

Again.

A carpenter crossed behind them carrying a board.

Nobody stopped because apparently that noise did not matter this time.

Then it did.

Again.

I started to see repetition.

Not rules.

Repetition.

There was a difference.

Maybe.

The woman in the seats eventually stood.

“Five minutes.”

Everyone dispersed.

The servant actor carried his tray off.

The seated actor immediately lay down across two chairs.

The woman turned to me.

“Why are you here?”

“I don't know.”

“That answer gets expensive.”

“Am I being charged?”

“Not yet.”

Good.

She sat on the seat in front of me backward, arms over the backrest.

“You came to watch?”

“I think so.”

“You think?”

“I walked here deliberately.”

“Congratulations.”

“I had other options.”

“So did I.”

That landed.

Not because profound.

Because obvious.

She looked toward the stage.

“What did you see?”

Danger.

“A servant.”

“Good.”

“A tray.”

“Excellent.”

“A man pretending to be dead before the physician arrives.”

“That's the scene.”

“The servant knew the next line once.”

She looked back at me.

“Did he?”

“He put the tray down before the other man said he was dead.”

“Yes.”

“And you stopped him.”

“Yes.”

“Because the servant could not know.”

“Because it looked like he knew.”

“Difference?”

She smiled.

“Maybe.”

Fuck.

“What?”

“You keep asking for the thing behind the thing.”

“There usually is one.”

“Sometimes there are six.”

“That is worse.”

“Yes.”

She stood.

“Come on.”

“Where?”

“Stage.”

I looked at the three steps.

She looked too.

“Oh.”

Not pity.

Memory.

Yesterday.

She pointed toward the side.

“There is a loading ramp.”

I had not known that.

Of course there was.

Scenery did not climb three decorative stairs.

She started walking.

I did not follow.

She looked back.

“You coming?”

“Why?”

“You came here.”

“That is not permission to do anything you want.”

“No.”

She waited.

Good.

“What are we doing?”

“Walking.”

“That sounds false.”

“It is mostly true.”

“Mostly?”

“You'll survive.”

“I have recently become skeptical of that phrase.”

She considered.

“Fair.”

I followed.

The side route went around the front of the stage and through a wide door.

Level.

Then a broad wooden ramp.

Not designed for me.

Designed for scenery.

Better.

The surface had grooves.

My crutch tips caught one slightly.

I adjusted.

Slow.

The woman did not tell me to hurry.

At the top, the stage was flatter than it looked from below.

Also more dangerous.

Edges.

Cables.

Marks.

A trapdoor seam.

Two loose cups.

A nail.

I pointed.

“Nail.”

Someone nearby said, “Where?”

I pointed.

They picked it up.

Useful.

Do not get excited.

The woman led me to the center.

“Stand there.”

“No.”

She blinked.

“I can stand.”

“You just said no.”

“You told me where.”

“Yes.”

“Why there?”

“Because I want to see something.”

“What?”

“You.”

“No.”

She laughed.

“Fine. Pick somewhere.”

That was different.

I looked.

Stage left had the tray table.

Center had open floor.

Right had a chair.

I chose near the chair.

Not because chair.

Because less traffic.

Probably because chair.

She nodded.

“Good.”

“What now?”

“Nothing.”

“Again?”

“You're very talented at it.”

“I have references.”

She walked away.

Actually walked away.

Left me standing onstage with two crutches and no instruction.

I waited.

People moved around me.

The servant actor came back carrying the tray.

Saw me.

“Who is he?”

The woman said, “Nobody.”

“Good.”

He walked around me.

Not close.

Adjusted path.

The seated actor returned.

Looked.

“Fire boy.”

“No.”

“What?”

“I am not fire boy.”

“Crutch?”

“Worse.”

“What's your name?”

“Gregory.”

There.

No ceremony.

“Gregory,” he said.

Then forgot me.

Also good.

The woman clapped once.

“From entrance.”

They reset.

I was still onstage.

“This seems wrong.”

“Probably,” she said.

“Am I supposed to leave?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Stay out of their way.”

“That is a skill I have not demonstrated here.”

“Practice.”

The scene began.

I stood near the chair.

Servant entered.

Tray.

“My lord, the physician has arrived.”

The seated man said:

“Then tell him I have died.”

The servant looked at him.

“Shall I ask him to confirm?”

The man did not laugh this time.

Good.

Scene continued.

I watched from inside it.

Different.

The servant crossed closer than expected.

I moved.

Wrong.

The woman said:

“Stop.”

Everyone stopped.

I froze.

“What?”

“Why did you move?”

“He was coming toward me.”

The servant said, “I wasn't.”

“You were.”

“I was going there.”

He pointed behind me.

“You were between me and there.”

“Yes.”

“So I was going around you.”

“I did not know that.”

The woman nodded.

“Again.”

“What am I supposed to do?”

“Decide.”

“That is not enough.”

“It is today.”

No.

This was Pessa language.

Not same thing.

Do not connect.

I stayed.

They reset.

The servant crossed again.

This time I did not move.

He passed in front.

Close.

Not collision.

The tray occupied space before his body did.

Interesting.

The seated actor stood and moved toward the chair beside me.

I shifted.

He sat.

Nobody stopped.

Scene continued.

Then he stood abruptly and I shifted again.

The woman said nothing.

Maybe right.

Maybe irrelevant.

Scene ended.

“What was that?” I asked.

“Rehearsal.”

“I mean me.”

“You were furniture.”

“I object.”

“You moved more than the chair.”

“Then I was bad furniture.”

“Yes.”

The servant laughed.

I looked at him.

“You are the one I liked.”

Silence.

Fuck.

He smiled slowly.

“Am I?”

“In the play.”

“That did not improve it.”

“I meant your performance.”

“Stop helping.”

The woman was laughing.

I hated all of them.

The servant bowed.

Tiny.

“Thank you, Gregory.”

“You're welcome.”

He looked at the woman.

“Can we keep him?”

“No,” I said.

She said, “Nobody asked you.”

“I was included grammatically.”

“Five minutes are over.”

The servant returned to his mark.

I should leave.

I had done the thing.

Whatever the thing was.

Nothing.

Furniture.

Standing in a scene.

No line.

No audience.

No payment.

No need.

I wanted another run.

There.

Clear.

Terrible.

The woman looked at me.

“You staying?”

“Yes.”

No probably.

She nodded.

“Then sit for this one. You're blocking the physician.”

I moved to the chair at stage right.

Sat.

Crutches angled close.

Out of traffic.

They ran the scene again.

I watched from three feet away.

The servant's tray trembled slightly when he stopped.

Not nerves.

Weight.

Maybe.

His thumb pressed under the edge.

The seated actor's dead line came.

The servant waited.

Not the same wait as before.

I knew that much.

Did not know why.

“Shall I ask him to confirm?”

This time nobody laughed.

The woman said:

“Again.”

I smiled.

Not because funny.

Because apparently that was allowed.

We did not finish anything.

That was the strange part.

No answer arrived.

No one told me why the audience had laughed.

No one explained acting.

I did not become useful.

I did not even read.

For almost an hour, I watched people repeat pieces of a scene until some versions were kept and others vanished.

Then the soot-shirted woman shouted that she needed the stage for the repaired wall.

Everyone moved.

Immediately.

The actors complained while moving.

Professional.

I stood.

The unnamed woman said, “Tomorrow?”

I looked at her.

Same question.

Different day.

“I might train.”

“With us?”

“No.”

“Then why would I care?”

“Pessa.”

“Who?”

“Exactly.”

She shrugged.

“Tomorrow?”

I thought.

Not about magic.

Hessa had forbidden it.

Not about whether theatre was useful.

Not about career.

Not about the fire.

I wanted to come back.

That was inconveniently simple.

“Yes.”

The word came out before I could improve it.

The woman nodded.

“Afternoon. If we're here.”

“If?”

“Wall.”

She pointed at the repaired scenery.

“If it falls, we're somewhere else.”

“That is not reassuring.”

“It isn't structural.”

“You people use that phrase too loosely.”

She walked away.

No time.

No contract.

Afternoon.

If they were there.

Barely an invitation.

Enough.

I took the loading ramp down.

Outside, I realized I had not asked her name.

She had not asked mine.

The seated actor had.

Gregory.

Fine.

I headed home.

Then changed direction.

Pessa.

Not because I needed to prove theatre had not replaced training.

Because I had wanted to train this morning too.

It was later now.

Maybe she would say no.

At the Guild yard, Pessa was finishing with someone else.

She saw me.

“Baseline?”

“Yes.”

“Magic?”

“None today.”

“Hessa?”

“No objection if you choose.”

Pessa looked at my hands.

“Where have you been?”

“Theatre.”

She did not care.

Good.

“Too tired?”

I checked.

Hands fine.

Right calf fine.

Residual limb fine.

Standing onstage had not been much.

Walking there and back had been something.

“Not yet.”

Pessa looked at the light.

“Twenty minutes.”

I smiled.

“No shield.”

“I didn't ask.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to ask eventually.”

“Not today.”

Fine.

We trained twenty minutes.

Pessa put four stakes out this time instead of six.

I noticed.

“Why four?”

“Because I picked up four.”

“That is not experimental design.”

“No.”

Good.

She called destinations.

Not directions.

Post.

Wall.

Gate.

Barrel.

Sometimes I chose the turn.

Sometimes the shortest route was stupid because a stake or Pessa occupied it.

Once I started left, saw her step into the opening, stopped, and went right.

No catch.

No hop.

Also no elegance.

Pessa said nothing.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You made a face.”

“I have a face.”

“Was that better?”

“Than what?”

“Last time.”

“Different day.”

I hated experts.

Turns.

Changing destinations.

Nothing new enough to name.

One ugly left turn.

One clean right turn that immediately became ugly when Pessa changed where she stood.

Good.

She stopped at twenty.

I did not argue.

By the time I got home, my palms were warm and my right calf tired.

Residual limb quiet.

No magic.

No theatre theory.

Tomorrow was another living day.

I had somewhere I wanted to be in the afternoon.

That was new.

Not the wanting.

The somewhere.
