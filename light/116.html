LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 116

THE PROMPTER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

My right calf objected to ambition before breakfast.

Not badly.

Enough.

I sat on the edge of the bed and waited for it to decide whether yesterday had been a mistake.

It did not.

Tired.

That was all.

Palms normal.

Thumb normal.

Residual limb baseline.

Good.

No magic.

Second living day.

Apparently living involved restraint.

I went downstairs.

The keeper had burned something.

Not food.

Wood.

“Pantry?”

“Hinge.”

“Again?”

“Different hinge.”

“Your pantry is structurally ambitious.”

“Eat.”

I ate.

No theatre until afternoon.

This had been specified.

I respected schedules.

Mostly.

That left morning.

Lyssa was working.

I knew that.

I also knew where.

Different fact.

I went anyway.

Not to interrupt.

This distinction was becoming important in several areas of my life.

She was outside when I arrived.

Good.

Carrying a wrapped bundle.

Better.

Not busy with a customer.

She saw me.

“You're alive.”

“Still.”

“I heard.”

“From who?”

“Half the market.”

“About which part?”

She smiled.

“The fire.”

“Old information.”

“And apparently you went back.”

There.

Carrow.

Confidentiality remained theoretical.

“Who told you?”

“Jorren.”

Traitor.

“What did he say?”

“That you returned a page and somehow stayed for rehearsal.”

“I did not rehearse.”

“You read a line several times.”

“That was earlier.”

She looked at me.

“Earlier?”

Fuck.

“I went back again yesterday.”

Lyssa stopped walking.

“You went back again.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I wanted to.”

That was easier to say than expected.

She smiled.

Not huge.

Not approving.

Just pleased by something.

“Okay.”

“That is all?”

“What do you want me to say?”

“I don't know.”

“Then okay.”

Excellent.

We walked half a block.

She handed the wrapped bundle to someone at a doorway.

Coins exchanged.

Work.

Then she turned back.

“You going again?”

“This afternoon.”

“Invited?”

“Loosely.”

“What does loosely mean?”

“If they're there.”

“That is very loose.”

“Yes.”

“You like them.”

“I don't know them.”

“You like being there.”

I considered lying.

“No.”

She stared.

“Yes.”

“Better.”

We bought tea.

Not a date.

Probably.

We stood because the stall had no seats.

Lyssa looked at my hands.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You looked.”

“You used to hide them when they hurt.”

“I do not hide my hands.”

“You absolutely do.”

“I reposition them.”

“Secretly.”

“That is not hiding.”

She took my right hand before I could object.

Not medical.

Different.

Thumb.

Palm.

Fingers.

She did not examine anything.

Just held it.

“Fine?”

“Yes.”

“Hessa today?”

“Tomorrow.”

“No magic today?”

“No.”

“You hate that.”

“Less than before.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“Do not make a face.”

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“Your face is not a reliable instrument.”

“It has excellent field performance.”

I laughed.

She kept my hand for another second.

Then let go.

No lesson.

Good.

“What happens tomorrow?”

“Hessa tests something.”

“What?”

“I don't know.”

“You really don't know?”

“No.”

“That must be terrible.”

“It is.”

She smiled into her tea.

I realized she enjoyed this more than was appropriate.

“You like when I don't know things.”

“Sometimes.”

“Why?”

“Because then you look around.”

That sounded suspiciously meaningful.

“No.”

“What?”

“I refuse that sentence.”

“You asked.”

“I revoke the question.”

“Okay.”

She let it go.

Better.

We stood because the stall had no seats.

I lasted six minutes before choosing a low wall nearby.

Lyssa joined me without comment.

“Did you act?”

“No.”

“What did you do?”

“Furniture.”

She laughed.

“I am serious.”

“That makes it worse.”

“I stood in a scene.”

“As furniture.”

“Yes.”

“Did anyone sit on you?”

“No.”

“Then bad furniture.”

“That was established.”

She drank.

I told her about the servant actor with the tray.

Not all of it.

Enough.

How he put the cup down too early because he knew the next line.

How the woman stopped him.

How he eventually improvised the physician confirmation line.

Lyssa listened.

Then said:

“You are thinking too hard.”

“That is a rude response.”

“You're doing the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where something has become a table in your head.”

I hated that she knew this.

“I have not made a table.”

“Yet.”

“No.”

“Good.”

She bumped my shoulder.

“Go be furniture.”

“I am not doing that again.”

“Sure.”

We separated because she had work.

No kiss.

Then she turned back, walked three steps, kissed my cheek, and left again.

Fine.

Morning complete.

I did not go to the Guild.

I went home.

Rested.

This was strategic.

Also I wanted to not arrive at theatre already tired.

That was a new sentence.

I disliked it.

Afternoon came.

I went west.

The theatre was there.

Good.

The wall was also there.

Better.

No relocation.

The mismatched patch looked worse in daylight than yesterday.

Someone had painted around it.

This had made it more obvious.

Inside, the audience hall was emptier.

Most covered seats were uncovered now.

The smell was sawdust, oil, cloth, old smoke.

The soot-shirted woman was onstage.

She had acquired clean sleeves.

Temporary.

She pointed at me.

“Ramp.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

That was almost welcome.

I used the side route.

Broad door.

Grooved ramp.

Slow.

No hurry.

At the top, the stage was busy.

Not rehearsal yet.

Props.

Pages.

A wooden bench.

Two fake doors.

The servant actor was there.

He saw me.

“Gregory.”

“Hello.”

“You came back.”

“Yes.”

“Poor judgment.”

“Repeatedly.”

He nodded.

“Worse.”

The unnamed woman from yesterday was arguing with someone over pages.

She looked up.

“You.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

There.

Good.

Not destiny.

Not talent.

Attendance.

She shoved three pages at the servant actor.

“Where's Teren?”

“Late.”

“Again?”

“Technically absent.”

“That is not better.”

Teren.

Finally relevant while still not present.

The woman looked around.

Then at me.

“No.”

I had not said anything.

“What?”

“You can read.”

“Yes.”

“Sit there.”

She pointed to a chair off to the side of the stage.

Near a small table.

“Why?”

“Because Teren is late.”

“What does Teren do?”

“Today? Apparently nothing.”

The servant actor said, “Normally he prompts.”

I looked at the woman.

“Prompts what?”

She stared.

“Actors.”

“That is not specific.”

“It is enough.”

She put a marked page in my hand.

Not Teren's from the fire.

Different.

More marks.

Names.

Lines.

Tiny notes in the margins.

I did not understand half of them.

“I am not replacing Teren.”

“No.”

“Good.”

“You are sitting in his chair until he arrives.”

“That sounds like replacing him.”

“No one pays furniture.”

Lyssa would enjoy this.

“What do I do?”

“Follow the page.”

“I can do that.”

“When someone stops because they forgot the next line, give them the first few words.”

“How do I know they forgot?”

“They stop.”

“People stop for many reasons.”

“Yes.”

“So.”

“Try.”

This was not instruction.

This was abandonment.

She walked away.

I looked at the servant actor.

“Help.”

“No.”

“Excellent.”

He smiled.

Rehearsal began.

Different scene.

Three actors.

The servant.

The wife from the fire performance.

Another man.

I had seen him before, maybe magistrate.

They started.

I followed the page.

Badly.

Not reading aloud.

Eyes moving.

Finding current line.

Losing it when someone skipped three lines.

Finding it again.

They stopped.

I looked up.

Nobody said anything.

Was this forgetting?

The woman looked at me.

“What?”

“I don't know whether that was a forgotten line.”

The wife said, “It wasn't.”

Good.

They reset.

Again.

The servant changed one word.

Page did not.

I looked at the word.

Then at him.

Nobody cared.

Again.

The other man stopped.

Silence.

He looked toward me.

There.

Probably.

I read:

“Your brother said...”

He snapped his fingers.

“Yes.”

Then continued.

Success.

Tiny.

Functional.

No audience.

No praise.

The woman said, “Earlier next time.”

“What?”

“He had to ask you with his face.”

“I do not read faces.”

“Learn faster.”

“No.”

She laughed.

Rehearsal continued.

Next stop.

Wife.

She looked down.

Not at me.

Silence.

I waited.

Too long.

She said, “Prompt.”

Oh.

“Then why did you marry him?”

She continued.

The woman pointed at me.

“Too late.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Again.

I started noticing different silences.

Danger.

Some were acting.

Some were thinking.

Some were waiting for someone else.

Some were missing the line.

I did not know which until after.

This was horrible.

The page was supposed to solve this.

It did not.

The page told me words.

Not when they were needed.

No table.

I refused.

For almost half an hour, I was bad at prompting.

Bad in several distinct ways.

Too slow.

Too fast.

Wrong actor.

Right actor, wrong line.

Right line, too much of it.

Once I gave almost the entire sentence.

The wife stopped.

“Do you want to play me?”

“No.”

“Then leave me something.”

“How much?”

“Enough.”

I looked at the woman.

She shrugged.

“Her problem.”

“This is a workplace built entirely from insufficient quantities.”

The wife laughed.

“First few words.”

“That is what I was told.”

“You gave seven.”

“I did not count.”

“That's obvious.”

Next time she stopped, I gave three.

She continued.

No one commented.

Three might be right.

Or only right that time.

Danger.

I refused the table again.

Another actor lost his place because the page in his hand had been reordered after repairs.

Not forgotten line.

Wrong page.

I almost prompted.

Stopped.

He looked down, swore, changed sheets.

Good.

The woman saw me not speak.

“Why didn't you?”

“His problem was paper.”

“Good.”

That was praise.

Tiny.

I felt it anyway.

Annoying.

Once I prompted when the servant had intentionally paused.

He turned his head toward me without breaking character.

The audience would probably have laughed.

There was no audience.

The woman stopped the scene.

“Why?”

“He stopped.”

“He was waiting.”

“That is stopping.”

“Not the same.”

“How do I know?”

“You don't yet.”

Good.

Hated.

“Again.”

Another time the wife lost a line and I caught it before she looked over.

First words.

She continued.

Nobody stopped.

That felt better than praise.

Then I prompted the wrong actor.

The servant said my words anyway.

Wrong line.

The scene collapsed.

Everyone laughed.

Including me.

“Page,” the woman said.

“I had the page.”

“Apparently not.”

“I was one line down.”

“Yes.”

“That is a very small error.”

“In theatre it became four people saying nonsense.”

Fair.

We reset.

Teren arrived forty minutes late.

I knew him immediately because everyone yelled.

Not appearance.

Function.

“Teren!”

He entered through the side door carrying food.

“I'm here.”

The soot-shirted woman shouted from somewhere backstage:

“You were here yesterday too. It did not help.”

Teren looked at me in his chair.

“Who are you?”

“Gregory.”

“Why are you in my chair?”

“You were late.”

He looked at the woman.

She shrugged.

“He reads.”

Teren handed me one of the food packets.

Compensation.

Maybe.

“Move.”

I moved.

No offense.

His chair.

I sat on a nearby crate.

Teren took the page.

Rehearsal restarted.

He was better.

Immediately.

Annoying.

Not magically better.

He missed things too.

Once the wife stopped and looked straight at him.

Teren looked straight back.

Silence.

She lifted both hands.

“Prompt.”

“I thought you were breathing.”

“I was drowning.”

“Similar posture.”

The woman said, “Again.”

Good.

Human.

Next run he prompted too early.

The wife snapped, “I had it.”

Teren said, “You had fear.”

“I had the line.”

“You had one of those.”

“Fuck you.”

They continued.

No one treated prompting as sacred.

That helped.

It also made the work harder to classify.

Teren was not a machine that knew the script.

He was another person trying to notice when other people stopped being able to continue.

That sentence became too broad in my head.

I killed it.

Specific room.

Specific scene.

Specific people.

Better.

He barely looked down.

He knew when to speak.

Sometimes before the actor looked.

Sometimes after.

Once he did not prompt during a long silence I would absolutely have interrupted.

The actor eventually continued.

Correct.

I leaned forward.

Teren noticed.

“What?”

“How did you know?”

“Know what?”

“That he had not forgotten.”

“He hadn't.”

“How did you know?”

“He was doing the scene.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is from here.”

Everyone said things like this.

I hated professional dialects.

Teren looked back at the page.

“Watch his hand.”

I did.

Next run.

The actor stopped again.

Hand open.

Still.

Then continued.

Later he actually forgot.

His hand tightened around the edge of his coat.

Teren prompted.

I stared.

“That cannot be universal.”

Teren laughed.

“No.”

“Then why tell me?”

“For him.”

Oh.

Specific person.

Specific habit.

Not rule.

Good.

Dangerous.

The unnamed woman glanced at me.

I put my face neutral.

She laughed anyway.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“Stop.”

“Not doing anything.”

Liar.

I watched Teren prompt.

Not system.

Person.

Scene.

Day.

Maybe.

Do not build.

At the break, Teren reclaimed his food.

I had already eaten half.

He looked at the packet.

“You ate it.”

“You gave it to me.”

“I was late.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn't mean you get lunch.”

“It apparently did.”

He considered.

“Fair.”

I liked him.

Too soon.

The servant actor sat beside the crate.

“You were bad.”

“I know.”

“You got better.”

“I got less bad.”

“Same thing.”

“No.”

He stole a piece of Teren's food from my packet.

Teren slapped his hand.

Theatre ecology.

No one explained it.

The unnamed woman came over.

“Tomorrow?”

I remembered Hessa.

Morning after two living days.

“That is Hessa.”

“Who?”

“Medical.”

“Afternoon?”

Maybe.

Hessa morning.

No guarantee about after.

Magic test might mean no physical/theatre? Not necessarily.

Hessa could forbid.

Unknown.

“I don't know.”

“Good.”

No pressure again.

She walked away.

That was becoming important.

Not everything demanded yes.

I finished Teren's food.

He objected.

Weakly.

Rehearsal resumed.

I did not prompt again.

I watched.

Teren used almost none of the page compared with me.

Not because he knew every line.

Because he watched people.

Sometimes page.

Sometimes stage.

Sometimes the woman.

Once he looked toward a door before anyone entered.

I looked too.

Nothing.

Then someone came through carrying a replacement prop.

Not acting.

Work.

Teren shifted his chair so the person could pass.

Did not stop reading.

Theatre was full of overlapping jobs that pretended not to overlap.

No.

Stop.

I watched.

That was enough.

When I left, the soot-shirted woman was measuring the patched wall.

“Still wrong?”

I asked.

“Very.”

“Performance tomorrow?”

“If the wall behaves.”

“Does it?”

“No.”

“Good luck.”

“Don't say that in theatre.”

I stopped.

“Why?”

“Because actors are idiots.”

She walked away.

I had learned something.

Probably not important.

Outside, late afternoon light made the theatre look ordinary again.

I headed home.

At the first corner I caught myself checking whether I could remember the wife's missed line.

I could.

Then the servant's.

Also could.

I stopped.

Memory was not rehearsal.

Probably.

Still, I deliberately counted market awnings until the lines loosened.

Five red.

Three blue.

One striped badly enough to be offensive.

Better.

No Pessa.

Enough walking.

Enough standing.

Enough stage.

Palms fine.

Calf mildly tired.

Residual limb quiet.

No magic.

Tomorrow morning Hessa.

That occupied one part of the day.

Theatre maybe occupied another.

Maybe not.

I liked maybe more than I used to.

Not generally.

Specifically.

That seemed safer.
