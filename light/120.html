LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 120

THE REPEATER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Hessa asked about the crutch before she asked about magic.

“Which one?”

“Right.”

“Tip slipped?”

“Began to.”

“Fell?”

“No.”

“Caught yourself?”

“I unloaded it before full weight.”

“With what?”

“The other crutch and right leg.”

She looked up.

“That is not what I meant.”

I knew.

“Hands?”

“Hot after training. Fine by later afternoon.”

“Calf?”

“Tired. Fine this morning.”

“Residual limb?”

“Baseline.”

“Thumb?”

“No.”

“Fingers?”

“No.”

“Any urge to move them?”

“No.”

“Forearm?”

“No.”

“Cold?”

“No.”

“Anything below the left knee that felt different from ordinary phantom sensation?”

“No.”

She wrote.

I sat on the same stool.

My right sleeve was already rolled above the elbow because experience had finally defeated ceremony.

Hessa noticed.

“Efficient.”

“I am capable of learning.”

“Occasionally.”

I looked at the tray.

Copper.

Charcoal.

Cloth.

Water.

Nothing else.

No object waiting to be affected.

No external-effect apparatus.

I had known there would not be.

Probably.

I was still disappointed.

That seemed unreasonable.

She had said closer.

Closer was a direction, not a destination.

I hated directions without distances.

Hessa finished writing.

“Yesterday was Pessa?”

“Yes.”

“And no magic.”

“Yes.”

“The day before?”

“Theatre. No magic.”

“After our last test?”

“No symptoms.”

“None you forgot to mention because they seemed unrelated?”

I thought.

This was a trap only if I deserved it.

“No.”

“Good.”

She closed the notebook.

“What do you think we're testing today?”

I did not answer immediately.

That was growth.

Or suspicion.

“Repeatability.”

“Too broad.”

“Whether I can reduce an alteration again while maintaining the underlying draw.”

“Better.”

“Without recruiting my hand.”

“Also relevant.”

“And without pushing the alteration somewhere else.”

“Relevant.”

“And whether I can tell when it is gone.”

She tilted her head.

“That one may be too ambitious.”

“Why?”

“Because last time you could not.”

“That is why we should test it.”

“That is why I am not making it the instruction.”

I disliked this.

“What is the instruction?”

“The same useful part as last time.”

“Same target?”

“Approximately.”

“Approximately is a new variable.”

“Yes.”

“That defeats repeatability.”

“No.”

“It changes location.”

“Barely.”

“Barely is not zero.”

Hessa took the charcoal.

“Gregory.”

“Yes.”

“I am not trying to prove you can reproduce a laboratory result.”

“We do not have a laboratory.”

“Then we agree.”

“That was not agreement.”

She drew a short line on the intact right forearm, near the wrist-side area she had used last time.

Not identical.

I could tell because I had stared at the previous mark for far too long.

“This is slightly farther inward.”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“I did not measure.”

I stared at her.

She smiled.

Cruel woman.

“Why?”

“Because your arm is not a board.”

“It has dimensions.”

“So does bread. I do not use calipers before breakfast.”

“That is because bread is not mana.”

“Good. We have established one difference.”

I looked at the line.

“Instruction?”

“Minimal draw.”

“Then shape toward the line.”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“Small.”

“Same as last time?”

“As close as you can without turning that into the task.”

Impossible sentence.

“And then?”

“I say RETURN.”

“One return?”

“One.”

“No refinement?”

“No.”

“If one edge remains?”

“You leave it.”

“If it shifts?”

“You tell me.”

“If I cannot tell?”

“You tell me that.”

“If reduction stops?”

“You tell me.”

“If I think I can improve it?”

“You do not.”

“That seems targeted.”

“It is.”

“At me.”

“Yes.”

Fair.

She moved the copper into place.

“Before we begin, hands.”

I opened and closed the right hand.

Normal.

She pressed along the forearm.

“Anything?”

“No.”

“Thumb?”

“No.”

“Good. Minimal draw only.”

I settled.

The first part had become familiar enough to be dangerous.

Not easy.

Familiar.

There was a difference.

I found the draw.

Small.

Supervised.

Nothing like Barrier.

Nothing completed.

No external movement.

Copper responded.

Hessa watched it.

“Hold.”

I held.

The phantom left foot existed because it usually existed.

Heel.

Toes.

A faint sense of the ankle being somewhere it was not.

Ordinary.

Not involved.

“Right forearm only,” Hessa said.

“Yes.”

“Toward the mark. Small.”

I shaped.

The alteration gathered near the charcoal line.

Narrower than the old broad pattern.

Not perfectly narrow.

I could feel the temptation to improve it before she said anything.

Do not.

That was not an instruction from Pessa.

Not theatre.

Not philosophy.

Hessa had literally told me not to refine.

Useful distinction.

“Hold.”

I held.

Copper response changed.

Hessa leaned slightly closer.

My fingers stayed open.

No urge.

No cold.

No thumb.

“RETURN.”

I stopped feeding the alteration and tried to reduce it while keeping the underlying draw.

At first, nothing obvious happened.

I waited.

No.

Not waited.

Continued the reduction.

The wrist-side edge softened.

Then the center.

The upper edge remained clearer.

Last time I had tried to fix that.

This time I did not.

It was irritating.

The upper edge persisted.

I could feel it.

Probably.

The rest diminished.

“Report,” Hessa said.

“Reduction. Wrist side first. Center dimmer. Upper edge remains.”

“Any movement?”

“No.”

“Urge?”

“No.”

“Continue RETURN. Do not correct the edge separately.”

I continued.

The upper edge dimmed.

Not smoothly.

It seemed to hold, then drop.

Copper diminished.

I felt the alteration become harder to distinguish.

This was where last time I had lost certainty.

I wanted to search.

Did not.

“Report.”

“Less.”

“Specific.”

“Upper edge less distinct. Center maybe still present.”

“Maybe?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

I hated when uncertainty earned approval.

“Continue.”

I did.

The alteration thinned further.

Or my ability to perceive it did.

Those were not the same.

I knew that.

Could not separate them.

“I cannot tell whether the center remains.”

“Do not search.”

“I know.”

“Then stop trying.”

“I am not.”

She looked at my face.

I stopped trying harder.

That sentence made sense only internally.

Probably.

“Maintain underlying draw.”

I did.

This was the strange part.

The draw remained.

The alteration was uncertain.

I could still tell those apart.

I thought.

KNOW versus THINK.

“I know the draw remains,” I said.

“Do you?”

“I am more confident of that than whether the alteration remains.”

“Better answer.”

Not yes.

Better answer.

Hessa watched the copper.

“Hold.”

I held.

My right hand stayed still.

No fingers.

No wrist.

No thumb.

The copper response stayed at the quieter level.

“Release.”

I released.

Everything settled.

Hessa immediately took my hand.

“Open.”

It was open.

“Close.”

I closed it.

“Again.”

Open.

Close.

Thumb.

Wrist.

She checked strength against her fingers.

“Any tingling?”

“No.”

“Cold?”

“No.”

“Numb?”

“No.”

“Pain?”

“No.”

“Urge?”

“No.”

“Left side?”

“Ordinary phantom foot.”

“Different?”

“No.”

She waited.

I waited.

Neither of us moved.

“This is where you tell me what happened.”

“I am deciding.”

“That is concerning.”

“It should be reassuring.”

“Not historically.”

She wrote first.

I tried to read upside down.

She moved the notebook.

“That is hostile.”

“It is medicine.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

She finished the line.

Then looked at me.

“You reduced it again.”

“Yes.”

“While maintaining the draw.”

“Yes.”

“No observed shift toward the elbow.”

“Yes.”

“No motor urge.”

“Yes.”

“No separate correction.”

“Because you prohibited it.”

“Yes.”

“The remaining edge reduced when you continued the same RETURN instruction.”

“Yes.”

“And you again reached a point where you could not confidently distinguish residual alteration.”

“Yes.”

I waited.

She did not continue.

“That is three.”

“Three what?”

“Successful reductions.”

“No.”

“Ch111.”

“Different instruction and different observation.”

“Ch114.”

“Messy.”

“Ch117.”

“Yes.”

“Today.”

“Yes.”

“That is at least three.”

Hessa tapped the charcoal with one finger.

“You are counting categories I am keeping separate.”

“Why?”

“Because Ch111 was stop-feeding after ENOUGH. Ch114 was the first deliberate RETURN and included an unintended elbowward shift plus finger-curl urge. Ch117 was cleaner overall but you sharpened an edge when you tried to refine. Today I removed the refinement question and asked for one continuous reduction.”

“All are reduction.”

“All involve reduction.”

“That is what I said.”

“No. You said they are the same count.”

“I said three.”

“You are trying to make a score.”

I leaned back.

“I did not bring columns.”

“I am proud of you.”

“That sounded insulting.”

“It was mostly sincere.”

Mostly.

I looked at the fading charcoal line.

“So what did today answer?”

“That you can repeat the broad action under a slightly changed target without the two specific unwanted responses we saw before.”

“Broad action.”

“Deliberately reduce a small shaped alteration while keeping an underlying draw active.”

“That sounds less broad.”

“It is broad compared with precise control.”

“What would precise control be?”

“Not today's question.”

Of course.

“Does today increase confidence?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“No.”

“Hessa.”

“No.”

“Does it change external effect?”

“It changes my confidence in considering it.”

My attention sharpened.

“That is almost clearance.”

“No.”

“Scheduling?”

“No.”

“Then what changed?”

She looked at me for long enough that I regretted asking in that tone.

Then she answered anyway.

“I am less concerned that the moment you deliberately alter a draw, your only options are continue, lose the draw, or recruit some other pattern.”

I stopped.

That was more than I expected.

She continued.

“I have now seen you reduce an alteration while the draw remains. More than once. Under instructions that were not identical. Today you did it without the elbowward shift, without the finger urge, and without trying to repair the remaining edge separately.”

“So the next question is external.”

“No.”

I stared.

She stared back.

“You enjoy this.”

“I enjoy accuracy.”

“You enjoy accuracy when it hurts me.”

“That is still accuracy.”

“What is the next question?”

“Not today.”

“You know.”

“I have possibilities.”

“Which?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because then you will spend the next two days practicing a test you are not allowed to practice.”

“I cannot practice without mana.”

“You can rehearse decisions.”

That was offensive because it was true.

“I would only think about it.”

“Yes.”

“That is not practicing.”

She gave me the same look Pessa had given me yesterday when I suggested choosing the route she was less likely to block.

Apparently my face was becoming a liability across professions.

I changed direction.

“How many living days?”

“One.”

I blinked.

“One?”

“One full day. No magic.”

“Then return?”

“Yes.”

“Morning?”

“Yes.”

“That sounds almost like momentum.”

“It sounds like a schedule.”

“What symptoms change it?”

“Any thumb tingling. Any finger-curl urge. Any involuntary movement. New weakness. Cold. Numbness. Pain. Anything in the left-side phantom pattern that is distinctly unusual for you. Anything you think is probably nothing but are tempted not to tell me.”

“That last category is too broad.”

“It is tailored.”

I sighed.

“One day.”

“One.”

“That is shorter than last time.”

“Yes.”

“Because today was cleaner?”

“In part.”

“What is the other part?”

“You tolerated the previous interval without delayed symptom.”

“That sounds like progress.”

“It is information.”

“You have an allergy to the word progress.”

“I have an allergy to you using it as permission.”

“I can progress without permission.”

“Not in here.”

That was fair enough to be irritating.

I rubbed my thumb against the crutch grip, then realized I was checking it.

Normal.

Hessa noticed.

“Symptom?”

“No. Checking.”

“Stop checking.”

“That seems medically irresponsible.”

“Checking once because something feels wrong is useful. Checking every thirty seconds because you want certainty creates noise.”

I lowered my hand.

“You cannot possibly know I check every thirty seconds.”

“I know you.”

“That is not measurement.”

“No.”

She seemed completely comfortable with this.

“And then?”

“Assessment.”

“Not external effect.”

“I did not say that.”

I went still.

Hessa's expression changed immediately.

Not alarm.

Annoyance.

“Do not do that.”

“Do what?”

“Turn an omitted answer into permission.”

“I did not.”

“You absolutely did.”

“I observed that you did not exclude it.”

“That is not clearance.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“This is humiliating.”

“Good.”

I hated that word.

“External-effect testing is not cleared.”

“Or scheduled.”

“Or scheduled.”

“Casting?”

“Not cleared.”

“Barrier?”

“Not cleared.”

“Left shaping?”

“Excluded.”

“Independent draw?”

“Not allowed.”

“Independent shaping?”

“Not allowed.”

She nodded.

“Now you may be pleased.”

“I was already pleased.”

“That was the problem.”

She wiped the charcoal from my arm.

I watched the black line smear, then disappear under the damp cloth.

“Can I ask one question?”

“You have asked approximately forty.”

“One more.”

“You will ask it regardless.”

“Probably.”

She put the cloth down.

“What would make you decide not to test external effect?”

Her expression changed.

Small.

More serious.

“Several things.”

“Such as?”

“A delayed symptom.”

“Obvious.”

“A repeat control failure that looks meaningfully different from ordinary inconsistency.”

“Meaningfully how?”

“I will decide.”

I disliked that.

“Motor recruitment again?”

“Yes.”

“Broad familiar pattern?”

“Yes.”

“Loss of ability to release?”

“Yes.”

That one landed differently.

I had not lost release.

Not once.

Still.

“What else?”

“If I decide we do not yet understand enough about what you are doing to make an external question worth the added uncertainty.”

“External effect is added uncertainty.”

“Of course.”

“But isn't that the point?”

“No.”

I frowned.

“The point is to ask a question we can interpret.”

That was annoyingly good.

“If something happens externally but we don't know what internal change caused it, we learn less.”

“Yes.”

“If nothing happens externally, we may also learn less.”

“Depending on the test.”

“So you need internal control good enough that external failure or success means something.”

Hessa's mouth tightened.

I had gone too far.

Not wrong.

Too far.

“Approximately,” she said.

I wanted the rest.

I wanted the apparatus.

I wanted the exact threshold.

I wanted to know whether tomorrow's living day was the last ordinary day before something new.

No.

That was not available.

I put my sleeve down.

Hessa watched me do it.

“You can go.”

“That was abrupt.”

“The appointment is over.”

“I am accustomed to more resistance.”

“To leaving?”

“Yes.”

“That explains much.”

I stood.

Crutches.

Right hand normal on the grip.

Left.

Right foot.

No dizziness.

No symptom.

At the door I stopped.

Hessa sighed before I turned.

“I have not asked yet.”

“You stopped walking.”

“That is not a question.”

“It is with you.”

Fair.

“What should I do today?”

She stared.

Then pointed toward the door.

“Live.”

“That instruction remains poorly specified.”

“Excellent.”

Outside, Carrow was offensively normal.

A cart had lost part of a load of onions near the corner.

Three people were collecting them while a fourth argued about whose fault it was.

A dog had acquired one onion and seemed disappointed.

I stood there longer than necessary.

One living day.

Then Hessa.

Maybe external effect.

Not scheduled.

Not cleared.

Maybe another control test.

Maybe draw only.

Maybe nothing.

I could build a decision tree.

I did not have the notebook open.

That was the only thing preventing it.

I started toward the Guild.

Not theatre.

Not because I had decided against theatre.

It was morning.

Rehearsal, if they were rehearsing, would be later.

Pessa might be working.

Lyssa might be working.

Jorren was certainly doing something he would refuse to explain properly.

The Guild would contain work I could accept or decline.

Food.

People.

Nothing.

Options.

My right forearm felt ordinary beneath the sleeve.

That was becoming an achievement I did not know how to value.

At the Guild entrance, I paused.

A posting board had three new slips.

Someone I knew only by face was arguing with a clerk about a route.

Two people came out carrying packs.

The day was already happening.

Tomorrow had a rule.

Today did not.

I went inside.

For once, that was enough.
