TEXT READER · CHAPTER 121

THE REGULAR

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I did not wake thinking about external effect.

This was technically true.

I woke because the room was hot.

Then I thought about external effect.

Progress.

Probably.

No symptoms.

Right thumb normal.

Fingers normal.

Forearm normal.

Residual limb baseline.

No unusual phantom change.

Hessa tomorrow morning.

Today no magic.

Fine.

I went downstairs in the blue shirt.

The keeper looked up.

“Not theatre shirt.”

“I have more than one shirt.”

“Suspicious.”

“I am going to the theatre.”

She looked at the blue shirt.

“Then the system has failed.”

“It was never a system.”

“Good.”

I hated that word.

I ate.

Then did not leave immediately.

That mattered less than I wanted it to.

I sat downstairs another ten minutes because breakfast was still settling and my shoulders were mildly tired from yesterday's Pessa session.

Not injured.

Just present.

Then I went west.

Nobody had asked me to.

Again.

The theatre doors were open.

Again.

Inside, someone was sweeping the aisle.

Again.

The patched curtain was still ugly.

Good.

The painted doorway had acquired a brace.

Better.

Teren was in his chair.

He saw me before I reached the stage.

“Blue.”

“What?”

“Shirt.”

“Yes.”

“Thought you were green.”

“I own multiple.”

“Show-off.”

He looked back at his pages.

That was apparently greeting.

The servant actor crossed the stage carrying the cup.

He did not drop it.

I had stopped expecting this.

Mostly.

The unnamed rehearsal woman was standing near the front row while the actual magistrate argued with the merchant about a line.

Not acting.

Argument.

“You said ‘my wife.’”

“It says ‘the wife.’”

“It said ‘my wife’ yesterday.”

“Yesterday you were wrong.”

“I was right enough that nobody stopped me.”

“That is not how right works.”

“Apparently it is here.”

I liked him more for this.

The woman saw me.

“Gregory.”

There.

No question.

No surprise.

Just name.

“Yes.”

“Ramp.”

“I know.”

She turned back to the argument.

Progress apparently included being ordered toward existing infrastructure without eye contact.

I went around.

At the top of the loading ramp, two flats leaned against the wall and someone had left a small wooden box directly where my right crutch needed to land.

I stopped.

Looked at it.

The soot-shirted woman came through carrying a bundle of cloth.

She looked at the box.

Then at me.

“Kick it.”

“I am not kicking anything.”

“Then move it.”

“With what?”

She looked at both crutches.

“Fair.”

She hooked it with her boot and dragged it aside.

“Thanks.”

“Don't make it a habit.”

She kept walking.

No accommodation speech.

Good.

At stage level I moved toward the chair I had used before.

Teren looked at me.

“No.”

I stopped.

“What?”

“Not that one.”

“That is not your chair.”

“I know.”

He pointed at a different chair farther down.

“That one.”

“Why?”

“Because if you sit there, I can't see around you.”

“You could move.”

“So can you.”

Fair.

I moved.

There.

Tiny thing.

Not invitation.

Placement.

Because my being there had become predictable enough to affect sightlines.

I sat.

The chair was worse than the one I had used before.

Lower.

Harder.

One front leg shorter.

I shifted until it stopped rocking.

Teren heard it.

“Folded paper under left front.”

I looked down.

There was already folded paper under the right front.

“That seems inconsistent.”

“Different floor.”

“Same chair?”

“No.”

Of course.

I tore a scrap from an abandoned blank margin page on the small table beside me.

Teren looked over.

“Not that.”

I stopped.

“What?”

“That page is today's cut list.”

It appeared blank.

I held it up.

“Where?”

“Other side.”

I turned it.

CUT:
SECOND MERCHANT ENTRANCE
WIFE CROSS
WINDOW JOKE?
KEEP CUP

I stared at KEEP CUP.

Theatre priorities.

I put it back.

Teren handed me an actual scrap.

“Use this.”

“Thank you.”

“Don't get emotional.”

I folded it twice and put it under the left front leg.

Chair stabilized.

Useful theatre work.

No applause.

The scene began.

Not mine.

Not the magistrate scene.

Different section of the same play.

The wife was arguing with the merchant.

The servant entered halfway through.

Teren followed the pages.

I watched.

The wife lost a line.

She stopped.

Not long.

Teren waited.

Then prompted two words.

She continued.

I looked at his hand.

No tension.

Wrong actor.

Different habit.

Good.

Not rule.

The woman stopped them.

“Again from the entrance.”

They reset.

Teren held out a second stack of pages without looking at me.

I stared at them.

He wiggled the pages.

“What?”

“Take.”

“Why?”

“Because my hand is full.”

It was.

Food.

Again.

I took the pages.

He continued eating.

I looked at them.

Same scene.

Different markings.

“Whose?”

“Mine.”

“Then why do I have them?”

“Because I gave them to you.”

“That is not a reason.”

“Hold page twelve open.”

I did.

He leaned over, compared something on his current pages with mine, and marked a line.

Then took them back.

That was all.

I had contributed approximately one functioning wrist.

Useful.

The scene restarted.

I stayed.

For a while nobody asked anything else of me.

That was comfortable now.

Dangerous.

Not dangerous.

Comfortable.

Words had meanings.

A worker passed behind my chair carrying a narrow painted column.

“Gregory, foot.”

I looked down.

Right foot was half in his path.

I moved it.

He passed.

No thanks.

No apology.

Good.

Ten minutes later another worker said:

“Crutch.”

My left crutch was angled too far into the wing.

I pulled it closer.

Again, no discussion.

They had learned that I occupied space.

I had learned that they would tell me when the space mattered.

This seemed healthier than guessing.

Do not make rule.

Specific room.

Specific people.

Still.

It was pleasant.

The stage manager, if that was what the rehearsal woman was, was arguing with the merchant actor about whether a joke had to happen before or after the doorway closed.

I did not know her job title.

I had assumed director.

Maybe.

Nobody had introduced titles.

People simply did things until it became obvious that certain arguments belonged to certain people.

Teren prompted.

Soot-shirt woman repaired.

Servant acted.

Merchant complained.

The unnamed woman stopped everyone.

I sat.

At some point this had become enough information to navigate the room without knowing what anyone was called.

That was unusual for me.

Usually names came before categories.

Here categories were arriving first and staying incomplete.

The servant actor finished a run, came offstage, and stopped near my chair.

“You're back.”

“Yes.”

“You miss us?”

“No.”

“Liar.”

“I missed the cup.”

He looked at the cup in his hand.

“Cup misses you.”

“That is concerning.”

“Deeply.”

The woman called him.

He went back.

No one introduced us.

No need.

We knew each other enough to be annoying.

That was apparently a category.

The next scene involved a letter.

Not Teren's pages.

An actual prop letter.

The servant was supposed to hand it to the wife, who was supposed to read part of it.

Except the letter had disappeared.

“Where is it?” the woman asked.

Someone backstage said, “Which one?”

“The red seal.”

“There are four.”

“The dead uncle.”

“That narrows nothing.”

The merchant actor sat down on the edge of the stage.

The wife looked at the ceiling.

Teren turned two pages backward.

Nobody moved with urgency.

The theatre had several grades of emergency now.

This was low.

The soot-shirted woman appeared with two letters.

“Red seal.”

“Both red,” the rehearsal woman said.

“One is merchant.”

“Which?”

“I don't know. Read them.”

She handed one to Teren and one to me.

Me.

No ceremony.

I looked at the folded paper.

“Am I allowed?”

“No,” Teren said.

The soot-shirted woman said, “Yes.”

The rehearsal woman said, “Open it.”

Good workplace.

I opened mine.

Inside:

MY DEAREST ALINA,

I stopped.

Not Lyssa.

Different name.

No issue.

The text continued with enough melodrama to kill livestock.

I read silently.

This was clearly not the dead uncle.

“Merchant,” I said.

“How do you know?” Teren asked.

“It begins ‘My dearest Alina’ and then apologizes for buying a horse without asking.”

The merchant actor raised one hand.

“Mine.”

The wife looked at him.

“You buy a horse?”

“In the play.”

“Coward.”

Teren opened his.

“Dead uncle.”

The correct letter went to the wife.

The soot-shirted woman took mine back.

“Thanks, Gregory.”

There.

Name again.

From someone else.

Small.

I noticed.

Did not make it important.

Probably.

Rehearsal resumed.

The wife read the letter.

Stopped.

“Prompt,” she said.

Teren did.

She continued.

Then the servant entered early.

The woman stopped them.

Again.

Then again.

Then the letter tore.

Actual tear.

Everyone groaned.

“Not another one,” someone said.

The soot-shirted woman took the pieces.

“Can it be taped?”

“With what?”

“Hope.”

“Excellent.”

She left.

The wife sat.

The servant leaned against the wall.

The rehearsal woman looked around.

“Five minutes.”

Nobody moved much.

Teren stretched.

The servant came back toward me.

“Three lines today?”

“No.”

“Disappointed?”

“No.”

He sat on the floor near my chair.

“Liar.”

“You are overusing that.”

“It keeps working.”

“I have not lied yet.”

He looked up.

“You said you didn't miss us.”

“I said I missed the cup.”

“That is worse.”

Maybe.

Teren said, “Gregory.”

I looked.

He tossed me a folded page.

I caught it badly.

Not dropped.

Close.

“Why?”

“Read the merchant lines.”

“Why?”

“He went to piss.”

The servant actor laughed.

I looked toward the stage.

The merchant was gone.

The wife was still sitting.

The woman was talking to the soot-shirted worker about the torn letter.

“Teren.”

“What?”

“Are we rehearsing?”

“Soon.”

“You want me to read?”

“Yes.”

“From here?”

“Unless you want to stand.”

I did not.

“Here.”

“Fine.”

The servant actor stood.

“Come on, Gregory Fire Boy.”

“No.”

“Gregory Blue Shirt?”

“Worse.”

He went back to his mark.

The wife stood.

Teren pointed at my page.

“Start at the mark.”

I found it.

Merchant line.

Not acting.

Reading.

Fine.

The woman turned.

Saw what was happening.

Did not stop it.

That mattered a little.

“Go,” she said.

The wife began.

I followed.

When my line came, I read from the chair.

“Your concern for my affairs is touching.”

Flat.

Probably.

The wife answered.

The servant entered.

I looked down.

Next line.

“I have no idea what you are implying.”

The servant said:

“You rarely do.”

Not on page.

I looked up.

He smiled.

Character?

Him?

Unknown.

The wife continued.

I found the next line late.

Teren prompted me.

Me.

“Then perhaps...”

I picked it up.

“Then perhaps you should speak plainly.”

We continued.

No one stopped.

At one point the wife crossed closer to me than expected.

I stopped reading long enough to make sure she was not about to collide with the front of my crutch.

She wasn't.

Teren prompted the first word I missed.

I resumed.

No one commented on the pause.

That was different from being ignored.

They had noticed what mattered to the scene and let the rest pass.

I thought.

Maybe.

No table.

At the end, the woman said:

“Again. Gregory, louder.”

“That is direction.”

“Yes.”

“I was reading.”

“You are still too quiet.”

Practical.

Fine.

Again.

Louder.

Not shouting.

The servant changed something.

The wife changed something.

I missed a line because I was watching them.

Teren prompted.

I continued.

Again.

Third run.

The actual merchant returned midway through.

He stood in the wing and watched me reading his part.

I saw him.

Lost my place.

Teren prompted.

The merchant said:

“Terrible.”

I looked at him.

“You were absent.”

“I had reasons.”

“So did I.”

He laughed.

The woman pointed.

“Finish.”

I did.

At the end she waved the merchant back in.

“Gregory, done.”

Done.

No ceremony.

I handed Teren the page.

The merchant took his place.

I sat back.

The servant passed me and said quietly:

“Louder was better.”

“Why?”

He shrugged.

“I could hear you.”

Good.

Actual measurable criterion.

Excellent.

The scene resumed without me.

Again.

There it was.

I was useful until I was not.

Then not.

No injury.

No insult.

No promotion.

I liked that more than I should have.

Five minutes became thirty.

Then an hour.

The theatre kept discovering small things wrong with itself.

A chair used in the second act had a loose back.

A coat button had gone missing.

One fake book was accidentally a real account ledger and had to be returned to someone furious.

The repaired doorway brace held.

The curtain patch sagged slightly at one corner.

Soot-shirt woman saw it.

Swore.

Climbed three steps of a ladder.

Swore again.

Came down.

Got different thread.

No one asked whether the fire had traumatized the curtain.

They fixed the curtain.

I liked that.

No.

I liked watching competent people deal with material facts without turning every problem into identity.

That sentence was too large.

The curtain sagged.

She fixed it.

Enough.

Teren at some point handed me a cup without looking.

I took it.

Drank.

Immediately realized it was not mine.

“What is this?”

“Water.”

“Whose?”

“Was mine.”

“You gave it to me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“You looked thirsty.”

I stared.

He looked back at the stage.

No ceremony.

I drank the rest.

Apparently this place occasionally practiced kindness under hostile naming conventions.

The repaired letter came back with a strip of cloth glued behind the tear.

Ugly.

Functional.

The wife complained that it would not fold correctly.

The soot-shirted woman told her not to tear it correctly next time.

Teren ate something else.

The servant dropped the cup.

Finally.

It hit the floor, bounced once, and rolled under the table.

I laughed before I could stop.

Everyone looked at me.

The servant pointed.

“See?”

“What?”

“He came for that.”

“I did not.”

“You admitted it.”

Earlier.

Fuck.

The rehearsal woman looked at me.

“You've been waiting for him to drop the cup?”

“No.”

Teren said, “Yes.”

Traitor.

The servant bowed.

“Worth the journey.”

I hated all of them.

Rehearsal continued.

The afternoon moved.

At some point my palms warmed from the crutches even though I had mostly been sitting. Walking there had been enough to register.

Not a problem.

Right calf fine.

Residual limb quiet.

No thumb.

No magic.

Tomorrow Hessa.

I did not think about that for almost twenty minutes.

That might have been the longest stretch all day.

When rehearsal broke, Teren collected his pages.

The soot-shirted woman took the repaired letter.

The servant actor took the cup and deliberately dropped it again near me.

I stared.

“Cheap.”

He picked it up.

“Still laughed.”

“I did not.”

“You did inside.”

“That is not observable.”

He looked at my face.

Apparently my face had become evidence everywhere.

The rehearsal woman walked past.

“Tomorrow?”

I looked at her.

“Hessa morning.”

She kept walking.

“I didn't ask about morning.”

“I know.”

“Then?”

“I don't know.”

“Fine.”

Not good.

Fine.

Interesting.

No.

Do not categorize.

I left by the ramp.

Outside, I realized something small had changed.

Not that they needed me.

They did not.

Not that I belonged.

Too large.

They had stopped asking why I was there.

That was all.

I walked home.

The keeper looked at my blue shirt.

“Theatre.”

“Yes.”

She nodded.

System restored.

I went upstairs.

Tomorrow morning belonged to Hessa.

Today had mostly belonged to a room where nobody had asked why I kept coming back.

That was not the same as being wanted.

It was not nothing either.

I did not need to decide which.

Probably.
