LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 133

THE OTHER WAY

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Hessa had removed the charcoal. Again. I liked that. Which probably meant I had developed opinions about blank forearms. Not ideal.

“Sit.”

I sat. Morning baseline was boring. Excellent. Hands normal. Right calf normal. Residual limb quiet. Phantom foot present in the vague way it usually was when I thought about it and less present when I did not.

No thumb. No fingers trying to participate. No unusual cold. No pain. Hessa checked anyway. She always checked anyway. Then she put the folded white towel beneath my right forearm. Copper sat farther up the table.

Not near enough to be interesting. I looked around. No threads. Still.

“Disappointed?” Hessa asked.

“No.”

“You looked.”

“I have eyes.”

“Tragic.”

I rested my arm. The outer forearm was unmarked. No charcoal target. No line. No point. Nothing to accidentally obey. Good. Hessa sat across from me with her notes. She had rewritten something. I could tell because one strip of paper had been pasted over another.

“What did you change?”

“My wording.”

“Why?”

“Because I wrote that you directed the edge wristward.”

“That is what happened.”

“That is what you reported.”

I looked at her. She looked back.

“Fine.”

“Good.”

“I hate your notes.”

“They are not for your emotional comfort.”

“I know.”

She put the pen down.

“Last time I told you the direction before shaping.”

“Yes.”

“You reported a small asymmetry toward your wrist.”

“Yes.”

“Today I want the same sequence with one change.”

“Elbowward.”

She stared at me.

“That was my guess.”

“Stop guessing.”

“You said one change.”

“I did.”

“And opposite direction is the cleanest.”

“Probably.”

I smiled. She did not.

“Your confidence is unattractive.”

“That seems unrelated.”

“It isn't.”

I stopped smiling. Mostly. Hessa touched two fingers lightly to the table near my elbow. Not my arm. Just direction.

“Elbowward.”

“Yes.”

“Not toward my fingers.”

I frowned.

“You are indicating with your fingers.”

“I am indicating a direction. If I move my hand, the direction does not move with it.”

“Good.”

“You understand?”

“Yes.”

“Say it.”

“Toward my elbow. Not toward your hand.”

She moved her hand away. Good. No target. No destination. Only direction. That was cleaner. Probably. Hessa watched me.

“One draw.”

“One shape.”

“One directional instruction.”

“One RETURN.”

“One release.”

“Yes.”

“No external question.”

“Yes.”

“No attempt to make the effect larger because you think a larger effect would be easier to report.”

“I was not going to.”

“You thought it.”

“I think many things.”

“That is the problem.”

I breathed. Not special breathing. Just enough to stop talking.

“Draw.”

I reached. Carefully. The draw came more easily than it had weeks ago. That did not mean easy. It meant familiar. A narrow opening. A small pull. Mana. Contained. No broad flood. No rush into the left.

No fingers. No thumb. No urge to move my hand. I held. Hessa watched my face more than my arm.

“Stable?”

“Yes.”

“Anything else?”

“No.”

“Hold.”

I held. The temptation was to prepare for the next instruction. I noticed that. Then I noticed that noticing it was also preparation. Annoying.

“Shape.”

Neutral region. Outer right forearm. Small. Localized. Not direction yet. That mattered. I let the familiar alteration form. Not a point. Not a patch exactly. An area with an edge I could feel more clearly than I could describe.

The center changed first. Then the boundary became legible. Not visible. Mine. Internal.

“Report.”

“Localized.”

“Size?”

“Small.”

“That is not size.”

“About the same as last time.”

“Comparison is not size.”

I almost opened my eyes. They were already open. I had been staring at the wall.

“Two fingers across. Maybe less.”

“Shape?”

“Uneven oval.”

“Already directional?”

I waited. Checked. Not pushed. Checked.

“No.”

“Certain?”

“No.”

“Useful.”

She wrote. I hated that too.

“Hold.”

I held. The edge stayed. No broadening. No elbow pull. No wrist pull. Nothing external. Copper remained copper. Wonderful object. Hessa waited long enough that I started wondering whether she had forgotten the next instruction.

She had not.

“Smallest directional change toward your elbow.”

I did not move. That was the first thing. No hand. No wrist. No fingers. No shoulder. Inside. Elbowward. The previous wristward attempt wanted to become relevant. I tried not to use it. Impossible.

Memory existed. Fine. I did not reverse it. There was nothing to reverse. This was a new shape. Elbowward. For several breaths nothing happened. Or nothing I could distinguish happened. I felt the center.

The wrist-side edge. The elbow-side edge. The elbow-side edge seemed softer than the wrist side. Wrong. Maybe. I waited. Hessa said nothing. I wanted to fix it. That was dangerous wording. Not fix. Observe.

Then the elbow-side boundary sharpened. A little. I thought. The wrist side stayed where it was. The center stayed. Elbow side clearer. Not farther. Clearer. Maybe.

“Report.”

“Elbow-side edge changed.”

“How?”

“Sharper.”

“Extension?”

“I don't know.”

“Other edges?”

“Wrist side same.”

“Center?”

“Same.”

“Anything else?”

“No.”

“Motor?”

“No.”

“Force?”

“No.”

“Broadening?”

“No.”

“Left?”

“No.”

“Hold.”

I held. The sharpened edge remained. Or my expectation of it remained. That was the problem. I knew what I was supposed to find. Hessa knew what I was supposed to find. The arm did not come with a second observer.

“Still?”

“Yes.”

“Same?”

“I think.”

“Think or know?”

“Think.”

“Good.”

She waited.

Then:

“RETURN.”

I reduced. The elbow-side distinction softened first. Then the rest. The localized alteration diminished without sliding. No familiar broad response. No hand urge. No thumb. No fingers. No wrist. No elbow movement. Underlying draw stayed.

“Report.”

“Reduced.”

“Gone?”

I checked.

“Maybe a little center.”

“Hold.”

I held. It faded.

“Now?”

“Gone.”

“Draw?”

“Still.”

“Release.”

I released. The draw closed. Nothing chased it. Nothing jumped left. Nothing moved outside me. Hessa waited. Then checked my hand. Forearm. Wrist. Thumb. Asked about fingers. Asked about phantom foot. Asked about the left.

All boring. Excellent. She sat back. I looked at the copper. It had contributed nothing. Also excellent.

“Well?”

“No.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is my answer to the answer you want.”

“What answer do I want?”

“That you can direct it.”

“I would accept that.”

“You would enjoy it.”

“Yes.”

“That is why I am saying no.”

I leaned back.

“So what happened?”

“You reported a small asymmetry toward the instructed elbow side under a direction-first condition with no prepared destination.”

“Yes.”

“That is the second time your report has favored the instructed side.”

“Opposite sides.”

“Yes.”

“So.”

“So my confidence increases.”

I waited. She did not continue.

“How much?”

“More than last time.”

“That is not a number.”

“I did that intentionally.”

“Can I direct it?”

“I am not writing that.”

“Can I probably direct it?”

“I am not writing that either.”

“What are you writing?”

She turned the page enough that I could read the line. REPORTED ASYMMETRY FOLLOWED OPPOSITE INSTRUCTED DIRECTION UNDER OTHERWISE SIMILAR DIRECTION-FIRST CONDITION.

Below it:

NO INDEPENDENT CONFIRMATION. I read it twice.

“That is obnoxious.”

“It is accurate.”

“Yes.”

“That is usually why you dislike things.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

I looked at my arm. Nothing visible. Nothing externally measurable. Two reports. Two directions. Same problem. But not the same problem as before. That mattered. I wanted to do it again. Of course.

“Next time wrist again?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I have not decided.”

“We could alternate.”

“We could do many stupid things in orderly patterns.”

“That is unfair.”

“It is extremely fair.”

She closed the notes.

“One full no-magic day.”

I stared.

“Again?”

“Yes.”

“I am fine.”

“Yes.”

“That argument should eventually help me.”

“It does.”

“How?”

“You get one day instead of several.”

Annoying. Fair.

“Then next assessment?”

“Yes.”

“Direction?”

“Maybe.”

I narrowed my eyes. She noticed.

“What?”

“Everyone says maybe now.”

“Perhaps you are contagious.”

“That is worse.”

Hessa stood. Assessment over. I rolled my sleeve down. My forearm felt normal. My whole body felt normal. That should have been satisfying. It was. Also I wanted a third direction. There were not many useful directions on a forearm.

Wrist. Elbow. Up. Down. Inward. Outward. That was already too much thinking. Hessa looked at me. I stopped.

“You are doing it.”

“I am putting on my sleeve.”

“You are designing.”

“I can do both.”

“No.”

I finished the sleeve.

“Tomorrow is not an unrecorded assessment.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“Tomorrow is not an unrecorded assessment.”

“Good.”

I got my crutches.

At the door she said:

“Gregory.”

I turned.

“If you go to the theatre, do not decide that acting counts as neurological training.”

I stared at her.

“What?”

“The herb seller.”

“Oh, fuck all of you.”

Hessa smiled. Barely. I left. The bread seller was outside. I crossed the street before he could speak. He shouted anyway.

“LANDLORD.”

I kept going.

“MESSENGER.”

Better. I raised one crutch without turning. Probably rude. He laughed. I had no magic tomorrow. I had Hessa again after that. And I had two opposite directional reports that were not proof. Good.

I could live with that. For one day. Probably. I turned toward the theatre without deciding to. That was becoming a lie I told myself. Fine. I decided. The theatre doors were open. I heard voices before I reached them.

Not rehearsal voices. Argument voices. Better. Inside, the soot-shirt worker was standing onstage with a sword. Not a real sword. A prop. Long. Silver-painted. Ridiculous crossguard.

A red stone in the pommel large enough to purchase a small house if it had been real. The servant actor stood three paces away with both hands spread.

“It sounds stupid.”

“It is a sword,” the soot-shirt worker said.

“It is a legendary sword.”

“It is wood.”

“Onstage it is legendary.”

The recurring woman sat on the edge of the stage. Teren stood below with pages. Nobody noticed me for several seconds. I liked that. The servant actor pointed at the sword.

“It cannot sound like Teren.”

Teren looked offended.

“I do not sound like that.”

“You do.”

“I absolutely do not.”

The soot-shirt worker saw me. Her face changed. Danger.

“No.”

I said it immediately. She held the sword out.

“Gregory.”

“No.”

The recurring woman turned.

“Landlord.”

“No.”

Teren looked at his pages. Then at me. Then at the sword. I should have left. I did not.

“What?” I asked.

The servant actor grinned.

“The sword talks.”

I looked at the prop. Of course it did.

“Why?”

Everyone answered at once.

I caught:

“curse”

“king”

“third act”

“because it's funny”

and from Teren:

“Page forty-two.”

He held out the pages. I did not take them.

“What happened to whoever talks for it?”

“Nothing,” Teren said.

“He is standing right there,” the recurring woman said.

The servant actor pointed at himself.

“I hate doing both.”

“You have three lines,” Teren said.

“I hate all three.”

The soot-shirt worker extended the sword another inch toward me.

“Voice it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“I am not a sword.”

The recurring woman smiled.

“You were a person.”

“That was broader.”

The servant actor laughed.

Teren said:

“Gregory.”

I looked at him.

“Read the lines.”

“From here?”

“From wherever.”

That was suspiciously permissive. I took the pages. Page forty-two. Three sword lines. Not many.

The first was:

YOU HAVE DRAWN ME, LITTLE KING. TRY NOT TO EMBARRASS US BOTH. I read it silently. Then again. The line was stupid. I liked it. No. The servant actor climbed onto the stage. He took the sword from the worker.

Held it in front of him.

“Start before.”

Teren pointed. The recurring woman stood. Another actor moved into place. Apparently we were doing this. I stayed below the stage. Seated would have been smarter. There was a chair. I took it. Good.

Teren started them. The servant actor crossed. The recurring woman said something about the sealed tomb. The servant actor drew the sword. Held it up. Everyone looked at me. I looked at the line.

“You have drawn me, little king. Try not to embarrass us both.”

Silence. The servant actor lowered the sword.

“That is Greg.”

“Yes,” I said.

“The sword cannot be Greg.”

“Then use someone else.”

The soot-shirt worker said:

“Again.”

I looked at her. She shrugged. Apparently everyone had learned Teren's word. The servant actor reset. They started again. Sword came out. I tried lower.

“You have drawn me, little king. Try not to embarrass us both.”

The recurring woman made a face.

“Now it is Greg pretending to be old.”

“I am old.”

Everyone looked at me. Fuck.

“Older.”

The servant actor laughed. Teren did not. He was listening.

“Again.”

Third time. I stared at the sword. Wood. Silver paint. Ridiculous red stone. Legendary. Supposedly. What did legendary sound like? Wrong question. What did this sword want? Also probably wrong. I had three lines.

The first insulted a king. Not loudly. Not angrily. It expected to be obeyed. I hated that I knew the difference.

“Again,” I said.

Teren looked at me.

Then:

“Again.”

The actors reset. The servant actor drew the sword. I waited half a beat longer than before. Not much. Enough that everyone was looking at the blade.

Then I said:

“You have drawn me, little king. Try not to embarrass us both.”

Different. The servant actor smiled. Not as himself. As the idiot king. The recurring woman looked at the sword. Not me. That was interesting. Teren tapped the page.

“Continue.”

I had forgotten there were more lines. Of course.

The king said:

“You know me?”

Sword:

“I knew better kings.”

I said it. Too fast.

Teren:

“Again from sword.”

“I knew better kings.”

The servant actor flinched. Small. Funny. The recurring woman laughed. Not audience laugh. Actor laugh. She covered it badly. The servant actor pointed the sword toward me.

“Fuck you.”

“That is not in the play.”

“It should be.”

The soot-shirt worker said:

“Keep it.”

Teren said:

“No.”

Everyone argued. I looked down at the third line. The sword apparently had opinions about doors. Of course. I wanted to read it. That was the problem. Not a large problem. A very small one.

Three lines. Wooden sword. No magic. No role. No casting. Nothing. I looked at Teren.

“Again?”

He smiled.

“Page forty-one.”

That was more than three lines. I opened to forty-one. The sword had been talking before it was drawn. From inside the scabbard. Ridiculous. Excellent. There were seven more lines before page forty-two. One complained about darkness.

One told the king his grip was embarrassing. One refused to answer a question because the king had asked it badly. I looked at Teren.

“Who wrote this?”

“Why?”

“It is rude.”

“Yes.”

“Constantly.”

“Yes.”

The servant actor said:

“You like it.”

“No.”

“You moved your chair.”

I looked down. I had. The recurring woman leaned toward the sword.

“Ask it something.”

“That is not how scripts work,” Teren said.

She ignored him.

“Oh, great blade, should Gregory be allowed near a stage?”

I looked at the sword. Then at her. Then back at the line in front of me. There was no answer for that. Good. I lowered my voice slightly.

“No.”

The servant actor folded over laughing. The recurring woman pointed at me.

“That one stays.”

Teren said:

“It absolutely does not.”

The soot-shirt worker said:

“Again.”

I was starting to understand how nothing ever got finished here. I moved the chair closer. Not onto the stage. Just closer. Tomorrow was no magic. Fine. Apparently I was a sword.
