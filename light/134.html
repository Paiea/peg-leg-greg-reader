LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 134

THE VOICE

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

I was not going to the theatre for the sword. I was going because I went to the theatre. The sword happened to be there. This distinction mattered. Probably. No magic today. Hessa had made that very clear.

No draw. No shaping. No directional practice. No sitting at a table pretending I was merely thinking about direction. Fine. I ate breakfast.

The keeper said:

“Sword.”

I stopped.

“No.”

She looked pleased.

“How?”

“Bread man.”

“I am killing him.”

“You keep saying that.”

“One day.”

“Pay your rent first.”

“I am not a landlord.”

“Apparently sword now.”

I left. The bread seller saw me. Raised both hands. I crossed the street.

He shouted:

“BLADE.”

Worse. I kept going. The theatre doors were open. Inside, the sword was lying on a chair. Alone. That was offensive. It looked even more ridiculous without someone holding it. Wood. Silver paint. Huge red stone.

Crossguard like two knives trying to escape sideways. Legendary. Supposedly. I stopped beside it. The soot-shirt worker was crouched behind a painted wall tightening something.

Without looking:

“Don't touch.”

“I wasn't.”

“You leaned.”

Everyone had learned this.

“I was looking.”

“More dangerous.”

I sat in the chair beside the sword. Not its chair. Separate chair. Boundaries. Teren was sorting pages. The servant actor was onstage with the recurring woman and two others. Different scene. No sword.

Good. I watched. For maybe twelve minutes. Not waiting. Then the servant actor came offstage. Saw me. Saw the sword. Smiled.

“No.”

“You came for it.”

“No.”

“You sat beside it.”

“There were chairs.”

“There are many chairs.”

“This one had sightline.”

Teren looked up.

“No.”

“Fine.”

The servant actor picked up the sword. Held it vertically. The fake red stone caught light.

“Say something.”

“No.”

“Blade commands.”

“No.”

He pointed the sword at me.

“Speak.”

I looked at Teren.

Teren said:

“Page forty-one.”

Traitor. I took the pages.

The sword's first line from inside the scabbard:

IF YOU DROP ME AGAIN, I WILL ARRANGE FOR YOUR HEIRS TO BE STUPIDER THAN YOU. I read it silently. Good. Very good. The servant actor moved into position. He was playing the little king.

Not actually little. Younger than the recurring woman. Shorter than me. Most people were. He slid the sword halfway from the scabbard.

Teren said:

“From knock.”

The recurring woman knocked on an imaginary door. The king ignored it. Sword from inside scabbard.

I read:

“If you drop me again, I will arrange for your heirs to be stupider than you.”

Too normal. The servant actor looked at me.

“Greg.”

“Yes.”

“That is just you insulting me.”

“The line insults you.”

“The sword insults me.”

“That is what I did.”

“No.”

He put the sword back.

“Again.”

Teren did not say it. Interesting. They started. I tried deeper.

“If you drop me again, I will arrange for your heirs to be stupider than you.”

The servant actor stopped.

“No.”

“What?”

“I can't understand you.”

“I spoke clearly.”

“You spoke from a grave.”

“That seems appropriate.”

“Why?”

“Ancient sword.”

He held up the wood.

“It has been in a box.”

“Tomb.”

“Box.”

“Tomb in the play.”

“Box backstage.”

The recurring woman said:

“You sound constipated.”

I looked at her.

“Thank you.”

“Again.”

Third attempt. I removed some depth. Kept weight. Slower.

“If you drop me again, I will arrange for your heirs to be stupider than you.”

The servant actor waited.

Then:

“You took so long I forgot I dropped it.”

“That was one sentence.”

“It lasted a winter.”

The recurring woman nodded.

“Too grand.”

I looked at Teren.

He said:

“Again.”

Practical. Excellent. Fourth. No deep voice. No old voice. Less slow. But I tried to make every word precise.

“If you drop me again, I will arrange for your heirs to be stupider than you.”

The servant actor smiled. Then shrugged.

“Better.”

The recurring woman said:

“I looked at Gregory.”

Damn.

“Why?”

“You were doing the line.”

“The sword was doing the line.”

“No. You were.”

“How can you tell?”

She shrugged.

“I looked at you.”

That was not useful. It was extremely useful. I hated it.

“What changed last time?” I asked.

“Which?”

“Yesterday. When you looked at the sword.”

She thought.

“You waited.”

“That is timing.”

“Yes.”

“What else?”

“Nothing.”

“That cannot be true.”

“It can.”

The servant actor lifted the sword.

“You stopped trying so hard.”

“That is not measurable.”

“Neither is legendary.”

I looked at the sword. He looked at me.

“Again?”

“Yes.”

Good. We reset. Knock. King ignores it. Sword. I waited. Not long. Enough for the servant actor to glance down at the scabbard.

Then:

“If you drop me again, I will arrange for your heirs to be stupider than you.”

The servant actor's eyes stayed on the sword. Recurring woman watched the sword too. Better. But the line was less funny. Nobody laughed.

The servant actor continued:

“You cannot do that.”

Sword:

“I can barely imagine how I would make them worse, but I welcome the challenge.”

I read it with the same restrained weight. Still no laugh. Teren stopped.

“Too much.”

“What?”

“Too much sword.”

I stared.

“That sentence is impossible.”

“It isn't.”

The servant actor lowered the prop.

“You made it mean something.”

“It should mean something.”

“It means I dropped it.”

“It threatened your descendants.”

“Yes. Funny.”

“So the threat should be credible.”

“No.”

The recurring woman said:

“Credible enough.”

“That is meaningless.”

She pointed at the sword.

“Right now it sounds like it actually cursed six generations.”

“Yes.”

“So I don't laugh.”

“Why?”

“Because six generations are cursed.”

The servant actor nodded.

“I also stop being an idiot king and start wondering if I should put it down.”

“You should wonder that.”

“Not in this scene.”

Teren said:

“Again. Less.”

I sat back. Less what? Not voice. Not volume. Not speed. Less consequence. That sounded stupid. We ran it. This time I let the threat be casual. Almost bored.

“If you drop me again, I will arrange for your heirs to be stupider than you.”

The servant actor immediately said:

“You cannot do that.”

“I can barely imagine how I would make them worse, but I welcome the challenge.”

The recurring woman laughed. Servant actor fought a smile. Teren tapped the page.

“Keep.”

I looked at him.

“Keep what?”

“That.”

“That is not a note.”

“It is mine.”

“How do I repeat ‘that’?”

“Do it again.”

Of course. Again. I tried. It was worse. Too amused. The sword sounded pleased with its own joke.

Recurring woman said:

“No.”

“Why?”

“Now it knows it's funny.”

“The sword wrote the joke.”

“No.”

“Someone wrote the joke.”

“Exactly.”

I hated this conversation.

The servant actor said:

“Don't sell it.”

“What does that mean?”

“Say the insult. I will be offended.”

“That is division of labor.”

“Yes.”

Finally. Useful. Again. I said the line. He reacted. The laugh came from the recurring woman. Small. Good.

Teren:

“Next.”

I wanted to stay on it. Of course he moved on. The next sword line was from inside the scabbard. The king was arguing with a minister.

Sword interrupted:

HE IS LYING. Simple.

I read:

“He is lying.”

Too neutral.

Teren:

“Again.”

I tried ominous.

“He is lying.”

The servant actor looked down at the scabbard.

Recurring woman said:

“Too murder.”

“What does that mean?”

“It sounds like you're about to kill him.”

“The sword might.”

“Not here.”

Again. I tried contempt.

“He is lying.”

Servant actor looked at the minister. Better. Teren did not stop.

Minister:

“How would a sword know?”

Sword:

“Because you breathe differently when you lie.”

I liked that line. Too much. I gave it weight.

“Because you breathe differently when you lie.”

Teren:

“Less.”

I looked at him. He pointed at the king.

“He needs the line.”

What?

The servant actor said:

“You gave it to the room.”

“I spoke to the room.”

“You are talking to me.”

“The minister asked.”

“Yes. I am holding you.”

That was true. I looked at the sword. Then at him. Again. Minister asked. I answered toward the king. Not literally facing him. I was seated offstage. But I watched him instead of the page.

“Because you breathe differently when you lie.”

The servant actor reacted before the minister did. Tiny. A glance. Then minister answered. The scene continued. Teren stopped after three lines.

“Good.”

I looked down at the page. That felt different. Not bigger. Smaller. More specific. I wanted to know why. Dangerous. At break I took the sword. Not without permission. Soot-shirt worker handed it to me because I asked.

Progress. I weighed it. Light. Too light.

“It's wrong.”

She looked at me.

“What?”

“Balance.”

“No.”

“The pommel is huge.”

“Fake stone.”

“Yes.”

“The blade is wood.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“It should feel heavier.”

“To who?”

“The king.”

She pointed at the servant actor, who was eating something near the stage.

“He knows.”

“That is not the same.”

“It is exactly the same.”

“The audience can see how he handles it.”

“Yes.”

“So if it is too light, he may move it too easily.”

She stared.

Then:

“Tell him.”

I did. Servant actor listened while chewing. Then lifted the sword one-handed. Twisted it. Flipped it around the grip. Caught it. I winced.

“That.”

“What?”

“You cannot do that with the sword.”

“I just did.”

“Legendary sword.”

“Wood.”

“Onstage legendary.”

He grinned. My own argument used against me. Fine.

“Hold it like it matters.”

“Why?”

“Because otherwise it looks like wood.”

He stopped chewing. Looked at me. Then at the sword. He took it again. This time two hands. Not because heavy. Because deliberate. He drew slower. The ridiculous prop looked different. Slightly. Damn.

He said:

“Like this?”

“Yes.”

He overdid it immediately. Shoulders rigid. Arms straining. Face solemn.

“No.”

“What?”

“Now it weighs a hundred pounds.”

“Legendary.”

“Fuck you.”

He laughed.

“Show me.”

“I cannot hold it and voice it from here.”

“Convenient.”

Soot-shirt worker took the sword. Held it naturally. No. Too normal. I pointed.

“Not that.”

She gave it back to servant actor.

“His problem.”

Good. We spent several minutes arguing about how heavy the fake sword should look. Nobody decided. Teren called us back. Apparently there were scenes. The next one was the draw from yesterday. Little king.

Sword.

“You have drawn me, little king. Try not to embarrass us both.”

I did it. Too prepared. Recurring woman looked at me.

“No.”

Again. I looked at the actor. He drew too casually. I stopped.

“No.”

He lowered the sword.

“What?”

“You ruined it.”

His face lit up.

“Oh, excellent.”

“You just pulled it out like a kitchen knife.”

“It is a sword.”

“Legendary sword.”

“Wood.”

“No.”

He laughed.

Teren said:

“Again.”

The servant actor drew it with too much ceremony. I waited. Too long. Everything became stupid.

Recurring woman said:

“You're both terrible.”

“Yes,” Teren said.

Again. This time servant actor drew it cleanly. Not casual. Not holy. Just with a little care. I waited until his eyes found the blade.

Then:

“You have drawn me, little king. Try not to embarrass us both.”

He looked offended. Good. The recurring woman looked at the sword. Good. Then servant actor said his next line too loudly. Bad. Scene fell apart. We laughed. Again. That became the afternoon. Not solving the sword.

Making it worse in new ways. I tried gravel. Bad. I tried age. Bad. I tried bored. Sometimes good. Too bored. Bad. I tried cruel. Killed the joke. I tried cheerful. Horrifying for unrelated reasons.

The servant actor tried holding the sword like it was fragile. Wrong. Like it was heavy. Wrong. Like he was afraid of it. Interesting for one scene, terrible for another. At one point he drew it, looked directly at me, and waited.

I stared back.

“What?”

“You're the sword.”

“Yes.”

“So talk.”

“You are looking at me.”

“Yes.”

“Don't.”

“Why?”

“Because then they look at me.”

The recurring woman, from across the stage:

“I did.”

Good. The servant actor turned his eyes to the blade. I delivered the line. The recurring woman kept looking at the blade. Better. Then he ruined it by nodding at the sword like it had just given excellent advice.

“No.”

“What now?”

“The sword did not say anything worth nodding at.”

“It insulted my bloodline.”

“Exactly.”

“How should I react?”

“Not like you agree.”

He pointed the sword at me.

“Then give me something else.”

“I gave you the line.”

“You gave me the same line.”

“That is literally my job.”

“No. Give me something I can use.”

I looked at Teren. He was enjoying this. Quietly. Traitor.

“Again,” Teren said.

We did. This time I made the insult drier. Not funny. Not cruel. Certain. The servant actor rolled his eyes at the sword. That worked. The recurring woman laughed. Small. He looked at me.

“See?”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“What did you do?”

“Something.”

“That is not useful.”

“Exactly.”

I hated acting. I loved acting. Teren cut one sword line entirely. I objected.

“Best line.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Scene is slow.”

“Then cut his line.”

Servant actor said:

“Traitor.”

Teren cut his too. Fair.

Later the recurring woman asked:

“What is the sword?”

I answered too quickly.

“Old.”

“No.”

“Legendary.”

“No.”

“Arrogant.”

“No.”

I looked at her.

“What answer do you want?”

“I asked what it is in this scene.”

I looked at the page. The king had just been insulted by a minister. Sword interrupted to make it worse.

“Unhelpful.”

She smiled.

“There.”

“That is not a character.”

“It is today.”

Then she left. I stared at the page. Unhelpful. The sword was ancient. Legendary. Probably powerful. Maybe cursed. It had seen better kings. It knew when people lied. It hated bad questions. And in that scene it was unhelpful.

That was somehow easier to play than legendary. I did not like that. I liked it a lot. By late afternoon my throat felt dry. Not hurt. Used. Teren gave me water before I asked.

I drank. The servant actor sat beside me on the stage edge. Sword across his knees.

“Better?”

I looked at it.

“Some.”

“Which?”

“I don't know.”

He nodded.

“That is acting.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“No.”

He laughed. Then held the sword up.

“What does it sound like?”

I thought. Not deep. Not old. Not always slow. Not always threatening. Sometimes bored. Sometimes cruel. Sometimes petty. Sometimes very sure it was the smartest thing in the room. That last one felt familiar.

Dangerous. I did not say it.

“It depends.”

He groaned.

“You've become Teren.”

“Fuck you.”

“Again.”

I laughed. My palms were warm when I left. Shoulders tired from the walk and crutches. Right calf tired. Residual limb quiet. No magic. Good. At home, the yellow paper bird still sat on the table.

I put my pages beside it. Teren had let me borrow pages forty-one through forty-three. Not take. Borrow. I read the sword lines again. Quietly. Not performing. Hessa had specifically prohibited directional practice. Nothing about swords.

Still, I stopped myself from saying them out loud. Not because magic. Because my throat was tired. Reasonable. I looked at the first line. IF YOU DROP ME AGAIN, I WILL ARRANGE FOR YOUR HEIRS TO BE STUPIDER THAN YOU.

The line was funny. The sword could make it not funny. The actor holding it could make it not legendary. I could make everyone look at me instead of the blade. Three separate ways to fail.

Probably more. Excellent. I turned the page. How do I make this better? That was a dangerous question. Good.
