TEXT READER · CHAPTER 135

THE REPEAT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

My throat was normal. Hessa checked anyway. Not because of the sword. Because Hessa checked everything.

“Say ah.”

I stared at her.

“Why?”

“Because you said your throat was used yesterday.”

“It was acting.”

“Your throat does not care why.”

Fair. I said ah. She looked.

“Fine.”

“I knew that.”

“Yes.”

Palms normal. Shoulders mildly tired. Right calf normal enough. Residual limb quiet. Phantom heel present. Thumb quiet. Fingers quiet. No unusual left response. No pain. No cold. No numbness. No weakness. Boring. Excellent. Hessa placed the folded white towel under my right forearm.

No charcoal. No thread. No frame. Copper on the table. Same general position as before. I looked at it.

“Wrist?”

“No.”

“Elbow?”

“No.”

“New direction?”

“No.”

I frowned.

“You said no to all three useful options.”

“I said no to the three options you guessed.”

“That leaves many bad ones.”

“Yes.”

She opened her notes. Page from Ch131. Page from Ch133. I recognized the wording. Wristward. Elbowward. Self-reported asymmetry. No independent confirmation.

“You want replication,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Which?”

“Wristward.”

“Why not elbowward?”

“Because wristward was first.”

“That is not a reason.”

“It is enough of one.”

“No.”

She looked at me.

“Would you prefer I choose because I liked the result more?”

“No.”

“Then first is clean.”

Annoying. Fine. Same broad neutral outer-right-forearm region. No prepared destination. Direction given before shaping. Wristward. One attempt. No second attempt even if nothing happened. No external effect question. No thread. No casting. No Barrier.

No cleverness. I repeated the rules back. Hessa adjusted my forearm by less than an inch.

“Why?”

“Position.”

“It was fine.”

“It drifted.”

“It was comfortable.”

“That is not the same thing.”

She repositioned it. Fine.

“Direction?”

“Toward your wrist.”

“Not toward your fingers.”

“I am not indicating with my fingers today.”

She folded her hands. Good. Cleaner. Maybe.

“Draw.”

I did. Minimal. The draw established. Not instant. Familiar. That difference mattered. I held. No broadening. No hand. No thumb. No left. Hessa watched.

“Report.”

“Stable.”

“Anything unusual?”

“No.”

“Shape.”

I shaped. Localized. Small. Outer forearm. The center altered. Boundary formed. Not strongly. Enough. I waited.

“Report.”

“Localized. Maybe two fingers across.”

“Shape?”

“Oval.”

“Directional already?”

I checked. No. Maybe. I checked again.

“No.”

“Confident?”

“Moderate.”

“That is new.”

“What?”

“You used moderate.”

“I know degrees.”

“Rarely verbally.”

“Do you want the report or commentary?”

“Both.”

Of course.

“Hold.”

I held. The temptation was obvious. Wristward was coming. I knew it. My arm knew I knew it. That was not useful. Expectation problem again. But at least the direction was the same one we had already tried.

Replication was supposed to be contaminated by memory. That was part of replication. Probably. Hessa waited.

Then:

“Smallest directional change toward your wrist.”

I did nothing externally. Inside, I tried. Not force. Not shove. Not stretch. Direction. That word had become less clear the more often we used it. Toward wrist. At first nothing. Then the wrist-side edge seemed sharper.

Immediately. Too immediately. I distrusted it.

“Report.”

“Possible wrist-side sharpening.”

“Possible?”

“Too fast.”

“Does speed make it false?”

“No.”

“Then report sensation, not argument.”

“Wrist-side edge sharper.”

“Center?”

“Same.”

“Elbow side?”

“Same.”

“Extension?”

“No confidence.”

“Broadening?”

“No.”

“Motor?”

“No.”

“Force?”

“No.”

“Left?”

“No.”

“Hold.”

I held. The wrist edge remained sharper. Or remained expected. Same problem. I tried not to inspect too hard. That was impossible. Inspection was the task.

“Still?”

“Yes.”

“Same?”

“Yes.”

Hessa waited longer than I wanted.

Then:

“RETURN.”

I returned. The wrist-side edge softened. Center reduced. The whole alteration diminished. Underlying draw maintained. No hand. No thumb. No fingers. No wrist movement. No elbow response. No left.

“Residual?”

“Small center.”

“Hold.”

Held. It faded.

“Gone?”

“Yes.”

“Draw?”

“Still.”

“Release.”

Released. Clean. Hessa checked. Everything normal. Then she wrote. I waited. She kept writing.

“That repeated.”

She did not look up.

“Something repeated.”

“That is Hessa for yes.”

“No.”

“It followed wristward again.”

“Yes.”

“Under substantially similar conditions.”

“Yes.”

“No prepared target.”

“Yes.”

“Direction first.”

“Yes.”

“Same reported edge behavior.”

“Yes.”

“So.”

She put down the pen.

“So I am more confident than I was before.”

“How much?”

“Enough that I would now describe directional control as provisionally supported under this narrow condition.”

I stopped. That was more than I expected.

“Provisionally supported.”

“Yes.”

“Not established.”

“No.”

“Not freely chosen.”

“Not generally.”

“Not external.”

“No.”

“Not casting.”

“No.”

“Not Barrier.”

“No.”

I smiled. She did not.

“Can I have one good sentence without you ruining it?”

“You asked five questions.”

“Because you leave traps.”

“I leave boundaries.”

“Same architecture.”

“No.”

I looked at my arm. Provisionally supported. Narrow condition. Wristward. Direction-first. Internal report. No independent confirmation. Still. Better.

“What would establish it?”

“More replication. Another direction repeated. Better independent observation if we find one that does not distort the task. Stability over time.”

“Same wrist again?”

“Possibly.”

“Would that help?”

“If it repeats again under similar conditions, yes.”

“But still self-report.”

“Yes.”

“So eventually we need something outside my report.”

“Eventually I would like something outside your report.”

“Difference?”

“I will not spend unsafe attempts simply because independent confirmation is aesthetically satisfying.”

“That is not aesthetics.”

“It can become aesthetics very quickly.”

I looked at the copper. It had sat there through three directional assessments contributing almost nothing beyond familiarity.

“Could copper ever show direction?”

“Not with anything I trust from these attempts.”

“So stop looking at it.”

“I mostly have.”

“Then why keep it?”

“Because your draw response to it is familiar, and changing six conditions at once is how people invent stories.”

Fair. I hated how many useful sentences she owned.

“What if wristward is simply easier?”

“That remains possible.”

“Anatomically?”

“Possibly.”

“Then repeating wrist does not prove choice.”

“No.”

“Repeating elbow would help more.”

“Possibly.”

“Because it is opposite.”

“Yes.”

“So elbow next.”

“Maybe.”

I groaned. She smiled. Barely.

“Do not build the next assessment.”

“I am not.”

“You are.”

“I am considering it.”

“Same behavior.”

“No.”

“Gregory.”

“Fine.”

She closed the notes.

“One full no-magic day.”

I stared.

“Again.”

“Yes.”

“That is becoming insulting.”

“You recover well.”

“That sounds positive.”

“It is.”

She checked the notes from the last four assessments again. I could see the repeated words even upside down. LOCALIZED. RETURN. NO MOTOR. NO LEFT. The interesting words changed. The boring ones did not.

“What would make you give me two attempts in one day?”

“Reason.”

“I have reasons.”

“Medical reason.”

“Efficiency.”

“No.”

“Comparison.”

“Maybe.”

I looked at her.

“That was almost yes.”

“It was exactly maybe.”

“You enjoy this.”

“No.”

“You absolutely do.”

“I enjoy not injuring you.”

That was difficult to argue with without sounding deranged. I chose not to. Probably growth. Or fatigue.

“Does the rest day change the data?”

“It changes your body.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It is the part I control.”

“And the interval between attempts?”

“Also recorded.”

“Could improvement just be recovery between attempts?”

“Possibly.”

“Could worse performance be fatigue from theatre?”

“Possibly.”

I stared.

“Everything is possibly.”

“No. Some things are no.”

“Casting?”

“No.”

“Barrier?”

“No.”

“External?”

“No.”

She had a point. I did not reward her for it.

“Then why no magic?”

“Because recovering well is not permission to remove recovery.”

I hated good sentences when they were used against me.

“Next assessment after?”

“Yes.”

“Directional?”

“I have not decided.”

“External?”

“No.”

That answer came quickly. I noticed.

“You decided that.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I do not yet need an external question.”

I waited. She elaborated because she was occasionally humane.

“You have just produced a third internally consistent directional report under a controlled sequence. That is exactly when I do not throw away the controlled sequence to chase something more dramatic.”

Fine. Good. Annoying.

“Can I say progress?”

“Yes.”

I looked at her. She looked back. That was almost suspiciously easy.

“Progress.”

“Yes.”

Good. I stood. She pointed.

“Pages.”

I froze.

“What pages?”

“The ones in your coat.”

I looked down. Sword pages. Forty-one through forty-three. Folded carefully inside my coat.

“How do you know?”

“Corner.”

A page corner had escaped. I pushed it in.

“They are theatre pages.”

“I assumed.”

“Not magic.”

“I did not say they were.”

“You looked judgmental.”

“I always look judgmental.”

True. She waved me out.

At the door:

“No magic tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“No private directional rehearsal.”

“Yes.”

“No testing whether voice work changes anything in your arm.”

I turned slowly.

“What?”

“You have a face.”

“That is not a thing I was going to do.”

“Good.”

“That is insane.”

“Good.”

“Hessa.”

“Goodbye, Gregory.”

I left.

The bread seller called:

“SWORD.”

I ignored him.

Then:

“BLADE.”

Still ignored.

Then:

“LANDLORD.”

I turned.

“Pick one.”

He laughed. I hated him. The theatre doors were open. I had pages to return. That was why I went in. Obviously. The sword was onstage. The servant actor held it badly. I could tell from the doorway.

This was unfortunate. I crossed to my chair. Teren saw the pages in my hand.

“Returned?”

“Yes.”

He took them. Then gave them back.

“What?”

“Page forty-three.”

“You just took them.”

“Now I am giving them back.”

“This is inefficient.”

“Sit.”

I sat. The scene was already underway. Different scene. The sword had been awake for some time, apparently, and was speaking to the king while two soldiers tried to open a stone door. The servant actor looked at me.

Then deliberately looked at the blade. Good. He remembered. Teren pointed. Sword line. I read it the way I had yesterday. Dry. Controlled. Slightly bored. It sounded fake. Not terrible. Constructed. I heard the construction now.

The recurring woman, off to the side, said:

“No.”

I stopped.

“What?”

“You are doing Sword Voice.”

I hated the accuracy. The servant actor nodded.

“Yes.”

“That is the sword.”

“No. That is you doing the sword.”

I looked at Teren.

He said:

“Again.”

Of course. I tried less. Still Sword Voice. Less obvious. Still there.

The servant actor said:

“You are sitting differently too.”

“What?”

“You do this.”

He straightened, lowered his chin, and became an insulting imitation of me trying to be stately. The recurring woman laughed. I did not.

“Fuck you.”

“Accurate.”

“No.”

Teren:

“Again.”

I stared at the line. The sword was telling the king not to touch the door. Not because the door was dangerous. Because the king had already touched three things he had been told not to touch.

The line:

IF YOU PUT YOUR HAND ON THAT DOOR, I WILL PERSONALLY ASK THE DOOR TO BITE YOU. I had been making it legendary. Old. Certain. Above everyone. What if I did not? Not casual exactly.

Just spoke. Normally. That felt wrong. Good. I looked at the servant actor. He held the sword. Not reverently. Not carelessly. Just present.

I said:

“If you put your hand on that door, I will personally ask the door to bite you.”

My voice. Mostly. No depth. No added age. No deliberate weight. But something underneath changed without me planning it. A remembered cadence. Older. Not an old-man voice. Not gravel. Not theatrical age.

Just the way I had sometimes spoken when I had already explained something twice and someone was about to do the stupid thing anyway. Flat patience nearly gone. The servant actor stopped reaching toward the door.

Very slowly looked at the sword. The recurring woman laughed. Not huge. Immediate. Teren did not say anything. I looked at him. He lifted one finger. Continue.

The king said:

“You cannot speak to doors.”

I answered:

“I can speak to almost anything. Most things have better judgment than you.”

Same voice. Mine. But not nineteen. Not exactly. The sentence came out with an old irritation I had not used on purpose. The servant actor smiled despite himself. Then recovered. Soldier actor laughed. Teren stopped.

“Again.”

I blinked.

“What?”

“Again.”

“What was wrong?”

“Nothing.”

That was worse. I looked at the recurring woman.

She said:

“I looked at the sword.”

The servant actor said:

“I could play that.”

I looked at him.

“What was it?”

He shrugged.

“You.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

“That was not me.”

“It was your mouth.”

“That is anatomy.”

“Again.”

I did. Worse. Too normal. Just Greg. The line flattened. The servant actor reached for the door too soon. Teren stopped.

“No.”

I laughed once. Of course. The good version had happened once. Again. I tried to remember the old cadence. Bad idea. Immediately became performance. Older. Too deliberate.

Recurring woman:

“No.”

“Fuck.”

Servant actor:

“Very legendary.”

“Fuck you.”

Again. I stopped trying to sound old. Stopped trying to sound like the sword. Looked at the actor. He put his hand near the door.

And I thought, with sudden perfect irritation:

Do not touch the fucking door. Then said the written line.

“If you put your hand on that door, I will personally ask the door to bite you.”

Closer. The servant actor froze. Recurring woman smiled.

Teren:

“Keep moving.”

The scene continued. I did not know what I had done. Good. Maybe. The next line failed. Then another worked. Then I rushed one. Then the servant actor held the sword too high and I told him so.

He told me to voice better. Normal. Excellent.

At break I asked the recurring woman:

“Did it sound old?”

She thought.

“No.”

“Older?”

“Maybe.”

“That is not useful.”

“It sounded like you already knew he was going to be stupid.”

I looked at her.

“That is specific.”

“Yes.”

“Did it sound nineteen?”

She stared.

“What does nineteen sound like?”

Fair. The servant actor came over.

“It sounded like the sword had dealt with kings before.”

I looked at him.

“That is in the text.”

“Yes.”

“So.”

“So it helped.”

“How?”

He shrugged.

“I believed you were tired of me.”

“I am tired of you.”

“Exactly.”

He walked away. I sat. The sword remained on the stage. Wood. Paint. Fake stone. No magic. I had spent yesterday trying to build a voice for something ancient. Today, for one line, I had mostly stopped.

And something older had shown up anyway. Not the sword. Me. That was strange. Not frightening. Just strange. I did not want to explain it. Not yet. Teren called us back. I stood halfway, remembered I was voicing from the chair, sat again.

Servant actor saw.

“Legendary.”

“Fuck you.”

He drew the sword. I looked at the page. No Sword Voice. No invented age. No gravel. No throne-room nonsense. Maybe. The problem was that I could hear the bad version now. That had not been true two days ago.

Two days ago deep sounded ancient because I wanted ancient. Slow sounded important because I wanted important. A lowered chin felt like authority because I had not yet watched everyone look straight at Gregory instead of the blade.

Now the failures announced themselves. Not before I made them. That would have been useful. After. Still useful. The servant actor shifted the sword in his hand. Not too casual. Not holy. He had changed too.

I hated that this was becoming shared. Which probably meant it was working. Teren lifted the page.

“Again.”

Fine. Again.
