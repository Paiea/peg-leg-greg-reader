TEXT READER · CHAPTER 138

THE SHOPKEEPER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The theatre had become a warehouse while I was gone. Not metaphorically. There were boxes everywhere. A painted wall lay face-down across two benches. Three costumes hung from a rope where the alley usually was. Someone had stacked cups inside a helmet. The soot-shirt worker was on his knees wrapping the legendary sword in cloth. I stopped.

"No."

He looked up.

"What?"

"You are packing it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because we need it tonight."

I looked toward the stage. Teren was standing in the middle of it with pages in one hand and a piece of bread in the other.

"Tonight?"

He pointed the bread at me.

"Good. Sword."

"What is tonight?"

"Show."

I waited. He ate the bread.

"A show."

"Yes."

"Here?"

"No."

That was worse.

"Where?"

"East market hall."

"That is still Carrow."

"Yes."

"Why are you packing?"

"Because the market hall does not contain our scenery."

Reasonable. Annoying. The servant actor came through the side door wearing one boot and carrying the other.

"Gregory."

"What happened?"

"To what?"

"All of this."

"Thursday."

"It is Thursday?"

"I think."

Someone behind us said, "It is." The servant actor shrugged.

"Thursday."

I had apparently been attending theatre long enough to lose days and not long enough to know Thursdays were dangerous. A woman I had seen twice before crossed the stage with a red coat over one arm.

"Where's Meren?"

"Not coming," Teren said.

She stopped.

"What?"

"Not coming."

"He's in the second act."

"Not anymore."

She thought for perhaps two seconds.

"Who has Shopkeeper?"

Teren looked at me. I looked behind me. Nobody.

"No."

"Page twenty-six."

"I don't know page twenty-six."

"Excellent."

"That is not excellent."

The servant actor sat to put on his second boot.

"You're a shopkeeper."

"I am not."

"You look furious about money."

"That is not a profession."

"It is several professions."

Teren tossed me pages. I caught them badly against my chest because catching paper while using crutches remained stupid.

"Sit," he said.

"I am sitting for Sword."

"Sit somewhere else for Shopkeeper."

"How many roles am I doing?"

"Two."

"No."

"Probably."

"Probably is worse."

Teren had already turned away. I looked at the pages.

SHOPKEEPER.

Fourteen lines. Fourteen.

"That is not a small part."

The recurring woman passed me carrying a basket of fake vegetables.

"It is."

"Fourteen lines."

"Some are 'no.'"

"That still counts."

"Not emotionally."

I hated theatre. I sat. The scene was simple. A young man owed the shopkeeper money. The young man wanted more goods. The shopkeeper refused. The young man claimed his uncle would pay. The shopkeeper did not believe in the uncle. Then a constable entered. Then something about a goose. I read it again.

"Why is there a goose?"

Nobody answered. I read it a third time. The debtor's first line was an apology.

The shopkeeper's answer was:

YOU HAVE BEEN SORRY FOR ELEVEN DAYS.

That I understood.

The next:

IF I MUST COME OUT FROM BEHIND THIS COUNTER, THE PRICE INCREASES.

I looked at my crutches. Then at Teren.

"Teren."

"No."

"You don't know what I am asking."

"Yes."

"This line is stupid."

He looked.

"Why?"

I held up one crutch. He looked at it. Then at me.

"Fix it."

"What?"

"Fix it."

"You want me to rewrite it?"

"I want you to say something when he says his line."

"That is not the same thing."

"Correct."

The servant actor leaned over my shoulder.

"You expect me to move and grab your money?"

I looked at him. He smiled.

"That is terrible."

"You thought of something better."

"Yes."

"Then use yours."

"I haven't thought of it yet."

"Very Greg."

He left. I read the scene again. The actor playing the debtor was apparently the same man who had been arguing about belts two weeks ago. He arrived while I was still reading.

"You're Meren?"

"No."

"Good."

"What?"

"He spits."

"While speaking?"

"Sometimes."

"Why?"

"Teeth."

I stared. He had already moved on. Nobody was worried. That was the disturbing part. A person was missing. I had fourteen lines I had never performed. The market hall was somewhere else. The sword was wrapped in cloth. One actor had only just found both boots. And someone was painting a moon. Now. The show was tonight. Nobody was worried. I found Teren.

"What happens if this is bad?"

"The moon?"

"The show."

He considered.

"People don't like it."

"And?"

"And what?"

"Patrons."

"Some complain."

"Then?"

"Sometimes they don't come back."

"That matters."

"Yes."

"You seem unconcerned."

"We're busy."

"What if the entire show fails?"

He looked genuinely confused by the question.

"It has."

"And?"

"We did another one."

That was not enough. Apparently it was. The debtor actor appeared beside me.

"Shopkeeper."

"I have a name."

"Not tonight."

Fair. We ran the scene standing around a crate because the counter was already being carried outside. I sat behind it. He entered.

"Master, I come in humility and regret."

"You have been sorry for eleven days."

Too fast.

Teren, from somewhere:

"Again."

The debtor left. Returned.

"Master, I come in humility and regret."

I waited. Not old Greg. Not Sword. Just looked at him. He had the face of a man about to ask for something after announcing regret. I knew that face.

"You have been sorry for eleven days."

Better. He put both hands on the crate.

"And on the twelfth I will make you whole."

"No."

"That is not the line."

"It is now."

He grinned.

"Again."

We did it. He changed his apology. I changed no. He claimed his uncle had been delayed by rain. I asked whether the rain had also delayed his uncle's purse. That was not on the page. The recurring woman laughed from behind us. Teren said nothing. The debtor actor leaned closer.

"My uncle is a respected man."

"Then he will be embarrassed to learn you owe me money."

That was not on the page either.

"Keep moving," Teren said.

The debtor looked toward the imaginary door.

"I could simply leave."

There was a line.

IF YOU LEAVE, I WILL FOLLOW YOU TO YOUR HOUSE.

Stupid. I looked at my crutches. Then at him.

"You expect me to come over there and collect it?"

He stopped. I continued.

"No. Bring it here."

He glanced toward Teren. Teren made a small circular motion. Continue.

"And if I don't?"

I thought.

"Double."

The debtor laughed. Not acting.

"Double?"

"You made me consider standing."

The recurring woman barked a laugh. Someone dropped something behind the stage. The debtor pointed at my leg.

"You cannot charge a man because you are missing a foot."

I looked down.

"That sounds exactly like something a man who owes me money would say."

He lost the scene. Bent over laughing.

Teren:

"Again."

I smiled. That had felt different. Not old. Not young. Not manufactured. I knew what the shopkeeper wanted. Money. Everything else was obstruction. Easy. We ran it again. Worse. Of course. The double line came too early. The leg line sounded prepared because now it was. The debtor tried to feed me the same opening. I waited for the laugh that had happened before. There was no audience. Nothing. Teren stopped us.

"You're doing the last one."

"I know."

"Don't."

"I was trying to repeat it."

"I know."

"How do I repeat it without repeating it?"

He looked at me.

"Tonight."

Then walked away. Terrible answer. The market hall smelled like onions. That was my first impression. Second was that it was larger than the theatre and worse in almost every useful way. Low platform. Broad room. Support posts in terrible places. Doors on both sides. No proper wings. A rear curtain that moved whenever anyone opened the loading door. People were already coming in while we were still carrying things. I looked at the audience. Then at the stage. Then at Teren.

"They're here."

"Yes."

"We aren't ready."

"No."

"Why are they here?"

"Doors opened."

"Why?"

"Because it was time."

I hated all of them. The soot-shirt worker passed carrying a lamp.

"Move."

I moved. A painted doorway went up. The moon was still wet. Someone wiped paint off it with a sleeve and made it worse. The servant actor was now dressed as the king. I stared.

"You're the king?"

"Tonight."

"Who is carrying the sword?"

He pointed. The recurring woman. I looked at her. She had the sword.

"You're carrying it?"

"Tonight."

Everything was wrong. The sword was supposed to answer him. Not her. Different grip. Different height. Different timing. Different eyes. I found Teren.

"No."

"Yes."

"I rehearsed with him."

"Now rehearse once with her."

"We have people coming in."

"Yes."

"That is not rehearsal."

"Correct."

The recurring woman drew the sword. Cleaner than the servant actor. Too clean. I missed the line. She looked at me.

"Late."

"I know."

Again. No Teren. She resheathed. Drew.

I said:

"You have drawn me, little king. Try not to embarrass us both."

Wrong. She wasn't the king. She stared at me. I stared back. Then she smiled.

"Excellent."

"That line doesn't work."

"Then fix it."

"What is your character?"

"Tonight?"

"Yes."

"Princess."

"Why does the princess have the legendary sword?"

"She stole it."

"From whom?"

"King."

"Why?"

"Because he is an idiot."

That helped. A lot. She drew again. I waited until she looked at the blade.

"You stole me from a king and this was your plan?"

She smiled.

"What was wrong with my plan?"

"You are still here."

She laughed.

"That'll do."

Teren shouted:

"Places."

I had not finished panicking. Nobody cared. The show started. There was no Again. That was the first thing. A line landed badly. Gone. An actor entered too early. Now he was there. A cup rolled. Someone kicked it behind the counter. The moon leaned. Nobody died. The audience laughed in places I did not understand.

They did not laugh in places that had worked every rehearsal I had seen. The first scene dragged. I could feel it without knowing how. People shifted. A child talked. Someone near the back coughed for approximately a year. Then the servant actor entered as king and said something about taxes that was not on any page I had read. The room woke up. Not all of it. Enough. The actors moved faster after that. Nobody announced the change. They simply did. My Shopkeeper scene came before Sword. Good. Terrible.

The debtor entered. Different first line. Of course.

"Master, before you become unreasonable, I have an explanation."

The audience laughed slightly. I looked at him. He was waiting. I had a line. I did not use it.

"You've had eleven days to improve it."

More laughter. He put a hand to his chest.

"You wound me."

"No. Then you would owe me for the floor."

That got them. Oh. Not huge. But I felt it. The room gave something back. The debtor felt it too. His posture changed. Small. He leaned farther into being offended.

"My uncle will pay."

"Your uncle has become very expensive for a man I have never met."

He started toward the imaginary door. I knew what was coming.

"You're not going to follow me."

The old written line waited uselessly in my head. I looked at my crutches. Then at him.

"No."

The room laughed before I continued. Interesting. I waited. Not because Teren told me. Because the laugh occupied the space.

Then:

"That's why your debt doubles."

Bigger. The debtor stopped.

"Doubles?"

"You made me almost stand."

That hit harder than rehearsal. He pointed at my missing leg. I saw the line coming. He changed it.

"You have one foot. How expensive can standing be?"

Noise. Not laughter yet. Anticipation. I looked down. Then back at him.

"Would you like to lose one and compare?"

The room broke. Not everyone. Enough. The debtor stared at me. I had surprised him. Good. Then he smiled.

"Is that a threat?"

"No. Consultation."

More. I was good at this. That was dangerous. The constable entered late. Very late. The debtor and I both looked toward the door. Nothing.

The debtor whispered:

"Constable."

I said, louder:

"Apparently law enforcement has also decided not to stand."

The constable came through the wrong entrance laughing. The audience thought it belonged. Maybe it did now. The goose never appeared. Nobody mentioned it. After the scene I got behind the curtain and nearly walked directly into a painted wall. The servant actor caught my shoulder.

"Shopkeeper."

"That worked."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"No idea."

"Some of it was obvious."

"Good."

"I need to know which."

"Later. Sword."

Right. The sword was harder. Much harder. The recurring woman changed everything. She moved before lines where the servant actor held still. She looked at the sword less. She used it like something stolen rather than something inherited. My prepared tired familiarity did not fit. The first line got a small laugh. The second died. I tried to fix the third before she arrived at it. Missed her. She had already turned. I spoke to where she had been. Bad. She corrected without looking at me.

"Are you always this slow?"

Not on page. The audience laughed. I answered from behind the curtain:

"Only when carried by thieves."

Better. She lifted the sword.

"Then perhaps I should return you."

"To him?"

She looked toward the actor playing the king. The king was picking something off his sleeve. I waited.

"No."

Big laugh. The king looked offended.

"Excuse me?"

I had no line. The sword did. Apparently.

"You heard me."

That got another. Then the king improvised something too long and killed it. Completely. The room went quiet. He knew. I knew. Everyone knew. He finished anyway. The recurring woman looked at the sword. I could not save that. So I didn't. She moved on. Two scenes later an effect failed. A voice was supposed to come from behind a closed door. The actor backstage was not there. I knew because I was backstage. The onstage actor knocked. Nothing. Knocked again. Nothing. The soot-shirt worker hissed something obscene.

Then the servant actor, changing coats beside me, cupped a hand near his mouth and answered in a voice that seemed farther away than it should have. Not magic. Mostly. Maybe. The onstage actor continued. I looked at him.

"How?"

"Later."

"You threw it."

"Yes."

"From here?"

"Yes."

"That was you?"

"Yes."

"Do it again."

"No."

He pulled the second coat on.

"Show."

Right. The final act was better than the first. Not because the play improved. They did. Cuts happened without announcement. A scene I had watched in rehearsal lost nearly a third of its lines. A joke from the Shopkeeper scene came back when the king complained about taxes.

The servant actor, now somebody else entirely, looked toward where I sat behind the curtain and said:

"Careful. He charges double if he stands."

The audience remembered. They laughed before I did anything. I leaned around the curtain just enough for them to see one crutch. Nothing else. Bigger laugh. I disappeared. That was all. At the end people clapped. Some stood. Most did not. One man near the front looked annoyed. A woman beside him was laughing while she put on her coat. A child had fallen asleep. I could not classify it. Backstage, everyone started taking things apart immediately. I found Teren.

"Was that good?"

"Parts."

"That is not an answer."

"It is."

"The first act was bad."

"Yes."

"The king's long speech died."

"Yes."

"The Shopkeeper worked."

"Yes."

"The second Sword scene was better than the first."

"Yes."

"The door voice failed."

"Then didn't."

I looked toward the servant actor. He was carrying two coats and half a painted tree.

"He did that."

"Yes."

"How?"

Teren pointed at the tree.

"Carry."

"I cannot carry that."

"Then don't."

He pointed at a box of pages.

"Carry those."

I could hang the string around a crutch. Fine. I did. Outside, the troupe loaded the wagon under market lamps. Not our theatre wagon. A hired one. The soot-shirt worker was arguing about which flat went first. The recurring woman sat on the wagon edge eating something from paper. The servant actor threw a bundle up to her.

Someone said:

"Tomorrow we need the south-road wheels checked."

I looked over.

"Why?"

Nobody answered me. Then Teren did.

"Because we're leaving."

I stopped.

"Leaving Carrow?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Two days."

"For how long?"

"Five, maybe six."

"Where?"

He named a town east of Carrow. I knew it. Not well. Old memory. A place with an old municipal hall whose roof had been rebuilt twice because the original load paths were strange and everyone kept blaming the stone. I had wanted to see it. Years ago. Decades ago. Different body. Different life. I looked at the wagon.

"The show goes?"

Teren looked at me like I was stupid.

"Yes."

"The sword?"

"Yes."

"Shopkeeper?"

"Depends who arrives."

I looked at my crutches. Road. Wagons. Loading. Unfamiliar stairs. Different rooms. People waiting. I did the arithmetic automatically.

"I'd be a hindrance."

The servant actor walked past carrying the wrapped sword.

"No."

I looked at him.

"Yes."

He lifted the bundle.

"You're the legendary sword."

"I voice the legendary sword."

"Yes."

"From a chair."

He nodded seriously.

"We have chairs in other towns."

The recurring woman called from the wagon:

"And shopkeepers."

"I am not a shopkeeper."

She laughed. Teren handed me another tied packet of pages.

"Tomorrow no magic?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"What does that have to do with anything?"

"Nothing."

He walked away. I looked at the packet. Then at the wagon. Then east. Five days. Maybe six. Not forever. The old hall was there. The show was there. Apparently the legendary sword was there. I wanted to go. That was becoming a problem. Good.
