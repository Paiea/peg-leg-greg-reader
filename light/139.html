LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 139

THE BASELINE

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Hessa had moved the towel. I noticed before I sat down. Not far. Perhaps two fingers toward the end of the table. I put both crutches against the wall and looked at it. Hessa was grinding something in a small stone bowl. The room smelled bitter and green, with something sharper underneath that caught in the back of my nose. Morning light came through the narrow window beside her shelves and made every suspended bit of herb dust unnecessarily visible.

"You moved the towel."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"It was wet."

I looked at her. She kept grinding.

"You replaced it."

"Yes."

"In a different position."

"No."

"It is farther down the table."

Hessa stopped. She looked at the towel. Then at me. Then back at the towel.

"It is a towel, Gregory."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer you are getting before you sit down."

I sat. The cot creaked under me. Hessa resumed grinding. I looked at the towel. Yesterday I had performed in front of an audience. Today I was suspicious of folded linen. This seemed like a decline.

"How was the show?" Hessa asked.

I looked at her.

"You know about the show?"

"I know you went."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

She scraped the crushed leaves into a small paper fold. I waited. She did not ask again.

"Parts were good," I said.

"Mm."

"Parts were bad."

"Sounds like a show."

"You've been?"

"To shows?"

"Yes."

"No. I've spent my entire life avoiding rooms with chairs."

I frowned. Hessa glanced at me.

"That was a joke."

"I know."

"You looked injured."

"I was determining whether you were mocking Teren."

"I don't know Teren."

"Then probably not."

She put the paper fold aside and washed the pestle in a basin. Water tapped against stone. Somewhere below us, a door opened hard enough to rattle the window, followed by a man's voice complaining about somebody's cart. Carrow was awake. I had slept badly. Not because of magic. That distinction mattered. I had spent too much of the night remembering an audience laughing. Also not laughing. The not laughing had been more educational. Possibly. I had no idea. Then there was the trip. Five days. Maybe six. East. Temporary.

There was an old municipal hall there with a roof I had wanted to see before I died the first time. That was a perfectly legitimate reason to travel. The theatre was also going. Coincidence. Mostly.

"Sleep?" Hessa asked.

"Enough."

"How much?"

I told her.

"Water?"

"Yes."

"Food?"

"Yes."

"Alcohol?"

"No."

"Pain?"

"No."

"Cramping?"

"Ordinary."

"Residual limb?"

"Baseline."

"Right hand?"

"Normal."

"Forearm?"

"Normal."

"Phantom?"

"Ordinary."

She dried her hands.

"You said ordinary very quickly."

"It is ordinary."

"You checked?"

"No."

That was not entirely true. I had noticed. Noticing was not checking. Probably. Hessa watched me for another moment, then wrote something.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You wrote."

"I often do."

"What did you write?"

"That you answered too quickly."

"That contaminates the record."

"It is the record."

I disliked her profession. Hessa pulled the stool over and sat beside the cot. The copper strip was already on the small tray. So was charcoal, paper, and the same narrow measuring cord she had used before. No thread apparatus. Good. I had not expected it. I was still glad.

"What are we doing?"

"Drawing mana."

"Yes."

"Then shaping."

"Yes."

"Then reporting."

"Yes."

"And then?"

"We stop."

I stared at her.

"That is the entire assessment?"

"If nothing interesting happens."

"That is barely an assessment."

"You have become very difficult to impress."

"Nineteen draws."

"Yes."

"Fifteen shaping attempts."

"Yes."

"Three external attempts."

"Yes."

"And now we're going backward."

"No."

"We are doing something I could already do."

"That is not backward."

"It is not forward."

Hessa leaned over and tapped the paper with one finger.

"Last time you gave me a more detailed description of the neutral shape than you had before the directional tests."

"Yes."

"The neutral shape was not neutral in the way you assumed."

"I did not assume it was symmetrical."

"You drew it symmetrical."

"Because I thought it was."

"Excellent. We have located the assumption."

I shut my mouth. She continued.

"If your ordinary shaped state already has a more distinct wrist-side boundary, then a later report that the wrist-side boundary became more distinct under instruction means less than it would if the starting shape were even."

"It still changed."

"Perhaps."

"It did."

"You reported that it did."

I hated that sentence too. Hessa did not look pleased about saying it. That made it worse. She set my right forearm onto the folded towel. The position was familiar. Palm down, forearm supported, elbow free enough that I was not pressing hard into the table edge. I looked at the towel.

"It is different."

Hessa closed her eyes.

"Gregory."

"Two fingers."

"You may leave."

"I didn't say it invalidated the test."

"You are looking at cloth instead of your arm."

"I can do both."

"That is precisely the problem."

Fair. I looked at my arm. Hessa adjusted the copper strip.

"Minimal draw."

I breathed. Reached. Mana came. Not dramatically. It had stopped feeling dramatic several assessments ago, which was probably dangerous in its own way. There. Present. Contained. The copper responded.

"Confirmed," Hessa said.

I held. Right side normal. Hand normal. Thumb quiet. No motor urge. No cold. No pain. My left phantom foot existed somewhere below the cot, vague and familiar and completely uninvolved.

"Shape," Hessa said.

I formed the small alteration in the right forearm. No direction. No external reach. Just the shape. It gathered slowly enough that I could feel the temptation to help it. I did not. There.

"Hold."

I held.

"Report."

"Localized. Similar size to last time."

"Similar is interpretation. Edges."

Right. I closed my eyes. Not because I needed to. Because the room became less annoying.

"Wrist-side boundary is clearest."

Hessa said nothing.

"Upper edge less distinct. Elbow side softer. Lower edge..."

I waited.

"Lower edge is difficult."

"Meaning?"

"I can tell where I think it stops. I cannot tell whether I'm feeling the end of the shape or the point where my attention stops being useful."

"Good."

"That wasn't a success."

"It was a report."

Fine.

"Anything changing?"

I waited. The shape stayed. Or mostly stayed. The upper edge felt broader than it had at the start. I did not like that.

"Upper edge may be spreading."

"May?"

"Yes."

"Direction?"

"Not instructed."

"I asked what direction."

"Oh. Toward the outside of the forearm. Slightly."

"Other edges?"

"Wrist side still clearest. Elbow side soft. Lower unclear."

Hessa wrote. I could hear the charcoal moving. Scritch. Pause. Scritch. It was surprisingly irritating.

"Draw it," she said.

"With my left hand?"

"Yes."

"My left hand draws badly."

"It drew badly last time."

"So we are preserving the variable."

"Yes."

I opened my eyes. Hessa put the paper where I could reach it without moving my right forearm. I took the charcoal in my left hand. The first line looked like a potato.

"This is useless."

"Continue."

I drew the wrist-side edge darker. Then the upper edge. Then the softer elbow side. The lower boundary got a broken line because I did not trust it. Hessa watched the paper, not my face.

"Done?"

"Yes."

"Look at the prior one."

She turned over a sheet beside her ledger. My drawing from the last assessment. I looked between them. Oh. The wrist-side boundary was dark in both. The elbow side was soft in both. The upper edge was not identical, but it was close enough that I could not dismiss it as nothing. The lower edge on the old drawing had been cleaner. Or I had drawn it cleaner. Those were not the same fact.

"Again," I said.

"No."

"One more neutral shape."

"No."

"Same setup."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you are already comparing."

"That is the point."

"Not while you are holding mana."

I looked at my right arm. Fuck.

"Return," Hessa said.

I reduced the shape. The wrist-side edge softened first. Again. Not completely. The center faded. Upper edge lingered. I continued the same return without separately correcting it.

"Report."

"Wrist side reduced. Center reduced. Upper edge remains clearer."

"Continue."

I did. The remaining edge softened. Then I reached the familiar ugly point where I could not tell whether something remained or whether I was searching for it because Hessa had asked me to.

"Uncertain."

"Hold draw."

I held the underlying mana. The shaped alteration was either gone or below my confidence.

"Copper?"

"Draw remains," Hessa said.

"Release."

I released. The mana went. Nothing jumped. Nothing hurt. Nothing moved outside me. No thread trembled. No revelation occurred. Hessa waited anyway. She checked my hand, wrist, forearm, breathing, residual limb, and the things she always checked even when I was increasingly convinced she could do half of it by glaring.

"Anything?"

"No."

"Stand."

I reached for the crutches. She watched me rise. Right foot planted. Hands loaded. Hips came forward. Normal. I settled.

"Anything?"

"No."

"Walk."

I went to the window and back. The room was not large enough to make this dignified.

"Anything?"

"My dignity is declining."

"Baseline."

I looked at her. She wrote it down.

"You did not."

"I did."

"Hessa."

She smiled without looking up. Barely. That was worse than laughter. I sat again. She put the two drawings side by side. They were not identical. They were also not very different. I leaned closer.

"If that's the ordinary geometry," I said, "then the directional tests are weaker."

"Yes."

"Not gone."

"No."

"Because there were changes after instruction."

"Reported changes."

"Yes, yes."

"And because the prior tests were not mapped this way before the instruction, we cannot compare their starting geometry at the same level of detail."

I stared at the pages. That annoyed me more than if she had simply said the tests were bad. Bad could be discarded. These were not bad. They were incomplete. Much worse.

"So we repeat them."

"Eventually."

"With a pre-map."

"Possibly."

"Same position."

"Possibly."

"Same towel."

Hessa looked at me.

"I will preserve the sacred towel."

"Thank you."

"I'll label it."

"That would actually help."

"I regret speaking."

She stacked the papers. I watched her. There was still something missing.

"What did today establish?"

"Establish is too strong."

"Fine. What did today support?"

"That your neutral shaped state may have a repeatable asymmetry under this setup."

"May."

"Yes."

"Wrist-side boundary more distinct."

"In two detailed reports."

"And upper edge changes without directional instruction."

"In both assessments, though not identically."

"So anatomy?"

"Maybe."

"Support pressure?"

"Maybe."

"Arm position?"

"Maybe."

"Natural magical geometry?"

"Maybe."

"Expectation?"

"Maybe."

"Day variation?"

"Maybe."

I sighed.

"You really do have one answer."

"I have several. You keep asking questions with the same answer."

I looked at the two drawings again. Then I remembered the wagon. Not because it had anything to do with my forearm. Because it was leaving. Tomorrow, apparently. I had known that before coming here. I had not intended to bring it up until after the assessment. That was sensible. The assessment was medical and magical. The theatre was theatre. Separate.

"Hessa."

"No."

"I haven't asked."

"You changed your voice."

I stared at her.

"What does that mean?"

"You have an administrative question."

"I do not have an administrative voice."

"You absolutely do."

I considered objecting. That would delay the question.

"The theatre is leaving Carrow tomorrow."

Hessa's hand stopped over the ledger.

"For how long?"

"Five days. Maybe six."

"Where?"

I told her. She knew the town. Of course she did. Hessa knew every place within a week's travel where somebody had once broken a wrist badly.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because I might go."

"Might?"

"Yes."

She waited. I disliked silence when other people used it deliberately.

"I want to see the municipal hall," I said.

Hessa nodded.

"The roof was rebuilt twice."

"Mm."

"The original load paths are strange."

"I'm sure."

"And the theatre is performing there."

"An extraordinary coincidence."

I looked at her. She remained completely serious.

"I would be traveling with them."

"Yes."

"There would be wagons."

"I know what theatres use."

"Do you?"

"No. But I know what roads use."

Fair.

"I wouldn't test anything independently."

"No."

"I wouldn't draw."

"No."

"I wouldn't shape."

"No."

"I wouldn't cast."

"No."

"I know."

"I am enjoying this part."

I shifted on the cot.

"What I am asking is whether going changes your restrictions."

"Yes."

My stomach tightened. Hessa closed the ledger.

"You will be somewhere else."

"That is not what I meant."

"It is what I mean. If you are injured, sick, exhausted, or develop a new symptom, I am not there."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"You said you slept enough this morning."

"I did."

"You slept badly."

I stopped. She had not asked whether I slept well. Only how much. Annoying woman.

"I slept enough badly."

"That sentence is why I worry about you."

"You don't worry about me."

"No."

She began wrapping the charcoal in cloth.

"I simply arrange my professional decisions around the possibility that you will do something stupid."

"That sounds like worry."

"It is risk management."

She tied the cloth twice. Too tightly for something she did not care about. I did not mention it.

"So can I go?"

"I don't control where you travel."

"That is evasive."

"It is accurate. I control what magical work I supervise and what I clear you to do."

"And?"

"And you remain uncleared for independent draw, shaping, casting, Barrier work, external testing, or whatever clever category you invent because you think it isn't technically one of those."

"I wasn't going to."

Hessa looked at me.

"I wasn't."

She kept looking.

"I probably wasn't."

"Better."

I rubbed my palm against my trouser leg.

"If I go, when is the next assessment?"

"When you return."

"That could be six days."

"Yes."

"We've been working on a shorter cadence."

"Yes."

"So the gap changes the sequence."

"Yes."

I waited. Hessa waited back.

"You're not going to tell me not to go."

"No."

"Why?"

"Do you want me to?"

"No."

"Then this is an odd complaint."

"I expected you to object."

"I object to unsupervised magic. I object to you converting uncertainty into permission. I object to you pretending symptoms don't count because they interfere with your plans."

"I don't do that."

She looked at me. Fine.

"Not consistently."

"Better."

She stood and carried the bowl to the shelf. I looked at the drawings again. Six days without an assessment. Possibly. Five. The theatre had chairs in other towns. Apparently. Also shopkeepers. I could stay. That would be easier. Known room. Known stairs. Hessa. Guild. Pessa. Lyssa. Jorren. Work if it appeared. Theatre again when they came back, assuming they came back when expected. Nothing required me to leave Carrow. That was important. I was not being forced onto a road. I was not fleeing anything.

I was not chasing work I needed to survive. I was not even formally part of the troupe. There were excellent reasons not to go.

"Hessa."

She turned.

"If I travel tomorrow, is today the last assessment before I leave?"

"Yes."

I nodded.

"That is irritating."

"Yes."

She came back to the table and pushed the newer drawing toward me.

"What?"

"Take it."

I stared.

"You keep the records."

"I have the record."

She tapped her ledger.

"Take the drawing."

"Why?"

"So when you spend six days thinking about it, you can at least think about the thing you actually reported."

I picked it up.

"That sounds like permission to analyze it."

"No magic."

"I said analyze."

"No reconstructing the sensation."

"I can't promise not to remember my own arm."

"You know the difference."

I did. Mostly. I folded the paper once. Hessa immediately said, "Don't." I unfolded it.

"Why?"

"You just folded through the wrist boundary."

I stared at her.

"You said the drawing isn't independent evidence."

"It isn't."

"Then why does the fold matter?"

"Because now I have to wonder whether you will later remember the fold as part of the line."

I looked at the charcoal mark. Then at her.

"That is extremely annoying."

"Yes."

She gave me a scrap of stiff paper to carry it between. I slid the drawing inside. At the door I collected my crutches.

"Hessa."

"What?"

"If I go east, and nothing changes, I come back and we continue?"

"Yes."

"Same restrictions."

"Yes."

"Same question?"

"We'll see."

I opened the door.

"And Gregory?"

I looked back. She had already reopened the ledger.

"What?"

"Go because you want to go."

I frowned.

"I told you. The municipal hall."

"Yes."

"The roof is genuinely interesting."

"I believe you."

"And I'm useful to the theatre."

"I didn't ask."

"They need the sword."

"Gregory."

"And possibly the Shopkeeper."

"Leave my office."

I did. The street outside was brighter than I expected. A produce cart blocked half the lane, and two women were arguing over whether a basket of onions belonged to one of them or to a shop three doors down. Someone had hung wet shirts from an upper window. They snapped in the breeze above the street like surrender flags nobody respected. I tucked Hessa's drawing safely inside my coat. The theatre left tomorrow. I had an entire day to decide whether I was going. I turned west.
