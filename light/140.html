TEXT READER · CHAPTER 140

THE TRAVELER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I turned west because Lyssa was usually west in the morning. That was not the same as going to find her. It was merely using information I already possessed while traveling through the city. This distinction survived for almost three streets. Then I passed a bakery I did not need, ignored the guild road entirely, and asked a woman unloading blue cloth whether she had seen Lyssa. Fine. I was going to find her. The woman pointed with her chin.

"South square."

"That is not west."

"She moved."

"I know people can move."

The woman looked at my crutches. I looked at her. She went back to the cloth. Carrow was warm already. The night damp had burned off the stones, leaving the streets bright and dusty wherever wagon wheels had ground dirt into the gaps. A fish seller was throwing water across his boards. Two apprentices carried a door between them without looking where they were going. I moved. The door missed me. Barely. One apprentice said, "Sorry." The other said, "You were in the way."

I considered following them to explain geometry. I had other work. Not work. Something else. Lyssa was in the south square beside a stack of baskets, talking to an older woman with silver rings on three fingers. The woman held a folded length of green cloth. Lyssa held the other end. Neither looked happy. Good. Normal. I stopped outside the argument. Lyssa saw me first. Her eyes went to my coat, then my face.

"Hessa?"

"Done."

The older woman looked at me.

"Who is that?"

"Gregory."

"That explains nothing."

"It usually doesn't," Lyssa said.

I disliked how easily that came to her. The woman tugged the cloth. Lyssa did not let go.

"It was dry when I gave it to him."

"It is wet now."

"Then he got it wet."

"He says you gave it to him wet."

"He lies."

"He says you lie."

"Then he lies twice."

I looked at the cloth. One end was damp. Not soaked. Damp.

"What happened?"

Both women looked at me. Lyssa said, "No."

"I only asked."

The older woman handed Lyssa the cloth abruptly.

"Half."

"No."

"Then take it back."

"I don't want wet cloth."

"You brought me wet cloth."

"I brought you dry cloth."

"You just said you didn't bring it."

"I said he brought it."

The woman pointed somewhere behind me. A boy was standing near a fountain trying very hard to become architecture. Ah. I looked at him. He looked away. Lyssa followed my eyes.

"No," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're about to solve it."

"I am not."

"You have that face."

"I have one face."

The older woman said, "He has several." This was becoming unreasonable. Lyssa finally let go of the cloth.

"Half now. Other half when it dries clean."

The woman considered.

"Quarter now."

"Half."

"Third."

"Half."

"You are bad at bargaining."

"You keep moving toward me."

The woman stared at her. Then laughed once.

"Fine."

Money changed hands. The boy near the fountain vanished. Lyssa folded the damp cloth over her arm. I watched.

"What?" she asked.

"You accepted the cloth."

"Yes."

"You said you didn't want it."

"I don't."

"You're carrying it."

"Gregory."

"Fine."

She turned to the older woman.

"Tomorrow?"

"Not with him."

Lyssa looked toward the fountain.

"No."

The woman left. Lyssa stood with wet green cloth over one arm and her work bag over the other shoulder. The ugly black repair still held. I had been right that it would. It remained ugly. She looked at me.

"Well?"

"Well what?"

"Hessa."

"Oh."

"You came over and said done like you finished breakfast."

"It was not breakfast."

"I know."

"Nothing happened."

Lyssa waited. I corrected myself.

"Nothing external happened."

"Good?"

"Unclear."

She smiled.

"Fuck you."

"You said it."

"It is becoming contagious."

We started walking without discussing where. I fell into the position beside her that kept my right crutch away from her feet. She had learned it too. She shifted the wet cloth to her other arm without looking.

"The neutral shape may not be neutral," I said.

Lyssa glanced at me.

"Those words sound like they are fighting."

"It is a label."

"For something that isn't what the label says?"

"Possibly."

"Good label."

"Hessa would like you."

"No."

"No?"

"She tells you not to do magic."

"Yes."

"I tell you not to do magic."

"Sometimes."

"When Hessa tells you."

"Exactly."

Lyssa bumped my shoulder with hers. Light.

"Continue."

I explained the wrist-side boundary. Not all of it. Enough. The two drawings. The repeated asymmetry. The problem with comparing the directional tests to a baseline we had not described well enough before testing direction. Lyssa listened while watching the street. That was one of the differences between her and Hessa. Hessa listened like she was building a cage around the answer. Lyssa listened like the answer could walk beside her if it wanted.

"So the old test might still mean something," she said.

"Yes."

"But less."

"Yes."

"And you hate that."

"Yes."

"Good."

I stopped. She kept walking two steps, then looked back.

"Why is that good?"

"Because last time you liked maybe too much."

"I did not."

"You did."

"I respected the evidence."

"You were excited."

"That is allowed."

"Yes."

"Then why are you saying it like a diagnosis?"

She smiled.

"Come on."

I caught up. We turned into a narrower street where awnings nearly met overhead. The shade was cooler. A cooper had barrels lined along one wall, forcing everyone into a single crooked path around them. Lyssa went first. I followed, planting the crutches carefully between iron hoops and people's feet. A man came the other way carrying three long poles. He turned sideways. So did I. Wrong choice. One pole caught my left crutch near the cuff. I stopped. The man stopped. Lyssa turned. Nobody moved.

The pole man said, "Well."

"Yes," I said.

Lyssa took the outer pole and lifted it over my shoulder.

"Go."

He went. I looked at her.

"You solved it."

"Yes."

"I had it."

"No."

"I was about to."

"You were attached to a pole."

"Temporarily."

She kept walking. I followed. This was why travel with other people was useful. Not that I was traveling. Yet. I had not decided. The theatre left tomorrow. Hessa had not objected. That was not permission because permission was not hers to give. The municipal hall remained structurally interesting. The troupe had explicitly stated that other towns contained chairs. There were several variables. Lyssa stopped at a doorway and knocked. I nearly continued past her.

"What?"

"Work."

"Oh."

A young man opened the door. He saw Lyssa. Then me.

"No."

I looked at him. Lyssa said, "He's not coming in."

"Good."

"What is inside?"

"Gregory."

"I am asking spatially."

The young man looked at Lyssa.

"Does he do this?"

"Yes."

"Constantly?"

"Not constantly."

"Frequently."

"Thank you."

Lyssa handed him the folded wet cloth. He frowned.

"This is wet."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because everyone is difficult."

He accepted that. Apparently this was a professional category. Lyssa took a small packet from her bag and gave it to him. He gave her another. Then he looked at me again.

"Landlord?"

"No."

Lyssa said, "Shopkeeper now." I turned.

"What?"

The young man smiled.

"Oh. That was you?"

No. Absolutely not.

"How do you know?"

"My sister was there."

"At the market hall?"

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

Lyssa leaned against the doorframe. The young man thought.

"That the king was terrible."

Correct.

"And?"

"The princess was good."

Correct.

"And?"

"The man with one foot threatened somebody about debt."

"That was not a threat."

He laughed.

"She said it was."

"It was consultation."

That made him laugh harder. Lyssa looked delighted. I hated public performance.

"Did she say anything about the sword?"

"The talking one?"

"Yes."

He shrugged.

"Funny."

"That is all?"

"What else would it be?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Fair. The young man shut the door. Lyssa waited. I started walking. She caught up.

"Funny."

"Stop."

"The talking one."

"Lyssa."

"Shopkeeper."

"That is not my name."

"It was last night."

"No."

She laughed. I let her. For three streets. Then she said, "You're going." I looked at her.

"Where?"

She did not answer. That was irritating.

"I have not decided."

"You came to find me after Hessa."

"I have done that before."

"Yes."

"I wanted to see you."

"I know."

"And I needed to tell you."

She looked at me. There. That had escaped. Fine.

"Tell me what?" she asked.

"The theatre leaves tomorrow."

Her face changed only slightly. Not fear. Not surprise exactly. Attention.

"How long?"

"Five days. Maybe six."

"Where?"

I told her. She knew the road but not the town.

"Why?"

"Theatre."

"I got that."

"There is also a municipal hall."

Lyssa stared at me.

"What?"

"You're leaving for a building?"

"The roof was rebuilt twice."

She kept staring.

"The original load paths are strange."

"Gregory."

"I wanted to see it years ago."

"How many years?"

I stopped. Ah.

"Many."

She understood. Not everything. Enough. The street moved around us. A woman squeezed past carrying a basket of onions. Somewhere behind us, a hammer struck metal in a steady rhythm. Lyssa stepped closer to the wall so we were not blocking the lane.

"And the theatre happens to be going there."

"Yes."

"Tomorrow."

"Yes."

"For five days."

"Maybe six."

"You're going."

"I said I haven't decided."

She reached out and straightened the edge of my coat where Hessa's stiff paper had made it bulge.

"You put the drawing in here."

"Yes."

"You came straight to find me."

"Mostly."

"You asked someone where I was."

"Yes."

"You're telling me before you told me you've decided."

"I have not decided."

Lyssa nodded.

"Fine."

That was too easy.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"No."

She started walking again. I followed.

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"Where you stop arguing because you think you already know."

"I don't know."

"You do."

"No."

"Lyssa."

She turned into the next square. I followed, irritated enough that I almost clipped a fruit crate.

"Lyssa."

She stopped beside a fountain. Not the pigeon fountain. Good.

"What do you want me to say?"

"I don't know."

"Then why are you angry?"

"I am not angry."

"You're hitting the ground harder."

I looked at my right crutch. Traitor.

"I am explaining that the decision is not made."

"Okay."

"You don't believe me."

"I believe you haven't said it."

"That is different."

"Yes."

I waited. She waited. People crossed between us and the fountain. A little boy leaned over the basin until his mother pulled him backward by his shirt.

"Do you want me to tell you not to go?" Lyssa asked.

"No."

"Do you want me to tell you to go?"

"No."

"Do you want me to be upset?"

I frowned.

"No."

She looked at me for another second.

Then softer:

"Do you want me to care?"

That was a much worse question.

"I know you care."

"Then?"

I looked away. A horse was drinking from the far side of the fountain. Its driver held the reins loosely and picked something from his teeth. Five days. Maybe six. Not long. I had traveled farther for worse reasons. I had also died. Different trip.

"I wanted to tell you," I said.

"I know."

"Before."

"I know."

"I don't know what you're doing tomorrow."

"Work."

"Obviously."

"Morning north side. Afternoon maybe west."

"Maybe?"

She smiled.

"Contagious."

I ignored that.

"I leave early if I go."

"If."

"Yes."

"Fine."

I looked at her. She did not rescue me. Good. Annoying.

"Could I see you tonight?"

"Yes."

Immediate. No bargaining. Something in my chest loosened enough that I noticed it.

"Where?"

"My room."

I blinked. Lyssa raised an eyebrow.

"You've been there."

"Yes."

"You look surprised."

"I am not."

"You are."

"I was calculating stairs."

"No, you weren't."

I smiled despite myself.

"Some."

"Liar."

"Fine."

She stepped close and kissed me. Not long. Public enough that a woman passing with a basket looked at us and then politely looked somewhere else. Lyssa drew back.

"Go tell your theatre."

"I haven't decided."

"Then go inspect the wagon for six hours until the decision becomes official."

"That is insulting."

"Will you?"

I thought about the south-road wheels. The loading. How much weight I could actually move. Whether there was space to ride if my hands got bad. Where the crutches would go. What happened if the town had stairs everywhere. Whether Teren had assumed I was coming or merely offered. Whether the servant actor could throw his voice from a moving wagon. No. That last one was irrelevant. Probably.

"I should ask about the road."

Lyssa nodded.

"And the wagon."

"Yes."

"And sleeping arrangements."

"Yes."

"And whether they expect me to carry anything."

"Yes."

"And how many shows."

"Yes."

"That is not the same as deciding."

"No."

She was smiling.

"Stop."

"I didn't say anything."

"You're doing it with your face."

"I have one face."

"That is my line."

"It is everyone's line now."

She kissed me again. Then she stepped away and adjusted her work bag.

"I have to go."

"Where?"

"North."

"You said morning north."

"It is still morning."

I looked at the sun. Barely.

"That assessment took longer than I thought."

"You talked."

"Hessa talks."

"Sure."

"She does."

Lyssa started backward for two steps.

"Tonight."

"Yes."

"Don't arrive after I'm asleep."

"What time?"

She gave me a look. Right. Not everything required measurement.

"Before late."

"Good."

She turned.

"Lyssa."

She looked back. I nearly said I was going. The words were there. Instead I said, "The black patch is still ugly." She looked down at her bag. Then at me.

"Five days might improve your taste."

"Six would not."

She laughed and walked away. I watched longer than two seconds this time. She did not turn around. Good. I headed for the theatre. Not because I had decided. I needed information. The theatre doors were open when I arrived. A flat painted like a stone tower was halfway into the alley, blocking most of it. The soot-shirt worker stood on the wagon bed with a coil of rope around his neck, shouting at someone inside.

"Not that one. The other ugly one."

A voice answered, "They're both ugly."

"The tall ugly one."

"That is not better."

I stopped beside the wagon. One rear wheel had been removed. Good. South-road wheels. The servant actor was underneath it. I could see his boots.

"Teren?"

"Inside," the soot-shirt worker said.

"Why is the wheel off?"

The boots under the wagon moved.

"Because it was bad."

"How bad?"

The servant actor slid out on his back. His shirt was dusty and his hair had something black in it.

"Bad enough."

"That is not a measurement."

"It is for wheels."

He sat up.

"You coming?"

I looked at the missing wheel.

"I have questions."

He smiled.

"Coming."

"Questions."

"Ask."

"How long on the road tomorrow?"

"Depends."

"On?"

"Road."

I stared at him. He wiped his hands on his trousers.

"Three hours to first stop if the axle behaves. Eat. Another three, maybe four. We sleep there."

"Where?"

He named a village I knew.

"Then?"

"Shorter second day."

"That makes the trip longer than five days."

"Five or six there and back if nothing goes wrong."

"That is impossible."

"Then six."

"Teren said five maybe six."

"He lies optimistically."

Useful.

"Sleeping?"

"Inns if rooms exist. Hall floor if they don't. Wagon once, probably."

"Probably?"

"Gregory."

"What?"

"You are asking theatre people for certainty."

Fair.

"Shows?"

"Two planned. Maybe three."

"Shopkeeper?"

"If Meren remains dead."

"He isn't dead."

"Then he should arrive."

"Do I have a role if he does?"

The servant actor looked at me.

"Sword."

"Yes."

"And whatever breaks."

"That is not a role."

"It is the oldest role."

The soot-shirt worker shouted from the wagon, "Can the oldest role lift a wheel?"

"No," I said.

"Then he's decorative."

"Fuck you."

"See? Actor."

The servant actor laughed. I looked at the wagon bed.

"Where do I sit?"

He pointed.

"Front if Teren loses the argument. Back if he wins."

"What argument?"

"Seats."

"With whom?"

"The wagon owner."

"Why?"

"Wagon owner wants cargo where people go."

"That seems backwards."

"Yes."

"Can I secure crutches?"

"We'll tie them."

"Where?"

He pointed to a rail. I inspected it. Solid enough. Probably.

"And if my hands get bad?"

"You ride."

"I am already riding."

"You ride more."

"I mean during loading."

"Then don't load."

"That makes me a hindrance."

He looked at me. Not joking now.

"No. It makes you a man who doesn't load."

"I can carry some things."

"Then carry some things."

"And when I can't?"

"Don't."

"That is not efficient."

He shrugged.

"We have actors."

"That does not answer anything."

"It answers most things."

From inside, Teren shouted, "Sword!" I looked toward the door. The servant actor grinned.

"See?"

I went in. Teren was at a table covered in pages, receipts, two apples, a broken buckle, and a small pile of coins. He did not look up.

"You're coming."

"I have questions."

"Tomorrow before sunrise."

"That is not a question."

"Bring food."

"I haven't said yes."

"Bring food anyway."

"How many shows?"

"Two."

"He said maybe three."

Teren looked toward the alley.

"Who?"

"The king."

"Never trust kings."

"He said you lie optimistically."

Teren considered.

"Fair."

I put both hands on the table edge.

"Where am I sleeping?"

"Wherever we all sleep."

"That is not useful."

"No."

"Do you know whether the municipal hall has stairs to the stage?"

Teren finally looked at me.

"No."

"Back entrance?"

"No."

"Dressing rooms?"

"No."

"Floor height?"

"No."

"Chair?"

"Yes."

"You don't know the rest."

"I know chairs."

"How?"

"Town has people."

I stared. The troupe's faith in chairs was becoming theological. Teren picked up an apple.

"Gregory."

"What?"

"Are you coming?"

I looked at the pages. The receipts. The broken buckle. The coins. Outside, someone hammered the wheel back into existence. Tonight I was going to Lyssa's room. Tomorrow before sunrise the wagon left east. Hessa's drawing sat inside my coat. There were no good reasons left to pretend the decision required another category.

"Yes," I said.

Teren bit into the apple.

"Good. Bring food."

That was all. No welcome. No contract. No speech about the road. He pushed a packet of pages toward me.

"What is this?"

"Third show."

I stared at him.

"You said two."

"Maybe three."

"Fuck you."

"Learn page twelve."

I took the packet. Apparently I was traveling.
