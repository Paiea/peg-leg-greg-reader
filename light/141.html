LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 141

THE PACKER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Page twelve had seventeen lines. This was an unreasonable number of lines for a page Teren had handed me less than a day before leaving Carrow. I read them once at the theatre. Then again while someone dragged a painted tree past my chair and struck the doorframe hard enough to remove a branch. Nobody stopped. The tree continued outside with one fewer branch. I looked at the script. The role was a clerk. Of course it was.

Not a useful clerk. A comic clerk who had lost a document and spent most of the scene insisting the document had never existed. I understood the profession immediately.

"Do I need to learn all of this?"

Teren was tying pages into separate packets with red string.

"Page twelve."

"There are seventeen lines on page twelve."

"Yes."

"Why only page twelve?"

"Because if we do the third show, that scene is changing."

"Into what?"

"I don't know."

"You handed me lines for a scene you know is changing."

"Yes."

"That seems inefficient."

Teren pulled the knot tight with his teeth.

"Learn page twelve."

I took the packet home. By late afternoon I had packed three times. The first packing was practical. The second was because the first packing had been wrong. The third was because I had removed half the things from the second packing and could no longer remember why I had included them. Travel had been easier when other people decided what I carried. That was not a good argument for other people deciding things. I put the bag on the bed. Two shirts. One spare pair of trousers. Underclothes.

Cloth for the residual limb. Extra wrapping. Small repair kit. Soap. Cup. Food for the morning. Money. Script. Hessa's drawing. No. The drawing came out. I looked at it. Wrist-side boundary dark. Upper edge less certain. Broken lower line. It was information.

It was also exactly the kind of information I would look at in a wagon until I convinced myself the bumps in the road had revealed something about mana. I put it in the drawer. Then I took it out. Hessa had told me to take it. That did not mean she had told me to carry it east. I put it back in the drawer. Closed the drawer. Opened it. The drawing remained there. Good. I closed it again.

The room was warm. Evening light came through the window and caught the dust over the bed. Somewhere downstairs, someone was cooking onions in too much fat. The smell climbed through the floorboards and made the bread on my table seem inadequate. I looked at the bag. Crutches. Those were difficult to forget.

I added a second length of cord for tying them to the wagon rail. Then a third. No. Two. I removed one. There was a knock.

"Come in."

The door opened. Lyssa looked at the bag on the bed. Then at the folded shirt in my hands.

"You're packing."

"No."

She closed the door behind her.

"What are you doing?"

"Determining what to bring."

"That is packing."

"I haven't finished."

"That is still packing."

She carried a small paper parcel and a bottle. Her hair was pulled back loosely, though several curls had escaped around her face. She had changed clothes since the morning, simpler and softer, and the black-patched work bag was gone. I looked at the parcel.

"What is that?"

"Food."

"I have food."

She looked at the piece of bread on my table.

"No."

"That is bread."

"That is sadness with a crust."

"It was cheap."

"I can tell."

She put the parcel beside it. I opened it. Cheese. Two small meat pies. Dried fruit. Something wrapped separately in waxed paper. I opened that too. Sweet cake. I looked at her.

"This is too much."

"Then leave some."

"I won't."

"I know."

She sat on the edge of the bed, which made the bag tilt toward her.

"What are you bringing?"

I showed her. She picked up one shirt.

"This one?"

"Yes."

"It has a hole."

"It is small."

She put it on the floor.

"Lyssa."

"You have another."

"I have two others."

"Then why are you taking the one with a hole?"

"Because travel damages clothing."

"So you brought damaged clothing in preparation."

"Yes."

She stared at me.

"That makes sense."

"Thank you."

"I hate it."

She put the shirt back in the bag. I sat beside her. The mattress dipped. My left leg ended where it ended, and the empty space beyond it was no longer strange enough to notice every time. Lyssa noticed other things. She picked up the extra wrapping cloth.

"Enough?"

"Yes."

"For six days?"

"Yes."

"What if it gets wet?"

I looked at her. She smiled.

"Fuck you."

"You need more."

"I can buy cloth."

"In the town?"

"Yes."

"You've never been there."

"There will be cloth."

"Theatre has chairs. Town has cloth."

"Exactly."

"This is how civilizations collapse."

I added another piece. Not because she was right. Because there was room. She watched me do it and said nothing. I disliked successful silence.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're pleased."

"No."

"You are."

"I brought you food. I'm miserable."

I laughed. She leaned back on her hands. For a while we packed without making it important. She rolled the spare trousers tighter than I had. I objected, then kept them that way. I found a loose stitch on the bag strap and repaired it while she ate half of one meat pie despite claiming they were for me.

"You are stealing my travel food."

"I bought it."

"You transferred ownership."

"No contract."

"Verbal."

"No witness."

"I am a witness."

"Interested party."

I pointed the needle at her.

"You've spent too much time around me."

"Five days will fix it."

"Maybe six."

"Even better."

I tied off the stitch. The room became quieter as the street outside changed from work to evening. Wheels were less frequent. Voices carried farther. Somebody below laughed loudly, then shushed themselves for reasons that were not apparent. Lyssa took the clerk pages from the table.

"What is this?"

"Third show."

"You said there were two."

"There are two."

She looked at the pages.

"This says three."

"There may be three."

"You're leaving before sunrise for a trip that may be five or six days to perform two or three shows in a building you've never seen."

"Yes."

"And you know seventeen lines."

"On page twelve."

She flipped the page.

"There are more after twelve."

"I was instructed not to learn those."

"That seems dangerous."

"Thank you."

She handed it back.

"Do the voice."

"No."

"What voice?"

"I don't know yet."

"You have a voice."

"I have several."

She smiled.

"I knew it."

"That woman was wrong."

"Do the clerk."

"I haven't built him."

"Built?"

"Voice. Pace. How he answers."

"You could just talk."

"That would be me."

"You're playing a clerk."

"Yes."

"Have you considered that clerks can sound like people?"

I looked at her.

"That is radical."

"I know."

I picked up the page. The scene began with another character demanding a permit. The clerk denied that permits were issued in that office. The other character produced a permit issued by that office. The clerk explained that this proved the permit had already been issued and therefore there was no need to issue one. I liked him. That was concerning. I read the first line in a narrow, fussy voice. Lyssa's face changed.

"No."

"What?"

"Don't."

"Why?"

"You sound like a dying bird."

"He is bureaucratic."

"He is sick."

I tried lower.

"Now he sounds old."

"He could be old."

"Is he?"

"I don't know."

"Then why are you making him old?"

I lowered the page.

"You are interfering with my process."

"Yes."

I read it in my normal voice. Lyssa listened.

"Again."

I did.

"Slower."

"Why?"

"Because he knows the other person has to wait for him."

I looked at the page. Oh. I read it again. Not slow exactly. Unhurried. The permit seeker had urgency. The clerk did not. The line changed. Lyssa pointed.

"That."

"I know."

"You didn't."

"I was about to."

"You were a dying bird."

"That was exploratory."

She laughed. I read the next exchange. By the fourth line I stopped performing the clerk and started answering as if the permit seeker were wasting my time. That worked better. Annoyingly. Lyssa stretched out across the bed on her stomach, chin on her hands, while I went through the page. She interrupted whenever I sounded false. She did not use that word. Her words were worse.

"No."

"Too much."

"Why are you doing that?"

"Normal person."

"That one."

"Again."

Once she said nothing. I stopped.

"What?"

"Continue."

"You reacted."

"I breathed."

"You breathed differently."

She rolled onto her back.

"You are impossible."

"Was it good?"

"It sounded like you believed him."

"Believed what?"

"That the missing document is someone else's problem."

I looked at the line. Yes. That was the clerk. Not fussy. Not old. Protected. He lived inside a system where inconvenience moved downhill and he had arranged to be uphill. I read the next line. Lyssa closed her eyes.

"Better."

I kept going. At the end of page twelve she sat up.

"Again?"

"No."

I looked at her.

"No?"

"You know it enough to ruin it if you keep going."

I stared.

"That is what Teren said."

"I am smarter than Teren."

"You don't know Teren."

"I don't need to."

Fair. I put the pages down. The bag was packed. Mostly. There were still decisions available if I wanted them. Another shirt. More food. Different cord. A small knife. The drawing. I did not touch the drawer. Lyssa stood. For a moment I thought she was leaving. Instead she took my crutches and moved them away from the bed.

"What are you doing?"

"Making room."

"For what?"

She looked at me. Oh.

"That was a stupid question."

"Yes."

She came back and stood between my knees. I put my hands on her hips. She touched the side of my face. Neither of us said anything useful. Then she kissed me. The first kiss was easy. The second was not. I pulled her closer. She shifted one knee onto the bed beside me, careful of my left side without looking at it, and kissed me until I stopped thinking about page twelve. That took less time than expected. When we separated, she rested her forehead against mine.

"Before sunrise?"

"Yes."

"That's disgusting."

"It is."

"You'll be terrible."

"I am good in the morning."

"No one is good before sunrise."

"Farmers."

"Miserable."

"Bakers."

"Miserable with bread."

I smiled. She kissed the corner of my mouth.

"Come back."

The words were quiet. Not dramatic. Not a demand. I felt them anyway.

"It's five days."

"Maybe six."

"Six."

"Gregory."

"I'll come back."

She nodded. Then immediately reached past me and pulled the bag closer.

"What?"

"You forgot socks."

I stared at her.

"I did not."

She opened the bag. I had forgotten socks.

"Fuck."

"I know."

I got up. Lyssa sat in the warm hollow I left on the mattress while I crossed to the drawer. The socks were in the second drawer. The drawing was in the first. I opened the second. Took three pairs. Closed it. Lyssa watched me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I put the socks in the bag. She did not ask what was in the other drawer. Good. I tied the bag shut. Then untied it because the sweet cake was still on the table. Lyssa laughed.

"Shut up."

"I didn't say anything."

I packed the cake. Retied the bag. This time I left it closed. Tomorrow before sunrise I would get into a wagon with actors, scenery, bad wheels, too many scripts, and no reliable answer about where I would sleep. I was looking forward to it. That seemed unwise. I went back to Lyssa. There was still an evening left.
