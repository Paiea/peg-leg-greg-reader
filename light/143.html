TEXT READER · CHAPTER 143

THE STOP

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The road was awful. I was glad I had come. Both facts remained true while I stood beside the wagon drinking cold tea and waiting for my back to remember that it had not, in fact, been constructed out of curtain weights.

The crossroads inn had a yard of packed dirt with a stable on one side and the main building on the other. Chickens moved through both jurisdictions without respecting either. A low stone wall surrounded the well. Someone had tied a red cloth to the bucket handle, perhaps to make it easier to find, though the bucket was attached to a rope and directly above a well. The sign over the inn creaked in the breeze. I still could not tell what animal it showed.

"Fox," I said.

The servant actor followed my eyes.

"Dog."

"It has a tail."

"Dogs have tails."

"That tail is on fire."

"Sunset."

"It is coming out of the animal."

"Art."

I drank the tea.

"Who painted it?"

"No idea."

"Then why are you defending it?"

He shrugged. "Dog." The recurring woman passed us carrying the remains of the cabbage.

"Horse."

We both looked at her. She kept walking.

"That is not a horse," I said.

"Now you're defending it," the servant actor said.

I hated him. Teren came out of the inn. He was no longer carrying the boot. This suggested either progress or abandonment.

"Food," he said.

Everyone moved. I stayed where I was. Teren stopped.

"What?"

"Are we buying it?"

"Yes."

"Individually?"

"No."

"Is it included?"

"In what?"

"The trip."

Teren stared at me. I realized there had never been a discussion of what the trip included because there had never been a discussion of the trip at all.

"Do I owe you money?"

"Probably."

"How much?"

"Later."

"No."

Teren sighed. Behind him, the guard actor tried to pass through the door. Teren did not move. The guard said, "Food."

"I know."

"You're in the door."

"I know."

"Move."

Teren stepped aside. The guard went in. I said, "How much?"

"For what?"

"The trip."

"You're working."

"Am I being paid?"

"Yes."

That was new.

"How much?"

Teren rubbed his face.

"Gregory."

"What?"

"It is morning."

"It has been morning for hours."

"Not to me."

"You organized the departure."

"I made a mistake."

He looked toward the road as if Carrow might still be close enough to return to.

"Food now. Money later."

"That is how people get cheated."

"Then keep count."

"I will."

"I know."

He went inside. The servant actor leaned toward me.

"You're definitely theatre now."

"I am not."

"You asked about pay after getting on the wagon."

"That is bad practice."

"Yes."

I followed Teren inside. There were two steps. I disliked them immediately. Not because they were high. Because the first was shallow and the second was not, which was worse than two equally bad steps because it encouraged confidence before betrayal. I took the first. Adjusted. Took the second. The servant actor waited behind me without offering help. Good. Then he tripped over the threshold. Better.

The inn was warmer than outside and smelled of porridge, smoke, onions, horse leather, and people who had been traveling longer than we had. Long tables filled most of the room. A fire burned low in a wide hearth despite the day already warming.

Our troupe occupied one end of a table as if they had been starving for weeks. Bowls appeared. Bread appeared. Nobody seemed to know whether the bread belonged to us. They ate it anyway. I sat at the end where I could put my crutches against the wall. The older actor with the hat was already there. Without the hat over his face, he had a face. I had known this. I had seen him perform. Still, it felt strange.

He had a narrow gray beard and deep lines around his mouth. He was cutting an apple into slices with a small knife.

"Pel," he said.

I looked at him.

"What?"

"Seventh king."

"Oh."

He ate a slice. I had not asked.

"Was he good?"

"No."

"Why did he count?"

"Crown."

That was consistent. A bowl of porridge landed in front of me. The woman carrying it said, "Spoon."

"Yes."

She put one down.

"Anything else?"

"What is the animal on the sign?"

She stared at me.

"Food first."

Then she left. The older actor smiled at his apple.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You know."

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"Food first."

I ate. The porridge was better than the tea. This was not difficult. There were bits of onion and something salty in it, and the bread was fresh enough that I stopped worrying about whether it belonged to us. The recurring woman sat across from me. She had removed her blanket and looked more awake. The cabbage was gone.

"Did you finish it?"

"What?"

"The cabbage."

She looked under the table. Then beside her.

"Oh."

"You lost a cabbage?"

"It wasn't mine."

"You ate half."

"That doesn't establish ownership."

I looked at her. She smiled. Lyssa would like her. Probably. That thought arrived without warning. I ate more porridge. The guard actor came back with a second bowl.

"You already ate."

"No."

"I watched you."

"That was first breakfast."

"Are we walking to the village?"

"No."

"Then why do you need two breakfasts?"

He sat beside the recurring woman.

"Body."

That was apparently sufficient. Teren arrived last. He carried no food. Instead he had a conversation with the innkeeper in a low voice near the counter. I watched. Coins. Paper. The innkeeper shook his head. Teren said something. The innkeeper shook his head harder. Teren pointed toward us. The innkeeper looked at us. His expression did not improve.

"What is he doing?" I asked.

The servant actor tore bread with his teeth.

"Being Teren."

"Does that mean negotiating?"

"Sometimes."

"Does he owe money?"

"Always."

"That is not funny."

"It is if it isn't yours."

I kept watching. Teren produced another coin. The innkeeper took it. Teren returned to the table.

"What did that cover?" I asked.

He sat.

"Food."

"For everyone?"

"Yes."

"Then why did you say I probably owe you?"

"Because you probably do."

"How can both be true?"

He reached for bread. I moved it away. Teren looked at me.

"How much am I being paid?"

"Gregory."

"No."

The recurring woman said, "Oh, good." The guard actor kept eating. Teren leaned back.

"For the trip?"

"Yes."

"Depends how many shows."

"No."

"No?"

"You cannot answer a current pay question with a future number of shows unless there is a rate per show."

"There is."

"What?"

Teren looked around the table. Nobody helped him. The servant actor looked delighted. Teren named a number. I considered it.

"Per show?"

"Yes."

"Travel?"

"Food while moving. Sleeping if I arrange it. You buy anything else."

"Do I get paid if the third show doesn't happen?"

"For two shows."

"Yes."

"What if I perform Shopkeeper in addition to Sword?"

"That is still a show."

"What if I perform Clerk?"

"Still a show."

"So roles do not change pay."

"No."

"That encourages doing as little as possible."

The recurring woman raised her spoon.

"Finally."

Teren pointed at her.

"Don't."

I said, "What about loading?"

"What about it?"

"Work."

"You want to be paid for loading?"

"If loading is required."

"Do you want to load?"

"No."

"Then don't."

"That does not answer the principle."

The guard actor said, "Please stop." I looked at him. He had nearly finished the second bowl.

"Why?"

"I want to hear myself chew."

Teren tore off bread.

"You are being paid the same as the others for the shows. Food on travel days. Sleeping where I put you. If the town adds a show, you get another show payment."

"What if I don't appear in it?"

"Then no."

"Reasonable."

Teren blinked. Apparently he had expected more. I took the bread back and handed it to him.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

The servant actor looked disappointed. I ate. The innkeeper passed our table. I caught his attention.

"What animal is on the sign?"

He stopped. Everyone near me became quiet. The innkeeper looked toward the front door.

"Boar."

The servant actor said, "Bullshit." The innkeeper looked at him.

"You painted it?"

"My brother."

"Then your brother painted a fox."

The innkeeper's expression changed.

"Boar."

"It has a tail."

"Boars have tails."

"Not that tail."

The recurring woman said, "Horse." The innkeeper looked at her as if she had insulted his mother. The older actor continued eating apple.

"Dog," he said.

The servant actor pointed at him.

"Yes."

I said, "The tail is on fire." The innkeeper walked away. Nobody resolved it. That was probably best. Outside, the horses were being watered. The soot-shirt worker had one wheel blocked with a stone and was underneath the wagon again. I approached. He saw my crutches first. Then me.

"Don't."

"I haven't done anything."

"You have questions."

"Yes."

"Later."

"This is later."

He stopped moving under the wagon. That was satisfying. After a moment he slid out. His name remained a problem. I had spoken to him enough times that asking now would be embarrassing. That was not a reason not to ask. It was a reason I had not asked. He sat up and wiped grease from his fingers with a rag.

"What?"

"The knot."

He looked at the wagon rail.

"You remembered?"

"Yes."

"Good."

He reached for a loose piece of rope beside him. The rope was thicker than my cord and dirty enough that I did not want it near clothing. He looped it around the rail.

"Over."

I watched.

"Under."

"Yes."

"Back through."

"Yes."

He pulled. The knot tightened. Then he pulled the short end. It released. I stared.

"Again."

He did it again. Slower. I watched the path of the rope instead of his hands. Over. Around. Bight through. Load on one part. Release on another.

"Let me."

He handed me the rope. I put one crutch under my arm and used both hands. The first attempt was wrong. I knew before I pulled.

"Wrong."

"Yes."

I undid it. Second attempt. Also wrong. He did not speak. That was irritating. Third. I formed the loop, passed the bight, loaded it. It held. I pulled the release. It opened.

"Oh."

He took the rope.

"Done."

"That was three attempts."

"You know it."

"I can reproduce it once."

"Then reproduce it later."

"That is poor instruction."

"I fix wagons."

"What is your name?"

He looked at me. There. I had done it. He wiped his hand on the rag again and held it out.

"Davin."

I shook it.

"Greg."

"I know."

"Apparently."

Davin went back under the wagon. I stood there.

"Davin."

"No."

"I was only going to ask about the wheel."

"No."

Fine. I walked toward the well. A girl of perhaps ten was standing beside it staring at the first wagon. Specifically at the painted tower. She had a bucket in both hands. Her mother, or someone performing the role, was filling another bucket. The girl looked at me.

"Are you with them?"

"Yes."

"What are you?"

I considered.

"Passenger."

She frowned.

"That isn't in plays."

"Sometimes."

"Are you an actor?"

"No."

Davin laughed from under the wagon. I looked toward him. The girl followed my gaze.

"Are you?"

"Sometimes."

"What do you play?"

"A sword."

She stared at me.

"No."

"Yes."

"You don't look like a sword."

"I don't have the costume on."

This was technically true because there was no costume. She looked at my left trouser leg. Then at the crutches. Then back at my face.

"How?"

"With my voice."

"Oh."

She accepted this immediately. Children were better audiences than adults in some ways and much worse in others.

"Do you fight?"

"No."

"Swords fight."

"This one complains."

She smiled.

"What does it say?"

I looked toward the inn. Teren was nowhere visible. Good. I used the Sword voice.

"Put me down."

The girl's eyes widened. Her mother turned. I continued.

"You are holding me incorrectly."

The girl laughed. Her mother looked from me to the wagon.

"Is that from the show?"

"Not exactly."

"Do it again," the girl said.

I almost did. Then stopped. Not because of magic. Not because of rules. Because I could feel myself reaching for the exact sound that had made her laugh. Last one. I looked at the servant actor's observation from the wagon. You stopped trying. Annoying. The girl waited. I said, in the Sword voice, "No." She laughed harder. Oh. I smiled.

"Why no?" she asked.

"Contract dispute."

She did not understand that. Her mother did. The mother laughed. Different line. Different audience. Same sword. Maybe. I did not pursue it. The mother picked up both buckets.

"Come on."

The girl followed her. She turned once and waved. I waved back. Davin slid out from beneath the wagon.

"You're an actor."

"No."

"Fine."

He checked the wheel. I went to use the privy. It was behind the stable. There were three steps down into a narrow patch of mud, then one step up into a structure built by someone who hated knees. I stood at the top of the first step. This was not theatre. This was architecture. Architecture was worse. I managed. Nobody learned anything. Good.

By the time we loaded again, the sun was fully up and the inn yard had become crowded with carts. A merchant wanted our second wagon moved. Teren wanted the merchant to wait. The merchant had money. The innkeeper therefore wanted Teren to move. We moved. This involved everyone pretending to know where everything belonged. I tied my crutches to the rail with the slipped hitch. Davin walked past. He tugged it once. Did not add a knot. That was better than praise.

I sat on the blanket over the cup crate. The recurring woman climbed in with two apples.

"No cabbage?"

"Lost it."

"You ate it."

"Not all of it."

"Then where is the rest?"

She looked toward the inn.

"Traveling."

I decided not to ask. The servant actor returned to the first wagon because apparently the king had stopped snoring while awake. We rolled east. The second stretch of road was worse. Not dramatically worse. Persistently worse. Ruts came in pairs. Dust rose behind the first wagon and settled over us. The sun warmed the canvas and made the inside smell like cloth, wood, leather, old paint, apples, and the guard actor's boots.

"Your boots smell."

His eyes opened.

"No."

"That is not a matter of opinion."

He looked down.

"They're boots."

"They smell like something died inside them."

"Feet."

The recurring woman covered her nose with the edge of her blanket.

"Take them off."

"That would be worse."

She considered.

"Keep them on."

He smiled. I read page twelve again. This time I could manage it. Not because the road improved. Because I stopped trying to read every word. I knew the scene now. Permit. Denial. Existing permit. Therefore no need for permit. The logic was stupid. The clerk was not.

He understood the only important fact: the other person needed something from him. I read the lines silently. The recurring woman watched me.

"What?"

"You move your mouth."

"No."

"You do."

"I am not speaking."

"Still moving."

I stopped. She smiled.

"Do you know the clerk scene?"

"Yes."

"Who are you?"

"Apparently the clerk."

"No. Who are you with?"

I looked at the packet. There were names written above the scene. One had been crossed out. Another written in. Then crossed out. A third written smaller.

"I don't know."

She took the pages.

"That's Iven."

"Which?"

She pointed.

"The permit man."

"Who is Iven?"

"Guard."

I looked at the guard actor. He was asleep again.

"That is Iven?"

"Yes."

I had been traveling with him for five hours.

"Why did nobody tell me?"

"You didn't ask."

I looked at her.

"What is your name?"

She smiled slowly. Oh, fuck.

"You don't know."

"I know who you are."

"That is not my name."

"You've never told me."

"You've never asked."

"That is different."

"How?"

I considered several answers. None survived.

"Fine. What is your name?"

"Serra."

I repeated it.

"Serra."

"Yes."

"You knew mine."

"Everyone knows yours."

"That is becoming concerning."

She handed the pages back.

"Iven," I said, pointing across the wagon.

He opened one eye.

"What?"

"Your name is Iven."

"Yes."

"Good."

He closed his eye. Serra laughed. I looked toward the front wagon.

"The servant actor."

Serra frowned.

"Who?"

"The man who was king last night."

"Oh. Marek."

"Marek."

"Yes."

"Older actor?"

"Orin."

"Davin I know."

"You learned Davin before me?"

"He taught me a knot."

"I gave you a blanket."

"That did not require identification."

Serra threw an apple at me. I caught it against my chest. Barely.

"That could have gone under the wagon."

"Then you'd know where the cabbage went."

I ate the apple. Names changed things slightly. Not enough to explain. Iven remained asleep. Serra remained irritating. Marek was now in the first wagon somewhere, probably doing something unsafe. Orin's hat was visible above a stack of fabric. Davin rode beside the driver of the first wagon after apparently deciding the wheel would remain attached. Teren was not visible. This did not mean he had been lost. Probably. We stopped once because a strap broke. Not ours. The first wagon. Everyone got down except me and Iven.

Iven remained asleep. I stayed because nobody asked me to get down and because climbing in and out of the wagon for a five-minute repair seemed stupid. Davin and another crew worker pulled a chest sideways. Serra held a section of canvas out of the dirt. Marek stood on the wheel and passed rope over the load. Teren appeared from nowhere. Good. Still present. The repair took twelve minutes. I timed it. Not deliberately. I happened to know when we stopped.

When we started again, I noticed the broken strap had not been repaired. It had been replaced by rope arranged in a pattern I did not understand. I considered asking Davin later. Then stopped myself. Not every rope needed to become mine. That was a strange thought. I ate dried fruit. The road continued.

Around the sixth hour, conversation returned because everyone had slept enough to become annoying again. Marek came back to our wagon at a stop slow enough to cross without jumping. Progress. He sat beside Iven. Iven opened his eyes.

"No."

Marek sat anyway. Serra said, "Greg doesn't know anyone's name."

"I know names."

Marek looked at me.

"Mine?"

"Marek."

He seemed disappointed.

"Her?"

"Serra."

"Old man?"

"Orin."

From the first wagon, Orin's voice carried back.

"Not old."

I looked at Marek.

"How did he hear that?"

Marek shouted, "Old!" Orin raised one hand above the wagon side. One finger. Serra laughed. I did too.

"Davin," I said.

Marek nodded.

"Teren."

"Yes."

"Iven."

Iven said, "Unfortunately."

"Who am I missing?"

Marek looked around.

"Most of us."

"How many?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Show."

I sighed. Serra leaned toward me.

"You'll learn."

"I have been there for weeks."

"Yes."

"I know roles."

"Exactly."

That was true. Princess. Servant. Guard. Older actor. Soot-shirt worker. I knew what people did around me before I knew what they were called. That was not entirely unusual. It was still embarrassing.

"Do you know Lyssa's name?" Serra asked.

I looked at her.

"How do you know Lyssa?"

"You said it."

"When?"

"Sleeping."

I stared. Marek became very interested.

"I did not."

Serra smiled.

"You did."

"What did I say?"

"Lyssa."

"That is not evidence of anything."

"I didn't say it was."

"What else?"

"Nothing."

I looked at Iven. He shrugged.

"I was asleep."

"You are always asleep."

"Good system."

Marek said, "Who's Lyssa?"

"No one."

Serra's eyebrows rose. I corrected.

"Not no one."

Marek smiled.

"Excellent recovery."

"Fuck you."

"Is that Lyssa?"

"No."

Serra laughed so hard she had to turn away. I regretted learning their names. The village appeared late in the afternoon. At first it was only smoke beyond trees. Then roofs. Then a narrow road with houses pressed closer together than the farms we had passed all day. My body had stopped complaining individually. Everything had joined into one general objection. Shoulders. Back. Right hip. Hands slightly tired but not bad. Residual limb warm from sitting and wrapping and the day. No new pain. No new symptom. Just travel.

I knew enough now not to call ordinary discomfort nothing. I also knew enough not to turn it into prophecy. The wagons slowed. Children appeared first. They always did. One ran beside us for half the street, shouting questions at the painted tower. A dog barked at the horse. The horse ignored it.

A woman outside a shop recognized the theatre before she recognized any of us.

"Players?"

Marek leaned over the wagon side.

"Unfortunately."

She laughed.

"Performing here?"

"No."

"Then keep moving."

"We intend to."

Teren shouted from the first wagon, "Marek."

"What?"

"Stop advertising."

"I denied everything."

We turned into the yard behind an inn smaller than the crossroads place. Much smaller. I looked at the building. Then at the troupe. Then at the building again.

"No."

Serra followed my gaze.

"Yes."

"There are too many of us."

"Yes."

"There cannot be enough rooms."

"No."

"Where are we sleeping?"

She smiled.

"Now you're traveling."

The wagons stopped. I reached for my crutches. The slipped hitch released on the first pull. Good. I untied the second. Climbed down. My right leg nearly folded. Not fully. Enough that I caught myself hard on both crutches. Serra was beside me immediately but did not touch me.

"You good?"

"Yes."

I waited. My leg steadied.

"Again," she said.

"I said yes."

"You said it fast."

I looked at her. Hessa would like Serra. That was terrible. I shifted my weight carefully. Right leg held.

"Stiff."

"Good?"

"Not good. Ordinary."

She nodded. Then picked up her shoe bag and walked away. No hovering. Also good. The innkeeper came into the yard. Teren met him. They spoke. The innkeeper looked at the wagons. Then at us. Then at the inn. His expression became familiar.

"No," he said.

Teren said, "We booked."

"You booked eight."

"We are eight."

I counted. We were not eight. The innkeeper counted too.

"You are twelve."

Teren looked around as if four of us had appeared spontaneously. Marek raised his hand.

"I'm not staying."

Teren looked at him.

"Where are you going?"

"I don't know yet."

"That doesn't help."

"Iven can sleep outside."

Iven said, "No." Orin climbed down from the first wagon.

"Stable."

Everyone looked at him. He put on his hat.

"I've slept in worse."

Teren said, "No one is sleeping in the stable." The innkeeper said, "Two can." Teren turned.

"You just said no."

"I said you booked eight."

"How many rooms?"

"Three."

"How many beds?"

"Eight."

"How many stable spaces?"

The innkeeper looked toward the stable.

"For people?"

"Yes."

He considered.

"Four."

Teren looked at us. Nobody volunteered. I looked at the inn. There were stairs visible through the open door. Narrow ones. Steep. Of course. Marek followed my eyes.

"Stable?"

I looked at him.

"You just said you weren't staying."

"I found somewhere."

"Where?"

"Stable."

I laughed. Teren pointed at both of us.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because if the legendary sword gets kicked by a horse, I have paperwork."

"I am not sleeping with the horse."

"Good."

"I said with the horse."

Teren closed his eyes. Serra walked past him.

"I'm taking a bed."

"Serra."

"I brought shoes."

"That is not a reason."

"It is to me."

Iven followed her.

"Bed."

Orin said, "Stable." Marek said, "Stable." Davin said, "Wagon." Teren looked at him.

"You're not sleeping in the wagon."

"I am."

"You drove half the day."

"Yes."

"You need a bed."

"I need you to stop talking."

The innkeeper watched all of this. Then looked at me.

"What do you do?"

I leaned on my crutches.

"Passenger."

He nodded.

"Stable."

I laughed hard enough that my shoulders hurt. The road had been awful. The inn was too small. My body was tired. Nobody knew where I was sleeping. Tomorrow we would do it again. I had been away from Carrow for less than a day. It felt farther than that. Not worse. Just farther. Teren was still arguing when I picked up my bag. I went to inspect the stable.
