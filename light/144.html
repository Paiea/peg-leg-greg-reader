TEXT READER · CHAPTER 144

THE STABLE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The stable was better than the inn. This was not praise. It was larger. The roof did not leak in any place I could immediately identify. The floor was packed earth with straw over it, and the horses occupied stalls along one wall behind wooden partitions polished by years of rubbing shoulders and teeth against them. It smelled exactly like a stable. Strongly. Marek stood in the middle aisle with his hands on his hips.

"Good."

"No."

"You haven't seen the loft."

"I don't need to."

"There's hay."

"That is generally where hay goes."

Orin passed us carrying his bag.

"Loft is warmer."

I looked up. A ladder rose through a square opening in the ceiling.

"No."

Orin followed my eyes.

"For you, yes."

"No."

"I meant no loft."

"Oh."

He kept walking. Marek looked up at the ladder, then at me.

"Could carry you."

I stared at him. He smiled.

"I could kill you."

"From down here?"

"I have reach."

He laughed and went to inspect a stall. I moved farther inside. The first empty stall had a latch I could operate easily and enough clear floor for a bedroll. The second had a broken board near the bottom. The third contained tack, two sacks, a bucket, and something under a cloth that I did not investigate because travel had already taught me that unidentified objects became my problem if I looked at them too long. The stable door was wide. The privy was across the yard. No stairs. Good.

The inn had stairs to every room. Stable. I had slept in worse places. That sentence carried more history than I wanted at the moment. I set my bag in the first empty stall. Marek appeared behind me.

"You picked."

"Yes."

"Good one."

"Why?"

"Far from Orin."

Orin's voice came from the next stall.

"I can hear you."

Marek lowered his voice.

"He snores."

"I do not."

"Everyone snores."

"I don't," I said.

Serra's voice came from the stable entrance.

"Lyssa."

I closed my eyes. Marek laughed. I turned. Serra had changed shirts. I had not seen her leave the yard. Her hair was loose now, and she carried a shallow wooden basin under one arm with two damp cloths hanging over the edge.

"You're in the stable?"

"Yes."

She looked at my stall.

"Good."

Marek pointed at me.

"He chose it himself."

"I assumed."

"Why?"

"Because nobody else would inspect three stalls before putting down a bag."

"I inspected two."

Serra looked toward the third.

"What is in that one?"

"I don't know."

"You didn't inspect it?"

"Something under a cloth."

Marek immediately walked toward it.

"No," I said.

He stopped.

"Why?"

"I don't want to know."

Serra nodded.

"Good."

Marek looked between us.

"You've both become boring."

He lifted the cloth. A saddle. He stared at it. I stared at him.

"Happy?"

"No."

He put the cloth back. Serra went toward the inn.

"Where are you going?"

She looked over her shoulder.

"Work."

"What work?"

She raised the basin. I waited.

"That explains nothing."

"It doesn't need to."

She left. Marek watched her go. Then looked at me.

"You ask many questions."

"Yes."

"Does Lyssa like that?"

I picked up a handful of straw. He moved. Good. The sleeping problem was solved in less than ten minutes. Not by Teren. Teren was still inside arguing with the innkeeper about the difference between booking eight beds and booking space for eight people, which appeared to be a distinction only Teren recognized. Davin solved it.

He walked into the stable, looked at the loft, looked at the stalls, then pointed.

"Orin loft. Marek loft. Greg stall. Me wagon."

"Why are you deciding?" Marek asked.

"Because you're talking."

Marek accepted that.

"Who else?"

"Pell and Nessa stable."

I did not know either of them.

"Who?"

Davin pointed toward the yard. A broad woman with cropped brown hair was carrying a costume trunk by one handle while a younger man carried the other. They were arguing. Not about the trunk.

"You changed it."

"I fixed it."

"You cut six inches."

"It dragged."

"It was supposed to drag."

"Then it was stupid."

"It was a mourning dress."

"She's mourning. She isn't sweeping."

The woman stopped. The younger man nearly kept going and had to back up.

"You do not cut a principal costume without asking."

"I asked."

"You asked after."

"I asked whether you noticed."

"That is not asking."

Davin pointed again.

"Pell. Nessa."

"Which is which?"

He looked at me.

"The one yelling is Nessa."

Both were yelling. Davin walked away. Useful. The broad woman saw us.

"Davin!"

He did not turn.

"You cut this?"

"No."

"Who did?"

The younger man said, "I did." She rounded on him again. Davin continued toward the wagon. I watched. That argument had started somewhere before the village. Probably before Carrow. Possibly before I had ever walked into the theatre. Neither person knew I existed. Good. I moved my bag deeper into the stall. Marek had gone. Orin was unfolding a blanket in the next stall.

"Who are Pell and Nessa?"

"Costumes."

"That is not names."

"Yes."

"Which?"

Orin looked toward the yard.

"Pell is the boy."

"He is not a boy."

"Everyone under thirty is a boy."

"How old are you?"

"Not old."

He shook out the blanket. I left him alone. My stall needed almost nothing. That was suspicious. I found a nail on the partition where I could hang my bag. Another lower hook held a coil of old lead rope. I moved that. There was a feed box in the corner, low enough to sit on but not wide enough to sleep on. Straw. Blanket. Bag. Crutches against the partition where I could reach them from the ground. The getting-down part would be manageable.

The getting-up part would be annoying. I tested it before becoming tired enough to make the test dishonest. I lowered myself to the blanket. Right foot planted. One hand on the feed box. Other on a crutch. Down. Fine. I sat. The floor was hard beneath the straw. I lay back.

The stable roof had dark beams crossing it and a gap near the eaves where evening light came through. A horse exhaled. Something small moved in the straw. I sat up. No. Not because of the horse. The something small. I looked. Nothing. Probably mouse. Fine. I had slept with worse. Again. History. I reached for the crutches and got up. Harder than down. Not impossible. My shoulders objected.

I ignored them only long enough to finish standing, then stopped ignoring them. Tired. Expected. I adjusted my grip. No sharp pain. Good. A horse in the nearest occupied stall watched me.

"What?"

It chewed.

"Useful."

It kept chewing. The stable door opened. A girl came in carrying two buckets. Not the crossroads girl. Different child. She looked at me. Then at the horse.

"Move."

I moved. She took the buckets into the stall. The horse immediately became interested in her.

"You work here?"

She looked at me like the answer was visible.

"Yes."

"Which horse is ours?"

She pointed with her chin.

"Those two."

"How are they?"

"Tired."

"Anything wrong?"

"No."

"Wheel horse?"

"What?"

"Nothing."

I had been around Davin too long. She poured water.

"Don't feed them."

"I wasn't going to."

"People do."

"Why?"

"People are stupid."

She could not have been more than twelve.

"Fair."

She moved to the next horse. I returned to my stall. She did not ask why I had one leg. That was pleasant. She did ask, "You actors?"

"Some."

"You?"

"Apparently."

She shrugged. Less interesting than horses. Also pleasant. Dinner happened in shifts because the inn did not have room for all of us at once. I learned this when Marek appeared at the stable door and shouted, "Second food!"

"What?"

"Second group."

"I ate at the crossroads."

"So did everyone."

"That was hours ago."

"Yes."

I looked at the light. It was later than I thought. Travel did strange things to time. We went inside. The two steps remained badly designed. I hated them with greater confidence now. The room had changed since arrival. Local people filled half the tables. A farmer and two women were playing cards near the fire. Someone had brought a baby. The baby was winning an argument with everyone. Our first group was leaving. Serra came down the stairs carrying a bundle of cloth. Not dinner. Not socializing. Cloth.

"Where were you?"

"Upstairs."

"Doing what?"

She looked at the bundle. I sighed.

"Fine."

She stopped beside me.

"You know the blue dress?"

"No."

"Yes, you do."

"I know several blue dresses."

"The court one."

"Princess?"

"Not mine. The other one."

"Then no."

"Pell cut the hem."

"I heard."

Serra frowned.

"You heard?"

"Stable."

"Oh."

She looked toward the door.

"Nessa is going to kill him."

"Which is Nessa?"

"Costumes."

"Yes. Which person?"

Serra pointed toward the stairs. A narrow woman with dark hair was coming down carrying pins in her mouth. Not the broad woman. I looked at Serra.

"Davin lied."

"About what?"

"He said the one yelling was Nessa."

Serra laughed.

"Everyone was yelling."

"Yes."

"Nessa."

The narrow woman reached the bottom. She removed the pins from her mouth.

"Serra."

"What?"

"Where is Pell?"

"Stable?"

"No."

"Wagon?"

"No."

"Dead?"

"Not yet."

Nessa went out the door. Serra watched her.

"Soon."

"Who was the broad woman?"

"Rinna."

"What does she do?"

"Depends."

Of course. Serra went upstairs again with the cloth. She had not eaten. I noticed because I was hungry. That was probably not the only reason. Marek pushed me toward the table by placing one finger against my shoulder. I moved before he could make it annoying. Dinner was stew. Real stew. Meat, potatoes, carrots, something green. Iven was already seated. Awake. This was concerning.

"You're awake."

He looked at me.

"Eating."

"Does that require consciousness?"

"No."

He ate. Orin sat beside him. Davin was not there. Neither was Teren. Marek took the bench across from me.

"Where is Teren?"

"Problem."

"What problem?"

"Money."

"His or ours?"

"Yes."

I looked toward the counter. Teren was speaking to a man I had not seen before. The man wore a dark coat too clean for the stable yard and held a folded paper. Neither looked angry. That made it more serious. Teren read the paper. Read it again. Said something. The man answered. Teren's shoulders changed. Not much. Enough.

"What is that?" I asked.

Marek looked.

"Oh."

"Oh what?"

"Don't know."

"You reacted."

"I know him."

"The man?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

Marek tore bread.

"Owner's agent."

"Whose owner?"

"Hall."

"Here?"

"No. East."

I stopped.

"The municipal hall?"

"No."

"Then which hall?"

"The one we're performing in."

I looked at him.

"We are not performing in the municipal hall?"

"No."

I had assumed. That was stupid. The theatre was going to the eastern town. The municipal hall was in the eastern town. I had combined them.

"They're different buildings."

"Yes."

"How far apart?"

"No idea."

"Do you know anything about the municipal hall?"

"No."

I ate stew. Fine. Still there. Different building. Good.

"What does the agent want?"

Marek shrugged.

"Teren."

"That is not an answer."

"It is from here."

He pointed with bread. Teren and the agent were still talking. Nobody at our table seemed interested. Orin ate. Iven ate. Marek stole one of Iven's potatoes. Iven stabbed his hand with a spoon. Marek withdrew. Teren's problem remained Teren's problem. I watched anyway. The agent unfolded another paper. Teren laughed once. Not happily. Then he saw me looking. He pointed at my bowl. Eat. I looked down. Fine. I ate. Halfway through dinner, Nessa came back in. She had Pell by the sleeve.

Pell was younger than I had thought. Still not a boy. Maybe twenty-five. He looked furious. Nessa sat him at the far end of the table.

"You will fix it."

"I improved it."

"You shortened it."

"It was dragging in dirt."

"It drags on stage."

"The stage isn't dirt."

"Exactly."

Pell looked at me. Not because he knew me. Because I was there.

"Do you think a mourning dress should drag?"

I had a spoon halfway to my mouth.

"No."

Nessa said, "Don't ask him."

"You asked me."

"I did not."

Pell pointed at me.

"He agrees."

"I don't know the dress."

"See?"

"That is not support."

Nessa said, "It is cut for the actress's height, the shoe, and the scene. It drags by design. The hem moves when she turns." Pell said, "It catches."

"Because you put her in the wrong shoe."

"I didn't put her in anything."

"You packed the shoe."

"That is not the same thing."

Rinna appeared behind them. Broad woman. Not Nessa. Good. She put a plate down.

"You packed the rehearsal shoe."

Pell looked at her.

"No."

"Yes."

"That was Serra."

From the stairs, Serra shouted, "Fuck you." Rinna shouted back, "Blue ribbon!" Serra's voice came down.

"Black ribbon!"

Nessa closed her eyes. Pell smiled. Rinna looked toward the ceiling.

"Shit."

The argument changed direction. Immediately. Now it was about shoes. I ate. Nobody asked me anything else. Excellent. After dinner I went back outside. The light had softened. The village street beyond the yard was still active, though quieter than Carrow would have been at the same hour. A cart rolled past carrying empty baskets. Somewhere nearby, a man was singing badly while hammering something.

Davin sat on the rear wheel of our wagon with a plate balanced on one knee. He was eating cold stew.

"You missed dinner."

"No."

"You're eating outside."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He pointed at the wagon. One of the canvas ties had torn. Of course.

"You could eat inside."

"No."

I stood beside him. He kept eating. The tear was small. A crew problem. Not mine. I looked away from it deliberately. Davin noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You looked."

"I decided not to ask."

"Good."

He ate. I stayed.

"Why are you still here?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Good."

We sat in silence. Or he sat. I stood. That became irritating after a while, so I moved to an overturned bucket. Better. The inn door opened. Teren came out with the clean-coated agent. They crossed the yard without looking at us. The agent said, "He wants the old terms."

"He can want them," Teren said.

"He owns the hall."

"He owns a roof and a floor."

"And the seats."

"We bring half the seats."

"You bring benches."

"Seats for people with realistic expectations."

The agent did not laugh. They went toward the road. I looked at Davin. He ate.

"What old terms?"

Davin scraped the bottom of the plate.

"Don't know."

"You heard."

"Yes."

"And?"

"Don't know."

Fair. The agent and Teren stopped near the gate. I could not hear them now. Teren had both hands in his coat pockets. The agent talked. Teren listened. This was different from Teren talking to actors. He did not interrupt. Not once. I wondered how bad that was. Davin stood.

"Hold this."

He handed me the plate. I took it. He climbed into the wagon and started untying the torn canvas. I held the plate.

"This is work."

"Yes."

"You said I didn't have to load."

"This isn't loading."

"What is it?"

"Holding a plate."

I considered dropping it. I did not. Davin replaced the torn tie with cord. Not the slipped hitch. Different knot. I watched despite myself. He noticed.

"Don't."

"I didn't ask."

"Your face did."

"I have one face."

"No."

He climbed down and took the plate. I laughed.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome."

Teren came back alone. He walked past us.

"Teren."

He stopped.

"What?"

I had several questions. Hall. Terms. Agent. Seats. Whether our shows were changing. Whether this affected pay. Whether the third show existed. He looked tired. Not road tired. Different.

"Nothing," I said.

He nodded once and went inside. Davin looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Good. The stable became crowded after dark. Not with sleepers. With work. Apparently the innkeeper objected to costume repairs in the rooms because Nessa had spilled dye in one before.

"Once," Nessa said.

The innkeeper stood in the stable doorway.

"Purple."

"It came out."

"It is still purple."

"It is less purple."

"No dye."

"We are sewing."

"No fire."

"We don't need fire."

"No paint."

"That was Rinna."

Rinna said, "Once." The innkeeper looked at all of them.

"No."

Then he left. Nessa set up on an overturned crate. Rinna sat on a stool. Pell sat on the floor with the mourning dress spread across his knees. Serra brought down three pairs of shoes and placed them in a row. I sat in my stall and watched the stable become a workshop. Nobody invited me. Nobody needed to. Marek lay in the straw outside Orin's stall and shuffled cards. Iven had disappeared. Davin was still outside. Teren was somewhere in the inn. The costume argument continued without repeating itself.

This was impressive.

"The black ribbon is the performance shoe," Serra said.

"No," Rinna said. "Black ribbon is second act."

"Second act is the performance."

"Blue ribbon is court."

"The mourning scene is court."

"No. After court."

Pell held up the dress.

"Does any of this matter if I put the six inches back?"

Nessa looked at him.

"How?"

He hesitated.

"Fabric."

"Which fabric?"

He hesitated longer. Nessa smiled without warmth.

"Exactly."

Pell put the dress down. I looked at the hem. It had been cut cleanly. Very cleanly. That seemed like part of the problem. Marek dealt cards onto the straw.

"One."

I looked at him.

"What?"

"Playing?"

"No."

He dealt another hand anyway. Orin emerged from his stall.

"Yes."

"You don't know the game."

"I know all games."

Marek looked at me.

"He cheats."

Orin sat.

"Only when losing."

"That is cheating."

"Then don't let me lose."

I joined. The game involved four cards, two hidden, one shared, and rules Marek explained differently each time I asked.

"This is not a real game."

"It is."

"Then explain scoring."

"Highest pair unless road."

"What is road?"

"Three in sequence."

"You said four cards."

"Shared counts."

"Then why did Orin win with two queens?"

"Pair."

"I had three in sequence."

"No."

"Yes."

"You had four, five, six."

"Road."

"Wrong suit."

"You did not say suit mattered."

"Obviously suit matters."

Orin took the coins. I stared at him. He smiled.

"You cheat."

"I won."

"Those are not mutually exclusive."

Marek dealt again. Serra sat beside us while Nessa and Pell worked. She took my cards from my hand.

"Hey."

"You're holding them where everyone can see."

"I am not."

"I can see."

"You are beside me."

"So can Orin."

Orin said nothing. That was evidence. Serra rearranged my cards and gave them back.

"Don't help him," Marek said.

"He needs it."

"I do not."

"You lost to Orin."

"Apparently everyone does."

"Correct," Orin said.

We played. I lost again. Less badly. The costume people argued. A horse shifted in its stall. The stable girl came through once to check water and looked at all of us with open contempt.

"You're loud."

Nessa said, "Sorry." Marek said, "We're artists." The girl said, "You're loud." Then she left. Marek looked offended.

"Children used to respect theatre."

Orin took another coin.

"No."

At some point Serra stopped playing and went back to the shoes. At some point Rinna and Pell stopped arguing and started sewing. At some point Nessa left without explanation and came back with bread. She handed half to Pell. He took it. Neither apologized. The mourning dress lay across both their knees while they worked. I watched the hem grow again. Not six inches. Different fabric. A narrow strip on the inside. It would not be the same dress it had been. Maybe that was the point.

I lost another hand.

"Fuck."

Marek grinned.

"You're improving."

"I lost."

"Closer."

"That is not improvement."

Orin took my coin.

"It is in theatre."

I looked at him. He smiled. I did not ask. Later, when the stable had quieted, I washed. Not fully. There was a basin behind a partition near the tack room and water cold enough to hurt my fingers. I cleaned road dust from my face, neck, hands, and right foot. Then I took care of the residual limb. Private enough. Not completely. Travel rarely was.

I sat on the feed box in my stall with the blanket hung partly across the opening. The skin was warm and irritated in two places but intact. I checked properly. Not Hessa-properly. Greg-properly. No breakdown. No swelling I disliked. No new pain. I cleaned it. Dried it. Rewrapped. Outside the blanket, people continued having lives. Marek and Iven argued quietly about who had cheated at cards. Orin denied being involved. Serra was upstairs again. Nessa and Rinna had taken the repaired dress inside. Pell had gone somewhere.

Davin still intended to sleep in the wagon. Teren had not appeared for over an hour. Nobody asked what I was doing. Good. When I finished, I moved the blanket. Marek was sitting against the opposite stall wall. He looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

He went back to shuffling cards. I pulled on my trouser leg.

"Where is Teren?"

"Meeting."

"With the agent?"

"Probably."

"At night?"

"Hall owners don't become nicer in daylight."

I considered asking what the old terms were. I didn't. Instead I said, "Do we still have two shows?" Marek shrugged.

"Ask Teren."

"When?"

"When he knows."

That was the end of it. Interesting. My acting problem had become very small. I liked that less than expected. I took out page twelve. Marek glanced at it.

"Clerk?"

"Yes."

"Don't."

"I am only reading."

"Fine."

I read. He shuffled. After several lines, he said, "Too fast." I looked up.

"You said don't."

"I changed my mind."

"Why too fast?"

He dealt cards to himself.

"Don't know."

"That is not useful."

"Then ignore me."

I read again. Slower. Not because he said. Because Lyssa had. The clerk had nowhere to be. The permit seeker did. Marek stopped commenting. I finished the page.

"Better?"

He shrugged. I hated actors. I put the script away. The stable lights went out by degrees. One lantern remained near the door. Orin climbed to the loft despite claiming the stall earlier. Marek followed. The ladder creaked.

"Don't fall," I said.

Marek looked down.

"Catch me."

"No."

"Then don't tell me what to do."

He disappeared into the loft. Iven had somehow acquired a bed inside. Traitor. Pell took the other stable stall. Rinna went inside. Nessa went inside. Serra had a room. Davin climbed into the wagon. I was alone on the stable floor except for Pell, horses, and whatever small thing lived in the straw. I lay down. The floor remained hard. The straw remained straw. My shoulders remained tired. I put my bag beneath my head. Bad. I replaced it with folded clothing. Better. The horse nearest me shifted. Leather creaked.

Someone in the loft whispered. Someone else said, "Shut up." Silence.

Then:

"Lyssa."

I sat up. Marek laughed from the loft.

"Fuck you."

Orin said, "Sleep." I lay back down. I was smiling. That was irritating. The stable door opened. Teren came in carrying no lantern. He stopped until his eyes adjusted. I could barely see him.

"Teren?"

"Go to sleep."

"What happened?"

"Tomorrow."

"Are the shows still happening?"

A pause.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"Gregory."

"Fine."

He moved farther into the stable. Not toward a stall. Toward the ladder.

"Marek," he said quietly.

No answer.

"Marek."

"What?"

"Come down."

"Why?"

"Need you."

There was movement above. Marek appeared at the opening.

"For what?"

"Tomorrow."

That was all. Marek came down. He did not joke. Teren looked toward the stall where Pell slept.

"Pell."

A groan.

"What?"

"Up."

"Why?"

"Costume."

Pell swore. He got up. Neither Teren nor Marek looked at me. Good. They went outside. Pell followed, still pulling on one boot. The stable door closed. I lay in the dark. Whatever had happened with the hall was not mine. Not yet. I listened to the horses breathe. After a while, I slept.
