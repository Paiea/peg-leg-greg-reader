TEXT READER · CHAPTER 145

THE ARRIVAL

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I woke to someone saying, "No." Not loudly. Firmly. The kind of no that had already been said several times. I opened my eyes. Gray morning light came through the stable door. My shoulder hurt because folded clothing was not a pillow no matter how carefully I arranged it. Straw had found its way inside my shirt. One horse was chewing. The voice came from outside.

"No," Nessa said again. "If you put him in that, he looks like a priest."

Pell answered, "He is supposed to look respectable."

"Respectable is not priest."

"It has a collar."

"Exactly."

I lay still. There was movement in the yard. Harness leather. Buckles. A bucket set down hard. Someone coughed. Marek said, "I could be a priest."

"No," Nessa and Pell said together.

I smiled. Then I remembered they had been awake after I went to sleep. I sat up. My body objected. Not one part. All of it. The objection was less severe than yesterday's first attempt to stand after the wagon, but more comprehensive. Back, shoulders, right hip, hands. My residual limb felt warm under the wrapping. I sat for a moment. The horse chewed.

"Good morning."

It ignored me. I checked the limb before standing. No new problem. Warm. Mild irritation. Same places. Fine. Not nothing. Fine. I dressed properly, repacked the blanket, and used the feed box to get upright. The stable was mostly empty. Orin's loft blanket was gone. Pell's stall held a heap of fabric and one shoe. Not his shoe. Probably. I took my bag and went outside. The yard had changed. Yesterday we had arrived as a pile. This morning we were becoming wagons again.

Davin and Rinna were already loading. Rinna stood inside the second wagon arranging trunks while Davin passed them up. Neither spoke much. I carried my bag toward them. Davin looked at it.

"Keep."

"What?"

"With you."

"Why?"

"Need room."

"For?"

He pointed at a long wooden box near the inn wall. I had not seen it yesterday. It was narrow, nearly as long as I was tall, with iron corners and fresh rope around it.

"What is that?"

"Box."

"I can see that."

"Good."

He lifted one end. Rinna took the other. I moved aside. They carried it toward the first wagon.

"Can I help?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Rinna knows where it goes."

Rinna did not look at me. Good. I kept my bag. Near the stable door, Nessa had Marek standing in a dark coat that did, in fact, make him look slightly like a priest. He looked tired. Not wagon tired. He had shadows under his eyes and a small cut near his jaw I had not seen yesterday. Pell knelt at his feet pinning the coat hem. Nessa stood back.

"No."

Marek looked down at himself.

"I like it."

"You like everything if someone tells you not to."

"Not true."

"Take it off."

Pell looked up.

"Then what?"

"The gray."

"The gray is packed."

"Unpack it."

"Davin will kill me."

"Then die usefully."

Pell stood. He saw me.

"Morning."

"Morning."

That was all. He went toward the wagon. I looked at Marek.

"What happened to your face?"

He touched the cut.

"Oh."

"Yes."

"Door."

"What door?"

Nessa pulled the coat off him.

"Don't."

I looked at her. She bundled the coat over one arm.

"He'll tell it badly."

"I can tell my own injury."

"You walked into a door."

Marek looked at me.

"Door."

Fine.

"What were you doing?"

Nessa said, "Greg."

"What?"

"Food."

She pointed toward the inn. I looked at Marek. He smiled.

"Food."

I went. Teren was not at breakfast. Neither was Serra. That was noticeable because Serra's place from dinner had a cup sitting at it, untouched. Iven was eating eggs. Of course. Orin had bread, cheese, and something pickled. I sat.

"Where is everyone?"

Iven looked around.

"Here."

"No."

"Yes."

"Teren."

"Gone."

"Where?"

He ate egg. Orin said, "Town."

"What town?"

"The one we're going to."

I stopped.

"Teren left already?"

"Agent had a horse."

"When?"

"Early."

"Why?"

Orin spread something on bread.

"Problem."

"What problem?"

He looked at me. I waited.

"Hall."

"Yes. What about it?"

He bit the bread.

"I don't know."

Iven said, "Door." I looked at him.

"What?"

"Marek."

"Yes."

"Door."

"I know."

"He walked into it."

"I know."

Iven smiled. This was useless. I ate. The eggs were good. The innkeeper passed and put a small paper on the table.

"Your man owes."

I looked at it.

"Which man?"

He pointed toward the yard. There were several men.

"Teren?"

"Yes."

"For?"

"Four extra."

"Stable?"

"People."

I looked at the paper.

"Why are you giving this to me?"

"He left."

"I am not responsible for him."

The innkeeper looked at my crutches. Then at me. Then at the paper. I slid it toward Orin. Orin slid it back.

"No."

"I don't know Teren's accounts."

"Neither do I."

Iven moved his plate away from the paper. The innkeeper said, "Someone pays." I looked toward the door. Davin was visible through it, lifting the long box.

"No."

The innkeeper waited. I considered paying simply to end the conversation. That was exactly how one became responsible for things.

"No," I said.

He took the paper.

"Your company."

"I am a passenger."

Iven laughed into his eggs. The innkeeper walked away. Orin looked at me.

"Learning."

"Nothing was learned."

"Good."

I ate the rest of breakfast. Serra came in as I finished. She was dressed for travel but carried no food. There was a dark mark on one sleeve and her hands smelled faintly of something sharp when she reached for my cup.

"That is mine."

She drank.

"What is on your sleeve?"

"Dye."

"I thought the innkeeper banned dye."

"He did."

I waited. Serra drank again.

"Did you dye something?"

"No."

"Then why are you covered in dye?"

"Nessa."

"That explains almost nothing."

"It explains enough."

She put the cup down.

"Do you have food?"

"I ate."

"For the road."

"Yes."

"Good."

She turned to leave.

"Serra."

"What?"

"Where were you?"

She looked at me.

"When?"

"Breakfast."

"Busy."

"With?"

She smiled.

"Gregory."

Fine. She left. I sat for another moment. Other people had apparently completed half a day before I finished eggs. I did not like mornings. Outside, Pell was carrying the gray coat. Marek wore a shirt. Better. Nessa had moved on to someone else entirely.

A woman I had not met stood on a crate while Nessa adjusted a red overskirt. Rinna argued with Davin about whether a trunk could be turned sideways.

"Not if the lid opens."

"It doesn't need to open."

"It will."

"When?"

"Before yours."

"That is not an answer."

"It is to me."

Davin turned the trunk sideways. Rinna swore. I went to the second wagon. The cup crate was gone. I stopped. My seat. Not a good seat. Still. Gone. In its place was a narrow bench attached along the side. I looked at Davin.

"What happened?"

"Cups moved."

"Why?"

"Box."

The long box was now in the first wagon.

"That is not this wagon."

"No."

"Then why did the cups move?"

"Weight."

"What weight?"

Davin pointed at the rear axle. I looked. Then at him.

"Did something change?"

"Yes."

"What?"

He walked away. I hated opacity. I put my bag under the new bench. My crutches went on the same rail. Slipped hitch. First try. I pulled the release. Opened. Retied. Good. Nobody noticed. Better. Marek climbed into the wagon wearing the gray coat. I stared. He sat across from me.

"Why are you dressed?"

"Because Nessa hates joy."

"Are you performing in the wagon?"

"Maybe."

"That is not funny."

"I know."

He leaned his head back. His eyes closed. I looked at the cut again. Door. Fine. Serra climbed in next. Then immediately climbed out.

"Wrong wagon."

"Where are you going?"

"First."

"Why?"

"Shoes."

Of course. She disappeared. Iven arrived with bread.

"Move."

"There is an entire bench."

"Your bag."

I moved it. He sat. Orin chose the first wagon. Pell rode with us, carrying a sewing kit on his lap. Nessa did not ride. I noticed when the first wagon started moving.

"Where is Nessa?"

Pell threaded a needle.

"Staying."

I looked back. Nessa stood in the inn yard beside Rinna. Rinna was staying too. They had three trunks between them.

"What?"

Pell bit the thread.

"Coming later."

"How?"

"Cart."

"Why?"

"Work."

I waited. Pell started sewing something gray.

"Mourning dress?"

"No."

"What?"

"Coat."

"Marek's?"

"No."

I stopped asking. The wagons rolled out of the village. Teren was gone ahead. Nessa and Rinna were behind. Davin drove the first wagon with someone else beside him. Our driver was a man I had not learned the name of. The troupe had become pieces. Nobody had asked me whether that was acceptable. It was. Mostly. The second day road was shorter and better. Not good. Better.

The land rose gradually. Fields gave way to more trees, then open stretches again. We crossed a narrow stone bridge over water clear enough to see the bottom. Marek slept. Iven ate. Pell sewed. I watched the road. After an hour, I said, "What happened with the hall?" Nobody answered. I looked at Pell. He kept sewing.

"Marek?"

His eyes stayed closed.

"Door."

"That is not what I asked."

"Good."

Iven smiled. I looked at Pell again.

"Were you working on something for the hall last night?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"Costume."

"I know that."

He held up the gray fabric.

"Then why ask?"

I sighed. Pell looked at me. Not unfriendly. Just tired.

"Teren will tell you if it changes you."

"That is a terrible sentence."

"It works."

"Does it change Marek?"

"Yes."

Marek opened one eye.

"Unfortunately."

"How?"

Pell said, "Greg."

"What?"

"I'm trying not to stab myself."

I stopped. He sewed. That was fair. A while later Marek woke on his own. He stretched, winced at the cut on his jaw, and looked out at the road.

"Where are we?"

Pell named a farm. Marek nodded as if that meant something.

"You know it?"

"No."

"Then why did you nod?"

"Polite."

He looked at me.

"You're staring."

"I have questions."

"No."

"You don't know them."

"I know you."

"Fine."

I looked at the gray coat.

"Why are you dressed?"

Marek looked down.

"Oh."

"Yes."

"Meeting."

"With?"

"Hall."

"Owner?"

"Maybe."

"Why you?"

He smiled.

"Face."

Pell snorted. Marek looked offended.

"What?"

"Your face walked into a door."

"It remains handsome."

Pell kept sewing. I looked between them.

"Are you pretending to be someone?"

Both stopped. Not dramatically. Enough. Interesting. Marek said, "No." Pell said, "Don't." I held up both hands.

"Fine."

They resumed. I looked at the road. I had learned something. Not what. Good enough. Around midday, the eastern town appeared from a ridge. Not all at once. First a church tower. Then roofs. Then a pale strip of wall and clustered chimneys in a shallow valley where two roads met. Larger than I had expected. Smaller than Carrow.

The roofs were mostly red or brown tile rather than dark slate. A river curved along the southern edge, broad enough for barges. Beyond it, low hills rose in patches of green and exposed stone. I leaned forward. The municipal hall would be somewhere in there. Probably. I did not know what it looked like. That was inconvenient. The road descended. Traffic increased. Farm carts. A wagon of timber. Two riders. A woman walking six geese. The geese had better road discipline than actors.

We passed through an outer market without a wall or gate. Stalls had spread along both sides of the road. Someone sold copper pans. Someone else sold onions in braided ropes. A boy carried a tray of cups between wagons and nearly lost all of them when our horse shifted. I reached instinctively. Too far. The cups survived without me. Good. People noticed the scenery. A painted tower was difficult to hide. Children followed for half a street. One shouted, "Players!" Marek opened his eyes.

"No."

The child laughed. Apparently that worked everywhere. The town tightened around us. Stone buildings, timber upper floors, narrow fronts, signs projecting over the street. The road changed from packed dirt to uneven paving. My body recognized the improvement before my eyes did. The wagon still shook. Different frequency. Better. We turned twice. I lost direction immediately. That annoyed me. I had studied maps of the region years ago. Maps did not include a bakery cart blocking an alley. The first wagon stopped. Ours stopped behind it. I looked ahead.

A man in an apron was shouting at Davin. Davin pointed at the wagon. The man pointed at the alley. Davin pointed at the painted tower. The man pointed at the sky. I could not hear the words.

"Problem?" I asked.

Our driver said, "Bakery." That was his first contribution to the trip.

"Thank you."

Marek climbed down.

"Where are you going?"

"Meeting."

"We aren't there."

"I am."

He walked into the alley. Pell grabbed the back of his coat.

"Not that way."

Marek stopped. Pell pointed across the street. Marek changed direction. No shame. They disappeared between buildings. I looked at Iven.

"Do you know where they're going?"

"No."

"Good."

The bakery cart moved. We continued. The performance hall was on a wider street near the river. I knew it was the performance hall because Teren was standing outside it arguing. Comforting. The building was older than the shops around it but not ancient. Two stories. Broad front doors. Tall windows. A faded carved emblem above the entrance that might once have been painted. A man in a clean dark coat stood with Teren. The agent. Another man stood beside him. Older. Better coat. Owner, probably. Teren saw the wagons.

He did not wave. He pointed toward the side street. Davin turned the first wagon. We followed. That was the entirety of our welcome. The side entrance was narrow. Not catastrophically. Enough. The first wagon stopped beside a double door. Crew appeared from inside. Not ours. Three local workers. One woman, two men. They began opening the wagon before Davin had fully climbed down. He slapped one man's hand away from a rope. The man shouted. Davin shouted back. I could not hear the words. They were probably about knots.

Rinna would have enjoyed this. I untied my crutches. The slipped hitch opened. I got down. My right hip was stiff. Less than yesterday. Progress. No one cared. Good. A local worker came toward the second wagon.

"Costumes?"

Pell was gone.

"Some."

"Which?"

"I don't know."

He stared at me.

"What do you do?"

I considered saying passenger.

"Actor."

The word came out before I decided whether it was accurate. The worker nodded.

"Then move."

He climbed into the wagon. Apparently actors were obstacles. Also comforting. I moved. Inside the side door was a corridor. Narrow. Then a turn. Then three steps down. Of course. I took them. The hall smelled like dust, old wood, lamp oil, and damp stone. Not the same as Carrow's theatre. Larger backstage. Worse floor. People moved everywhere. Local crew carried benches. One of our people rolled fabric through a doorway. Someone shouted for Nessa. Someone else shouted that Nessa was not here. That caused a third person to swear.

I stood against the wall. Present. Irrelevant. Excellent. Teren appeared.

"Greg."

I straightened.

"Yes."

"Stage."

"Where?"

He pointed. I followed the direction.

"Why?"

"Sword check."

"What check?"

"Voice."

That was all the attention I received. He turned away.

"Teren."

He stopped.

"Clerk?"

"Later."

"Shopkeeper?"

"Maybe."

"What happened with the hall?"

"Later."

"How many shows?"

"Two."

"Third?"

"Dead."

"Why?"

"Gregory."

"Fine."

He was already moving. I went to find the stage. The corridor opened behind a curtain. Not our curtain. A heavy brown one with patched lower corners. Beyond it was a stage larger than the market hall platform and smaller than Carrow's theatre stage. Real wings. Good. Shallow. Less good. The house beyond was empty except for workers moving benches. I stepped onto the stage. The floor flexed under my right crutch. I stopped. Pressed again. Not dangerous. Loose board. I marked it mentally.

A local woman stood in the house with a ledger. She looked up.

"Speak."

"What?"

"Speak."

"Why?"

"Teren said voice."

Right. I used my normal voice.

"Where?"

She pointed to the back wall.

"Again."

I projected. Not Sword. Me.

"Again."

Louder. She wrote.

"What are you measuring?"

"Whether they hear you."

"Who?"

She pointed. A man at the back raised one hand.

"I hear him."

She wrote again.

"Now sword."

I stared. She looked at me.

"Teren said sword."

Of course. I used the Sword voice.

"I object to this procedure."

The man at the back laughed. The woman did not.

"Again."

I almost repeated it. Stopped.

"Put me somewhere respectable."

The man at the back laughed again. The woman wrote.

"Fine."

"That is the test?"

"Yes."

"What did you write?"

"Fine."

"I can see that."

"Then we're done."

She looked toward the wing.

"Next!"

A woman I did not know walked past me carrying a wooden crown. My assessment was over. Three seconds from Teren. Two lines from me. A local woman had decided I was fine. I stepped offstage. Behind me, someone else began speaking. Nobody watched me leave. I liked this place. Possibly. I went back toward the side door.

Davin was in the corridor arguing with a local worker about the long box.

"Not flat."

"It fits flat."

"Not flat."

"Why?"

Davin put both hands on the box. The worker put both hands on the other end. Neither moved. I approached.

"Can I help?"

Both said, "No." Good. I kept going. Outside, Teren was still with the owner. Serra had arrived somehow. Not with us. She stood near the front doors speaking to a woman in a yellow scarf. They were not arguing. The woman touched Serra's arm. Serra's face changed. Not much. Enough. Then they walked away together. I stopped. I had no idea who the woman was. Serra did not see me. Or saw me and had somewhere else to be. Either was possible. I looked down the street.

The river was visible at the far end between buildings. Somewhere in this town was the municipal hall. I could ask. Later. Right now the theatre was unloading without me. That was fine. I went back inside. A local worker handed me a bundle of wooden cups.

"Kitchen."

I looked at him.

"Where?"

He pointed. I took them. Apparently I had found my role.
