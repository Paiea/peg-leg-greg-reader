TEXT READER · CHAPTER 148

THE ROOF

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I turned left. This immediately became difficult because left contained a cart. The cart contained cabbages. A man was unloading them directly into a shop while another man stood in the street objecting to the location of the horse.

"Move him."

"Where?"

"Not there."

"That isn't a place."

"It is if you move him."

I waited. Nobody moved the horse. I went around the cart. The street rose gently away from the river, which meant Edda's directions were probably correct. The paving was better here than near the market and worse than directly outside the performance hall. Flat stones had been laid down the middle with narrower gutters on either side, except where repairs had replaced stone with brick, and where repairs to the repairs had apparently been made with whatever was available. I liked it. Not walking on it. That was worse.

My right crutch landed on a brick edge and slipped half an inch. I corrected. Slower. The bread in my pocket was becoming flatter every time my coat pulled across it. I ate it. Problem solved. The first fountain was wrong. I discovered this after turning right. Edda had said fountain, right. There was a fountain. I went right. The street descended. That was suspicious. I continued for another minute because perhaps the hill curved. It did not. A woman sold flowers beneath a striped awning.

"North square?"

She pointed behind me.

"Other fountain."

"Of course."

"You want the court?"

"Municipal hall."

"Court's in the hall."

"I want the building."

She looked at me.

"Yes."

"No. I mean I want to see the building."

She looked at my crutches. Then at my face.

"It's not going anywhere."

"I know."

"Back. Second fountain. Up."

"Thank you."

She had already turned to another customer. I went back. The horse with the cabbages was still there. The men were still arguing. Good. The world remained stable. The second fountain was larger. Not dramatically. Enough that I should have known. Four stone fish spat water into a round basin. One fish had lost most of its face and produced a stronger stream than the others. A boy was filling a bucket. Two women sat on the rim talking. A dog drank from the lower overflow. Right. Up.

The hill became less theoretical. I stopped after half a block. Not because I had to. Because there was a low stone wall beside a baker's shop and sitting on it was clearly more efficient than pretending the hill did not exist. I sat.

My hands were tired from rehearsal and two days of travel. Not bad. The web between thumb and forefinger on my right hand had a tender place from the crutch grip. I opened and closed my fingers. Fine. A man came out of the bakery with a tray. He looked at me.

"You waiting?"

"No."

He waited.

"For someone?"

"No."

"Then why are you on my wall?"

I looked down.

"Is it your wall?"

"Yes."

"Sorry."

I started to stand. He sighed.

"No, stay."

I stopped. He went back inside. Fine. A few minutes later he came out again and put a cup on the wall beside me. Water.

"Thank you."

He nodded. No conversation. Excellent. I drank. From where I sat, the town changed with the slope. The river appeared between roofs below. Beyond it, the road we had come in on crossed the flatter southern ground. I could see part of the market, the church tower from the ridge, and one of our painted wagon pieces sticking above a wall near the performance hall. I could not see the performance hall itself. Good. I finished the water. The baker came out for the cup.

"Hall?"

I pointed uphill. He nodded.

"Ugly one."

"Yes."

He took the cup. Apparently everyone agreed. I continued. The municipal hall appeared before the square did. The roof gave it away. I stopped in the middle of the pavement. Someone behind me said, "Move." I moved. Then stared. The building was ugly. Not badly ugly. Interesting ugly.

It occupied the uphill side of a broad square and looked as though three different buildings had agreed to share a front wall. The lowest level was stone, pale originally, darkened by age and weather. The central entrance had an arch large enough for a wagon, though the doors inside it were newer and narrower. On either side, tall rectangular windows had been cut or enlarged at different times. The left pair matched. The right pair almost matched.

Above that, the second story was plaster over masonry with heavy timber visible in places where the plaster had failed. And the roof--- The roof was wrong. I smiled. There were two ridgelines. Not separate roofs. One roof that had become two answers.

The western half rose at a steeper pitch and carried newer tile. The eastern half was lower, broader, and sagged slightly between supports despite obvious repair. A short raised section crossed the center where the two systems met, like someone had built a small roof to stop the larger roofs from arguing. Drainage ran everywhere. Stone gutters. Copper gutters. One wooden channel that could not possibly be original. Two downspouts on the west. Four on the east. One of those emptied onto a lower roof. Why? I crossed the square.

Slowly. The front facade had cracks. Old ones. Most had been filled. A vertical repair line beside the central arch told me the opening had once been wider or differently shaped. The stone above it changed color three courses up. I moved to the left. From there I could see the western roof better. New timber projected beneath the eaves. Not decorative. Ends of rafters. Thicker than expected.

The wall below had three shallow buttresses that did not continue around the corner. Added. Probably after the first roof problem. Maybe before. I wanted the records. That was unreasonable. I wanted them anyway. A bell rang inside. People began coming out. I moved away from the door. Not a few people. Many. A man carrying rolled papers. Two women arguing about a property boundary. A family with a boy who looked furious. Three laborers. A clerk with ink on both cuffs.

An older woman who stopped directly in the doorway to continue an argument with someone still inside.

"Twenty-seven years," she said. "You cannot discover a drainage right after twenty-seven years."

A voice from inside answered, "We didn't discover it."

"You wrote it yesterday."

"We recorded it yesterday."

"That is discovering with ink."

I liked her. The clerk with ink cuffs saw me staring at the roof.

"Court?"

"No."

"Registry?"

"No."

"Tax?"

"No."

He waited.

"Roof."

He looked up.

"Yes."

"I came to see it."

He looked at me.

"Why?"

"It's been rebuilt twice."

"Three."

I stared. He kept walking.

"Wait."

He did not. I followed. This was difficult because he had two legs and no interest in me.

"Three?"

He stopped at the edge of the square.

"Roof?"

"Yes."

"Three."

"I heard twice."

"Then you heard twice."

"That doesn't answer---"

He crossed the street. A wagon passed between us. When it cleared, he was gone into a side lane. I stood there. Three. Interesting. I went inside. Nobody stopped me. This was less exciting than it sounded because the entrance opened into a public hall full of people who had better things to do. A long counter divided the room. Three clerks worked behind it. A line formed in front. Benches ran along one wall. Doors led deeper into the building. One door had a guard.

Another had a sign I could not read from where I stood. The ceiling was high. I looked up. Wrong again. The main beam ran east-west. I had expected north-south from the exterior roof. Unless--- I moved sideways. A woman in line said, "There's a line."

"I'm not in it."

"Then don't stand in it."

I moved. The beam was not centered. It sat nearly two feet west of the room's centerline. A second, smaller beam ran parallel near the east wall. Both disappeared into plaster. The western one was older. Dark timber. The eastern one looked newer. Not new. Newer. I followed it with my eyes.

"Sir."

I looked down. A clerk behind the counter was watching me.

"Yes?"

"What do you need?"

"Nothing."

She waited.

"The roof."

"No."

"I haven't asked yet."

"No roof."

"I only want to know when it was rebuilt."

"Records."

"Good. Where?"

"Records office."

"Where?"

She pointed to the guarded door. The guard looked at me.

"Can I---"

"No."

The clerk had already called the next person. I moved to a bench. Apparently records existed. Excellent. Apparently I could not have them. Less excellent. I sat and looked at the ceiling. That was still free. A man beside me held a bundle of papers tied with blue string. He noticed.

"Leak?"

"What?"

"You looking for leak?"

"No."

He looked up.

"There's one there."

He pointed. There was. A pale stain spread from the plaster near the newer eastern beam.

"That's not under the roof valley."

He looked at me.

"What valley?"

"Outside. Where the roof sections meet."

"Water moves."

"Yes."

He returned to his papers. Fair. I studied the stain. If water entered higher on the roof, it could travel along a rafter before appearing there. Or the gutter behind the raised center section leaked. Or there was another roof surface I could not see from the square. I wanted upstairs. The stairs were behind the guard. Of course. I sat for another minute. Then a door opened beside the counter. Not the guarded one. A woman emerged carrying a stack of ledgers. Behind her I could see a corridor.

And stairs. Different stairs. I stood. The woman nearly walked into me.

"Sorry."

She adjusted the ledgers. One began sliding. I caught the bottom edge.

"Thanks."

She looked at my crutch tucked under my arm. Then at the ledgers.

"Can you open that?"

She nodded toward the door. I opened it. She passed through. I held it. The corridor beyond smelled like dust and wax. The stairs rose at the far end. I looked. The woman looked back.

"You coming?"

"Can I?"

"Why?"

"Roof."

She stared.

"No."

Fair. I let the door close. I had not been invited into a secret architectural kingdom. Disappointing. Reasonable. I went outside. The square had changed again. A vegetable seller had arrived. Children chased each other around the fountain. The older woman with the drainage argument was now arguing with a man holding a measuring chain. I walked around the building.

The western side street was narrow enough that I could touch the hall wall with one crutch and nearly touch the opposite building with the other. I did not test this. The buttresses were clearer here. Three added supports. The middle one was thicker. Above them, the roof pitch changed approximately over the second. Not coincidence. Probably. The stone around the middle buttress had been cut back. Retrofit. I crouched slightly to look at the base. Bad idea. Not dangerous. Awkward. I straightened. A door opened beside me.

A man carrying a bucket stepped out.

"Don't piss there."

"I wasn't."

He looked at me.

"I was looking at the buttress."

He looked at the buttress.

"Why?"

"It was added."

"Yes."

"When?"

"Before me."

"How long have you worked here?"

"Eleven years."

"Do you know why?"

"Wall leaned."

I stopped.

"Which way?"

He pointed across the alley.

"Out."

"Because of the roof?"

He shrugged.

"Because wall leaned."

He walked away with the bucket. That was enough to make me happy for several minutes. The wall had leaned. The buttresses were added. The roof changed above the middle support. Cause and response. Maybe. I continued around the back. The rear was worse. Better. Much better. The public front had been repaired to look intentional. The back had not. Old stone met newer brick. Two windows had been filled. A third had been shortened when a roofline dropped across it.

There was a low annex attached to the eastern side, probably after the main building, and the eastern roof emptied partly onto it. That explained one downspout. Not the stupid one. The stupid one emptied directly onto a tiled slope, where the water had carved a dark path through moss. I stood looking at it.

"Terrible."

Someone beside me said, "Yes." I turned. An old man sat on an overturned crate beside the rear door. Not troupe old. Actually old. Gray beard. Brown coat. One boot unlaced. He held a small knife and was cutting an apple.

"Why would they do that?"

He bit a slice.

"Cheap."

"That cannot be the whole answer."

"It usually is."

I looked at the downspout.

"They rebuilt the roof three times?"

He cut another slice.

"Depends what you call rebuilt."

"What do you call rebuilt?"

"Roof comes off."

"All of it?"

"Enough."

"That is not a definition."

He smiled.

"You work here?"

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

"My daughter does."

"What does she do?"

"Complains."

I waited. He ate apple.

"What actually happened to the roof?"

"Which time?"

Excellent. I moved closer. He pointed the knife at my crutches.

"Sit if you're staying."

There was another crate. I sat. He cut apple. I waited. He did not speak.

"You said which time."

"Yes."

"Which times?"

"Old roof burned."

I knew about the fire.

"First rebuild."

"Maybe."

"Maybe?"

"Wasn't here."

"Neither was I."

"Then we're equal."

Fine. He pointed upward with the knife.

"After fire, they put it back wrong."

"How?"

"Too much roof."

I frowned.

"That means nothing."

"It held snow."

"All roofs hold snow."

"That one held the wrong snow."

I stared. He smiled around the apple.

"You know roof?"

"A little."

"Then look."

I looked. The eastern half. Broader pitch. Longer run. If the post-fire roof had extended farther than the original---

"They changed the span."

"Maybe."

"Why?"

"Wanted bigger room."

That made sense. Too much sense.

"They removed supports?"

"Maybe."

"You keep saying maybe."

"I wasn't there."

Good.

"Then the wall leaned."

"Later."

"And they added buttresses."

"Probably."

"You know that?"

"My daughter says those are buttresses."

"They are."

"Then yes."

"What was the second rebuild?"

"Storm."

"Roof failed?"

"Part."

"Which part?"

He pointed vaguely west.

"Other side."

"The steeper roof."

"Sure."

"You don't know."

"No."

I laughed. He offered me apple. I took a slice.

"Third?"

"Council."

"What did the council do?"

"Argued."

"That does not rebuild roofs."

"It does eventually."

He cut another slice.

"Leak. Rot. Money. More arguing. New wood."

"When?"

"Six years?"

He thought.

"Seven."

"That would be the western rafters."

He shrugged. Maybe. I ate the apple. This was not reliable information. It was wonderful.

"Do you know who has the records?"

"Clerks."

"They won't let me in."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Means they're working."

"That is optimistic."

"My daughter is a clerk."

"Which one?"

He smiled.

"Complains."

Of course. The rear door opened. A woman leaned out.

"Papa."

He looked over. Ink on both cuffs. The clerk from the square. She saw me.

"Oh, for fuck's sake."

I smiled.

"He said three."

"I know what he said."

"You didn't tell me."

"I was going home."

The old man offered her apple. She took it.

"Why is he here?"

"He likes roof."

"I know."

"Strange boy."

"I know."

I objected.

"I am sitting here."

They ignored me. She looked at the sky.

"You have ten minutes."

I stopped.

"For what?"

She pointed at me.

"Not upstairs. Not records. Not anywhere with a locked door. I will show you the council chamber because if you keep circling the building someone will report a suspicious cripple and then I have paperwork."

I stared.

"Really?"

"Ten minutes."

I stood too quickly. My right shoulder objected. I ignored it. No. I acknowledged it and stood anyway. The old man said, "Roof boy."

"I have a name."

He cut apple.

"Roof boy."

Fine. The clerk opened the rear door. I followed. Her name was Mara. I learned this because someone in the corridor said, "Mara, did Fen sign---"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"No."

"Then what do I tell---"

"I don't care."

She kept walking. I liked Mara. The back corridor was narrower than the public room and older than the front suggested. Stone showed through the plaster at shoulder height. The floor sloped slightly toward the east.

"Is that settlement?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because the floor has always been shit."

"That does not exclude settlement."

She kept walking.

"Ten minutes."

We passed the guarded door from the other side. The guard looked at me. Then Mara.

"No."

Mara said, "Chamber." He sighed.

"Ten."

Everyone agreed on ten. We climbed a staircase. Slowly. Not because Mara slowed. She did not. The stairs turned tightly at the middle landing. My crutches fit, barely. The inside corners of the treads were worn down from centuries of feet. At the top, Mara waited. Not looking concerned. Just waiting. I appreciated that.

"Council chamber."

She opened double doors. I forgot my hands hurt. The room was large. Larger than the ground-floor public hall. That was the problem. Immediately. The ceiling rose into the eastern roof. There were no central columns. None. The span crossed almost the entire building. I moved inside.

The council table occupied the center, long and dark, surrounded by mismatched chairs. Tall windows faced the square. The rear wall had been patched in at least four different materials. Above us, roof timbers crossed at angles that did not agree with the walls. I smiled.

"Oh."

Mara leaned against the door.

"Ten minutes."

"They took the columns out."

"I don't know."

"They must have."

"Why?"

"The ground floor beam isn't centered because it is carrying something that used to land differently."

She looked up. I moved toward the middle. A heavy tie beam crossed overhead. Newer than the rafters around it. Iron straps reinforced both ends. One strap had been doubled.

"There."

"What?"

"The load changed."

Mara said nothing. I traced it. Not with my hands. Eyes. Roof down to tie. Tie to wall. Wall to--- But the eastern wall had the newer parallel beam below. The western side had buttresses. Different fixes for different consequences. They had not rebuilt one bad roof three times. They had kept changing the building underneath it. Or around it. Or both.

"Why remove the columns?"

Mara said, "Council wanted a larger chamber."

"When?"

"Before I was born."

"After the fire?"

"Probably."

"Before the wall leaned?"

"I don't know."

"Do you have drawings?"

"No."

"You do."

"No for you."

I laughed. She checked the corridor clock.

"Six minutes."

I moved to the eastern wall. A vertical timber disappeared behind paneling. I crouched to see where it met the floor. Mara said, "Don't." I stopped.

"Why?"

"Because council meets here and if you break your neck under the table I have paperwork."

"I am not going under the table."

"You looked like you were."

"I was looking at the post."

"From standing."

Fine. I stood. The post did not align with the window spacing. Added. Almost certainly. I looked at the roof again. It was not elegant. It was not a hidden masterpiece. It was a series of decisions. Some good. Some cheap. Some forced by fire, weather, money, politics, or people wanting a bigger room. The building had survived them. Not unchanged. Not correctly. Just continuously. I loved it. Mara watched me.

"You're smiling."

"Yes."

"It's a roof."

"Yes."

"Bad roof."

"Complicated roof."

"It leaks."

"That is separate."

"It is literally the roof."

I considered.

"Fair."

She checked the time.

"Two minutes."

I turned slowly once more. Not to memorize it. Impossible. Just to see. The mismatched rafters. The iron straps. The western tie. The added eastern post. A patch where plaster had cracked around one joint. Water stain near the rear. Chairs stacked against a wall. A broken window latch. Someone had left a cup on the council table. A building used by people. Not a diagram.

"Time."

I went. Downstairs was slower. My shoulders had tightened while I stood in the chamber. At the rear door, Mara held it open.

"Thank you."

"You get ten minutes."

"I got more."

"Don't tell anyone."

"I think the guard knows."

"He doesn't count."

The guard, somewhere inside, shouted, "I heard that." Mara closed the door. Her father was still on the crate. He had finished the apple.

"Well?"

I looked up at the roof.

"Too much roof."

He smiled.

"See?"

"That still means nothing."

He laughed. I started back toward the square.

"Roof boy."

I turned. He tossed something. I nearly dropped a crutch trying to catch it. Apple. Whole.

"Thank you."

He waved me away. I went downhill. Much easier. The town had moved toward evening while I was inside. Shop awnings came down. Lamps appeared in windows. The fountain fish continued spitting water with complete indifference to municipal architecture. I found the wrong fountain first. Again. That was impressive.

By the time I reached the river street, my shoulders were tired enough that the performance hall looked farther away than it had any right to. I stopped. Across the street was a bench. I used it. The apple sat in my lap. I could see the hall entrance from there. People were going in. Not many. Open rehearsal. Right. I had not checked the time. That was stupid. I stood. A voice behind me said, "Gregory." I turned. Serra stood in the street. Alone. No yellow scarf.

She looked at the apple.

"Where did you get that?"

"Roof."

She stared. I pointed uphill.

"Municipal hall."

"That explains less."

"I saw the roof."

"Was it good?"

"Yes."

"Good."

She kept walking toward the theatre. I fell in beside her. Not quickly. She adjusted to my pace after half a block without saying anything. I did not ask where she had been. She did not ask why a roof had given me an apple. The performance hall doors were open. Inside, someone shouted for the Princess. Serra sighed.

"They found me."

"You walked in the front door."

"Still."

Someone shouted for the Sword. I looked at her. She looked at me.

"Apparently they found you too."

I put the apple in my pocket. Then we went inside.
