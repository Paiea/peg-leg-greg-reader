THE ADVOCATE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Forty-three lines was not forty-three lines.

This was the first lie.

I discovered it before I got back upstairs.

Iven took the pages from me, spread them across the bakery table, and
crossed out six.

"What are you doing?"

"Helping."

"You removed them."

"Yes."

"Those are my lines."

"Not anymore."

He crossed out another.

I took the charcoal from him.

"Stop."

"The joke is bad."

"That is not the point."

"It is one point."

The sick man who had been the Advocate sat wrapped in a blanket near the
ovens. His name was Lorn. I had learned that because Rinna had asked
whether Lorn wanted broth and he had said no before coughing for long
enough to reconsider.

He leaned over the pages.

"Keep the magistrate."

I looked at him.

"Which magistrate?"

Iven pointed.

"That."

"Why?"

Lorn said, "To arrest the Chancellor."

They looked at each other.

I said, "Excellent."

Iven tapped the page.

"Forgery first. Treason later."

Lorn said, "No. Treason accusation first. Forgery proves it."

"You've been saying it backward."

"I've been saying it for six months."

"Backward for six months."

Lorn coughed.

Then raised one finger.

"Paid."

I liked him.

The Advocate, as far as I could tell, existed because everyone else in
the play had committed too many crimes to explain them efficiently.

He entered after the Princess accused the Chancellor of falsifying a
royal order. The Advocate represented someone called the Merchant
Council, which had apparently lost three wagons because of the false
order. He brought a copy of the order, identified a seal, accused the
Chancellor of forgery, then argued with the King about whether the crown
could prosecute its own officer.

Later he returned to establish that the Chancellor had also redirected
soldiers away from a bridge.

That allowed Orin, playing the magistrate, to enter and arrest him.

Except Orin said treason.

Except Iven said forgery.

Except the pages contained both.

I read them again.

The words moved.

Not magically.

Tiredly.

"Who is the Merchant Council?"

Iven said, "Doesn't matter."

Lorn said, "Matters to me."

"You are the Advocate."

"Was."

"Fine. Why do I care about them?"

"You don't."

"Then why am I advocating?"

"Money."

Iven nodded.

Finally, a character I understood.

Lorn pointed at a paragraph.

"This matters."

It was eleven lines.

"All of it?"

"No. Seal. Three wagons. Bridge."

"The bridge is later."

"Right."

He closed his eyes.

Iven said, "Go to bed."

Lorn said, "I'm helping."

"You're dying on the bread."

"I am not."

Rinna put a bowl of broth in front of him.

He looked at it.

Then at her.

"Traitor."

"Drink."

He drank.

Iven took the charcoal back from me.

"First entrance. You need the order. You need the seal. You need the
wagons. You need to make Marek refuse to act."

"Why?"

"So Serra can force him."

"Why?"

"Because that is the scene."

"Useful."

He crossed out two more lines.

"Stop doing that."

"You won't learn them."

"I might."

"No."

That irritated me enough to try.

I took the pages upstairs.

Pell was asleep.

The red dress watched me from the chair.

I sat on my bed and learned the first page.

Not all of it.

I learned the beginning.

"Your Majesty, I appear on behalf of the merchants of the eastern road,
whose goods were seized under an order bearing your seal."

Good.

Again.

"Your Majesty, I appear on behalf of the merchants of the eastern road,
whose goods were seized under an order bearing your seal."

Again.

Then the next line.

Then the next.

The Advocate had too many nouns.

Merchants.

Order.

Seal.

Wagons.

Chancellor.

Authority.

Bridge.

Council.

Crown.

I learned a sentence about the seal and forgot which wagons had been
seized.

Three.

Three wagons.

I remembered three because Lorn had said three.

Then I forgot whether they carried wool or grain.

The page said wool.

The next page said grain.

I stared.

Pell snored.

I went downstairs.

Lorn was asleep at the table.

Iven was gone.

Rinna was gone.

The bakery woman was shaping dough.

"Wool or grain?"

She looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I went back upstairs.

Page one said wool.

Page three said grain because they were different wagons.

Of course.

I hated the Advocate.

I learned until the words became shapes.

At some point I woke with my cheek on the second page.

Morning had happened.

Pell was gone.

The red dress was gone.

I sat up too fast.

My neck hurt.

My mouth was dry.

The pages had lines from my face pressed into them.

I checked the window.

Gray light.

No hard rain.

Good.

Then I remembered forty-three lines.

Bad.

Downstairs, the bakery was full of people who had apparently decided to
have a day without waiting for me.

Nessa stood near the door drinking tea.

Serra was eating bread.

Marek was arguing with Bessa about tickets.

Davin carried a coil of rope through the room.

Teren was not there.

Iven saw me.

"First line."

I stopped on the last stair.

"Your Majesty, I appear on behalf of the merchants of the eastern road,
whose goods were seized under an order bearing your seal."

"Second."

"That was one sentence."

"Second line."

I knew it.

I absolutely knew it.

Nothing happened.

Iven waited.

Serra ate bread.

Marek continued arguing about whether ninety people could become
ninety-two.

I said, "The order was presented at the south gate by men acting under
the Chancellor's authority."

Iven shook his head.

"North gate."

"Fuck."

"Doesn't matter."

"Then why correct me?"

"Because you asked."

"I did not."

He handed me bread.

"Eat."

Lorn was not there.

"Where is he?"

"Bed."

"Here?"

"Bessa's sister."

"Who is Bessa's sister?"

Iven looked at me.

"Exactly."

He left.

I ate.

The hall roof had mostly held.

That was the morning report.

Mostly.

One bucket remained near the left rear stage, but the canvas was gone.
The boards underneath were damp rather than wet. The gray-haired roof
man stood onstage in muddy boots while Teren pressed a heel against the
floor.

"Dry by evening," the man said.

Teren looked at the bucket.

"Mostly."

"Bucket's for confidence."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

Teren accepted this.

The left entrance might reopen.

Might.

Nobody restored the moved bench.

Bessa had sold eleven more tickets.

Fifty-three.

Marek knew before I did.

"Money," he said.

"People," Bessa corrected.

"People with money."

"People with tickets."

"Which were money."

"Yesterday."

Marek looked at me.

"She is difficult."

Bessa said, "You are standing in my hall."

"Beautiful hall."

"You called it a bucket with walls."

"That was before sales."

I went to the stage.

The route through the house remained open.

Good.

I could enter either way depending on whether the boards dried.

Bad.

Two possible entrances meant I now had two possible places to forget I
was supposed to enter.

Serra came up behind me.

"Advocate."

"Yes."

"Show me the order."

"What?"

"Your prop."

I looked around.

"I don't have it."

"Then you cannot accuse anyone of forging it."

"Where is it?"

She pointed toward props.

I went.

There were three rolled papers on the table.

One had a red ribbon.

One had blue wax.

One was blank.

I chose blue wax.

Pell, from the floor behind the table, said, "No."

I looked down.

He was sewing a shoe.

"Which?"

"Red ribbon."

"Why?"

"Blue is marriage contract."

"Whose?"

"No idea."

I took red.

Serra was gone when I returned.

Of course.

I put the order in my belt.

Then remembered the Advocate probably did not carry it in his belt.

I found Iven.

"Where do I keep this?"

He looked at the paper.

"Hand."

"For the entire scene?"

"Yes."

"What do I do with my crutches?"

He stopped.

That was useful.

We went back to the stage.

The Advocate had been written for Lorn, who had two legs and apparently
fewer logistical objections.

I could not carry the order and use both crutches while entering.

Iven tried tucking the paper under my arm.

It fell.

He tried my coat.

Wrong costume.

He tried rolling it tighter.

Nessa shouted from across the hall, "No."

We stopped.

She came over, took the order, and shoved it inside the front of my
Advocate coat.

I was wearing only the shirt.

"There."

"It will wrinkle."

"It is forged."

"Fair."

She left.

Iven said, "First line."

I said it.

"Seal."

I said the seal line.

"Wagons."

"Three wagons of wool were seized at the north gate under the
Chancellor's authority."

"Good."

"That is not the actual line."

"Good."

I looked at him.

He pointed toward the stage.

"King refuses."

I said the next part.

Wrong.

I made the King agree.

Iven stopped me.

"No. He refuses."

"I know."

"You just made him prosecute."

"I said he should."

"You said he did."

"Difference."

"Yes."

I tried again.

This time I forgot the Merchant Council.

Iven let me.

We reached the bridge.

I accused the Chancellor of diverting soldiers to the bridge.

"From," Iven said.

"What?"

"From the bridge."

I looked at the page.

That was worse.

If I said it wrong in the show, the Chancellor would have protected the
bridge instead of exposing it.

"Fuck."

"Yes."

I circled FROM with charcoal.

Then I circled NORTH.

Then THREE.

Then SEAL.

My pages began to look diseased.

Teren passed.

"How is he?"

Iven said, "Bad."

Teren kept walking.

I said, "Thank you."

"You asked?"

"No."

"Then why are you upset?"

Marek found out I had forty-three lines.

He was delighted.

Not because he wanted me to succeed.

Because he had fewer.

"How many do you have?"

"Enough."

"How many?"

"No."

"Orin does that."

"Good man."

"Help me."

"I am helping."

"You are drinking tea."

"Morale."

He drank.

I said, "King refuses to prosecute Chancellor."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Chancellor is his cousin."

I looked at the page.

"No, he isn't."

Marek frowned.

"Brother?"

"No."

"Then why do I care?"

"You are the King."

"You think I read the whole play?"

I stared.

He laughed.

"Ask Serra."

Serra was unavailable because someone had finally put her in the red
dress.

I knew because she came through the dressing-room door wearing it while
Nessa walked behind her staring at the hem.

Nobody celebrated.

Nobody announced completion.

The dress simply existed on a person.

Pell knelt, adjusted one pin, stood, and said, "Done."

Nessa said, "No."

Pell closed his eyes.

Serra looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're staring."

"The dress."

She looked down.

"Oh."

Apparently she had suffered less from it than everyone else.

"Why does the King refuse to prosecute the Chancellor?"

"Because if he admits the order is false, he admits he lost control of
his own seal."

That made sense.

"Also the Chancellor is his cousin."

I turned slowly toward Marek.

He raised his tea.

"I knew."

Serra walked away.

The day continued without respecting my problem.

Tickets reached sixty-one.

The roof man removed the confidence bucket, then put it back when a
brief shower came through.

Bessa swore at the sky.

Davin moved the long box from the office to a dry rear corner after the
office man demanded his desk back.

Rinna bought lamp oil.

I knew because I saw her pay two copper and complain about the price.

A local baker sent over a tray of small rolls because we were sleeping
above his ovens and apparently actors made his daughter laugh the night
before in another town.

That explanation made no sense.

Nobody refused the rolls.

Marek ate three.

A woman who had bought tickets brought Bessa a basket of pears "for the
players."

That was the phrase.

For the players.

Bessa took two pears as management tax.

The rest reached backstage.

I put one in my bag for later.

Five copper per show was money.

Pears were also money, in the sense that I did not have to buy a pear.

This seemed obvious enough not to require philosophy.

By afternoon I knew perhaps twenty-five lines exactly.

That number was misleading.

I knew another ten if someone gave me the first word.

I knew five in the wrong order.

I knew one from a completely different scene.

The remaining lines hated me personally.

Iven crossed out four more.

"You cannot keep removing the role."

"We are shortening."

"Teren know?"

"No."

"Teren!"

From across the hall:

"What?"

"Iven is cutting the Advocate."

"Good."

Iven crossed out another.

"Traitor," I said.

Teren shouted, "Gregory."

"Yes?"

"Learn less."

I hated everyone.

The sick Advocate's forty-three lines became thirty-two.

Then thirty-one because Orin refused to wait through a legal argument he
called "three minutes of furniture."

"It establishes jurisdiction," I said.

Orin stared.

"You have been Advocate for twelve hours."

"Thirteen."

"Dangerous."

He crossed out the jurisdiction.

I put it back.

He crossed it out again.

I let it die.

At some point, Bessa asked Teren whether we could add seats.

He said no.

She added four.

Teren removed two.

They settled on two.

Sixty-eight tickets.

The rain stopped.

The roof man climbed up once more.

The confidence bucket remained empty.

At five, Teren gathered us on the stage.

Not for rehearsal.

"Left is open unless it rains."

That was the meeting.

Nessa asked, "If it rains?"

"House route."

Orin said, "Rear cross?"

"Open."

Davin said, "No scenery on west brace."

Teren nodded.

Bessa said, "Seventy-one."

Marek smiled.

Teren said, "Good."

Then everyone left.

I stood there.

"That's it?"

Teren looked back.

"What?"

"We don't run anything?"

"Do you want to?"

I looked at the pages.

No.

"Yes."

"No time."

"Why did you ask?"

He walked away.

Doors in ninety minutes.

I ate stew.

Not enough.

Then too much.

I changed.

The Advocate coat was dark brown, too broad through the shoulders, and
smelled faintly of Lorn.

Nessa pinned one side so it did not bunch under my arm.

"Order."

I showed her.

"Good."

"Do I look like an Advocate?"

"No."

"Useful."

"You look nineteen."

"I am nineteen."

"That is the problem."

She went away.

I looked in the mirror.

She was right.

The Advocate probably should have looked like a man entrusted by
merchants to accuse royal officers of treason.

I looked like someone entrusted with holding a lantern.

Fine.

I tried the first line again.

Perfect.

Second.

Perfect.

North gate.

Three wagons.

Seal.

King refuses.

Crown cannot prosecute itself without admitting seal compromised.

FROM the bridge.

"Money."
