TEXT READER · CHAPTER 160

THE SWORD

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The two copper were still in my pocket when Marek put a crown on his
head.

"Why are you here?"

"Structure."

"You weren't invited."

"I am the King."

"That has stopped explaining things."

Serra stepped between us and took hold of the mounting strap.

"Seven minutes."

"Apparently."

"What are we doing?"

Marek said, "I sell him."

"No."

"I present him."

"No."

"I discover him."

"You already did."

Marek considered.

"Again."

Serra looked at me.

I said, "He paid."

Lady Merra's father struck one walking stick against the floor from
across the room.

"Expensive!"

The guests laughed.

Teren stood beside the side-room door with his arms folded.

He made a small circle with one finger.

Go.

Apparently that was the plan.

Serra lifted the strap from its hook.

"My father," Marek announced, already walking into the room, "left me
three things."

I stared at him.

That was not a line from anything I knew.

Marek continued.

"A crown, a kingdom, and a sword of incomparable worth."

Lady Merra's father called, "Which did you lose first?"

Marek stopped.

The room laughed.

He pointed at the old man.

"This is why kings have guards."

"Your guards are drinking in my kitchen."

One of the servants in the corridor laughed much too loudly.

Marek looked toward the sound.

"Betrayal."

Serra carried me forward.

I understood the shape now.

Maybe.

King had a sword.

Old man wanted sword.

Sword did not want old man.

That could last seven minutes.

Probably.

Marek held out a hand toward me.

"This blade has served my family for generations."

I said, "Against my will."

Lady Merra's father laughed.

"How much?"

Marek said, "Not for sale."

I said, "Finally."

The old man leaned back.

"Everything is for sale."

That landed differently.

Not bad.

Closer.

He was looking at me, not the mounting board.

I said, "Then you should be worried."

He smiled.

"About what?"

"Your daughter has already sold your evening."

Lady Merra closed her eyes.

The room laughed.

Her father looked delighted.

"Again."

There it was.

He pointed his stick at me.

"Say that again."

I almost did.

It had worked.

He wanted it.

He was paying.

The room was waiting.

Serra shifted the strap in her hand.

Not much.

Enough.

I said, "No."

The old man blinked.

"No?"

"I'm expensive."

The servants laughed first.

Then the guests.

Marek stepped in.

"He charges by the insult."

"I've already paid."

"Her."

Marek pointed at Lady Merra.

Lady Merra said, "Do not involve me in this."

"Too late," her father said.

Serra said, "Perhaps we should establish what exactly you're buying."

That sounded like Serra and Princess at the same time.

Good.

The old man looked at her.

"The sword."

"No."

"His insults."

"No."

"What have I bought?"

Serra considered.

"Seven minutes of regret."

That got nearly everyone.

I laughed too.

Fuck.

Only once.

I stopped.

The old man pointed his stick at Serra now.

"I like her."

Marek said, "She's not for sale."

Serra looked at him.

Marek corrected.

"She is not mine to sell."

"Better."

That laugh was smaller.

Not for the line.

For the correction.

I could feel the piece changing under us.

The old man was not a scene partner in the way Iven was.

He did not care what information came next.

He cared about what amused him now.

If we answered everything, we would stand there answering forever.

Marek tried to move.

"So, as I was saying, this sword has served my family for generations."

"Poorly," I said.

"Quiet."

"See?"

The old man laughed.

Marek continued before he could interrupt.

"It cannot be bought."

Lady Merra's father said, "Ten silver."

The room made a sound.

Not because it was real.

Because silver was silver.

Marek looked at me.

I looked at Teren.

Teren's face did not move.

Lady Merra said, "Father."

"What?"

"You're not buying an actor."

"I said sword."

I said, "That's how it starts."

A few people laughed.

The old man didn't.

He looked at me for a second longer.

"Twenty."

Lady Merra said, "Stop."

There was no anger in it.

There was enough authority.

He waved one hand.

"Imaginary money."

"Use imaginary silver," she said.

"That's what I was doing."

Marek said, "Then I demand fifty."

The old man looked offended.

"For him?"

"Legendary."

"I've heard him."

I said, "So have I."

That restored him.

He laughed.

Good.

Serra turned toward Marek.

"Your Majesty, perhaps you should demonstrate the blade's value."

No.

Absolutely not.

Marek looked at me.

"Cut something."

The old man slapped the arm of his chair.

"Yes."

I said, "No."

"Can't?"

"Won't."

"Can't."

"Won't."

"Can't."

This was becoming five-year-olds.

I could feel it.

The room liked it.

That made it more dangerous, not less.

Marek reached toward a bowl on a nearby table.

Teren said, "No."

One word.

Marek stopped.

The old man looked at Teren.

"Why?"

"House property."

"It's my house."

Lady Merra said, "Mine."

The old man turned.

"What?"

"You gave it to me."

"When?"

"Eight years ago."

"Bad decision."

"Yes."

That got the room without us.

Good.

Teren pointed at the wooden fish.

I had not noticed Pell had brought it.

Of course he had.

It sat on the edge of our battered Shopkeeper table, painted eyes
watching.

Marek picked it up.

"Fish."

Pell, from the side room, said, "Careful."

Marek ignored him.

He held the wooden fish toward me.

"Cut this."

I stared at it.

The old man leaned forward.

"Yes."

I said, "It's already dead."

The fish twitched.

Spring.

Perfect timing.

Not mine.

The room erupted.

I stared at the fish.

Marek stared at the fish.

Pell made a strangled noise from the doorway.

Lady Merra's father hit the floor with both sticks.

"Again!"

"No," I said.

This time immediately.

Bigger laugh.

The fish had done more work than I had.

Marek set it down carefully.

"Legendary mercy."

"Professional caution."

The old man pointed at me.

"What are you when you're not a sword?"

There.

Closer again.

I knew what he meant.

Maybe.

The room changed only because I did.

Serra answered before I had to.

"Off duty."

The old man looked at her.

"I asked him."

Teren unfolded his arms.

Lady Merra said, "Father."

I could have let them end it.

Instead I said, "More expensive."

The old man laughed.

The room came back.

Fine.

He accepted the door I gave him instead of the one he had opened.

"Everything costs more here."

Lady Merra said, "You live here."

"Exactly."

Marek took the opportunity.

"And that is why the sword cannot be sold."

I said, "Because he couldn't afford me?"

"Because I cannot afford to replace you."

That was better.

I looked at him.

"Almost kind."

"Don't tell anyone."

Serra hung the strap back on the mounting hook.

That felt like an ending.

The old man said, "Wait."

Of course.

Teren looked toward Lady Merra.

Lady Merra looked at her father.

"One."

He thought.

Then pointed at Marek.

"Insult him."

Marek said, "No."

I said, "He dresses like a king."

Silence.

Then the old man laughed so hard he coughed.

Marek stared at me.

"That's not an insult."

Serra said, "It was."

The room broke again.

Teren stepped forward.

"Seven."

Done.

Lady Merra's father called, "Eight!"

Teren said, "Two more copper."

The old man laughed.

"No."

"Then seven."

That was the end.

Not because the old man stopped wanting more.

Because Teren ended it.

We bowed.

The servants in the corridor applauded.

One of the little girls had come back and was sitting on the floor
beside the door. She clapped the wooden fish against her palm until Pell
took it away from her.

"Careful."

"It bit me."

"It doesn't bite."

The fish twitched.

She dropped it.

Pell caught it against his chest.

"See?"

I came off the mounting board.

My shoulders had gone tight from sitting wrong.

Serra helped with the strap, then immediately left because Lady Merra
wanted her.

Marek followed the old man toward the drinks.

I sat for a moment.

Teren passed.

"Two copper."

"I know."

"You asked."

"I know."

"Good."

"Was that good?"

He kept walking.

"Paid."

I hated him.

Private performances did not end backstage because there was no
backstage.

They ended while the people who had watched you were still in the room.

A man in a dark blue coat came over while I was putting my crutches
under me.

"That fish."

"Not mine."

"Does it always do that?"

"I hope not."

He laughed.

"You're the Advocate too?"

Apparently someone had been talking.

"Sometimes."

"I saw something like that play in Carrow ten years ago."

"Not with me."

"I gathered."

He started telling me about it anyway.

Different King.

Different company, perhaps.

Or same name.

I did not know.

His wife came and rescued me by taking him away to say goodbye to Lady
Merra.

Then one of the girls asked if the sword slept.

"Constantly."

"Where?"

"Wall."

"Which wall?"

"Whichever pays."

She considered this.

"That's stupid."

"Yes."

She left satisfied.

I found the side room.

Rinna had already begun converting rich-house abundance into road food.

Not stealing.

I checked.

A kitchen woman was handing her wrapped parcels.

"These go bad tomorrow."

Rinna nodded.

"These?"

"Keep."

A loaf.

Half a cold roast.

Cheese ends.

Six apples.

Something wrapped in leaves.

Rinna opened it.

Cakes.

Marek appeared.

Rinna closed it.

"No."

"I haven't asked."

"You arrived."

The kitchen woman laughed.

"Give him one."

Rinna looked betrayed.

Marek received a cake.

"Private money," he said through it.

"That's not money," I said.

He swallowed.

"Better."

The kitchen woman handed me one too.

I accepted.

Fine.

Maybe better.

Harl came through with a list.

"Nothing from the upper rooms."

Rinna said, "We weren't in the upper rooms."

"Nothing from the east gallery."

"We weren't there either."

"One silver spoon is missing."

Marek stopped chewing.

Every head turned toward him.

He looked offended.

"I don't steal spoons."

Orin, from the corner, said, "Professionally."

Marek pointed at him.

"Exactly."

Harl did not appreciate this.

Teren appeared.

"Search our things."

Harl looked surprised.

"So quickly?"

"Yes."

Marek said, "Wait."

Teren looked at him.

Marek reached into his costume coat.

Everyone became very still.

He pulled out an apple.

Then another pocket.

A folded handkerchief.

Another.

Two corks.

"Why?" I asked.

"No."

"That's Orin."

Marek checked his last pocket.

Empty.

"See?"

Harl looked unconvinced.

A servant came in holding the silver spoon.

"Found it."

"Where?"

"The garden."

Silence.

Harl closed his eyes.

"Why?"

The servant shrugged.

"Master Olan."

Lady Merra's father.

Of course.

Harl left without apologizing.

Marek said, "I demand compensation."

Teren gave him the apple from his own pocket.

Marek accepted.

The silver purse moved.

I saw it because Teren took it out while Rinna was tying the food
parcels.

He counted three silver into her hand.

Not all of it.

Three.

Rinna put two into one cloth packet and one into another.

"Wheel," she said.

Davin, passing the doorway, said, "Yes."

"Food."

"Yes."

"East toll?"

"Maybe."

Teren gave her several copper too.

She counted them.

Then he closed the purse and put it back inside his coat.

That was enough explanation.

The money had already started becoming road.

Teren called names.

Payment.

My name came after Pell.

"Gregory."

I went over.

He put six copper into my hand.

I counted.

"Six."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Private rate."

"I had three roles."

"Selected work."

"Six."

"Yes."

"And two extra."

"Already paid."

"Eight total."

"You're learning."

I looked at the coins.

Public show, five.

Private selected performance, six.

Requested extra, two.

Eight copper for the day.

No silver.

Still more than I had earned from one show before.

Marek leaned over my shoulder.

"Rich."

"Go away."

"What did you get?"

"Eight."

He frowned.

"I got six."

"You didn't do extra."

"I was in extra."

"You invited yourself."

"I provided structure."

Teren said, "You provided crown."

Marek looked at him.

"One copper."

"No."

"Half."

"No."

"Cake."

"You already had one."

"Then paid."

He walked away.

I put the six copper with the two.

Eight.

Then checked the clasp.

Still there.

Good.

Serra returned with Lady Merra.

Lady Merra had removed her formal outer layer and rolled her sleeves
again.

"Sleeping."

Teren looked at her.

"Here?"

"Stable loft and two service rooms. Unless you'd prefer the village
inn."

"Cost?"

"Included."

"Food morning?"

"Included."

Marek shouted from somewhere, "Excellent house!"

Lady Merra ignored him.

Teren said, "Done."

That easy.

No one had to spend today's pay on a bed.

That mattered more now that I had enough money to notice keeping it.

The stable loft was clean by stable standards.

Hay smell.

Wood.

Dust.

No horses directly below us, only tack and an empty stall.

The service rooms went to people with costume trunks and people who had
already claimed them by moving faster.

Pell and I got the loft.

Again.

He brought the fish.

"Why?"

"It's ours."

I accepted defeat.

Before sleep, though, there was beer.

Worker beer.

The household had a long table in the service yard under an awning.
Stable hands, kitchen workers, two servants, Davin, Rinna, Orin, Iven,
Marek, Serra, Pell, Nessa, me.

Teren came later.

Lady Merra did not.

Her father did.

Of course.

He appeared at the top of the service steps in a different waistcoat
with one stick instead of two.

Harl followed him looking exhausted.

"Beer," the old man said.

Harl said, "You have wine."

"Beer."

A stable hand gave him beer.

Class structure survived approximately three seconds.

He sat at the end of the table.

Not beside me.

Good.

He talked to Davin about wheels.

Davin answered more words than I had heard from him all week.

Not many.

Enough.

Apparently the old man knew something about axle wood.

Or pretended convincingly.

I could not tell.

I drank one beer.

Food came again because apparently this house did not understand
scarcity.

Cold meat.

Bread.

Onions.

The leaf-wrapped thing turned out to be soft cheese with herbs.

Rinna had already packed most of it for tomorrow and allowed us only
one.

Marek complained.

Rinna said, "Road."

He said, "Today."

"Road tomorrow."

He lost.

Serra sat across from a kitchen woman I had not met and spent ten
minutes talking about someone named Seli who had married badly and knew
it.

I did not know Seli.

Nobody explained.

Orin played dice with two stable hands.

His bruise was none of their concern.

Pell demonstrated the wooden fish to the little girl from the salon.

It bit her.

Apparently Pell had lied.

She screamed, then laughed.

He showed her how the spring worked.

I looked away before I learned too much.

Teren sat beside Rinna.

A man from the dinner came out to find him.

Not blue coat.

Another.

Broad, trimmed beard, good boots.

They talked standing.

I heard only pieces.

"East."

"Two days."

"Harvest fair."

"Hall?"

"Square."

"Weather."

"Covered market."

Teren asked something.

The man named a place I did not know.

Teren said, "When?"

"Six days."

Teren looked at Rinna.

Rinna looked toward Davin.

Davin was still talking axle wood with the old man.

She said, "Possible."

Not yes.

The bearded man gave Teren a card or folded paper.

Teren put it away.

Work traveling through dinner guests.

No announcement.

No destiny.

Maybe another job.

Marek noticed.

"Money?"

Teren said, "Sleep."

"Is sleep money?"

"No."

"Then wrong answer."

The old man laughed from the end of the table.

"Keep the king."

Teren said, "Please don't."

Later, when the yard had emptied somewhat, Lady Merra came out.

She thanked Teren.

Then Serra.

Then Marek.

Then me.

Not separately because I was special.

She was going down the table.

"Father enjoyed himself."

"I noticed."

"He'll tell everyone he bought the sword."

"He didn't."

"I know."

She looked toward the old man, who was now arguing with Harl about
whether he could sleep in the stable.

"He won't."

"Good."

She started to go.

I asked, "Your nephew?"

She turned.

"The one who saw us in Carrow."

"Jerin."

"Why did he remember me?"

She looked at me for a moment.

"Because you were funny."

That was almost disappointing.

"And?"

"And you had one leg."

There.

Simple.

"Right."

"He described Marek as the loud King."

From the table Marek said, "Accurate."

"He described Serra as the woman who made the King look stupid."

Serra raised her beer.

"Accurate."

Lady Merra said, "People remember what they can describe."

Then she left.

I sat with that for perhaps half a minute.

Marek leaned over.

"I can describe you."

"Don't."

"Expensive sword."

"That's two words."

"Efficient."

Serra said, "Loud King."

Marek looked hurt.

I finished my beer.

The brass clasp in my pocket pressed against my hip when I shifted.

Lyssa would have liked Lady Merra.

Maybe.

Or she would have liked watching Lady Merra tell her father no.

More likely.

I wanted to tell her about the silver spoon in the garden.

That was better than Drell.

Better than the box.

Better than most things.

I climbed to the loft.

Pell was already there.

The fish sat between our bedrolls.

"No."

"It can't sleep by the wall."

"It is wood."

"It's ours."

I moved it to his side.

He moved it back.

We compromised by putting it at his feet.

Outside, the estate settled.

Doors shut.

A horse stamped.

Someone laughed in the service yard.

The old man's voice carried once, then Harl's, then neither.

I counted today's eight copper in the dark by touch.

Not because I needed to.

Because I could.

Five had felt like proof that the show had happened.

Eight felt like something else.

Not wealth.

Work with different terms.

Tomorrow the bed cost nothing.

Breakfast cost nothing.

Rinna had food packed.

Three company silver had already become wheel, food, maybe toll.

Somewhere east there might be a harvest fair in six days.

I put the coins away.

The clasp stayed wrapped.

The letter kept going south without me.

Down below, someone closed the carriage bay.

The long box remained dry.

I slept.
