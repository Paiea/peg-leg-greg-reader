TEXT READER · CHAPTER 161

THE PLAYER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Pell woke me by dropping the fish on my chest.

I made a noise that was not a word.

"It fell."

"From where?"

He pointed at the beam above us.

I looked up.

"Why was it there?"

"Drying."

"It's wood."

"Exactly."

I moved the fish onto his blanket.

It twitched once.

"Fuck you."

Pell was already pulling on his boots.

Morning had gotten into the stable loft through every crack in the
boards. Dust hung in the pale strips of light. Below us, something heavy
rolled across the carriage bay, stopped, rolled again, and Davin said a
word I couldn't hear.

Pell paused.

"That was bad."

"You heard the word?"

"No. Davin used one."

Fair.

My shoulders still knew about yesterday. My hands were better than they
had been after the northern shows. The skin at the base of my fingers
had stopped feeling newly offended by the crutches and settled into
ordinary complaint.

I checked my residual limb.

Fine.

I rewrapped it anyway.

Below us, Rinna shouted, "Breakfast before Marek finds it."

Pell was gone before I had my shirt buttoned.

That sounded less like urgency and more like experience.

Breakfast was in the service kitchen, not the dining room.

Good.

I did not want to meet thirty-five chairs again.

There were eggs, bread, porridge, two kinds of jam, apples, cold meat,
and enough butter that Marek had apparently decided it was a food.

He had three pieces of bread stacked in one hand.

Rinna took two away.

"Those are mine."

"They are breakfast."

"Yes."

"Eat breakfast."

"I am."

"Not tomorrow's."

Marek looked at the remaining bread in his hand.

"How do you know which is tomorrow's?"

Rinna pointed at the wrapped parcels stacked near the door.

"Because tomorrow's is wrapped."

Marek looked toward them.

Rinna said, "No."

He sat down.

I took eggs.

Private breakfast was better than public breakfast.

That was probably not a useful classification.

I kept it anyway.

Serra was already there, hair tied up, white flower gone from her coat.
She was writing something on a small piece of paper with the side of her
hand shielding it from Marek, who was not looking.

Or pretending not to.

Nessa drank something hot while staring at Pell's sleeve.

Pell noticed.

"What?"

"Turn."

He turned.

She reached across, pulled one loose thread free, and burned the end in
the candle.

"Done."

"Thank you."

"No."

Orin came in last.

His bruise was darker.

Both shoes still matched.

Progress.

Marek pointed at him.

"Did you sleep?"

"No."

"Did you fight?"

"No."

"Did you win?"

"No."

Marek stopped.

"Interesting."

Orin took porridge.

Iven was irritatingly awake again.

"You're getting predictable," I told him.

"What?"

"Functional."

"Drink water."

"Fuck you."

"Also predictable."

Teren was not eating.

He had the folded paper from last night's bearded man open beside his
cup and another smaller note under it. Rinna sat opposite him with her
own piece of paper and a little stack of coins.

Not the whole company money.

Enough.

"Six days," Teren said.

"Five if Davin likes the south fork."

Davin came through the doorway carrying a metal ring the size of both my
hands.

"No."

Rinna looked at him.

"Five?"

"No."

"South fork?"

"No."

"Road?"

"Yes."

Teren folded the paper.

"Six."

Davin put the ring on the table.

Rinna picked it up, turned it over, and frowned.

"How much?"

"Seven."

"Copper?"

Davin looked offended.

"Silver?"

"Part."

I stopped eating.

Marek did too.

Rinna said, "How much part?"

Davin held two fingers about half an inch apart.

That did not help me.

It apparently helped Rinna.

She pushed one of the silver coins toward him, then held out her hand.

Davin took the silver and returned several copper from his pocket.

The metal ring disappeared into his coat.

Marek watched.

"What is it?"

"Wheel."

"Which part?"

Davin left.

Marek looked at me.

"Wheel."

"I heard."

"I understand wagons now."

"You should tell Davin."

"No."

Good.

Lady Merra never appeared.

Neither did Master Olan.

Harl did.

He came in while Marek was trying to wrap an egg in a napkin.

"No."

Marek froze.

Rinna looked delighted.

Harl pointed toward the carriage bay.

"Clear by nine."

Teren said, "We will."

"The east carriage leaves at half past."

"We'll be gone."

Harl looked at the wrapped food.

"All permitted?"

Rinna said, "Ask Mara."

"Which Mara?"

"The kitchen."

"We have three."

"The one who gave it to me."

Harl stared at her.

Rinna ate another spoonful of porridge.

Harl left.

I said, "Does anyone here have one name?"

Serra folded her note.

"Yes."

"Who?"

"No."

"That's Orin."

Orin lifted his spoon.

Breakfast ended because the wagons existed.

The company did not announce packing.

People simply started carrying things.

I had learned enough to finish eating first.

That felt important.

Then I carried my bag to the correct wagon.

Rinna watched me put it down.

"Good."

"I know."

"You want praise?"

"No."

"Good."

Pell handed me the fish.

"Why?"

"Hold."

"For how long?"

He was already gone.

I looked at the fish.

It looked back.

Painted eyes.

Still counts.

Nessa came through with two costume bundles and said, "Not there."

I moved.

"Where?"

"Anywhere else."

I moved again.

"Not there."

"You said anywhere."

"I was optimistic."

Eventually I ended up beside the rear wheel holding the fish, my own
bag, and a bundle of cloth that did not belong to me.

I did not know who had given me the cloth.

Serra passed.

"Useful."

"I have no idea what I'm doing."

"Also useful."

She put an apple on top of the cloth and left.

By the time Davin called for the wagon rail, the long box was already in
the carriage bay.

Upright.

Iron corners.

Dry.

Two stable hands helped him bring it out.

I watched the knot.

Then looked away.

Davin saw me.

"Good."

"I didn't do anything."

"Yes."

That was becoming insulting.

Teren came around the wagon with Harl.

"Nothing left?"

Harl asked.

Teren looked at Rinna.

Rinna looked at Nessa.

Nessa looked at Pell.

Pell looked at me.

I held up the fish.

"It's ours."

Pell nodded.

Harl said, "I don't want to know."

Correct.

We left at nine minutes after nine.

Harl looked at the sky as if those nine minutes belonged to him
personally.

No one from the family waved.

A kitchen woman did.

Serra waved back.

Maybe Mara.

Maybe not.

The estate disappeared behind walls and hedges faster than I expected.

Then we were on the east road.

Again.

Marek lasted twenty minutes before asking where we were going.

Teren was in the other wagon.

Rinna answered.

"East."

"I know that."

"Then why ask?"

"Specific east."

"Meal stop."

"Then?"

"Depends."

"On?"

"Money."

Marek sat up.

"Now we're talking."

Rinna ignored him.

The road was dry enough to be irritating rather than dangerous. Stone
appeared in stretches, then vanished under packed dirt, then returned
broken at the edges. Ditches ran beside us with expensive-looking
stonework full of weeds.

I had started recognizing bad roads by the way Davin's shoulders
changed.

He leaned before the wagon did.

Not always.

Enough.

Pell had the fish again.

It sat in his lap wrapped in cloth.

I asked, "What is that cloth?"

He looked down.

"Mine."

"Good."

I still did not know whose bundle I had carried.

Probably solved.

Serra sat opposite me with the note she had written at breakfast tucked
into her sleeve.

She looked out at fields.

A little after the first hour she said, "Stop."

Davin stopped.

No question.

Serra got down.

There was a stone post beside the road and a boy sitting on it with a
basket.

She gave him the folded note.

He gave her another.

No money that I saw.

No explanation.

She climbed back in.

Marek opened one eye.

"Romance?"

"No."

"Debt?"

"No."

"Crime?"

Serra looked at him.

"Sleep."

He did.

I wanted to ask who the note was for.

I didn't.

Progress.

At midday we reached a toll house where the road narrowed between two
stone posts and a chain.

I had seen tolls before.

I had not seen Rinna pay one for me.

The toll keeper came out with a board and counted horses, wagons, then
people.

"Players?"

Teren said, "Yes."

The man pointed at me.

"Him too?"

I looked behind myself.

There was nobody there.

Teren said, "Yes."

The keeper marked the board.

No one paused.

No one corrected.

Rinna handed over copper.

Not mine.

Company.

The chain came down.

We went through.

I thought about it for approximately the length of the gate.

Then Marek said, "How much?"

Rinna said, "No."

"I am asking academically."

"No."

"Professionally."

"No."

"Personally?"

"Especially no."

We stopped in a village an hour later.

I assumed we were staying.

I was wrong.

I discovered this after I had untied my bag.

Rinna looked at it on the ground.

"What are you doing?"

"Lodging."

"Food."

I looked at the inn.

"There's an inn."

"Yes."

"With rooms."

"Probably."

"We're not using them."

"No."

I retied my bag.

Marek watched from the doorway.

"Veteran."

"Fuck you."

"Passenger."

That was worse.

I hit him with the end of the strap.

The village had one main street, a smithy, a baker, a produce stall, two
chickens in a place chickens should not have been, and an inn whose sign
showed either a crown or a burning chair.

I was learning not to ask.

No one recognized us.

Excellent.

I went into the baker because Rinna gave me four copper and said, "Two
loaves. Dark. Not sweet."

I held the coins.

"For the company?"

She stared at me.

"No, Gregory. For a private bread collection."

"Right."

"Bring change."

That part felt more dangerous than Sword.

The baker was a woman with flour up both arms.

"Two dark loaves."

She pointed behind me.

I turned.

There were six.

"Which?"

"Dark."

"They're all dark."

She looked at me properly for the first time.

Then at my crutches.

Then past me toward the door.

"Move. You're blocking the onions."

I moved.

A boy carrying onions came through.

Good.

Yesterday I had been the expensive one.

Today I was in the way of onions.

Better.

The baker selected two loaves.

"Three copper."

I paid.

She gave one back.

I nearly put it in my own pocket.

Stopped.

Company change.

Rinna's.

I carried the bread out.

Marek intercepted me.

"Food."

"Company."

"I am company."

That was annoyingly difficult.

I held the bread higher.

"You can ask Rinna."

He went away.

Good.

Rinna took the loaves and the copper.

"You remembered."

"I almost didn't."

"Still remembered."

That was enough praise for the week.

We ate beside the inn.

Cold roast from Lady Merra's kitchen.

Bread we had just bought.

Cheese.

Apples.

The rich-house food tasted less rich beside a ditch.

Still good.

I bought a cup of hot broth from the inn for myself.

One copper.

I did it without asking the price twice.

That was new.

The woman handed it over.

I paid.

Done.

I had money.

Not much.

Enough that hot broth on a cool road did not require a meeting with
myself.

Marek watched.

"Buy me one."

"No."

"I spent my money."

"That sounds complete."

"Cards."

"Yes."

"And beer."

"Yes."

"And an investment."

I looked at him.

"What investment?"

He looked toward Orin.

Orin said, "No."

Marek sighed.

"Failed investment."

I drank my broth.

He did not get any.

Serra disappeared during the stop.

Not dramatically.

She walked down the side street and did not return for almost forty
minutes.

Teren noticed.

He asked Rinna, "Serra?"

"Back before wheels."

Apparently that was enough.

She returned before wheels carrying nothing.

A woman in a green apron walked with her as far as the corner, touched
her shoulder, then went back.

Serra climbed into the wagon.

Nobody asked.

Orin spent the entire stop sitting behind the inn with a man who had
come out of the kitchen.

They spoke quietly.

The man gave Orin a folded cloth packet.

Orin put it inside his coat.

Marek saw.

He opened his mouth.

I said, "No."

Marek looked at me.

"You're learning."

"I hate it."

"Good."

Teren and Rinna used the meal stop to argue about the next six days.

Not loudly.

Close enough that I heard pieces because I was sitting on the wagon
step.

The harvest fair was at a place called Dast.

Two days east if the road stayed good.

That confused me.

"Six days."

Teren looked at me.

"What?"

"The fair."

"In six."

"Dast is two days."

"Yes."

"So we get there four days early."

"No."

I waited.

He did not continue.

Rinna did.

"We don't go straight to Dast."

"Why?"

"Because four days early doesn't pay."

That made sense.

Mostly.

Teren tapped the folded paper against his knee.

There was a town southeast, half a day off the road, with a public hall
and no booking.

There was another east-road village with a yearly market in three days.

There was a mill settlement where someone owed Teren an answer.

There was also Carrow.

Not east.

I noticed that last part without anyone saying it.

Rinna said, "If Dast guarantees."

"They haven't."

"Then no."

"Gate plus floor."

"Not enough."

"Covered market."

"If it rains."

"Covered."

"If it sells."

Teren looked toward Marek.

Marek was trying to convince the innkeeper that actors required free
broth for cultural reasons.

The innkeeper was unconvinced.

Teren said, "We need one paid stop before Dast."

Rinna nodded.

"Two if Dast stays gate."

Davin came around the wagon.

"South road bad."

Teren looked up.

"How bad?"

Davin considered.

"Wheel."

Rinna said, "New?"

"No."

"Old?"

"Maybe."

Teren rubbed his face.

"East village?"

"Fine."

"Mill?"

Davin shrugged.

That was apparently a road report.

Teren folded the paper.

"East village tonight. Ask market in the morning."

Rinna said, "Lorn."

I had almost forgotten.

Not him.

Where he was.

Teren's face changed a little.

"Paid through tomorrow."

"After?"

"If he's still down, Bessa holds him another two nights. I left it."

"You told her?"

"Yes."

"Did he know?"

Teren paused.

"No."

Rinna stared.

"He was asleep."

"Still."

"I wrote it."

"To a sick man."

"He can read sick."

Marek had abandoned free broth and joined us.

"Who?"

"Lorn."

"Oh."

That was all.

Then, "Is he alive?"

Teren looked at him.

"Yes."

"Good."

Marek sat down.

I asked, "He catches us?"

"If he can," Teren said.

"How?"

"Coach, freight wagon, another company, walking if he wants to die."

"Does he know where we're going?"

Teren held up the paper.

"I don't know where we're going."

Fair.

Rinna said, "Bessa knows east road."

That was apparently enough for now.

The road started again.

East village tonight.

Not a show.

Maybe market work tomorrow.

Maybe Dast in six days.

Maybe something between.

I tried to count how many days I had been gone.

That went badly.

Carrow had become a sequence of mornings instead of a date.

Hessa had said next assessment when I returned.

Lyssa had said come back.

I had said I would.

None of those things had changed.

The road still went east.

I put my hand inside my coat and felt the scrap paper around the brass
clasp.

Still there.

My letter might reach Carrow tomorrow.

Or the next day.

Depending on road.

Depending on someone else's wagon.

Depending on weather.

Apparently everything depended on road.

Marek woke from his latest sleep.

"Where are we?"

"East."

He looked offended.

"Specific."

"Village tonight."

"Show?"

"No."

He frowned.

"Money?"

"No."

He lay back down.

"Wake me when capitalism returns."

I did not know the word.

Neither, apparently, did he.

I said, "What?"

He opened one eye.

"What?"

"You said something."

"No."

"You absolutely did."

"Sleep."

Serra laughed into her sleeve.

I decided not to pursue it.

The afternoon became fields.

Then woods.

Then fields again.

The road narrowed.

We passed a funeral going the other direction, six people walking behind
a cart with flowers on it. Everyone in our wagon stopped talking until
they passed.

No reason given.

Later we passed three boys driving pigs.

One pig escaped.

Iven got down to help because he was nearest.

The pig did not respect theatre.

It took eleven minutes.

When Iven came back muddy to the knee, Marek applauded.

Iven said, "I will kill you."

Marek said, "Strong second act."

Nobody paid us.

Near sunset, the east village appeared around a low stone bridge.

Not much of a village.

Maybe forty buildings.

Smoke.

A square.

A chapel.

A long inn with a stable yard.

Teren went inside before we unloaded anything.

Rinna said, "Wait."

So we waited.

This, too, I had learned.

Do not unload until someone has bought the right to make you unload.

Davin checked the new metal ring near the rear wheel.

Nessa took one costume bundle down because she said damp did not care
about contracts.

Pell gave me the fish.

I accepted it without asking why.

That was probably a mistake.

A girl crossed the square carrying a bucket.

She looked at the wagons.

Then at me.

Then at the fish.

"What's that?"

I looked down.

"Problem."

Pell, from under the wagon, said, "Prop."

The girl nodded as if that made more sense.

It probably did.

Teren came back out.

"Rooms are too much."

Marek groaned.

"Stable loft?"

"Full."

"Floor?"

"Also money."

Rinna asked, "Food?"

"Cheap enough."

"Sleep?"

Teren looked across the square.

There was a low warehouse beside the bridge.

"Merchant keeps freight there. Dry floor. One copper each."

Marek sat up.

"Each?"

"Company."

"Oh."

That changed his expression immediately.

Teren continued.

"No fire. No singing. Out by seven."

Orin said, "Good."

Marek said, "Bad."

Teren ignored both.

"Market master comes at breakfast. He may want something tomorrow."

"Paid?" Marek asked.

"Maybe."

"Wake me then."

Teren looked at me.

"Fish."

I looked at the thing in my hands.

"What?"

"Keep it with the props tonight."

That was all.

Not Pell.

Me.

I carried it across the square with the others.

Our props.

I did not notice the word until much later.

The warehouse smelled of rope, grain dust, old river water, and somebody
else's onions.

Of course onions.

Rinna paid the merchant from the company coins.

Davin chose the driest wall for the long box.

Upright.

Dry.

Unopened.

Nessa claimed a corner for costumes.

Pell immediately tried to put the fish beside my bedroll.

I moved it to the prop pile.

"Work."

He looked at me.

Then nodded.

Outside, Teren and Rinna were still talking about Dast.

Six days.

East.

Maybe.

I sat on my blanket and took off my boots.

For the first time since leaving Carrow, I understood that I did not
know when I was going back.

That should have frightened me more than it did.

Instead I was tired.

Hungry again.

Five copper for a public show.

Six for private.

Two more if a rich old man wanted seven minutes of regret.

One copper for hot broth.

Company copper for bread.

Company silver for wheels.

Free breakfast if somebody negotiated it.

A warehouse floor if rooms cost too much.

Tomorrow maybe work.

Maybe not.

I could go back.

Not tonight.

Probably not tomorrow.

I looked east through the open warehouse door while there was still
enough light to see the bridge.

Then Pell's fish twitched in the prop pile.

I said, "No."

Nobody asked why.
