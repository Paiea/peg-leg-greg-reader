TEXT READER · CHAPTER 162

THE STREET PLAYER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Someone kicked my boot before dawn.

I opened my eyes.

Nothing.

Then it happened again.

I sat up.

The fish was beside my foot.

Pell was asleep three blankets away.

I looked at the prop pile.

There was a fish-shaped absence.

"No."

The warehouse was almost dark. A gray line had appeared under the door,
enough to make the ropes hanging from the rafters look like bodies if I
was stupid.

I was tired enough to be stupid.

The fish twitched.

I picked it up by the tail.

"You're going back."

"Talking to props?"

Iven's voice came from the dark.

"No."

"Good."

"Why are you awake?"

"You're talking to a fish."

"That happened after."

"Still."

Somewhere near the wall Marek snored once, stopped, then resumed with
more confidence.

I carried the fish back to the prop pile and wedged it between the
battered Shopkeeper table leg and a basket of wooden cups.

It stayed.

For approximately six seconds.

Then Rinna said, "Up."

Nobody argued.

We had to be out by seven.

That turned out to be earlier than my body believed seven should be.

The warehouse became a machine in the dark. Blankets rolled. Bags
closed. Costume bundles appeared from Nessa's corner. Davin opened the
carriage door and let in cold air that smelled like river water and
horse.

Pell found the fish.

"Why is it there?"

"Props."

"It was by you."

"It moved."

Pell looked at me.

"Did you move it?"

"Yes."

"Then it didn't."

I hated him.

Outside, the village was already awake in pieces. A baker had smoke
coming from his chimney. Someone was dragging a cart across the bridge.
The chapel bell rang once, paused, then rang again as if reconsidering.

Davin and two men from somewhere I had not seen the night before moved
the long box out first.

Upright.

Dry.

Still closed.

I held the warehouse door because that was useful and because nobody had
asked me to carry the thing.

Davin glanced at me.

"Good."

"You're going to ruin that word."

He kept walking.

Rinna paid the merchant.

One copper each had sounded cheap until I watched her count enough coins
for all of us.

Then it looked like a meal.

Or two loaves.

Or several hot broths I was not buying Marek.

The merchant counted the coins twice.

"No fire?"

Rinna said, "No fire."

"No singing?"

Marek, carrying a blanket bundle, said, "Debatable."

The merchant looked at Teren.

Teren said, "No singing."

Marek looked betrayed.

"Out by seven?"

"We are leaving."

The merchant looked around the empty floor.

The only thing left was one onion near the wall.

He pointed.

"Yours?"

Teren looked at us.

Nobody claimed it.

Orin said, "No."

The merchant sighed.

We were out by seven.

Breakfast happened on the edge of the square because the market had
started arriving before we finished leaving the warehouse.

Rinna gave us bread, cheese ends, and apples.

Marek looked toward the wrapped roast.

Rinna said, "No."

"I didn't ask."

"You looked."

"I have eyes."

"Use them on the bread."

The roast stayed wrapped.

I ate bread.

The square changed while I was sitting there.

At first there were empty patches of packed dirt and stone. Then carts
arrived. A woman claimed half the chapel wall with baskets of onions.
Two men unfolded a cloth awning between poles. Someone backed a cart
full of cabbages directly into another cart and both drivers became
personally offended by geometry.

Chickens appeared.

Nobody brought them.

They were simply there.

A fish seller laid silver bodies over crushed ice in shallow wooden
trays. Smoke from a sausage pan moved low through the square. A boy with
three belts over one shoulder shouted prices at people who were not
listening.

By the time I finished my apple, the place had become difficult to
cross.

My crutches made it worse.

People saw one crutch and stepped around it, then nearly walked into the
other. I learned quickly that moving slowly did not make people patient.
It gave them more time to make bad decisions.

"Players?"

The voice came from behind me.

I turned.

A woman stood with Teren near the well.

She was perhaps fifty, maybe older, with a red wool cap pulled low over
gray hair and a leather ledger tucked under one arm. She wore an apron
with three pockets and had chalk on one sleeve.

Teren pointed at her.

"Market master."

She said, "Dema."

I said, "Gregory."

"I didn't ask."

"Good morning."

"Maybe."

I liked her less than Bessa.

Not in a bad way.

Dema looked across the square at the wagons.

"How many?"

Teren answered.

"Depends what you want."

"I asked how many people."

"Enough."

Dema stared at him.

Rinna came over carrying a cup.

"Twelve traveling. More or less working depending on what you're
counting."

Dema looked at her.

"Better."

Teren did not appear offended.

That was probably why Rinna was there.

Dema opened her ledger.

"I have vendors paying for a market. I have a cooper who says music
keeps people near his stall and a fish woman who says music keeps people
from buying fish."

"Does she sell bad fish?" Marek asked.

Dema looked past me.

Marek had appeared with bread.

"Who is that?"

Teren said, "Problem."

"Actor," Marek corrected.

"Same rate?"

"Usually higher."

Dema ignored him.

She wanted something from eleven until one.

Not a play.

Not even close.

"Noise," she said.

Teren said, "You can get noise free."

"I have noise."

She pointed at the square.

A man shouted, "TURNIPS!"

Dema waited.

"See?"

Teren nodded.

"What do you want?"

"People to stay."

"That's not a performance."

"Then perform something that makes them stay."

"How long?"

"Two hours."

Teren laughed.

Dema did not.

Rinna said, "Continuous?"

"No. I'm not cruel."

"How many sets?"

Dema thought.

"Three."

"Length?"

"Ten minutes."

"That's thirty minutes."

"I can count."

"Then why say eleven to one?"

"Because if you do all thirty at eleven, you're stupid."

Fair.

Teren asked, "What are you paying?"

Dema named a number.

I did not hear it because the sausage seller dropped a pan.

Rinna heard it.

Her face did not change.

Teren's did.

"No."

Dema closed the ledger.

"Good breakfast."

She turned away.

Marek said, "Wait."

Teren looked at him.

Marek stopped.

Rinna said, "Food?"

Dema turned back.

"What?"

"For the company. Midday."

Dema looked at the square.

"Vendors contribute."

"To what?"

"Food."

"Guaranteed?"

"Yes."

"How much cash?"

Dema named another number.

This one I heard.

It was not enough for a full show.

I knew that much now.

Teren said, "Plus hat."

Dema said, "No begging."

"Collection."

"No."

"Tips."

"No."

Marek whispered, "Tyrant."

Dema heard him.

"Especially no."

Teren said, "Two sets."

"Three."

"Eight minutes."

"Ten."

"Three sets, eight."

Dema shook her head.

"Three tens."

Rinna said, "Lunch for twelve."

Dema looked at her ledger.

"Ten."

"Twelve."

"Your man said twelve traveling."

"Exactly."

"Some of them can buy lunch."

Rinna smiled.

Not pleasantly.

Teren said, "Twelve."

Dema looked between them.

"Three sets. Ten minutes. No hat. Lunch for twelve. Twelve copper
company fee."

I knew that number.

Not because I knew company finance.

Because it was more than two loaves and less than a private house.

Teren said, "Fifteen."

Dema said, "Twelve."

"Fourteen."

"Twelve."

"Thirteen."

Dema opened her ledger.

"Twelve."

Teren held out his hand.

"Fine."

Dema shook it.

Marek said, "What do we get?"

Teren said, "Lunch."

"I mean money."

"Company gets twelve."

"I am company."

Rinna said, "You said that yesterday when stealing bread."

"I was right yesterday."

Teren looked at him.

"Three short sets. Nobody gets show rate."

I asked, "Do we get anything?"

Teren looked at me.

"One copper each for anyone in all three sets. Company keeps the rest."

Marek brightened.

"Work."

Rinna said, "You spent six yesterday."

"That was private Marek."

"What is this?"

"Market Marek."

Orin said, "Worse."

Dema had already walked away.

That was the booking.

If it counted as a booking.

Three ten-minute pieces over two hours.

Lunch.

One copper for me if I worked all three.

I had paid one copper for broth yesterday.

That was a strange way to understand wages.

Teren looked around.

"Nothing complicated."

Marek said, "Excellent."

"That's for you."

"Less excellent."

Teren pointed toward the square.

"First set there. Second near cloth row. Third back here."

I looked.

"There are carts there."

"Not at eleven."

"How do you know?"

"I don't."

Good.

He started assigning people.

Not roles.

People.

Marek, Serra, Iven, Orin, me.

Pell if needed.

Nessa said no before anyone asked.

Rinna definitely no.

Davin had already disappeared toward the wagons.

"What are we doing?" I asked.

Teren said, "Things people stop for."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you have."

Then he left to find Dema again.

Marek rubbed his hands.

"I have ideas."

Serra said, "No."

"I haven't said them."

"Good."

The first set became Sword because the board was portable and because
Marek could be loud.

That was the entire planning process.

Pell and I carried the mounting board toward the well.

Mostly Pell.

I held the fish.

"Why?"

"Opening."

"What opening?"

He kept walking.

I followed.

The square was worse now.

A woman carrying eggs told me to move.

A man carrying cloth told her to move.

A dog stood in the middle of both of them and refused government.

We found a patch beside the well.

Teren looked at it.

"No."

"Why?"

"Cart."

There was no cart.

Then a cart arrived.

Fine.

We moved six yards.

Iven used chalk to mark a rough edge.

Not a stage.

A warning.

"Don't go past that," he told me.

"Why?"

"Fish water."

I looked behind me.

The fish seller had a bucket draining across the stones.

Good.

I shifted right.

That put me near onions.

I was developing enemies.

The first set began because Marek climbed onto an empty crate and
shouted:

"WHO HERE CAN AFFORD A KING?"

People looked.

Not everyone.

Enough.

A cabbage seller shouted, "Not me."

Marek pointed at him.

"Then you may look for free."

That got a few laughs.

Serra walked into the space.

"Your Majesty, nobody asked for you."

"That has never stopped monarchy."

More people looked.

Pell shoved the fish into my lap and ran away.

I was mounted beside a well, three feet from onions, holding a wooden
fish.

This was not how Sword usually began.

Serra looked at me.

Then at the fish.

"What have you done?"

I said, "I found dinner."

The fish twitched.

A child screamed happily.

That helped.

Marek said, "That is not dinner. That is the royal beast."

I looked at the fish.

"It has poor breeding."

A fish seller shouted, "So do you!"

The crowd laughed harder at her.

Correctly.

I looked toward her.

"Madam, your relatives are on ice."

That got her.

She slapped the side of her table.

People stopped.

Not everyone.

A man bought onions during my next line.

Two women walked directly through the edge of the set because they
wanted cloth.

A dog smelled the mounting board.

Sword had never dealt with a dog.

Serra did.

"Don't."

The dog looked at her.

Then left.

I said, "Teach me that."

"You're worse."

The set lasted maybe nine minutes.

Maybe eleven.

I could not tell because market time had no walls.

People arrived halfway through.

People left before the end.

A man who laughed at one line never heard the next because someone
showed him a knife for sale.

When Teren clapped once, Marek ended.

Not with the planned ending.

I did not know there had been one.

Marek pointed at me.

"The sword is not for sale."

I said, "Everything is for sale."

Serra looked at me.

I added, "Apparently not here."

Teren said, "Done."

We stopped.

The crowd did not applaud as one thing.

Some people did.

Some immediately went back to buying.

One child asked Pell whether the fish was real.

Pell said, "Mostly."

I said, "No."

The child touched it.

It bit him.

Not hard.

He laughed.

Pell looked at me.

"See?"

"You said it didn't."

"When?"

"Several towns ago."

"Different fish."

I stared at him.

He took it away.

I believed nothing.

The second set was supposed to be Shopkeeper.

Then Dema moved us.

The cloth row had become too crowded.

She pointed toward the far end of the square beside a potter.

"Here."

Teren looked at the ground.

Mud.

He looked at me.

I looked at him.

"No."

Dema said, "Why?"

Teren pointed at my crutches.

Dema looked.

Then at the stones behind the potter.

"Move his table."

The potter said, "No."

Dema said, "You paid for six feet. You're using eight."

The potter moved his table.

I liked Dema more.

We got stone.

Shopkeeper started with Iven trying to cross the market carrying a
basket.

I stopped him.

"Pay me."

"For what?"

"Passage."

"This is a public road."

"Not anymore."

A vegetable seller said, "He's right."

I pointed at her.

"Thank you."

She said, "You owe me."

"Never mind."

That worked.

Then a customer asked Iven how much the basket cost.

He answered.

Actually answered.

The customer tried to buy it.

The basket was a prop.

Iven looked at Teren.

Teren looked away.

Iven said, "Three copper."

I said, "Five."

The customer said, "Two."

We had accidentally become merchants.

The crowd liked this more than the scene.

Iven said, "Four."

The customer said, "Two and an onion."

I said, "The onion is mine."

The onion seller shouted, "No it isn't!"

I said, "Debt."

That got enough people laughing that the customer realized something was
happening.

He did not buy the basket.

Good.

We found the scene again.

Mostly.

At one point a cart came through and we stopped.

Not theatrically.

We moved.

The horse did not care about pacing.

Once it passed, Iven said, "Where were we?"

I said, "You owed me money."

"That doesn't narrow it down."

Good.

We continued.

I was beginning to understand why Teren had said things people stop for.

Not scenes people watch.

Stopping was the first problem.

Then staying.

Then leaving before the horse.

Nobody explained this.

They were too busy avoiding the horse.

Between sets I was given an ordinary responsibility.

Rinna handed me two copper.

"Water."

"For?"

"Us."

"How much?"

"Cheapest clean."

That sounded like a trap.

I found a woman selling cups near the chapel.

One copper filled a small jug.

I bought two jugs.

No change.

I carried one awkwardly with a finger hooked through the handle while
using my crutches.

This was stupid.

I made it twelve steps.

Then a girl selling buttons said, "Give me that."

I did.

She carried both jugs to Rinna.

Rinna gave her an apple.

No money.

The girl left.

I said, "I could have done that."

Rinna said, "Badly."

"Yes."

"Then don't."

Useful.

Annoying.

The third set nearly did not happen.

At twelve forty-five Dema found Teren.

"Enough."

Teren said, "We owe three."

"You did two."

"We were hired for three."

"I know."

"Then?"

"Vendors are complaining."

"Which?"

She pointed.

Fish woman.

Potter.

A man selling ribbons.

The cabbage seller was not complaining.

He had apparently sold out.

Teren said, "We do the third quieter."

Dema looked at Marek.

"No."

Marek looked wounded.

Serra said, "We can."

Teren thought.

Then pointed at Orin.

"Death."

Orin said, "No."

"Short."

"No."

"Quiet."

Orin looked at the market.

Then at the fish woman.

"Fine."

I had never seen Orin's little death piece.

Apparently neither had half the company.

It required a stool, one cup, Orin, Serra, and almost nothing else.

I was not in it.

That meant no copper.

I looked at Teren.

He looked at me.

"You're in the end."

"What end?"

"You'll know."

I hated that.

The piece began almost quietly.

Orin sat with the cup.

Serra asked him why he had come.

He said he was waiting to die.

A woman buying ribbons slowed.

Then stopped.

The piece was funny.

Not loud funny.

Orin complained that death was late.

Serra suggested he had the wrong day.

Orin accused death of poor administration.

I almost said jurisdiction from the side.

I bit the inside of my cheek.

Good.

More people stopped because other people had stopped.

That was different.

No shouting.

No King.

No fish.

At the end Serra looked around as if searching.

"Perhaps death took someone else."

Teren pointed at me.

I understood nothing.

Then Orin looked directly at me.

"One leg."

Fuck him.

I entered.

"What?"

Orin said, "Did you see death?"

I looked behind myself.

"No."

"Then he took the other one."

The crowd made a sound.

Not huge.

Good.

I looked down at myself.

Then at Orin.

"Late."

Orin nodded.

"Always."

I said, "Charge him."

Orin handed me the cup.

"Your department."

Shopkeeper.

Fine.

I took the cup.

"Pay me."

That was the end.

People laughed.

Then the sausage seller shouted lunch.

The market broke apart.

Work ended.

No bow.

No curtain.

No doors closing.

Just food.

Dema found Teren near the well and counted twelve copper into his hand.

He counted.

Rinna watched.

Dema said, "Three sets."

Teren said, "Three."

"Third was better."

Marek, close enough to hear, said, "I wasn't in it."

Dema said, "Yes."

Marek looked at Serra.

"Hostile village."

Dema walked away.

Lunch came from vendors in pieces.

Bread from one.

Sausages from another.

Apples.

A pot of beans.

Cheese.

The fish woman sent nothing.

Fair.

Rinna made sure twelve people ate.

Davin appeared exactly when food did.

I had not seen him for two hours.

"Where were you?"

"Wagon."

"Doing what?"

"Wheel."

Of course.

Teren paid the one-copper market share to those who had worked all three
sets.

Me.

Marek.

Serra.

Iven had missed the third.

Orin had missed the first.

I held out my hand.

One copper.

Marek held out both.

"Why?"

"Intensity."

"One."

"Private market rate?"

"No."

"King rate?"

"No."

"Starvation?"

Rinna handed him a sausage.

Marek considered.

"Accepted."

Teren gave the remaining company copper to Rinna.

Not much.

The job had paid for part of the warehouse floor.

Or bread.

Or water.

Or something else.

It had also fed us.

That was apparently enough to make it worth staying until one.

Barely.

After lunch Teren disappeared with Dema.

I thought they were negotiating another performance.

They weren't.

Dema came back alone.

Teren went to the wagons.

"Pack."

Marek said, "Show?"

"No."

"Tonight?"

"No."

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

"Money?"

"Maybe."

Marek looked at me.

"Strong industry."

Rinna asked, "Where?"

Teren held up another folded note.

"Mill."

"The answer?"

"Yes."

"Good?"

"Not no."

Davin said, "Road?"

Teren pointed southeast.

Davin frowned.

"Wheel."

Rinna said, "You said that yesterday."

"Still."

Teren looked at the sky.

Then at the square.

Then east.

"Dast?"

Rinna said, "Not yet."

He nodded.

"Mill. Slow."

That was the decision.

Half a day southeast if the road behaved.

Longer if Davin's face was accurate.

The mill settlement had not booked us.

Someone there had finally answered Teren.

That was enough to move.

I put my one market copper into my inner pocket.

It touched the scrap paper around Lyssa's clasp.

For a second I thought about buying something else in the market.

Then did not.

Not because I couldn't.

Nothing there needed buying.

That was new too.

Pell handed me the fish.

"Again?"

"Prop wagon."

"I know."

"Then why me?"

He had already walked away.

I carried it.

A woman with onions stepped directly into my path.

I moved.

She did not know I had been Sword beside her stall two hours earlier.

Maybe she did.

She did not care.

Good.

At the wagon, I put the fish with the props.

Our props.

I noticed it this time.

I left the word alone.

Davin checked the long box.

Dry.

Upright.

Unopened.

Rinna counted food.

Teren read the mill note again.

Serra stood near the bridge with her own folded note in one hand, not
reading it.

Orin had the cloth packet from yesterday inside his coat.

Marek had one copper and half a sausage.

Iven still had pig mud on one trouser leg.

The village market kept going without us.

We left at one forty.

Nobody applauded.

Nobody waved.

Dema was arguing with the potter before we reached the bridge.

The road southeast was bad.

Not dramatic.

Just slow.

Every rut took time.

Every bad edge made Davin choose.

The wagons moved carefully enough that walking people passed us twice.

I looked back once.

The village disappeared behind trees.

One copper in my pocket.

Lunch in my stomach.

No evening show.

Maybe work at the mill.

Dast somewhere beyond that.

Carrow somewhere behind us.

I had started thinking in one more stop.

That was not the same as deciding to stay.

It was also not the same as going home.

Marek woke when the wagon hit a hole.

"Money?"

"Maybe."

He nodded.

"Good."

Then he went back to sleep.
