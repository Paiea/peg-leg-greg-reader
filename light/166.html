TEXT READER · CHAPTER 166

THE ROADHAND

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

We left Kest with onions.

That was what the market became once the cattle, noise, collection bowl,
goat, and thirty-five copper were behind us. A sack of onions sat
beneath the wagon seat. Bread was wrapped beside it. The dried meat had
been divided into pieces that looked insufficient before anybody ate
them.

Marek looked into the food sack.

"Is there cheese?"

Rinna said, "No."

"Was there cheese?"

"No."

"Could there have been cheese?"

"Yes."

He looked wounded by possibility.

The road east was crowded enough that we did not feel alone for the
first hour. Market carts went both directions. Empty livestock wagons
rattled west. A family with three children and a cage of chickens passed
us, then stopped to fix a strap, then passed us again. Two men driving
sheep occupied the center of the road with the calm certainty of people
who knew everyone else would move.

Davin moved.

The wagons did not move quickly.

By evening, Kest had stopped existing behind us.

Not literally.

Probably.

I could no longer see anything that belonged to it.

We camped beside a low stand of trees where somebody had camped before
us and left a black circle of old fire stones. Davin checked the ground
before committing the wagons. Rinna checked the water. Teren checked the
folded card again.

I watched everybody check things and then carried the onion sack to the
wrong wagon.

Nessa said, "Why?"

"Food."

"Not here."

"Right."

I carried it back.

That was my contribution to logistics.

Dinner was bread, dried meat, onion, and water.

Marek held a piece of onion between two fingers.

"This is punishment."

"You wanted food," Rinna said.

"I wanted dinner."

"You have dinner."

"This is components."

Orin ate his.

Serra ate hers.

I ate mine.

Marek looked around for support.

Pell said, "I like onions."

"Of course you do."

Pell had the wooden fish beside him and the white stone from Kest
balanced on its back.

Marek pointed.

"That has more wealth than I do."

"It's lucky," Pell said.

"Sell it."

"No."

"Then it isn't wealth."

Pell considered this and put the stone in his pocket.

Nobody performed.

Nobody rehearsed.

After dark, Nessa worked on the chewed mounting cloth by firelight until
she decided firelight was making the color worse and stopped. Pell held
the damaged corner while she folded it.

"Goat," he said.

"I know."

"It could have been worse."

Nessa looked at him.

Pell reconsidered.

"It was exactly as bad as it was."

"Better."

Serra wrote something with the paper braced against her knee. I did not
ask what. Orin disappeared beyond the trees for long enough that I
noticed and not long enough that anybody else appeared concerned.

Marek told Iven a story about a baron who had once paid him three silver
to insult a cousin.

Iven said, "No."

"You weren't there."

"Neither were you."

"I was central."

"To a story you invented."

"That is what central means."

I lay down beneath the wagon.

The road had been quiet for almost an hour.

I could still hear one of the horses tearing grass.

No audience.

No mill wheel.

No market bell.

No money hitting a bowl.

Just people sleeping because there was another road tomorrow.

That was work too, apparently.

Nobody paid for it.

In the morning Davin asked, "Bridge?"

I was eating the last heel of yesterday's bread.

"Lerren's?"

He waited.

"Apparently."

He nodded.

That was the entire reward for remembering useful information.

The road split before noon.

The left branch went north toward the ford. The right dropped through a
shallow valley toward a line of trees and a stone marker whose lettering
had been repaired often enough that the newer cuts did not quite match
the old ones.

LERREN 3.

Someone had scratched FORD BAD beneath it.

Davin got down from the wagon and looked at the ruts.

Teren said, "Bridge?"

Davin said, "Bridge."

Nobody asked me.

That seemed reasonable.

Traffic thickened as we approached the river. Not fair traffic yet.
Ordinary bridge traffic. Grain carts. A wagon carrying clay jars packed
in straw. Two riders. A farmer with a pig tied in a small cart who spent
most of the queue telling the pig to stop doing something the pig
continued doing.

The bridge sat between two low stone abutments. Timber span. Narrow
enough that wagons crossed one direction at a time.

I looked at it.

Teren said, "Don't."

"I'm sitting."

"You can lean sitting."

"I've been told."

There was a toll shed.

Of course there was.

Rinna climbed down before we reached it.

"How much?" Marek asked.

"Company."

"That is not a number."

"It is the number that doesn't concern you."

The toll keeper counted wagons, horses, and people with less interest in
us than the Kest goat had shown. He looked at my crutches tied beside
me.

"Passenger?"

Before I could answer, Teren said, "Player."

The keeper marked something on a slate.

"Same."

Apparently my professional identity had achieved administrative equality
with sitting down.

Rinna paid several copper.

I did not see exactly how many.

The gate lifted.

The bridge took us.

The river below was high and brown enough that I understood the harness
seller's opinion of the ford without needing to see it. Branches moved
in the current. The horses disliked the hollow sound of wheels over
timber but kept going.

Halfway across, I looked downstream.

No mystery.

No broken support.

No secret.

Just water going somewhere else faster than we were.

On the far side, a woman selling apples from a cart had positioned
herself where everybody who had just paid a toll could immediately spend
more money.

I respected that.

I bought one apple for myself.

One copper got me two because one had a bruise.

I ate the good one while walking beside the wagon.

The bruised one went into my pocket.

Marek saw it.

"Second apple?"

"Yes."

"You've changed."

"I bought fruit."

"Exactly."

He held out his hand.

"No."

"Capital is trapped."

"Good."

"You don't understand finance."

"I understand this apple."

I ate it later too.

Lerren itself was barely a settlement. Bridge, toll shed, smith, inn,
stable yard, six or seven houses, and a shrine set back from the road
under trees. It existed because the river made people stop.

We stopped.

Not to perform.

The innkeeper did not know us.

The stable boy did not know us.

The woman carrying buckets through the yard did not know us and became
annoyed when I stood where she needed to walk.

"Move."

I moved.

"Other way."

I moved the other way.

"Thank you."

That was the whole interaction.

It was refreshing.

Davin wanted one horse checked because a shoe had started wearing
unevenly. The smith looked at it, looked at Davin, and said, "Tomorrow
if you want iron."

Davin said, "No."

The smith shrugged.

"Then next town."

Davin checked the hoof himself.

I asked, "Bad?"

"No."

"Then why check?"

He looked at me.

"Because."

Good.

Rinna paid for water and a small measure of oats. Teren asked the
innkeeper about the east road.

The innkeeper said, "Fair?"

"Dast."

"Then everybody's going."

"Road?"

"Dry enough. North side after the old orchard."

Davin said, "Why?"

"South shoulder washed last spring."

"Still?"

"Still."

Teren looked at me.

I said, "I didn't know that."

"No one expected you to."

It should not have sounded reassuring.

It did.

We stayed long enough to rest the horses and eat.

No room.

No performance.

No one asked what was in the wagons.

A little boy stared at the Sword mounting board until Pell told him it
was a table.

The boy said, "Bad table."

Pell said, "Yes."

Apparently criticism was becoming consistent across regions.

Serra sat alone behind the stable for part of the stop with folded paper
in her hand. She did not write while I could see her. She just read.

Orin bought something from the smith.

Small.

Wrapped in cloth.

It went into the same sort of private space his other things went.

Nobody asked.

I was beginning to understand that traveling with people did not mean
eventually receiving an explanation for everything they did.

It mostly meant being nearby while they continued doing it.

We left Lerren after midday.

The road beyond the river was quieter for several miles.

Then the fair began arriving from the opposite direction.

Not the fair itself.

Pieces.

A wagon stacked with painted wooden bowls.

A woman driving two mules with bundles of dyed cloth.

Three men walking beside a cart full of iron pans.

A family carrying poles that might become an awning.

Two musicians with narrow cases strapped to their backs.

Marek turned around to watch them.

"Competition."

One of the musicians heard him.

"From you?"

Marek put a hand to his chest.

The man kept walking.

Serra laughed.

"Brutal."

"I wasn't ready."

"Apparently neither is Dast."

More wagons appeared as the afternoon went on.

Some were going toward the fair.

Some were leaving nearby villages to sell there.

At a roadside well, a woman told Rinna that bread would cost more in
Dast by tomorrow.

Rinna bought two loaves from her.

Marek said, "We have bread."

"Now we have cheaper bread."

"That is almost investment."

Rinna looked at him.

He went away.

By the second night, road food had become road food.

Bread.

Onion.

Meat.

Water.

The new loaf was softer than the old one.

That was the event.

Pell dealt cards on an overturned crate after dinner. Iven played. Marek
watched for a while and did not join.

"You have money," I said.

"Exactly."

"You're learning?"

"Don't insult me."

He went to sleep with his copper intact.

I lost one hand to Pell and won the next.

No money.

The stakes were who had to refill the water pot.

I lost overall.

That cost walking.

I carried the pot carefully because spilling it would mean doing the
walk again.

On the way back, I passed Teren and Rinna talking beside the lead wagon.

"Bellan," Rinna said.

"If he's there."

"If he isn't?"

"Find whoever is."

"And if there's no space?"

"Find out before unloading."

"Dast prices?"

"We don't stay in town unless the work pays."

Davin sat on the wagon tongue repairing a leather strap.

"Two days," he said.

Teren looked at him.

"To Dast?"

"Edge."

"Fair?"

"Traffic."

That was close enough.

I brought the water back.

Pell said, "Took you long enough."

I set it beside him.

"Drink less."

He drank.

The next morning we joined a line of wagons without deciding to.

There were simply enough of them going east that our speed became their
speed.

A cart ahead of us carried carved spoons.

The wagon behind us carried three enormous pumpkins and one old woman
who stared at everybody like she suspected theft.

At midday, the road climbed.

From the ridge I could see more country east than I had expected.

Fields.

Low woods.

A pale line of road.

Smoke from two settlements.

Farther away, another road meeting ours.

Wagons on it.

All moving the same general direction.

Dast was not visible.

That made it worse somehow.

Or better.

I could not tell.

I had left Carrow to travel with the company.

That had been true.

I had gone to the eastern town.

Also true.

Then north.

Then private work.

Then a market.

Then a mill.

Then Kest.

Each place had been close enough to the place before it that I had never
needed to decide what the whole thing was.

One more show.

One more road.

One more morning.

Now I could not point west and pretend Carrow was just beyond the next
hill.

Lyssa might already have my letter.

She might have written back.

If she had, the reply had nowhere to find me unless she guessed better
than I had planned.

Hessa expected me back.

Not today.

I had never said today.

That was convenient.

I touched the inside of my coat where the brass clasp was wrapped.

Serra was sitting across from me.

She noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

She looked back at the road.

I almost asked whether she was going to Dast because she wanted to.

That would have been a stupid question.

She was already going.

So was I.

At the next stop, Teren handed me a bucket.

"Water."

"Where?"

He pointed toward a farmyard well.

"Ask."

I went.

The farmer charged nothing for water but told me not to use the first
trough because it was for calves.

I had already started toward the first trough.

"Other one."

"Right."

"Players?"

I looked back at the wagons.

"Yes."

"Dast?"

"Yes."

It came out before I thought about it.

Not maybe.

Not apparently.

Yes.

The farmer nodded.

"Busy."

"Good?"

He shrugged.

"For somebody."

I filled the bucket.

On the way back, I realized that was the first time I had told a
stranger where we were going without checking whether the company had
changed its mind.

Teren took the bucket.

"Thanks."

That was all.

We camped that night beside three other fair wagons.

One sold pottery.

One carried preserved fruit.

The third belonged to four people with painted poles, rolled canvas, and
a dog that could stand on its back legs for an unsettling amount of
time.

Marek watched the dog.

"Competition."

The dog's owner said, "He works cheaper."

Marek looked offended.

The owner fed the dog.

No one performed.

The painted-pole people did not ask us to perform.

We did not ask them.

They repaired canvas.

Nessa repaired cloth.

Davin checked wheels.

Rinna counted food.

Teren talked briefly with one of the pottery sellers about the road
ahead.

Pell tried to make the wooden fish bite the white stone and failed.

The dog watched him.

I sat against the wagon wheel and ate bread and onion.

The eastern sky went dark slowly.

Somewhere beyond the next ridges, people were building a harvest fair.

We would reach its outer traffic tomorrow.

Maybe Bellan would be there.

Maybe there would be work.

Maybe there would be nowhere to sleep.

Nobody knew.

For the first time, that did not feel like a gap in the plan.

It was the plan.

Davin walked past me.

"Up early."

"How early?"

"Early."

"Helpful."

He kept walking.

I looked west once.

There was nothing to see.

Then I got under the wagon and went to sleep.

The morning after Lerren also taught me that company work could be
assigned before I was fully awake.

Rinna kicked the sole of my boot.

"Up."

"I am."

"You are horizontal."

"Temporary."

She handed me a coil of cord.

"What?"

"Rear cover."

I crawled out and found one corner of the wagon cover hanging loose
where the knot had worked itself ugly during the night. Davin was
already with the horses. Pell was somewhere under the other wagon.
Nobody explained why the cord had come to me.

I tied what I thought was the right knot.

Rinna looked at it.

"No."

I untied it.

"Why?"

"Because Davin will cut it off and then look at me."

"Terrible."

"Yes."

She showed me which loop she wanted once, then left before I had
finished.

The second attempt held.

Davin checked it later without comment.

That felt dangerously close to approval, so I did not mention it.

At another farm stop, I carried a bucket and a sack of oats at the same
time because I did not want to make two trips. This was a poor decision
with crutches. I got halfway across the yard, put the oats down, carried
the water, came back for the oats, and discovered Marek sitting on the
sack.

"Move."

"I'm guarding it."

"From what?"

He looked around the empty yard.

"Success."

I pushed his knee with the end of one crutch.

He moved.

The farmer's wife watched us from the doorway without any sign that she
knew or cared that either of us had stood in front of crowds the day
before.

"Players," she said eventually.

Marek brightened.

Then she pointed at the wagon.

"Keep that wheel off my herb bed."

Davin had already done so.

Marek's brightness faded.

"She recognized us."

"She recognized wheels."

"Same profession."

It was not.

We loaded the oats and left.
