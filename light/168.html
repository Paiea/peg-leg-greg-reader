LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 168

THE PITCHMAN

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The north arcade had more wind than roof.

That was my professional assessment.

I arrived before breakfast with Pell, the Sword board, and instructions
from Teren to find out whether the loading slope worked. Pell carried
one end of the board. I carried the other badly until we reached the
first patch of churned ground, then we stopped pretending and dragged it
on its edge.

Dast was already awake.

Someone had washed the stone lane and pushed yesterday's cabbage leaves
into a wet pile beside a drain. Produce wagons backed toward the covered
market. A boy carried six empty baskets at once because apparently
making two trips was forbidden. Two women were fastening painted cloth
over a honey stall while arguing about whose sister had lied to whom.

The wind lifted one corner of their cloth.

One woman slapped it down without interrupting the argument.

At the north arcade, three carts were already using our performance
space.

Not parked in it.

Using it.

The arcade ran along the north side of the trading hall, roofed in tile
and open toward a lane wide enough for carts. Stone columns held the
roof. Between them, wind came straight through the fair. The south end
had the loading slope Bellan had mentioned, a broad stone incline
leading from the lane to the raised arcade floor.

I looked at the slope.

Pell looked at me.

"Well?"

"I can get up it."

"Good."

"Board can too."

"Good."

"We're done."

A man pushing a handcart up the slope said, "No."

We moved.

He passed us with two crates of turnips.

"What?" I asked.

"Loading."

"I know."

"Then don't stand there."

"We're performing here."

"When?"

"First bell after noon."

He shrugged.

"Then don't stand there now."

Fair.

Pell leaned the board against a column.

The man stopped.

"Not there either."

"Why?"

"Cheese."

I looked.

The stall behind the column had a painted board reading HARRA'S NORTH
FARM CHEESE. A woman inside was stacking pale wheels on cloth.

"That's my column," she said.

Pell moved the board.

"Do you own the roof?"

"Today."

"How much?"

She pointed farther down.

"Ask Bellan if you want to buy weather."

We moved again.

The south end of the arcade gave us enough room for the Sword board,
maybe eight performers, and an audience that would have to choose
between watching us and continuing toward onions.

The wind came through from the lane, struck the wall behind us, and
found new directions.

Pell's coat flapped.

A scrap of paper skittered past.

He caught it with his boot.

"Bad."

"Mostly covered."

He laughed.

We tested the board on the slope.

Up was easy enough with two people.

Turning it at the top was not.

The raised edge left less room than it had looked like from below. I
tried to pivot backward and nearly put my crutch through a basket of
leeks.

A woman slapped my crutch aside with a bunch of greens.

"Not those."

"Sorry."

"Those are paid for."

"They're still here."

"Paid for."

I moved.

Pell said, "You almost bought leeks."

"I don't want leeks."

"Then good save."

The loading man came back down with the empty cart.

I asked, "Do carts keep using this after noon?"

He looked at me as if I had asked whether gravity had afternoon hours.

"Yes."

"During performances?"

"During loading."

"Which is during performances."

"If you're performing during loading."

"Bellan gave us this."

"Bellan doesn't move turnips."

That seemed important.

"How often?"

"Carts?"

"Yes."

"Depends."

"On?"

"Turnips."

He pushed away.

Pell said, "Helpful."

"Very."

We found a place for the board against the rear wall where it would not
block the cheese woman, the leeks, or the loading slope.

Then the wind pushed it over.

Not all the way.

Enough.

Pell caught it.

"More helpful."

We took it back down.

At camp, Teren was eating bread.

"Well?"

"Slope works."

"Good."

"Carts use it."

"During?"

"Everything."

He chewed.

"Space?"

"Enough if we don't own any of it."

"Normal."

"Wind."

"I heard."

"Board needs tying or someone holding it."

Davin, beside the rear wagon, said, "Tie."

Nessa said, "Not cloth."

Nobody had asked her.

She was right.

Pell said, "Rope."

Davin gave him rope.

Apparently inspection was complete.

Breakfast cost company food.

Rinna had bought eggs from somewhere, but not enough eggs to make eggs a
major event. We each got one with bread. Marek looked at his.

"Protein."

I said, "Investment?"

He pointed the egg at me.

"Growth."

"Of what?"

"Potential."

He ate it.

Orin had returned sometime before I woke. His bruise had gone yellow at
the edges. Nobody mentioned where he had been. Serra still had the red
thread around one finger. She used that hand to peel her egg.

Teren explained the set while we ate.

"King first. Short."

Marek nodded.

"Sword."

I nodded.

"Then widow."

Orin looked up.

"Here?"

"Yes."

"Wind."

"Better than death."

"Everything is."

Serra said, "Not everything."

Orin considered.

"Most."

No Shopkeeper.

No Advocate.

Iven was collection.

Pell was props and board.

Nessa immediately said the red overskirt was not going anywhere near the
loading slope.

Serra said, "Good."

Marek said, "My coat?"

Nessa looked at him.

"My coat is fine."

"It will be if you stop touching it."

He stopped.

We carried only what we needed.

That was still too much.

The fair had thickened by the time we returned. The arcade lane was full
of people moving in both directions. Buyers stopped at cheese, honey,
tools, cloth. Children moved against traffic because children were
apparently exempt from roads. The musicians had started somewhere beyond
the north arch. I could hear the fiddle between shouts.

The puppet booth had a line.

The comic pair were not performing yet. One of them was eating something
from a paper wrapper while the other repaired the bench with a hammer.

Work before work.

We set the Sword board against the rear wall and tied it to an iron ring
meant for something else.

The cheese woman watched.

"That staying?"

"Twenty minutes," Pell said.

"Good."

"Don't like swords?"

"Don't like boards."

"Fair."

Iven put the collection bowl near the edge of the crowd lane.

The loading man appeared from nowhere.

"No."

Iven looked at him.

"Again?"

"Cart wheel."

He pointed.

The bowl sat exactly where a cart turning off the slope would clip it.

Iven moved it beside the rear column.

The cheese woman said, "No."

Iven looked at Teren.

Teren said, "Where?"

She pointed to the corner of her stall.

"Inside that line. People see it. Doesn't block me."

Iven placed it there.

"Thank you."

"Don't lean on cheese."

"I wasn't."

"You look like you might."

Iven stepped away.

First bell after noon was not a single bell.

Three bells sounded within perhaps a minute from different parts of
Dast.

Marek looked at Teren.

"Which?"

Teren said, "Ours."

"Confident."

"No."

He stepped into the lane anyway.

Marek followed.

The King piece began with twelve people watching.

That included two children who had been waiting for cheese.

Marek did not shout immediately.

I noticed because I expected him to.

He walked into the open space with the crown under one arm and said,
conversationally, "I have been informed this is a kingdom."

A man passing with a sack said, "Bad luck."

Marek turned.

"Citizen."

"No."

The man kept walking.

The children laughed.

Marek put on the crown.

The wind took it off.

He caught it against his chest.

More people laughed.

He looked at the crown.

"Rebellion."

That stopped four more.

Serra entered.

The short King piece was mostly theirs. I stayed behind the column with
the Sword board and watched people decide whether we existed.

Some did not.

A woman walked directly through the edge of the scene to buy cheese.

A cart came up the slope.

Marek saw it before I did.

He moved.

Serra moved.

The cart went between them.

The driver said, "Sorry."

Marek said, "Treason."

The driver kept going.

That got a laugh from the cheese woman.

By the time Teren signaled Sword, perhaps thirty people had stopped.

Not thirty still people.

Thirty fair people.

Some eating.

Some carrying bags.

Some looking over shoulders toward somewhere else.

Pell and I brought the board forward.

The rope helped.

The wind still pushed at the mounting cloth.

Nessa had weighted the bottom corners with two small sandbags.

I had not known we owned sandbags.

Apparently we owned everything except money.

Serra mounted me.

The woman selling leeks watched.

"That's him," one child said.

"Who?"

"The sword."

I did not know the child.

That was fine.

Marek began the piece.

A cart bell sounded behind him.

Nobody moved.

The loading man shouted, "Cart!"

Everybody moved.

Including the audience.

For several seconds the performance became a crowd making a lane.

The cart came down the slope loaded with empty grain sacks.

The driver looked at me on the board.

"Sorry."

"You're ruining the kingdom."

"Good."

He passed.

Half the audience returned.

The other half did not.

That was Dast.

Marek resumed.

He did not explain.

Serra picked up from the next useful line.

I answered late because I was watching a strip of mounting cloth trying
to escape the sandbag.

"Pay attention," Serra said in character.

"I am furniture."

"Expensive furniture."

"Then stop leaving me outside."

That worked.

Not huge.

Enough.

The wind pushed my coat against one side of the board.

A boy near the front pointed.

"He's tied."

"I'm mounted."

"Same."

"No."

His mother pulled him back.

"Don't argue with the sword."

"Thank you," I said.

She laughed and put a copper into the bowl.

I saw it.

That was dangerous.

I stopped looking at the bowl.

The Sword ended clean enough.

No goat.

No broken prop.

No accidental jurisdiction.

The crowd was larger than at the beginning and smaller than it had been
halfway through.

That seemed impossible until I looked at the lane.

People were replacing one another.

The widow piece began with Serra seated on the bench and Orin standing
over her with a paper that claimed her dead husband owed him money for
the chair she was sitting on.

The wind took the paper.

Orin watched it go.

Serra said, "Proof?"

He looked toward the lane.

"Traveling."

The paper slapped against a man's boot.

The man picked it up.

Orin held out his hand.

The man read it.

Or pretended to.

"Bad contract."

Serra said, "You hear that?"

Orin said, "He isn't a lawyer."

The man handed it back.

"Neither are you."

Then he left.

The crowd laughed.

Orin looked at Serra.

"I dislike this town."

"Chair's still mine."

"Temporarily."

The piece continued.

It was quieter than Sword.

That helped.

People leaned.

The lane kept moving behind them.

At the end, Serra stood, took the chair, and walked away with it.

Orin stared after her.

"That was collateral."

The applause came unevenly from three sides.

Real enough.

Teren stepped forward.

"Thank you."

Nothing else.

No extra.

Iven stayed beside the bowl while people dispersed.

That mattered.

Copper came after the applause.

Not much.

Then more.

A woman dropped something folded.

A child put in a red berry.

Iven removed the berry.

The child looked offended.

"Money," Iven said.

"It's good."

"Then eat it."

The child took it back and ate it.

We packed before counting.

That was also different now.

Pell untied the board.

I held the rope.

Nessa took the sandbags.

The cheese woman reclaimed her corner before we had fully left it.

"Done?"

"Done," I said.

"Good."

"Thank you."

She handed me a thin slice of cheese.

I stared at it.

"For moving."

I ate it immediately.

At the rear of the arcade, Rinna counted the bowl into her palm.

Teren watched.

I did not.

Marek did.

"Well?"

Rinna kept counting.

"Well?"

"Twenty-six."

Marek's face changed.

"Good?"

"Twenty-six copper," she said.

"I heard the number."

Teren said, "Good enough."

"For?"

"Find Bellan."

Rinna closed her hand over the money.

"Second slot?"

"If it exists."

"Another night?"

"Not yet."

Good.

We found Bellan at the west platform arguing with a man carrying three
drums.

Not the musicians we knew.

Different drums.

Different problem.

Teren waited until Bellan stopped speaking.

"North arcade."

Bellan looked at him.

"Yes."

"Twenty-six."

"Good."

"Cider yard?"

Bellan shook his head.

"Puppets extended."

Marek said, "Sabotage."

Bellan looked at him.

"No."

Teren said, "Then?"

Bellan unfolded a schedule.

"South yard after judging."

"You said mess."

"It is."

"When?"

"Third bell."

"How long?"

"Fifteen."

"Collection?"

"Yes."

"Better traffic?"

"Different."

"Guarantee?"

Bellan looked at him.

Teren said, "I know."

Bellan pointed with the schedule.

"South yard. Fifteen. Don't bring anything you can't clean."

Rinna said, "Today?"

"Today."

Teren looked at her.

She looked back.

"Twenty-six pays part of being here."

"Part?"

"Oats."

"Everything is oats."

"Yes."

Teren turned to us.

"South yard."

Marek smiled.

"We earned mud."

Bellan was already talking to the drummer.

The south yard was worse than mud.

It had been livestock.

The livestock had left.

Evidence remained.

Nessa stood at the edge and said, "No red."

Nobody argued.

The space itself was better.

Open sky.

Temporary rails.

A broad lane between livestock pens and cider sellers.

People had finished judging and were now doing the part of a fair where
they spent money because they were tired of evaluating animals.

Teren cut the bill.

No widow.

No bench.

King and Sword.

Fifteen minutes.

Pell put the collection bowl on an overturned crate where a fair worker
told him it would not be kicked.

The fair worker was right.

Marek was louder this time.

The space needed it.

The first joke died.

The second worked.

A man carrying two cups of cider stopped.

Then three women.

Then a group of boys.

The dog act was performing farther down the lane. I could see the dog
standing upright above shoulders.

They had a crowd too.

Nobody had stolen ours.

The Sword board went on packed dirt instead of stone.

Davin had wedged the rear support before leaving us.

"Don't lean left," Pell told me.

"Why?"

"Ground."

"Helpful."

"You wanted to know."

I did not lean left.

Halfway through, a woman in a green apron shouted, "I saw this
yesterday!"

Marek said, "Impossible."

"At Kest!"

That got my attention.

She pointed at me.

"He was cheaper there!"

I said, "Travel costs."

The crowd laughed.

Marek turned toward me.

"Do not discuss pricing."

"You started a kingdom."

"I didn't start economics."

The woman put a copper in the bowl.

Apparently pricing worked once.

I did not repeat it.

The fifteen minutes went fast.

Not easy.

Fast.

The crowd was larger than north arcade because the lane was broader and
the cider was helping people remain where they were.

It was also worse at listening.

A group talked through Serra.

A man walked between Marek and me carrying a wooden rake.

Two children tried to touch the board.

Pell stopped one.

The other touched my boot.

"Real?"

"Boot?"

"Leg."

"No."

The child looked alarmed.

"Boot is."

His father dragged him away laughing.

At the end, applause mixed with somebody shouting for cider.

Teren thanked them.

Again, no extra.

We packed.

Again, Iven stayed with the bowl.

Again, people paid while leaving.

This time Rinna counted thirty-one copper.

Marek said, "Fifty-seven."

Rinna looked at him.

"I can add."

"Just helping."

"Don't."

Teren said, "Another night?"

Rinna looked toward the wagon field.

"Four for wagons. Horses. Oats. Food."

"Tomorrow crowd?"

"Bigger."

"Work?"

"Not yet."

"Fifty-seven today."

"Gross."

"Yes."

She looked at the copper again.

"One more night."

That was the decision.

Not triumph.

Not disaster.

One more night.

Teren went to find Bellan.

I did not follow.

I went to courier row.

It was not romantic.

It was six tables under a long canvas roof, three clerks, two locked
chests, a wall of destination boards, and a line of people holding
folded paper.

I bought paper, ink use, and carriage west for one copper.

The clerk asked, "Destination?"

"Carrow."

"Name?"

"Lyssa."

"Family?"

I paused.

"No."

He looked at me.

"Where in Carrow?"

"East Market Hall can hold reply."

"That's reply. Where does this go?"

Right.

I gave Lyssa's usual place as carefully as I could.

Then I wrote.

Lyssa,

Still east.

That seemed inadequate.

I crossed it out.

Lyssa,

We reached Dast. It is a harvest fair and everything costs more because
everyone arrived at once. I have now performed beside cheese, livestock,
cider, and a loading slope. The road took longer than I expected.

That was true.

I added:

I am all right. The company is working. I bought two apples at a bridge
and did not share either with Marek.

That seemed more useful.

I stared at the page.

Then:

If you write back, have it held for me at East Market Hall. Teren says
letters usually miss moving companies. This seems like information I
should have asked before leaving.

I almost wrote when I was coming back.

I did not know.

So I wrote:

I miss you.

Then:

Greg

I folded it before I could improve it.

The clerk took the copper and the letter.

"When?"

I asked.

"Carrow?"

"Yes."

"Depends on wagon."

"Helpful."

He put it into a sorting box.

Apparently that was the answer.

When I returned to south three, Rinna had paid for another night.

The S3-41 token was still hanging from the wagon rail.

Davin was feeding the horses.

Nessa was cleaning dirt from the mounting cloth with an expression that
suggested she had personally warned the earth.

Marek was telling Pell that fifty-seven copper proved Dast understood
art.

Rinna said, "Fifty-seven copper proves we paid to stay another night."

"Same."

"No."

Serra sat on the wagon step with the red thread still around her finger.

Orin was asleep under the rear wagon.

Teren had not returned from Bellan.

I touched the inside of my coat.

The brass clasp was still there.

The letter was not.

That felt stranger than it should have.

Pell looked up.

"Where'd you go?"

"Courier."

"Home?"

"Letter."

"Oh."

He went back to cleaning the wooden fish.

No one asked what I wrote.

Dast shouted beyond the field.

Tomorrow would have more people.

Maybe more work.

Maybe more expensive oats.

For tonight, we had paid for our place.

That was enough to stay.
