TEXT READER · CHAPTER 169

THE STANDBY

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Teren came back after dark with no job.

He also came back with instructions.

"Second bell. West platform."

Rinna looked up from a heel of bread she was cutting into pieces.

"Pay?"

"No."

"Then that's not instructions."

"Bellan says be there."

"Why?"

"Something might break."

Marek, already chewing, said, "Something always breaks."

Rinna pointed the knife at him.

"Not helping."

Teren sat on the wagon tongue.

"The grain speeches are before second bell. Singers after. There's a
half hour in the middle that Bellan says is full."

"Then why are we there?"

"Because he doesn't believe it."

Rinna stared at him.

"Pay?"

"If he uses us, collection. No space fee."

"So Kest again."

"With speeches."

"That's worse."

"Probably."

She cut the last piece of bread.

"We already paid tonight."

"Yes."

"We eat tomorrow whether he uses us or not."

"Yes."

"Horses too."

"Yes."

Rinna handed him bread.

"Then if he doesn't use us by third bell, we stop waiting."

Teren ate.

"Fine."

That was our booking.

Maybe.

The next morning Dast was louder.

I had thought it was loud before.

That had been ignorance.

Market day had expanded while we slept. South Three filled with wagons
before I finished putting on my boot. A family camped two spaces over
had somehow acquired four geese during the night. The geese objected to
this arrangement continuously.

Breakfast was bread, a little cheese, and something Rinna called dried
apple because calling it leather would have reduced morale.

Marek bought a cup of hot milk from a passing woman.

I watched him pay.

He saw me watching.

"Personal expenditure."

"Investment?"

"Breakfast."

"Growth."

"Shut up."

Progress.

Teren wanted only what we could carry to the west platform.

No Sword board.

That surprised me.

"What am I doing?"

"Waiting."

"For?"

"I don't know."

"Useful."

"Very."

He pointed at a small prop basket.

"Carry that."

Apparently I was qualified.

Davin refused to move either wagon.

"Too many."

He meant wagons in the lanes.

Teren looked once at the traffic and agreed.

Pell took a stool. Iven took the collection bowl. Nessa carried two
costume pieces folded inside clean cloth. Serra had already disappeared.

"Where's Serra?" I asked.

Nessa said, "Coming."

That was all I got.

Orin was sitting on the ground lacing one shoe.

His bruise looked better.

The red thread was still around Serra's finger when she returned five
minutes later with a paper cup and no explanation.

Marek said, "Secret?"

She drank.

"No."

"What is it?"

"Tea."

"I meant where were you."

"I know."

We left.

The west platform was not west enough to be quiet.

It stood near a broad intersection between the tool market and the grain
exhibits, raised perhaps three feet on timber supports with steps on
both sides. A cloth roof covered half of it. Benches faced the front,
but the benches did not contain the audience. People stood behind them,
beside them, between carts, near stalls, and on a low wall across the
lane.

A man with a blue sash stopped Teren before we reached the platform.

"You're early."

"Bellan said second bell."

"Second bell hasn't gone."

"That's why we're early."

The man looked at our basket.

"What are you?"

Marek opened his mouth.

Teren said, "Players."

"Scheduled?"

"Standby."

The man made a face that suggested standby was a species of mold.

"Side."

He pointed to a strip of ground behind a canvas partition.

"Don't block the stair. Don't put anything under the platform. Grain
committee uses the rear table."

Teren nodded.

I put the basket beside the canvas.

The man said, "Not there."

I picked it up.

"Where?"

He pointed six feet farther.

"Why?"

"Procession."

"What procession?"

"Grain."

I moved the basket.

Ten minutes later six men carried a painted sheaf of wheat through the
exact place I had been standing.

I stopped asking.

The grain speeches began late.

There were five speakers.

I knew there were five because the blue-sash man counted them twice
while swearing.

The first speaker spoke about yield.

The second spoke about storage.

The third spoke about roads.

The fourth thanked seventeen people.

The fifth was missing.

Bellan appeared at the rear of the platform with a pear.

Different pear, I assumed.

He looked at Teren.

"Ready?"

"For what?"

"Ten."

"Now?"

"After this one."

The fourth speaker was still thanking people.

"Material?" Teren asked.

"Not my problem."

"Collection?"

"Allowed."

"Ten minutes?"

"Nine if he keeps thanking."

Bellan left.

Teren turned.

"Marek. Iven. Serra."

I said, "Me?"

"No."

Good.

Then, "Greg, bowl."

Less good.

Iven handed it to me.

"What do I do?"

"Don't lose it."

"I've done that."

"You lost sight of it."

"Different."

"Today don't do either."

The fifth speaker arrived while the fourth was still thanking people.

The blue-sash man grabbed him by the elbow.

The fifth speaker protested.

The blue-sash man pointed at Bellan somewhere in the crowd.

The fifth speaker stopped protesting.

Teren pulled Marek, Serra, and Iven together.

"King. Debt. Cut after seal."

Iven said, "Which debt?"

"The short one."

Marek said, "Crown?"

"No."

"King without crown?"

"You're still loud."

"Costume is information."

"Then inform them with your face."

Serra finished her tea.

"How long?"

"Nine."

"Eight," the blue-sash man said.

Teren looked at him.

"Eight."

The fourth speaker said, "And finally."

The blue-sash man whispered something obscene.

Teren pointed at the platform.

"Go."

They went.

No rehearsal.

No entrance music.

No board.

No scenery.

Marek walked onto the west platform while the fourth speaker was still
leaving it.

He looked at the benches.

Then at the fifth speaker waiting beside the stairs.

Then at the grain committee table.

"I understand someone here has been promised a turn."

The fifth speaker said, "Yes."

Marek pointed at him.

"Then you understand my problem."

People laughed.

Not many.

Enough to make the fifth speaker look annoyed.

Serra entered from the opposite stair.

Iven followed.

The King piece had been cut so hard I could see the missing parts.

Apparently the audience could not.

Or did not care.

Marek owed Iven money.

Serra claimed the crown had been pledged already.

There was no crown.

Marek used this as evidence that the pledge had been successfully
collected.

Iven said, "Then pay the remainder."

Marek said, "You see? Greed."

The fifth speaker checked the sky.

I stood at the side of the platform with the bowl.

That turned out to be wrong.

People watched from the front.

The bowl was beside me.

No one could reach it without walking around the platform.

I realized this after perhaps two minutes.

I moved toward the front.

The blue-sash man caught my shoulder.

"Not lane."

"I'm collecting."

"Collect there."

He pointed to the low wall across from the platform.

"That's farther."

"That's not lane."

I went.

The wall was better.

People leaving the benches passed it.

I set the bowl on the stone and stood beside it.

A woman dropped in a copper without looking at me.

Then another.

A man put in nothing and said, "Good."

I considered charging him for the word.

The piece ended at perhaps seven minutes.

Teren did not extend.

Marek bowed.

Serra did not.

Iven looked toward me.

I stayed with the bowl.

The fifth speaker climbed the platform.

People shifted.

Some left.

Some stayed for grain.

Copper came from four people.

Then none.

I took the bowl back.

Rinna was not there to count.

Teren poured it into his hand.

"Eleven."

Marek said, "For seven minutes."

"Eight."

"Efficient."

Teren gave the copper to Iven.

"Rinna."

Iven left.

I said, "Now?"

"Wait."

That was the work.

We waited.

The fifth speaker was better than the fourth.

I did not want him to be.

He spoke for six minutes about a grain blight that had apparently ruined
part of the northern crop two years before and what growers had changed
since.

People listened.

Actually listened.

A farmer near me asked another farmer whether he had tried the same
storage method.

The second farmer said it rotted his barley.

They argued quietly.

Nobody cared that we had just performed.

Good.

The singers arrived before the speech ended.

Three women, two men, matching dark green sashes.

They had instruments.

They had cases.

They had someone whose only apparent job was keeping people away from
the cases.

I moved our prop basket farther aside before anyone told me.

Pell noticed.

He said nothing.

I decided that counted.

The singers took the platform.

They were excellent.

Not interestingly excellent.

Annoyingly excellent.

The first song stopped people from the tool lane.

The second filled the benches.

By the third, the low wall where I had put the bowl was occupied by
people listening.

Their collector walked the edge of the crowd with a shallow wooden tray.

He did not wait until the end.

People paid during the song.

I watched him for perhaps thirty seconds.

Then Teren said, "Greg."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"No."

"Good."

He sent me to find Bellan.

That was worse than waiting.

"Where?"

"Ask."

"Who?"

"Anyone who looks angry."

It worked.

A woman at the tool-market gate said Bellan had gone to east judging.

A man at east judging said Bellan had gone to cider.

A cider seller said Bellan had been there ten minutes ago and left
because a rope walker wanted more space.

I found the rope walker first.

She was standing on the ground arguing with two men about a rope that
was not yet in the air.

Bellan stood behind her eating nothing.

Progress.

"Teren wants to know if we're still waiting."

Bellan looked at me.

"Who?"

"East Market."

"The Carrow company."

"East Market."

"Right."

He unfolded a schedule.

"West singers run to third bell."

"I noticed."

"Comic pair lost south corner."

"Why?"

"Pickle seller."

I waited.

"Why?"

"Pickle seller paid for it."

"Fair."

"Comic pair goes east yard after horse prizes. Dog act wants west lane.
Puppets have cider at fourth. You're..."

He looked at the schedule.

Apparently we were nowhere.

"Standby."

"Yes."

"Still?"

"Yes."

"For what?"

"Something breaks."

"Nothing broke."

"Day's young."

He started walking.

I followed.

"Rinna says third bell."

"Who's Rinna?"

"Money."

"Good person to know."

"Very."

He stopped beside a post.

"Tell Teren east yard. After horse prizes. Twelve minutes if comic pair
doesn't take it."

"I thought comic pair was taking it."

"They might not."

"Why?"

"One's sick."

"Which?"

Bellan looked at me.

"I don't know their names."

Fair.

"Pay?"

"Collection."

"Anything else?"

"No."

"Third bell?"

"Before."

"How before?"

"When horses finish."

"When is that?"

"When horses finish."

I hated fairs.

I went back.

Teren listened.

Rinna had returned with Iven.

"Eleven," she said before I could speak.

"East yard," I said. "After horse prizes. Twelve minutes if the comic
pair doesn't take it. One is sick."

"Which?" Marek asked.

"I don't know their names."

"Amateur."

"Collection only," I said.

Rinna looked at Teren.

"Third bell."

Teren nodded.

"If horses finish."

"When?"

"When horses finish."

Rinna closed her eyes.

"Of course."

We moved to east yard.

Not all of us.

Davin stayed with the wagons.

Nessa went back to camp because she refused to carry costume around Dast
for a maybe.

Orin disappeared again after telling Teren he would be at the north food
row.

Teren did not stop him.

Serra left with Nessa, then returned twenty minutes later without the
red thread around her finger.

I noticed.

I did not ask.

The horse prizes took forever.

The horses themselves were beautiful.

I knew this because everyone kept saying so.

Mostly I saw backs, tails, handlers, polished tack, and men standing in
places I wanted to stand.

The east yard had a low judging rail and a patch of open ground beyond
it. Bellan's assistant, not Bellan, told us where to wait.

"Behind the blue rope."

I looked.

There were three blue ropes.

"Which?"

She pointed.

"That one."

"Helpful."

"Don't cross it."

"What happens?"

"Horses."

I stayed behind it.

The comic pair arrived.

Both of them.

Neither looked sick.

Teren looked at them.

They looked at Teren.

One said, "You standby?"

"Yes."

"So are we."

"Thought one of you was sick."

The shorter one pointed at the taller.

"He was."

"I recovered."

"Miracle."

The taller one looked at me.

"Sword?"

"Sometimes."

"Today?"

"I don't know."

"Good fair."

Apparently.

Horse prizes ended.

The crowd did not.

Handlers led animals out through one side. Buyers moved in. Families
pressed toward the rail.

Bellan's assistant spoke to the comic pair.

Then Teren.

Then back to the comic pair.

I could not hear.

Finally she pointed at the platform.

The comic pair went.

Marek said, "We've been betrayed."

Teren said, "No."

"We were promised maybe."

"Exactly."

The comic pair performed the dead goose.

I had seen part of it before.

It was better here.

They used the judging rail as a property line. One claimed the goose had
died on the other's land. The other argued the goose had crossed after
death. A horse handler passing behind them shouted, "Check the feet!"

One of the comics looked at him.

"Expert testimony."

The crowd laughed.

They had ten minutes.

They used ten.

Their collector took a hat through the crowd.

They finished.

The assistant came to Teren.

"Six."

"Minutes?"

"Yes."

Teren looked at us.

"Marek, Greg."

I said, "What?"

"Shopkeeper."

"No Iven?"

"You're collecting from him."

"He's not here."

"You are now collecting from Marek."

Marek said, "I object."

"Good. Use it."

"Six?"

"Five by the time you climb."

There was no climbing for me.

The east yard was level.

For once.

Teren pointed.

"Go."

We went.

Marek entered first.

"I have no money."

I followed.

"Then this will be brief."

That stopped some people who had been turning away after the goose.

Marek put both hands into his empty pockets.

"See?"

"Those are pockets."

"They contain my finances."

"Then your finances owe me money."

A woman laughed.

Marek said, "I am being persecuted for liquidity."

I almost said employment was not evidence.

I felt the line arrive.

I let it die.

"Pay me."

"With what?"

"Those."

I pointed at his boots.

He looked down.

"These are rented."

"Then I know where to send the bill."

A man near the rail said, "Take his coat!"

Marek grabbed his coat closed.

"No."

I said, "Finally, an asset."

The man put a copper into the comic pair's hat.

Their collector was still moving through the edge of the crowd.

I saw it.

So did Marek.

He pointed.

"Wrong creditors."

The comic collector looked at us.

Then at his hat.

Then at our bowl, which Pell had put on the rail.

He took one copper from his hat and dropped it into ours.

The crowd laughed.

I bowed to him.

"Professional courtesy."

He bowed back.

That was the biggest laugh of the piece.

It was not ours.

That was fine.

Teren called, "Done."

We stopped.

Five minutes.

Maybe five and a half.

No ending.

No grand applause.

Marek said, "I remain solvent."

I said, "Temporarily."

Then we were off.

The assistant put another act into the space almost immediately.

A boy with three hoops.

He was better at hoops than I was at anything involving hoops.

Pell took the bowl.

This time we did not leave it behind.

The collection was nine copper.

Twenty for the day.

Not enough to make waiting feel clever.

Not nothing.

Rinna counted it at camp after third bell.

"Twenty."

Teren nodded.

"Yesterday fifty-seven."

"Yesterday we had two real slots."

"Today we had two pieces."

"Today we spent most of the day waiting for twenty copper."

Marek said, "I also heard grain."

Rinna looked at him.

"Then we've all suffered."

Teren sat on the wagon tongue.

"Leave tomorrow?"

Rinna looked toward the fair.

"Yes."

That came quickly.

I expected an argument.

There wasn't one.

Teren nodded.

"Morning."

"Before traffic."

Davin, under the wagon, said, "Early."

"How early?"

"Before."

Useful.

Rinna separated copper into small piles.

"Performers."

I watched.

She gave Marek two copper.

Serra two.

Iven one.

Orin one.

Then me two.

I looked at it.

"Dast?"

"Both days."

"That's all?"

She looked at me.

I put the copper away.

"Good talk."

Pell got copper too. I did not count how much.

Nessa got some.

The piles were not equal.

Good.

Marek held his two new copper beside whatever remained of his old money.

"Capital."

Orin, lying under the rear wagon again, said, "No."

"You were asleep."

"No."

That evening Dast kept going without us.

We were leaving in the morning.

The fair did not care.

The singers worked somewhere beyond the wagon field. A bell rang.
Someone shouted about pies. The geese two spaces over had become
quieter, which worried me more than the noise had.

Teren had one new piece of paper.

Not from Bellan.

A trader from the north food row had given it to Orin, who had given it
to Teren without explanation.

I caught only part of the conversation.

"Where?"

"North road."

"How far?"

"Day and a half."

"What is it?"

"Founder's supper."

"Town?"

"Rellin."

Rinna said, "Guaranteed?"

Teren looked at the paper.

"Three silver company if they still need us."

That got my attention.

Marek's too.

"If?" Rinna asked.

"Message is two days old."

"When?"

"Three nights."

"Can we make it?"

Davin said, "If road."

Everyone looked at him.

He shrugged.

"Road."

Teren folded the paper.

"Who sent it?"

Orin said, "Man I know."

Nobody asked which man.

Rinna said, "Rellin is north?"

"Northwest."

"Carrow?"

"West."

I knew what she meant.

Rellin did not take us home.

It did not take us wildly farther away either.

It was another direction.

Another maybe.

This one had silver attached.

If it still existed.

Teren looked at me.

Not because he needed my opinion.

"Pack light tonight."

"Why me?"

"Because you spread your things."

"I have one bag."

"You spread it."

Fair.

I put my two Dast copper with the rest.

My wages had become difficult to remember without counting.

Five here.

Five there.

One.

Two.

Eight.

Two.

Now two more.

I could count it if I wanted.

I did not.

The clasp was still wrapped inside my coat.

Lyssa's letter was somewhere west.

Tomorrow we would leave Dast before the traffic closed around us.

Northwest, if the road allowed.

Three silver, if the supper still needed players.

Maybe.

I had become very good at maybe.

That seemed like a job.
