TEXT READER · CHAPTER 171

THE SUPPER PLAYER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Rinna asked the question I had not.

"How much is the yard?"

I stopped.

She was standing beside the second wagon with one hand inside the food
sack and the other holding an apple that had gone soft on one side.

"I don't know."

"Food before or after?"

"After."

"Horses?"

"No."

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Pay?"

"One silver two. Half before. Half after."

Marek looked up from tying his shoe.

"One silver two?"

"It was three."

"I remember three."

"So does Harel."

"Does he remember where he put it?"

"Musicians."

Marek sat back.

"I dislike music."

Serra, beside the wagon, said, "You sing."

"I perform melody."

"You shout on pitch sometimes."

"Exactly."

Rinna ignored them.

"When?"

"After second course. Before speeches."

"What time is second course?"

"I don't know."

She looked at me.

"I carried the terms."

"You carried some terms."

"Apparently."

Davin came around the front wagon.

"Horses?"

"Not included."

He looked at Rinna.

She nodded.

He said, "Need oats."

"How much?"

"Tonight."

That was not a number.

It was enough for her.

Rinna bit the good side of the apple and pointed at the cooper's shed.

"Yard is two copper until morning. Water included. No hay. No sleeping
in the shed."

"Why?"

"Cooper."

That also explained nothing.

It did not need to.

Teren arrived before we finished deciding whether one silver two was
good.

He had Harel's half payment.

Rinna took it, checked it, and put it away.

Marek watched the silver disappear into her purse.

"There it goes."

"It was never yours," Rinna said.

"It could have been emotionally."

"It wasn't."

Teren looked around.

"Founder Hall in an hour. Nessa, Pell, Serra, Marek, Greg, Orin, Iven."

"What are we doing?" Marek asked.

"Deciding."

"You already decided."

"Then we're confirming."

That was apparently different.

We walked back into the old center carrying almost nothing.

Pell had the small prop basket. Iven carried two folded costume pieces.
Nessa carried a third over her arm and would not let anybody else touch
it. I had my crutches and no assigned object.

This made me suspicious.

Founder Hall was worse with people in it.

Not guests.

Workers.

The clear strip at the end of the hall had disappeared beneath stacked
serving boards. Three long baskets of bread sat against the wall.
Someone had found the green tablecloths, which meant somebody else was
now arguing about why there were only eleven.

Harel stood near the fireplace.

Teren said, "Space."

Harel pointed to the far end.

A woman carrying a tray said, "No."

Harel looked at her.

"We need the end."

"So do we."

"For twenty minutes."

"After second course?"

"Yes."

"Then after second course you can have it."

Teren looked at the stacked boards.

"Where do those go?"

"Kitchen."

"When?"

"When they're empty."

Harel said, "There."

The woman said, "After."

Teren nodded.

"After."

Harel seemed annoyed that this had been solved without him.

We went to look.

I had thought the side door would work for the Sword.

It was broad enough. The floor outside was level. There was a short
passage to the yard.

"That," I said.

The tray woman had followed us.

"No."

"Why?"

"Plates."

The passage was empty.

I looked at it.

She pointed through the door.

"Plates."

"I don't see any."

"You will."

Teren said, "Other door?"

"Coal."

"Gallery?"

"Food."

"Front?"

"Guests."

I said, "Fireplace?"

Everybody looked at the fireplace.

It was large enough to roast something I would have preferred not to
meet alive.

Nessa said, "No."

"I wasn't suggesting inside it."

"Heat."

"Yes."

Pell touched the wall near it.

Nessa slapped his hand away.

"Hot."

"I know now."

The woman with the tray pointed to a narrow space beside the second
table.

"After plates, you can come through there."

"With the board?" Teren asked.

"What board?"

That took us back to the wagons.

Not physically.

Argumentatively.

Teren had wanted Sword.

Nessa did not want the mounting cloth near food.

Pell said the board could come through the front if guests were not
seated yet.

Harel said guests would be seated.

I said I could mount outside and come in through the side door.

The tray woman said, "Plates."

Teren looked at me.

I said, "I know."

"No Sword."

That was it.

Marek said, "You traveled a day and a half to be unemployed."

"I'm still here."

"Decoratively."

Teren looked at the hall again.

"King. Shopkeeper. Widow."

"Twenty minutes?" Serra asked.

"Less if speeches start."

"Whose speeches?"

Harel said, "Founding addresses."

Marek brightened.

"How long?"

Harel's face changed.

"Scheduled?"

"Yes."

"How long?"

"Forty minutes."

Marek smiled.

Harel said, "No."

"I said nothing."

"You looked."

Greg had apparently not invented leaning.

The musicians arrived while we were still in the hall.

There were four of them. Two women, two men. Fiddle, narrow drum, a
little reed instrument, and something with strings that sat across one
woman's lap.

They knew where they were going.

That was the first thing I noticed.

The second was that Harel stopped looking irritated for almost ten
seconds when he saw them.

"Gallery until doors," he told them. "Then front corner. First course,
low. Between courses, whatever keeps them from talking too loudly."

The fiddler nodded.

"Speech?"

"Stop."

"After?"

"Depends."

That was their negotiation.

One of the women looked at us.

"Players?"

Marek bowed.

"Melody."

She looked at him.

"No."

Serra laughed.

The musicians went upstairs.

A few minutes later, the lap instrument sounded.

The note was too clean.

Not loud. Clean.

It carried through the hall and seemed to settle under the rafters
before it faded.

I looked up.

Pell did too.

"Wire?" he asked.

Nessa said, "Charm."

The musician plucked another note.

This one seemed to come from farther down the hall than she was.

Not much farther.

Enough.

I looked at Teren.

He was talking to Harel.

Nobody else had stopped.

The third note spread differently again, soft near us, clearer toward
the doors.

The musician adjusted a small silver clip on the instrument's frame.

Then she played normally.

Fantasy returned to the room as if it had never left.

Not a miracle.

Not a secret.

A working woman adjusting how her instrument carried in a bad hall.

I wanted to ask.

I did not.

Pell whispered, "Sound veil."

Nessa said, "No. Throw."

"Same family."

"No."

"Close."

"No."

That was all I got.

Teren clapped once.

"Back."

We went to the wagons.

I thought about the silver clip for most of the walk.

Then Davin made me move because I was standing where he needed to carry
oats.

The rest of the afternoon belonged to people who had jobs.

Nessa and Pell chose clothes.

Serra disappeared for nearly an hour and came back with two apples and
no explanation.

Orin found a barber.

I knew because his beard was shorter when he returned.

Marek went into town with Iven and came back carrying a small paper
twist of roasted nuts.

He offered me one.

I took two.

"Thief."

"Travel costs."

"That joke is old."

"So are your nuts."

He took the bag away.

I washed.

Changed my shirt.

Checked the clasp.

Put my coat back on.

At Founder Hall, guests were arriving.

Founding Week had apparently given everyone permission to wear green.

Not everyone did.

Enough did that the room looked as though somebody had scattered leaves
among the tables.

Small potted trees stood near the walls. Apple branches had been cut and
tied above the fireplace. Three old wooden barrels sat beside the front
table, polished enough that nobody was going to drink from them.

A man near the door was explaining to a child that one barrel had
belonged to the first pressing house.

The child asked if it still had cider.

The man said no.

The child lost interest.

I understood the child.

We were put in a narrow room off the passage behind the hall.

It had once been storage and had not entirely changed its mind.

There were shelves.

Two broken chairs.

A sack of onions.

Nessa moved the onions.

A kitchen boy moved them back.

Nessa moved our clothes instead.

"His room," she said.

"Onions?" Pell asked.

"Apparently."

The first course began.

We could hear it.

Plates.

The tray woman had been right.

The side passage became a river of people carrying food.

Bowls went in.

Empty bowls came out.

Bread went in.

A broken cup came out in pieces.

One server stood in the passage for three seconds swearing at a stain on
her sleeve, then disappeared again.

The musicians played softly from the front corner.

The sound reached us oddly.

Sometimes clear.

Sometimes covered by plates.

Once the fiddle seemed to come from the passage behind me.

I turned.

Nothing.

Pell saw.

"Throw."

Nessa said, "Eat your onion."

"I don't have an onion."

"Find one."

I asked, "How does it work?"

Nessa was pinning Marek's collar.

"What?"

"The sound."

"Prepared clip."

"I saw that."

"Then you know."

"No."

"Good."

Marek said, "This is why people don't teach you things."

"Nobody taught you your collar."

"Royal instinct."

Nessa pushed his chin up.

"Royal stillness."

He became still.

I looked toward the hall again.

The sound had moved.

Or I thought it had.

That was the interesting part.

I could not tell whether the instrument was making sound somewhere else
or making me judge the source wrong.

Those were different things.

I knew enough now to know that different mattered.

I also knew enough to leave it alone while we were working.

The first course ended.

The musicians played louder.

People talked louder anyway.

The second course arrived in large covered dishes that smelled of pork,
apples, onions, and something sweet.

We had not eaten since morning beyond nuts.

Marek watched a platter pass.

"I can perform hungry."

Serra said, "You've done everything hungry."

"Exactly. Experience."

Teren came in.

"King. Short. Shopkeeper. Widow. Greg, you're Shopkeeper only."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Probably."

He looked at me.

"Not a promise."

"Good."

He left.

Second course took longer than Harel expected.

We knew because Harel came through the passage three times.

The first time he said, "Ten minutes."

The second time he said, "Seven."

The third time he said, "Ready."

Teren asked, "How long?"

"Twenty."

"You found time?"

"I stole it from the third speaker."

From the hall, somebody called, "Harel!"

He closed his eyes.

"Ready."

Then he left.

The serving boards came out.

The clear end of the hall appeared.

Not all at once.

Workers carried boards, baskets, cloths, and one enormous pot through
the side passage.

We stayed against the wall.

I did not need to be told.

Pell did.

The tray woman pointed at him.

"Wall."

He moved.

When the last board cleared, Teren looked into the hall.

"Go."

Marek went.

No crown.

No platform.

No scenery.

He walked into a room of seventy-something people who were still
drinking cider and talking about pork.

"I have been told," he said, "that this town was founded."

A man at the nearest table said, "Allegedly."

Marek stopped.

"Sir."

"King."

"You accept my authority."

"No."

The table laughed.

That helped.

The next table noticed.

Serra entered before Marek could make the man his entire kingdom.

The King piece was short enough that I could hear its shape from the
passage.

Not every word.

Shape.

Marek took the room slowly.

Serra cut him whenever he began enjoying himself too much.

The musicians sat near the wall with their instruments down.

One of them was eating bread.

They watched some.

Talked quietly during some.

Workers.

When King ended, the room applauded.

Not wildly.

Enough.

Shopkeeper was next.

Iven went first.

Then me.

The hall was harder than the market and easier than the arcade.

Nobody left.

That did not mean they listened.

A woman at the second table was still telling a story when I entered.

A man near the fireplace had turned his chair toward somebody behind
him.

Two children were trading pieces of apple under the table.

Iven began the debt.

I answered.

The first exchange landed nowhere.

I could feel myself wanting to push.

Louder.

Sharper.

Make them look.

Then a cup fell.

It hit stone and did not break.

Half the room looked at the cup.

I waited.

The server picked it up.

She looked at me.

"Sorry."

I said, "Add it to his debt."

The room came back.

Iven looked offended.

"I didn't drop it."

"Your account is complicated."

"I wasn't near it."

"That has never protected anyone."

The merchant table laughed first.

I knew it was the merchant table because somebody had said cooperage
prices there during second course.

Or maybe they were coopers.

I did not know.

The rest followed enough.

Iven said, "I refuse responsibility."

"Excellent. Responsibility charges extra."

That worked.

I almost reached for something older.

Jurisdiction.

Employment.

A line that had paid before.

I felt it.

Then let it go.

Iven gave me something new instead.

"What if I leave town?"

I looked at the old wall through the open door.

"I've seen your road."

A few people groaned.

That was better than laughter.

It belonged here.

We finished.

I got out.

No bow that lasted too long.

No waiting for another laugh.

Widow followed.

Serra and Orin did less.

That was why it worked.

The room had been asked to listen twice already.

They made it listen smaller.

Orin's widow debt became a cider press instead of a chair because there
were three ceremonial barrels sitting behind him and apparently he had
eyes.

Serra said, "You sold my husband's press?"

"Only the useful half."

"Which half?"

"The half your husband wasn't using."

"He is dead."

"Exactly."

An older woman near the front laughed so hard she coughed.

Her neighbor hit her back.

Orin waited.

Serra waited.

The woman waved at them to continue.

They did.

Teren appeared at the passage.

Two fingers.

Two minutes.

Orin saw him.

Serra saw him.

They cut something.

I did not know what.

The ending arrived cleanly.

Applause.

Then Harel walked directly into it.

"Thank you. Master Ennet will now speak on the charter stones."

That was the end of us.

No encore.

No second bow.

No one more thing.

A gray-haired man stood.

The room turned away from us.

We cleared the end before he finished his first sentence.

I liked that.

Not emotionally.

Practically.

We had twenty minutes.

Then Rellin had itself back.

Harel did not pay us immediately.

Of course he did not.

He was standing beside the front table whispering to a woman who had
apparently become furious about the order of speakers.

Teren waited.

Rinna had arrived for the meal and the money.

She waited too.

The first speaker talked about stones.

The second talked about the first orchard planted beyond the old wall.

The third had lost time to us and seemed determined to recover it
through density.

Marek whispered, "We stole from this?"

"Be grateful," Serra said.

"I am."

The musicians began packing quietly.

The woman with the lap instrument removed the silver clip.

I watched.

She noticed.

I looked away too late.

"Actor?" she whispered.

"Sometimes."

She held up the clip.

"Throw clasp."

"Pell said throw."

"Pell was right."

Nessa, behind me, said, "Rare."

The musician smiled.

I asked, "It moves the sound?"

"Some."

"Or where people think it comes from?"

Her smile changed.

"Those aren't always different."

That was not an answer.

It was worse.

"How much mana?"

She looked at me more carefully.

"Not much. Prepared work does most of it."

"Prepared by you?"

"No."

Before I could ask who, somebody at the front shushed us.

The third speaker was still speaking.

The musician leaned closer.

"Buy one if you want one."

"Where?"

"Instrument maker."

"Here?"

She nodded toward the square.

"Blue shutters. Ask for Varo."

Then she put the clasp into a padded box and went back to packing.

I did not go to Varo.

Not then.

Harel finally paid.

The second half of one silver two disappeared into Rinna's purse beside
the first.

Teren said, "Done."

Harel said, "On time."

That seemed to be praise.

Then Harel was gone because somebody had put the wrong cider at the
front table.

We ate in the kitchen yard after the speeches began.

Fourteen bowls.

Fourteen pieces of bread.

Pork stew with apples and onions.

No ceremony.

The kitchen workers ate too, standing or sitting on overturned baskets.

The tray woman sat on a step and removed one shoe.

Pell sat beside her.

She looked at him.

"Wall."

He moved six inches away from the passage.

She nodded.

We ate.

Marek finished first.

"One silver two," he said.

Rinna pointed her spoon at him.

"No."

"I was only appreciating."

"Appreciate your stew."

He did.

Davin had come in from the yard for food. He ate fast and left before
most of us finished.

"Horses," he said.

That was his evening.

Nessa talked with one of the kitchen women about soap.

Serra disappeared before dessert, if there was dessert.

Orin stayed and ate two bowls.

Iven was arguing quietly with Pell about whether the wooden fish counted
as company property if Pell had repaired more of it than remained
original.

The world had magic in it.

I had known that.

I had spent weeks thinking about magic until Hessa had made me stop
thinking about it unsupervised.

Then the road had filled up with wages, wheels, rooms, mud, onions, bad
stages, good actors, letters, horses, and people who needed me to move.

A woman had just used a little prepared silver clasp to make music
arrive from the wrong part of a room.

Nobody had gasped.

Nobody had called a Guild warder.

Harel had told her when to stop playing.

That interested me almost more than the clasp.

Almost.

I finished my stew.

Outside, the old center had gone dark except for lamps around the square
and Founder Hall.

We walked back to the wagons.

Rinna had paid the cooper yard through morning.

No rooms.

No loft.

Flat ground, water, wagons, horses.

Good enough.

Teren sat on the wagon tongue.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Marek groaned.

"What?"

"West."

I stopped.

Rinna looked at him.

"Carrow?"

"Eventually."

That word had changed since we left.

Teren continued, "Southwest road gets us back toward it. Two towns
between if we want work. One if we don't."

Rinna said, "Do we?"

"Want work?"

"Yes."

Teren looked at the wagons.

"Ask me tomorrow."

Davin came out of the dark.

"Rain."

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

Marek pointed at me.

"His job."

I said, "I'm very good at maybe."

Nobody laughed because they had heard it before.

Fair.

I looked toward the old wall.

Blue shutters were somewhere beyond it.

Varo.

Throw clasp.

Prepared work.

Not much mana.

I could go in the morning.

I could look.

Hessa had not forbidden looking.

I knew exactly how dangerous that sentence was.

So I did not say it aloud.

For the first time since leaving Carrow, west was part of the route
again.

So was magic.

Neither had arrived dramatically.

They were simply there when I looked.
