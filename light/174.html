TEXT READER · CHAPTER 174

THE RETURNER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

We did not perform in the next town.

Teren tried.

That was different.

By noon we had reached a place called Brant, which had one stone bridge,
two inns, a tannery, a smith, and a square too small to deserve the
word. Teren went into the larger inn. Rinna went into the smaller one.
Marek found a woman selling meat pies.

The rest of us waited beside the wagons.

Ten minutes later Teren came out.

"No."

Rinna came out across the square.

"No."

Marek came back with a pie.

"Yes."

Teren looked at him.

"What?"

"Different negotiation."

We kept moving.

That should not have surprised me.

It did.

For most of the trip, every place had become work if we waited long
enough, asked enough people, accepted enough mud, or stood beside enough
cheese.

Brant did not want us.

A man was repairing the bridge rail with a stone-setting tool. It looked
like a short iron mallet with a pale mark down the handle. He tapped
mortar between two stones, touched the mark with his thumb, and the wet
seam tightened darker around the edge.

He did not want us either.

The road west left town between the tannery and a field full of geese.

Pell said, "We could have done the goose piece."

"We don't have a goose piece," I said.

"We could."

"With what goose?"

He pointed at the field.

"That goose."

One of the geese charged the fence.

I said, "No."

Pell laughed.

"What?"

"Real goose."

"So?"

"Bad actor."

"Good goose."

"Exactly."

Nessa, walking ahead of us, said, "Don't steal livestock."

"I wasn't."

Pell said, "He was casting."

"I was not casting."

"Different casting."

I hated him.

The goose followed us along the fence until the road bent away.

For the next hour I thought about the goose more than I should have.

Not magically.

Practically.

If we wanted a goose onstage, a real goose would be the most truthful
possible goose.

It would also shit where it wanted, leave when it wanted, bite Marek,
and refuse every entrance.

A wooden goose would be false and better.

Unless the point was the sound.

Then maybe you did not need a goose at all.

I stopped there.

Mostly because Davin said, "Rear."

I went to the rear wagon.

He had heard something in the left wheel.

I had not.

He crouched beside it while I watched the road.

A freight wagon approached from behind. I moved our water bucket, then
the small prop basket Pell had left too near the wheel.

Davin spun the wheel a fraction.

"Again," I said.

He looked at me.

"What?"

"The sound."

"I know."

Right.

He was not asking me.

I watched the lane.

The freight wagon passed.

Its driver lifted two fingers.

I did the same.

Davin tightened something I could not see.

"Done."

We walked.

By late afternoon, the road had widened enough for two wagons to pass
without negotiation. Old mile stones appeared at intervals. Most were
worn nearly blank.

One still had enough letters.

CARROW 19.

I stopped.

Marek nearly hit me.

"Again?"

"Nineteen."

"I can count."

"Can you?"

"Fuck off."

Nineteen miles was not two days.

Not really.

Not if the road held.

Davin looked at the stone.

"Tomorrow."

Teren said, "Tomorrow."

That moved through me strangely.

Not excitement exactly.

Something less useful.

I checked my coat.

The brass clasp was there.

The failed throw blank was in my trouser pocket.

I checked that too.

Pell saw.

"Still garbage?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"If it became expensive while you walked, I'd be worried."

We camped that night at a road house six miles past Brant.

Not an inn.

A road house.

The distinction was apparently that an inn had rooms and a road house
had walls you could stand near while paying for everything separately.

The yard cost two copper for both wagons.

Water was free.

Hay was not.

Hot stew was three copper a bowl, which meant none of us bought it
except Marek.

He stood beside the cook fire holding his bowl.

"Personal expenditure."

"You already used that."

"It remains accurate."

Rinna bought bread and a pot of beans for the company.

We ate under a lean-to while rain threatened and failed to happen.

A courier stopped after dark.

His satchel had a waxed flap with a narrow prepared seal stitched into
the leather. When he closed it, the line flashed once and went dull.

I watched.

He noticed.

"No."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"How does it know it's closed?"

"It doesn't."

That was interesting.

"What does it know?"

He stared at me.

"I don't know. I carry letters."

Then he went inside.

That was probably healthier too.

Serra laughed into her bread.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You laughed."

"I did."

"At me."

"Probably."

She ate.

Across the yard, Pell and Nessa were repairing the hem of Marek's King
coat. Orin sat on an overturned bucket talking quietly with the
road-house owner. Davin had disappeared into the stable. Teren and Rinna
were arguing about whether to leave before sunrise or merely at sunrise.

Nobody was discussing my questions.

I liked that.

I took the failed throw blank out.

Not to use it.

There was nothing to use.

The courier's seal had made me think about the marked line.

That was all.

The preparation marks were easier to see near the fire.

One line.

A branch.

A curve.

Stop.

I knew nothing.

That was becoming clearer.

I had spent weeks learning to draw mana under Hessa's supervision. I
could shape what I felt. I could repeat that much. We had tried to make
something outside me answer.

Three times.

Nothing we could establish.

Varo could mark a piece of metal so that, if she finished it correctly,
a tiny cue would make a sound seem to come from the wrong place.

The troupe could make a room feel cold with rain, stone, cloth, a lamp,
and a woman turning her face.

Pell could make a ghost with a tube.

A real goose was worse than a fake goose.

These did not fit together.

That was probably why I kept trying to fit them together.

Marek sat beside me with his stew.

"You look stupid."

"I'm thinking."

"Same."

"How much did that cost?"

"Do not make this about money."

"Three copper?"

He protected the bowl.

"Leave me."

I did.

Later, I checked the clasp again.

That was worse.

The little brass leaves were still wrapped.

I had bought them days ago.

Long enough that the purchase had stopped feeling like a decision and
started feeling like an object I was responsible for delivering.

I imagined giving it to Lyssa.

Then I imagined explaining why I had carried it halfway across the road
network instead of giving it to her immediately like a normal person.

Then I stopped imagining.

The road house had a loft.

We did not have the loft.

We had the yard.

I slept under the wagon.

In the morning, something screamed in the trees.

I woke hard enough to hit my shoulder on wood.

"What the fuck?"

Pell was already awake.

"Fox."

"That was not a fox."

"Fox."

"No."

Davin, somewhere near the horses, said, "Fox."

I lay back down.

It screamed again.

That was terrible.

I had heard foxes before.

Apparently not that fox.

Marek crawled out from a blanket.

"What died?"

"Nothing," Pell said.

"Then why is it making that sound?"

"Fox."

Marek stared toward the trees.

I said, "Good stage sound."

Pell looked at me.

"No."

"Why?"

"Too ugly."

"That's why."

Nessa threw a rolled stocking at him.

"Pack."

We packed.

The fox screamed once more while we were tying the rear cover.

I listened.

Not because I wanted to reproduce it.

Because I had been wrong about what a fox sounded like.

That felt relevant in a way I did not trust.

If somebody made that sound in a dark theatre and told me it was a woman
dying, I would believe them.

If they told me fox first, I would hear fox.

The sound did not change.

I did.

"Greg."

I looked up.

Rinna held out the Halver money.

"One."

"One what?"

"Copper."

"My share?"

"Yes."

"Only one?"

"You didn't eat company stew because there wasn't any."

"That doesn't seem related."

"It isn't."

I took the copper.

She moved on.

Pell got something too.

I did not count.

The road west was cold and clear.

No rain.

No fair traffic.

No market.

Just people moving.

A farmer with empty baskets.

Two women riding small horses.

A cart carrying glazed tiles.

A man walking six sheep and swearing at all of them equally.

At a low bridge, a prepared counter fixed to the toll post clicked once
for our first wagon and once for the second.

The keeper still looked outside and charged Rinna by hand.

I pointed at the counter.

"What does that do?"

"Counts."

"You count."

"Yes."

"Then why?"

"So I know if I counted."

That was the entire magical system.

I loved it.

We crossed.

Around midday the country began changing.

Not dramatically.

Fields became familiar in the way roads could become familiar before I
remembered why.

A ditch with white stones.

A stand of crooked trees.

A hill I had seen from the other direction.

Then a roadside shrine with one chipped corner.

I knew that shrine.

I stopped.

Serra looked back.

"You all right?"

"Yes."

She followed my eyes.

"Been here?"

"Yes."

She nodded.

Nothing else.

Carrow was no longer a direction.

It was around things I recognized.

We stopped for bread near a cluster of houses.

Teren did not ask for work.

That was when I knew we were actually going home.

Marek did ask whether there was hot food.

There was not.

He took it personally.

By midafternoon, the road grew busier.

Carrow traffic.

I knew that too.

Carts coming out with empty crates.

Workers going in.

A Guild wagon with the bronze mark painted on the side.

Two students in gray coats walking too slowly in the road.

A woman selling prepared lamp stones from a shoulder tray called prices
at passing carts.

Nobody cared that I had been gone.

Good.

Then the city appeared.

Not all of it.

First the outer roofs.

Then smoke.

Then the higher buildings.

Then the road widened.

I had expected something.

I did not know what.

Maybe for it to look smaller.

It did not.

Maybe for me to feel different.

I did.

That was less useful.

Teren called from ahead.

"Hall first."

Rinna said, "Wagons first."

Teren looked back.

"Hall after wagons."

Davin said, "Wagons."

The wagons won.

We entered Carrow through afternoon traffic.

A gate worker waved us through after looking at the company papers.

No recognition.

No problem.

A porter shouted at Marek to move.

Marek shouted back that he was moving.

He was not.

Serra disappeared at the first market turn.

Not metaphorically.

She said, "Later," and walked away.

Orin left two streets after that.

Pell asked where.

Nessa said, "Not your business."

Pell said, "I know."

He still watched Orin go.

I did too.

Then I saw East Market Hall at the end of the street.

That hit harder than the gate.

The building was exactly where we had left it.

Which was unreasonable.

I had crossed half the world.

Not actually.

Enough of it.

The hall had stayed still.

Davin took the wagons around back.

We unloaded.

Not everything.

Company things.

Costume chests.

Prop baskets.

The Sword board.

The long iron-cornered box.

Nobody opened it.

Two workers carried it inside with Davin directing them.

"Other side."

They turned.

"Up."

They lifted.

"Wall."

They put it against the wall.

That was all.

I carried the blue-lamp piece.

Nessa saw.

"Careful."

"I know."

"That's when people drop things."

I carried it more carefully.

Inside the hall smelled like dust, old wood, lamp oil, and somebody
else's lunch.

Home was too strong a word.

Familiar was not.

It was both.

Teren was already talking to someone about tomorrow.

Tomorrow.

Of course.

Rinna was counting something.

Pell had found the wooden fish and was making it bite a rope.

Marek had disappeared toward the kitchen.

I stood with my bag.

Nobody told me what to do.

That was rare enough that I waited.

Nessa walked past.

"Go."

"Where?"

She stopped.

"Wherever you've been trying to get back to."

"Oh."

"Bring your bag."

I looked down.

I still had it.

"Right."

Outside, Carrow kept happening.

I knew the streets.

That was almost inconvenient.

I did not need to ask directions.

I could go to Lyssa.

I could go to Hessa.

One of those choices involved a brass clasp.

The other involved a piece of failed magical garbage and a question I
had spent two days making worse.

I chose Lyssa.

Not because I had become wise.

Because I had been gone too long.

I made it three streets before realizing I was still carrying the
blue-lamp piece.

"Fuck."

I turned around.

At the hall, Pell was in the doorway.

He saw me.

He saw the wrapped glass in my hand.

He laughed so hard he had to hold the frame.

"Shut up."

"You went home with the lamp."

"I noticed."

"Did Lyssa want it?"

"I didn't get there."

"You could give it to her."

"No."

"Romantic."

"Fuck you."

Nessa appeared behind him.

She took the glass.

"Anything else?"

I checked.

Bag.

Clasp.

Throw blank.

Crutches.

Me.

"No."

"Go."

I went.

This time I made it farther.

The streets near Lyssa's usual place were busier than I remembered.

Or maybe I had forgotten.

A fruit seller had moved to the opposite corner. A sign over a tailor's
shop had acquired a tiny glamour that made the painted needle flash
silver when you passed beneath it.

I almost stopped.

I did not.

I reached the door.

Then I stood there.

That was stupid.

I had performed for rooms full of people.

I had argued about money with children.

I had been tied to a board.

I had bought educational garbage from a woman who thought I was an
idiot.

I could knock on a door.

I knocked.

Nobody answered.

Of course.

I waited.

Knocked again.

Nothing.

A woman coming down the street looked at me.

"You looking for Lyssa?"

"Yes."

"She's out."

"Where?"

The woman shifted the basket on her hip.

"North side, I think. Said something about cloth."

That could mean anything.

"When?"

"This morning."

"Right."

She looked at my bag.

"You Greg?"

I stared at her.

"Yes."

"She got your letter."

Then she walked past me.

I turned.

"Which one?"

She looked back.

"What?"

"Nothing."

She kept going.

I stood outside Lyssa's empty door.

She had gotten a letter.

At least one.

Maybe both.

I had come home.

She was somewhere else.

That felt correct.

I sat on the step because my arms hurt.

The brass clasp was still in my coat.

The failed throw blank was still in my pocket.

Hessa was somewhere in Carrow too.

Tomorrow, I could make her frown.

Tonight, apparently, I had to find Lyssa.

I smiled.

Then I got up.
