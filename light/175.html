LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 175

THE GIFT-GIVER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The woman with the basket had said north side.

That was not an address.

I knew this because I went north and found six streets, two cloth shops,
a dyer's yard, three women carrying folded linen, and no Lyssa.

The first cloth shop had blue bolts stacked to the ceiling and a
prepared measuring cord that crawled flat across the counter when the
shopkeeper tapped one end. I watched it straighten itself.

"Buying?"

"No."

"Then don't lean on the wool."

I moved.

The second shop knew Lyssa.

"She was here."

"When?"

The woman behind the counter looked at the window.

"Before."

That was useful.

"Did she say where she was going?"

"Yes."

I waited.

The woman folded a length of brown cloth.

"Are you going to ask?"

"I thought I did."

"You asked if she said."

"Where did she go?"

"Marra's."

"Where's Marra?"

She pointed with the cloth.

"Two lanes. Green door. Don't knock on the red one."

"Why?"

"That's Sen's."

I did not know Sen.

Apparently that was reason enough.

Marra's green door stood open.

I heard Lyssa before I saw her.

"No, if you cut it there, it will pull when she lifts her arm."

Another woman said, "She doesn't lift her arm."

"Everybody lifts their arm."

"Not like that."

"Marra."

"Fine."

I stopped in the doorway.

Lyssa stood beside a table covered in gray cloth. She had one end pinned
between two fingers while a shorter woman leaned over it with chalk.
Another woman, older, sat by the window unpicking a seam from a dark
green dress.

None of them looked at me.

I had traveled for weeks.

I had performed in markets, mills, halls, yards, and beside livestock.

I had crossed bridges.

I had slept under wagons.

I had come home.

Lyssa was arguing about a sleeve.

Good.

The shorter woman looked up first.

"Door."

"I'm not blocking it."

"You are."

I moved.

Lyssa turned.

She stopped.

Not for long.

Her face changed in a way I could not separate fast enough.

Then she put the cloth down.

"You."

"Me."

"You're back."

"Apparently."

She crossed the room.

I had time to think I should say something.

Then she put both arms around me.

That solved it.

I held her.

My crutch slipped against the floor.

The shorter woman said, "Careful."

Lyssa caught me before I fell.

"Excellent," she said into my shoulder. "You've been home thirty
seconds."

"I was doing well."

"You smell."

"I've been traveling."

"I know."

She pulled back enough to look at me.

Her hands stayed on my arms.

"You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"Not terrible terrible."

"Better."

"Tired."

"I am."

"Thin."

"I ate."

"Did you?"

"Mostly."

The shorter woman cleared her throat.

Lyssa looked over.

"Marra, this is Greg."

Marra looked at me.

"The road one?"

I looked at Lyssa.

She said, "Apparently."

Marra nodded once.

"Don't bleed on the cloth."

"I'm not bleeding."

"Good."

The older woman by the window said, "Yet."

Lyssa laughed.

I had missed that.

That was inconvenient.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

"Helping Marra fix a dress she promised yesterday."

Marra said, "I did not promise yesterday."

"You said tomorrow."

"Yesterday."

"Exactly."

Marra pointed chalk at her.

"You came here voluntarily."

"I know."

The older woman held up the green dress.

"This sleeve is wrong too."

Lyssa closed her eyes.

I said, "I can go."

"No."

"I can wait."

"No."

"You appear busy."

"I am."

That was not the same as wanting me gone.

I understood that now.

Mostly.

Lyssa pointed at a stool near the wall.

"Sit."

I sat.

For the next twenty minutes, nobody asked about my journey.

Marra cut cloth.

Lyssa pinned a sleeve.

The older woman, whose name was Jessa, complained that whoever had sewn
the green dress originally hated shoulders.

I watched.

A little prepared warming plate sat under a pot near the back wall.
Every few minutes Marra put her chalk down, touched the plate, and held
her fingers over it.

That was all the magic in the room.

The rest was needles.

At one point Lyssa glanced at me.

"You're still here."

"I was told to sit."

"Good."

Marra said, "He learns."

"Slowly."

"I've improved."

Lyssa looked at my bag.

"What happened to that?"

"Nothing."

"That seam."

"Serra fixed it."

"Who is Serra?"

"Actor."

Lyssa's eyebrows moved.

"She fixed your bag."

"After I fixed it."

"Ah."

"Mine was structural."

Lyssa smiled.

"What did she call it?"

I stared at her.

"How do you know she called it something?"

"Because I've met you."

"Criminal."

Marra laughed.

"She called the stitches criminal."

Lyssa nodded.

"Correct."

"I had been on the road."

"Needles work on roads."

"Not well."

"Did you try sewing while the wagon was moving?"

"No."

I had.

Not the bag.

Something else.

I decided that did not count.

The dress eventually became acceptable.

Marra paid Lyssa with a folded piece of pale cloth and what looked like
two copper.

Lyssa put both away without showing me.

Good.

She had not been helping for fun.

Outside, the light had started going gold between the buildings.

Lyssa took my bag before I could object.

"I can carry that."

"I know."

"Give it back."

"No."

"I have crutches."

"Yes."

"That is why I have a bag."

"Greg."

"What?"

"You walked here from wherever you came from."

"East Market Hall."

"Before that."

"Halver."

"Before that."

"Rellin."

She looked at me.

"Dast."

"Keep going."

"Kest."

"Give me the bag."

I let her carry it.

We walked south.

Not toward her place at first.

She turned into a food lane.

"I ate."

"When?"

"Morning."

"That's not what I asked."

"Road house."

"What did you eat?"

"Beans."

"And?"

"Bread."

"And?"

"Beans."

She bought two meat buns.

I paid for mine.

She noticed.

"You have money."

"I work."

"Do you?"

"Professionally."

"At what?"

"Waiting, mostly."

She handed me the bun.

I bit it too soon and burned my mouth.

Lyssa watched.

"Professional."

"Fuck you."

"Missed you too."

I nearly choked.

She smiled and kept walking.

That reminded me.

"The letter."

"Which one?"

I stopped.

She took another bite.

"Which one?"

"I got two."

"Both?"

"Unless you sent more."

"No."

"The first one made you sound sensible."

"I was sensible."

"The second one said you didn't share apples with Marek."

"He didn't deserve them."

"I don't know who Marek is."

"Exactly."

She walked.

I caught up.

"You got the Dast one?"

"Yesterday."

"That was fast."

"It was a letter, Greg. Not a wagon."

"It was on a wagon."

"Probably."

"Did you write back?"

"Yes."

I stared at her.

"Where?"

"East Market Hall."

"When?"

"This morning."

I turned my head toward the direction of the hall.

Lyssa caught my sleeve.

"No."

"What?"

"You are not going to chase your own reply across Carrow."

"I wasn't."

"You looked."

"I looked at a building."

"You can't see it from here."

"I looked in its direction."

"Eat."

I ate.

Her reply was probably sitting at the hall I had left less than an hour
ago.

That was irritating.

Also funny.

I had traveled faster than the answer.

We reached her place.

Inside, nothing had arranged itself around my absence.

A cup sat on the table.

A folded shawl lay over the chair.

There were three onions in a bowl and one had gone soft.

A book was open facedown where it should not have been.

I knew better than to say that.

Lyssa put my bag down.

"Bath."

"Now?"

"Yes."

"I just got here."

"You arrived dirty."

"I can sit dirty."

"You can sit after."

I obeyed.

The water was not hot enough to be luxurious.

It was hot enough.

I washed road dust out of places I had stopped noticing road dust could
be.

When I came back, Lyssa had put bread, cheese, and sliced onion on the
table.

"You bought food."

"I own food."

"Right."

"You've forgotten houses."

"Some."

I sat.

My body noticed the chair was not moving.

That was almost unpleasant.

Lyssa sat across from me.

For a while we ate.

Then she said, "How long were you supposed to be gone?"

"I don't remember."

"You told me a few days."

"That sounds wrong now."

"It sounded wrong then."

"I didn't know."

"I know."

No accusation.

That made it worse for a second.

Then she reached across and stole my cheese.

That fixed it.

"There was a goat," I said.

She chewed.

"Of course."

"It ate mounting cloth."

"What is mounting cloth?"

"Cloth for mounting."

"Helpful."

"For the Sword."

"You were the sword."

"Sometimes."

"You wrote that."

"I did?"

"Not clearly."

"That sounds like me."

"You also performed beside cheese."

"Yes."

"And livestock."

"Yes."

"And cider."

"Yes."

"Was any of this intentional?"

"Mostly."

She laughed.

I told her about the north arcade wind.

Not the whole thing.

Just the cheese woman and the carts.

Then the goose came out.

"A real goose is bad theatre," I said.

Lyssa stopped.

"What?"

"We didn't use one."

"You stole a goose."

"We did not steal a goose."

"You tried."

"No."

"Pell tried?"

"He suggested."

"Who is Pell?"

"Props."

"Person or job?"

"Both."

She looked delighted.

I told her about the fox.

That took less time.

"It sounded like someone being murdered."

"Foxes do."

"I know that now."

"You've never heard one?"

"Apparently not correctly."

She frowned slightly.

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know."

That was true.

I ate bread.

Then I remembered the clasp.

Not because of anything romantic.

Because it dug into my chest when I leaned forward.

"Oh."

Lyssa looked at me.

"What?"

"I bought you something."

Her expression changed immediately.

Not softer.

More suspicious.

"Why?"

"I saw it."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the whole answer."

I reached inside my coat and took out the wrapped little packet.

The wrapping looked worse than when I bought it.

Of course it did.

I put it on the table.

Lyssa looked at it.

"Is it alive?"

"No."

"Magic?"

"No."

"Food?"

"No."

"Then why are you nervous?"

"I'm not."

"You've performed tied to a board."

"Mounted."

"Your letter said tied."

"That was editorial simplification."

She opened the wrapping.

The brass clasp lay in her palm.

Two small leaves.

Nothing expensive.

Nothing magical.

Just brass worked carefully enough that the veins showed when the light
caught them.

Lyssa touched one leaf with her thumb.

I suddenly wanted to explain the price.

That would have been terrible.

So I said nothing.

"You carried this the whole way?"

"I bought it after the first northern show."

"That was before Dast."

"Yes."

"Before the mill?"

"I think so."

"You think?"

"There were many places."

She looked at me.

Then at the clasp.

Then she stood.

For one horrifying second I thought she hated it.

She went to the small mirror.

Her hair was loose enough that the clasp was not useful there.

She fastened it instead at the edge of her shawl.

It worked.

She looked at it.

"Good?"

I said, "Yes."

"That's all?"

"What do you want?"

"I don't know. You looked like you were going to give a speech."

"I was trying not to."

"Good."

She came back and kissed me.

That was better than a speech.

Later, I found out I had fallen asleep sitting up.

I knew this because I woke with my neck bent against the wall and
Lyssa's foot under my thigh.

It was dark.

A prepared street lamp outside made a pale bar across the floor.

Lyssa was reading.

"You slept."

"I noticed."

"You snored."

"I don't."

"You did."

"Road damage."

"Permanent."

I shifted.

Everything hurt.

"Bed?"

She closed the book.

"Eventually."

"Why eventually?"

"Because you're awake now."

That was fair.

I looked at the brass leaves at her shoulder.

She had not taken the clasp off.

I liked that more than I intended to.

"What's tomorrow?" she asked.

"The hall."

"What time?"

"I don't know."

"Useful."

"Teren was talking about tomorrow before we finished unloading."

"Of course he was."

"And Hessa."

Lyssa looked at me.

"Tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Did you practice?"

"No."

"At all?"

"No."

She studied me.

"Good."

"That sounded surprised."

"It was."

"I follow rules."

"You follow rules after arguing with them."

"Still counts."

"What are you taking her?"

I reached for my trousers where I had left them folded badly.

Lyssa said, "No."

"I need the pocket."

"You need sleep."

"There's a thing."

"Tomorrow."

"It's garbage."

"Excellent. Tomorrow."

I lay back down.

"You'll like it."

"I already don't."

"It cost one copper."

"Greg."

"What?"

"Sleep."

I did.

Morning in Carrow was louder than road morning.

Road morning had birds, horses, wagons, people pissing behind things.

Carrow had bells.

Cart wheels.

Vendors.

Somebody below shouting about hot cakes.

A prepared sign across the lane brightened from gray to yellow when the
sun reached it.

Lyssa was already dressed.

The brass clasp was gone from the shawl.

I noticed.

Then I saw it fastened at the side of her hair.

I tried not to look too pleased.

She caught me.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

"Good clasp."

"Go to work."

I ate one hot cake she bought downstairs.

Then another.

She had somewhere to be.

I did not ask if I could come.

That felt important enough not to think about.

At the corner, she kissed me once.

"When are you done?"

"I don't know."

"Find out."

"Yes."

"Are you leaving again?"

"No."

That came out too fast.

Lyssa looked at me.

I corrected.

"Not today."

"Better."

"Probably not tomorrow."

"Greg."

"I don't know."

"I know."

She touched the clasp once, then pointed at me.

"Find out."

"Yes."

She went east.

I went toward East Market Hall.

The reply letter was there.

Rinna had put it under a cup so Pell would not touch it.

I did not read it immediately.

That was growth.

I put it in my bag.

Teren was in the hall with two actors I did not know and a man arguing
about chairs.

Nessa had half the costume chest open.

Pell was on the floor with the wooden fish.

Davin was not there.

Marek was eating.

Of course.

Teren saw me.

"Tonight."

"What?"

"Show."

"What am I?"

"Shopkeeper."

"Only?"

"For now."

"Good."

He looked at me.

"What?"

"You sound disappointed."

"I'm not."

"You are."

"I have another thing."

"What thing?"

"Guild."

He waved me away.

"Be back by four."

That was the entire negotiation.

I went.

Hessa was not waiting for me either.

She had someone else at her table.

A woman with a wrapped forearm sat in my usual chair while Hessa
examined a row of tiny dark marks on the skin.

I stopped in the doorway.

Hessa looked up once.

"You're back."

"Yes."

"Sit outside."

"Hello to you too."

"Outside."

I sat outside.

For twenty-five minutes.

The woman came out with her arm rewrapped and instructions I did not
hear.

Hessa called, "Greg."

I went in.

The room had not changed enough.

That was becoming a theme.

The suspended-thread apparatus was still shelved.

My old notes were not on the table.

Hessa looked at me.

"You look tired."

"Lyssa said terrible."

"She was being kind."

"Good."

"Did you draw?"

"No."

"Shape?"

"No."

"Test?"

"No."

"Anything I prohibited?"

"No."

She watched me for another second.

"Good."

I sat.

Then I took the failed throw blank from my pocket and put it on her
table.

Hessa looked down.

She did not touch it.

Her mouth tightened.

That was the frown.

"What is that?"

"Educational garbage."

Her eyes lifted to mine.

"That is not a category."

"It was one copper."

The frown got worse.

I smiled.

"I have a question."

"I assumed."

"It's about how little something has to be real."

Hessa looked at the metal again.

Then at me.

"No."

"I haven't asked it yet."

"I know."

"That's unfair."

"Ask it."

I leaned forward.

The failed clasp sat between us.

Completely inert.

Completely useless.

For the first time since I had left Carrow, I was exactly where I was
allowed to make the question worse.
