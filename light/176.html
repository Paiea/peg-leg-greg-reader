LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 176

THE QUESTIONER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

"So," I said.

Hessa waited.

There were people who made waiting feel patient.

Hessa made it feel like I had already wasted something.

"The blue lamp makes the light blue."

"Yes."

"You haven't seen it."

"You told me what it does."

"Right. But it doesn't make the room cold."

"I would hope not."

"The room feels cold."

"To whom?"

"The audience."

"Did you ask them?"

"No."

"Then the room felt cold to you."

"It did."

"Good. Continue."

I disliked this already.

"There was rain."

"Inside?"

"Outside."

"Greg."

"The wall was pale. Serra was in the light. The light was blue. It
looked cold."

"So several real things produced an impression."

"Yes."

"That is theatre."

"Some of it."

"What part isn't?"

I pointed at the failed throw blank.

"The magic."

Hessa looked at it.

"The blue lamp uses a prepared piece."

"Yes."

"And this is not the blue lamp."

"No. That's sound."

"This is sound?"

"It was supposed to be."

"Supposed to be is doing a great deal of work."

"It doesn't do anything."

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"Because I can touch it."

She picked it up.

Varo's failed blank looked smaller in Hessa's hand. She turned it toward
the window, then reached for a little magnifying lens from the shelf
behind her. The lens had a brass rim and no magic that I could see. She
held the blank beneath it.

"Who made this?"

"Varo. Rellin. Blue shutters."

"Profession?"

"Instrument maker. Repairs. Prepared fittings."

"Did she tell you what failed?"

"Channel marked. Bias never set."

Hessa turned the blank.

"That is consistent."

"You can tell?"

"I can tell there is a marked preparation line and no completed bias
structure."

"What's a bias structure?"

"The thing you are not learning today."

"I was asking."

"And I answered."

She put the lens down.

"Anything held in it?"

"No."

"Did she say that?"

"She said it was garbage."

"Good maker."

"Educational garbage."

"Less good."

"One copper."

"I remember."

She put the blank on the table.

"Why did you buy it?"

"So I could look at it."

Hessa's face changed slightly.

Not approval.

Less disapproval.

That was probably the highest available grade.

"And what did looking tell you?"

"That I don't know what I'm looking at."

"Excellent."

"Fuck you."

"Also that."

I leaned back.

The room smelled like old paper, clean metal, and the sharp soap Hessa
used on the table. Outside the door, somebody walked past carrying a
rack of six narrow glass tubes. One glowed faintly amber at the bottom.
Nobody screamed.

I said, "The throw clasp doesn't make another sound."

"According to the maker."

"It changes enough about the sound that you put it somewhere else."

"You think."

"Varo thinks."

"Better."

"And the room helps."

"What room?"

"Her shop."

Hessa looked at me.

"You're changing examples."

"They're connected."

"That is what worries me."

I ignored that.

"The cheap one on her door wasn't very good. Pell heard it wrong."

"Pell?"

"Props."

"Person?"

"Both."

"Of course."

"But the door, the walls, where we were standing, all of that made it
work better. The clasp didn't have to do everything."

Hessa folded her hands.

"And?"

"And the blue lamp doesn't have to make cold. A real goose doesn't have
to be onstage to make a goose."

She blinked.

I kept going because stopping would have made it worse.

"A fox sounds wrong until someone tells you fox."

"What did you think it was?"

"Someone dying."

"Reasonable."

"Then Pell said fox and it sounded like a fox."

"The sound changed?"

"No."

"So."

"So what?"

"You tell me."

"The thing didn't change."

"Correct."

"I did."

"Possibly."

"That's annoying."

"Good."

I looked at the blank again.

"What if the magic only has to do one small real thing?"

Hessa did not answer immediately.

That was different.

I leaned forward.

"Not the whole thing. Not make the room cold. Not make another sound.
Not make a goose."

"Please stop making geese."

"I never made a goose."

"Continue."

"What if I don't need to make something big happen? What if I only need
to make one tiny thing happen, and then everything else is..."

I moved my hand.

Hessa waited.

"Theatre," I finished.

"No."

I stared at her.

"That was the good version."

"It was not."

"Why?"

"Because you have changed questions again."

She pointed at the blank.

"That object is prepared structure. Most of whatever useful behavior a
completed version has was designed into it by someone who knows how to
make one."

She pointed toward the window.

"Your blue lamp changes light."

"Yes."

"The audience's interpretation of that light is not a magical effect."

"Yes."

"Your own external work is a third problem."

"Yes."

"Say it without lying."

I frowned.

"I wasn't lying."

"You were making them sound like one thing."

"That's theatre."

"No. That is irritating."

I laughed.

Hessa did not.

I tried again.

"I want to know if I can make a smaller external change than we were
trying before."

There.

Hessa looked at me for several seconds.

"That is a question."

"I had a question before."

"You had weather, a fox, a goose, an instrument fitting, a lamp, and an
opinion."

"The opinion was good."

"The question is better."

She stood.

I looked toward the shelf with the suspended-thread apparatus.

Hessa said, "No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked at it."

"I can look."

"Not at that."

She moved past the shelf and opened a low cabinet.

"What time do you need to leave?"

"Four."

"What happens at four?"

"Teren becomes angry."

"Useful measurement."

"Show tonight."

"Doing what?"

"Shopkeeper."

"Of course."

She took out a shallow wooden box.

Inside were little squares of thin paper, two flat pieces of clear
glass, chalk, a narrow wooden frame, and several small metal washers.

None of it looked magical.

I liked that.

"What is that?"

"Things."

"That is not a category."

"It is in my room."

"Unfair."

"Sit."

She set the wooden frame on the table.

It was about the length of my hand and twice as wide. She laid one piece
of glass across the top, then slid two small folded paper tabs
underneath it on separate chalk marks. The glass did not touch the tabs.
It rested on the frame above them, making a low clear roof.

"Breath shield," I said.

"Yes."

"I knew that."

"You are breathing."

"Professionally."

"Stop."

She put one washer on each corner of the frame.

"Table movement?"

"Partly. Also because you touch things."

"I don't."

She looked at me.

"I touch some things."

"Both tabs are cut from the same sheet. Same size. Same fold. One
target, one control."

She marked a small circle beside the left tab.

"Why not glass all around?"

"Because I have an hour, not a workshop."

"I have more than an hour."

"You have until four."

"I could be late."

"No."

That surprised me.

Hessa looked at me.

"You have work."

"Yes."

"Then go to work at four."

"Three forty-five."

"Three thirty."

"Why?"

"Because you walk."

"Rude."

"Accurate."

She pulled my old chair into place.

"We are not proving your theory."

"I don't have a theory."

"Excellent. Keep it that way."

"What are we proving?"

"Nothing."

"That's inspiring."

"We are asking whether you can produce a small external displacement
under a cleaner condition than your previous attempts."

"That's what I said."

"No."

"It is."

"You said goose."

"Related."

"No."

I smiled.

She did not.

Then the smile went away because she said, "Draw."

The word changed the room.

Not dramatically.

Nothing glowed.

No prepared object sang.

I had simply not heard Hessa tell me to do that in weeks.

I put my hands flat on my thighs.

Breathed.

Not too much.

I knew what reaching felt like now.

I also knew what not reaching felt like.

The first few times with Hessa, I had expected mana to arrive like
something entering a room.

It did not.

It was closer to finding a pressure I had been standing inside the whole
time and learning which part of myself was allowed to answer it.

I drew minimally.

Hessa watched.

"Enough."

I stopped.

The draw was clean.

Not impressive.

Not difficult either.

That bothered me more than difficulty would have.

Weeks on the road, no practice, and my body still knew the shape of the
instruction.

Hessa wrote something.

"Twenty-one?" I asked.

"Yes."

I had not meant to sound pleased.

I did anyway.

"Again?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because we are testing something else."

She pointed at the target tab.

"Shape toward that."

"Move it?"

"Not yet."

"What am I shaping?"

"The smallest directed form you can hold without spilling it."

"That sounds like moving it."

"It is not."

I breathed again.

The mana was already gone from the draw. I drew minimally a second time
only after she told me to, then held it under her instruction and tried
to narrow what I was doing.

Hessa stopped me twice before I had anything.

"Too broad."

Again.

"You're pushing."

"I'm not."

"You are."

"How can I push if nothing is moving?"

"With enthusiasm."

"That's not a magical term."

"It is today."

Third time, I found something narrower.

Not a line.

Not a thread.

I was not going back to thread.

A direction, maybe.

That was too pretty.

A preference.

That was worse.

I held it.

Hessa watched my wrist.

Then my face.

Then the tab.

"Stop."

I released.

"That was good."

I looked at her.

"Actually?"

"Cleaner."

"Seventeen?"

"Yes."

That pleased me more.

Then Hessa moved the entire apparatus away.

"What?"

"Rest."

"I'm fine."

"I know."

"Then why?"

"Because you have started wanting the paper to move."

"That is the test."

"Later."

I hated her.

For six minutes.

During the six minutes, I remembered Lyssa's letter.

I took it from my bag.

Hessa was writing.

"Can I read?"

"Can you do it quietly?"

"No."

"Then no."

I opened it anyway.

Lyssa's letter was shorter than mine.

That seemed rude.

She said she was glad I had reached Dast without being eaten by
anything, which I had never suggested was a possibility. She said Marra
had finally admitted the gray cloth was wrong and they were replacing
it. She said the woman downstairs had started asking whether "the road
actor" was coming back, which explained yesterday.

Then:

You said you miss me. Good. Come back before I get used to having the
whole bed.

Below that:

Also stop being cruel to Marek about apples. I don't know him but I have
decided he deserved one.

I folded the letter.

Hessa said, "Bad news?"

"Terrible."

"Good."

"Lyssa has taken Marek's side."

"I don't know who Marek is."

"Nobody does."

Hessa moved the frame back.

"Ready?"

I put the letter away.

"Yes."

This time she marked the target tab's starting position with a fine
chalk line.

Then she marked the control.

"Your task is not to convince me it moved."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Probably."

"No."

"Fine."

"Your task is to attempt one small directional influence on the marked
target. Nothing else."

"How far?"

"I don't care."

"That's suspicious."

"If it moves at all, we measure. If it does not, we record that."

"What if it almost moves?"

"It does not."

"That's harsh."

"Reality often is."

I settled.

Draw.

Minimal.

Shape.

Small.

The urge to perform the attempt was immediate.

That was new.

Not perform for Hessa.

Perform for the result.

I wanted the tab to twitch at the right moment. I wanted the still
control beside it. I wanted the clean little story.

Target moves.

Control does not.

Greg did magic.

Perfect.

I could see the scene before anything happened.

That made me distrust it.

I stopped.

Hessa said, "Why?"

"I was making it good."

She stared at me.

Then she leaned back.

"Explain."

"I wanted it to move right."

"There is no right."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"No."

She tapped the table once.

"Again."

I drew.

Shaped.

Held the smallest directed form I could manage.

Then tried to let it touch the world.

Nothing happened.

I did not push harder.

That felt like failure.

It also felt correct.

"Stop," Hessa said.

I stopped.

We both looked at the tabs.

Neither had moved.

"External attempt four," she said.

"Nothing."

"Correct."

I exhaled.

The target tab trembled.

I pointed.

"It moved."

"You breathed."

"I know."

"Good."

"I was joking."

"No, you weren't."

"Mostly."

Hessa wrote.

I looked at the paper.

I had expected disappointment to feel worse.

Instead I was annoyed by the tab.

That seemed healthier.

"Again?" I asked.

"No."

"One attempt?"

"One controlled external attempt today."

"We have time."

"We also have a useful failure."

"Those are cheap."

"They are if you waste them."

She took the glass roof off and picked up the target tab.

"You did two things better than before."

"Only two?"

"You kept the shaping smaller."

"Good."

"And you stopped when you noticed yourself constructing the result."

I looked at her.

"That's not magic."

"No."

"Then it doesn't count."

"It counts for me."

That was irritatingly close to praise.

I touched the failed throw blank.

"What about this?"

Hessa slid it toward me.

"Take your garbage."

"You don't want it?"

"I have better garbage."

"Can I bring it next time?"

"If you have a question about prepared structure."

"I do."

"Then write the question before you arrive."

"Why?"

"So I do not have to hear about geese."

"The goose is important."

"Ask your players."

That shut me up.

Mostly.

I put the blank back in its pocket.

"Next time?"

"Two days."

"I have theatre."

"At this exact hour?"

"I don't know."

"Find out."

Everyone was telling me that now.

"What are we doing?"

"Repeating this."

"The paper?"

"Something similar."

"Can we make it smaller?"

Hessa looked at me.

"The effect?"

"Yes."

"It was zero."

"Right."

"Hard to make smaller."

"You know what I mean."

"I do."

She closed the wooden box.

"We can make the question narrower."

That was probably what I meant.

It sounded worse when she said it.

I stood.

"Twenty-one draws. Seventeen shaping."

"Successful supervised minimal draws," Hessa said. "Successful
supervised deliberate shaping attempts."

"Yes."

"Four supervised external-effect attempts."

"With no established external effect."

"Correct."

"I remember."

"Say it anyway."

"No established external magical effect."

"Good."

"No spell."

"Correct."

"No Barrier."

"Correct."

"Independent work still prohibited."

"Very."

"I bought inert garbage instead."

"I am trying to reward that choice."

"You have an unusual face for reward."

"Go to work."

It was three twenty-seven.

I went.

East Market Hall smelled different when I came in through the side door.

Not actually.

Paint.

Old cloth.

Lamp oil.

Dust.

People.

But after Hessa's room, everything felt dishonest.

That was unfair to theatre.

Theatre was honest about being dishonest.

I was still deciding whether that sentence meant anything when Nessa
shoved a folded coat into my chest.

"Put that down."

"What?"

"Your bag."

I had carried it into the costume lane.

I moved it.

"Where?"

"Not there."

I moved it again.

"Greg."

"You're becoming Hessa."

"I don't know who that is."

"Good."

She took the coat back.

The hall was fuller than the touring company had been. Two men were
rigging a painted interior wall I remembered from before we left. A
woman I had seen once in the prop room was sorting cups by size. Another
actor was walking a line under his breath while Pell tried to get him to
lift one foot so he could retrieve a strap.

Marek was not eating.

That concerned me.

Teren found me.

"Shopkeeper. Second."

"Second what?"

"Piece."

"What first?"

"King."

"Who's King?"

"Not you."

"Good."

"You're also carrying the ledger on."

"That's not Shopkeeper."

"It is now."

"Why?"

"Because the person who was carrying it isn't here."

"Who?"

Teren had already turned away.

Home.

I changed.

The Shopkeeper coat fit differently after the road.

Or I did.

I found the ledger.

Then I noticed a small round hole low in the wall behind the side cloth.

I had seen it before.

I crouched.

A leather tube disappeared through it.

Pell appeared beside me.

"Don't."

"I'm looking."

"That's the ghost."

"The whole ghost?"

"The mouth."

I looked at him.

"Where's the other end?"

He pointed vaguely beneath the stage.

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Where Nessa puts it."

"Does Teren know you explain things like this?"

"No."

"Good."

Pell left.

I looked at the hole again.

Nothing magical.

A hole.

A tube.

A voice from somewhere it should not be.

Enough, if you did not know where to look.

"Greg!"

Teren.

I stood too fast and nearly hit my head.

"Here."

"Why are you on the floor?"

"Ghost."

He looked at the hole.

"Later."

That was becoming another category of education.

The show started.

King first.

I waited in the side corridor with the ledger under my arm.

The audience was quieter than Dast.

Quieter than the mill.

Quieter than the private house in a different way.

They knew where the stage was.

They had come to watch it.

Nobody was carrying turnips through the performance.

That should have made everything easier.

I entered too large.

Not physically.

The first line came out pitched for a market edge.

The room gave it back to me.

Too much.

Marek, playing a minor official, looked at me.

Not Greg.

Shopkeeper.

But I knew the look.

Smaller.

I adjusted.

Three lines later I rushed a pause because I expected someone to walk
away.

Nobody did.

The silence stayed.

I had forgotten audiences could wait.

That was embarrassing.

Then the scene settled.

The ledger was real wood and paper.

The debt was not real.

The irritation was real enough.

A woman in the second row leaned toward the man beside her before I had
finished a sentence. He whispered something. She covered a laugh.

I did not know what they thought was happening between us.

I did not need to.

Marek said, "I have already explained the circumstances."

"You explained circumstances," I said. "You have not explained money."

That landed.

Not huge.

Clean.

I let it.

Later, I nearly used the road version of a line that depended on a
constable who was not in this cut.

I felt it arrive.

Wrong room.

Wrong version.

I let it die.

Marek continued.

Good.

At the end, applause.

Not a lot.

Enough.

I came offstage and Teren said, "First entrance was shit."

"I know."

"Second half good."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"No."

He left.

I sat on a prop chest.

Pell was under the stage.

Somebody above me dragged a chair.

The ghost tube shifted beside my boot.

I thought about the paper tab.

Hessa had covered it with glass so my breath could not lie for me.

Here, we put a tube through a wall so a voice could.

That was not the same thing.

I knew that.

It still felt close enough to be annoying.

Rinna passed with a small cash box.

"You're in tomorrow."

"What?"

"Maybe."

"That's not in."

"Then you're learning."

She kept walking.

I took Lyssa's letter from my bag and read the line about the bed again.

Then I folded it and put it away before Pell could crawl out and ask
what I was smiling at.

He crawled out anyway.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That's suspicious."

"Go be props."

"I am props."

"Exactly."

Someone called him.

He went.

I sat there another minute.

Magic had done nothing today.

The show had convinced a room of several things that were not true.

Neither fact made the other simpler.

At four tomorrow, I might be Shopkeeper again.

In two days, Hessa wanted another piece of paper not to move.

For once, both sounded like work.
