LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 177

THE STAGEHAND

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The ghost had a kink in it.

I found that out because Pell said, "Talk."

I put my mouth near the wooden cup.

"What?"

From somewhere behind the painted wall, Pell shouted, "Not you."

"I am talking."

"Other end."

"You told me to hold this end."

"I told you to hold the ghost."

"Which end is the ghost?"

Nessa's voice came from under the stage.

"The end he didn't give you."

Pell looked offended.

"I was getting there."

"You were not."

It was morning.

East Market Hall had already become impossible.

A painted wall leaned against two braces in the middle of the floor.
Someone had hung three shirts from the balcony rail. A woman I did not
know was arguing with one of the lamp men about whether a prepared
warming stone belonged in costumes or properties. He said properties
because it was a stone. She said costumes because it went inside a coat.

Neither asked me.

Teren crossed behind them carrying a stool.

"Ghost in the third," he said.

Then he kept walking.

Pell looked at me.

"You heard him."

"I heard words."

"Tube."

"Why me?"

"You have arms."

"I have one leg."

"The tube doesn't."

That was unfortunately reasonable.

The ghost was twenty feet of narrow leather tube with a wooden
mouthpiece at one end and a flatter wooden opening at the other. The
leather was old enough to have opinions. It wanted to bend where it had
always bent and nowhere else.

Pell had given me the mouthpiece.

Nessa wanted the flat end.

I passed it down.

"Where?"

"Here."

Her hand appeared between the stage boards.

I gave her the end.

"No."

"You said here."

"I meant me."

"That is here."

"Greg."

I lowered it farther.

She took it.

Pell crouched beside me and fed the tube through the low hole I had
noticed yesterday.

"Don't twist it."

"I'm not."

"You are."

"That's the tube."

"Tube doesn't twist itself."

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

We both looked at the tube.

It had twisted itself.

"Fine," Pell said.

We fed more through.

The third piece was something called The Mourning House.

I had never seen it.

Apparently everyone else had.

That was becoming normal.

A woman named Veya played a widow who had inherited a house from an
uncle she hated. Marek played the uncle in the first scene and then
died. This pleased him because it meant he could spend most of the piece
backstage.

The dead uncle spoke twice after death.

That was the ghost.

"Why not just put Pell behind the wall?" I asked.

"Because the wall moves," Nessa said from below.

"Why not put him under the stage?"

"Because the audience can hear under the stage."

"Why can't they hear the tube?"

"They can."

I waited.

Nessa's head appeared through a gap beside the steps.

"They hear the wrong part."

That did not help.

Pell said, "It's obvious."

"Then explain it."

"No."

"Because you can't."

"Because Nessa will yell."

"I can hear you," Nessa said.

Pell whispered, "See?"

We routed the tube behind the painted wall, down through the low hole,
under the stage, and back up beside a dark hanging on the opposite side.

I thought the outlet should go farther.

"If it comes from over there," I said, pointing toward the rear corner,
"then it will sound more impossible."

Nessa looked at the corner.

"No."

"Why?"

"Mud."

"What?"

"Sound."

"That's not mud."

"It will be."

She took the flat outlet from me and fixed it behind the hanging less
than eight feet from where Veya would stand.

"That's too close."

"Good."

"How is close better?"

"Listen."

Pell went behind the wall.

Veya came onto the stage holding a cup.

She was maybe forty. I had seen her yesterday sorting nothing and
assumed she was crew.

She looked at the room.

"Where's my chair?"

Nobody answered.

"Where is my chair?"

A man on the balcony said, "King."

Veya looked up.

"Why?"

"King needed another."

"My dead uncle needs it."

Marek called from backstage, "I can die standing."

"You die in the first scene."

"I can do it."

"You're already dead in the third."

"Then I definitely don't need the chair."

Veya closed her eyes.

This problem had existed before I arrived.

Good.

Someone found another chair.

Pell said through the tube, "Veya."

His voice came from the hanging.

It sounded exactly like Pell speaking through a tube.

I laughed.

Pell emerged from behind the wall.

"What?"

"That was terrible."

"It was a test."

"You sounded like yourself in a bucket."

"It's leather."

"Leather bucket."

Nessa adjusted the outlet.

"Again."

Pell disappeared.

Veya stood beside the chair.

"Veya."

Still Pell.

Less bucket.

Veya turned toward the hanging before he finished saying her name.

Nessa said, "No."

Veya looked at her.

"What?"

"You're early."

"I heard him."

"So did everyone."

"That's why I looked."

"After."

Veya sighed.

Again.

Pell said, "Veya."

Veya waited.

Then turned.

It was better.

Not good.

Better.

Nessa lowered the nearest lamp.

The hall changed.

Only a little.

The hanging became less brown and more black.

She moved Veya's chair half a pace toward center.

Then she told Pell, "Less."

"Less what?"

"You."

"That's all I have."

"Find less."

Pell muttered something.

"Again."

This time the voice was quieter.

"Veya."

Veya did not turn immediately.

She stopped moving.

That was different.

She had been folding a cloth. Her hands stopped with the cloth half
open.

Then her eyes went toward the dark hanging.

Not her head.

Her eyes.

"Uncle?"

Pell did not answer.

Nothing happened.

I looked at the hanging.

There was nothing there.

I knew there was nothing there.

I had put the fucking tube there.

Still, for about half a second, the space beside it had somebody in it.

Then Pell coughed under the stage.

The person disappeared.

Veya said, "Excellent."

Nessa kicked the boards.

Pell said, "Sorry."

I was still looking at the empty space.

Nessa noticed.

She did not smile.

"Tube."

"What?"

"You're standing on it."

I moved my crutch.

I had been.

That afternoon I learned three more things about ghosts.

The first was that leather hated corners.

I routed the tube too tightly behind a brace because it hid better
there. Pell tried the line and sounded like a dying kettle.

Nessa made me pull the whole section loose.

"Visible," I said.

"To who?"

I stepped into the audience.

From the seats, the tube disappeared behind the painted edge.

From backstage, it looked obvious.

I had hidden it from the wrong people.

The second thing was that the outlet could not touch cloth.

It buzzed.

Not magically.

Just badly.

The third was that actors were part of the mechanism.

Veya got tired of waiting for Pell's second line and started
anticipating it.

Every time she did, the ghost got worse.

"You know it's coming," Nessa told her.

"Because we've done it six times."

"They don't."

She pointed at the empty seats.

Veya looked at them.

Then at Nessa.

"Fine."

The seventh time, she let herself not know.

That was better too.

Nobody explained why.

They had work.

By three, the hall had acquired an audience of people who did not pay.

A carpenter sat in the back while waiting for somebody to move the
painted wall.

Two costume women watched from the side.

A boy delivering lamp oil stayed through most of Mourning House until
someone realized he had not delivered the lamp oil.

The prepared warming stone was eventually declared costume property.

I never learned who won.

At some point Rinna found me under the stage with Pell.

"Greg."

"Yes."

"Two copper."

"For what?"

She held them out.

"Yesterday."

I took them.

"Show?"

"Show."

"Company share?"

"Your local rate."

I put them away.

"What about tonight?"

"If you do it."

"Helpful."

She looked at Pell.

"You owe me a copper."

Pell said, "No."

"You broke the latch."

"It was already weak."

"You made it two pieces."

"That's how we found out."

Rinna held out her hand.

Pell stared at it.

I crawled away before the economics became violent.

Outside, Carrow had decided to be warm.

A fruit seller had a shallow cooling plate under cut melon. The plate's
worked edge glimmered once when she set a fresh tray on it, then
stopped. Beside her, a man sold warm beer because apparently not
everyone made good decisions.

I bought bread.

Marek found me.

"Food?"

"You have eyes."

"Share."

"No."

"Lyssa says I deserved the apple."

He stopped.

"Who is Lyssa?"

"Nobody."

"You said Lyssa."

"I did not."

"You did."

I walked away.

He followed.

"Who told Lyssa about the apple?"

"Lyssa."

"That doesn't answer anything."

"Good."

He bought his own bread.

Back inside, Teren had written the evening bill on a board.

SHORT KING

MOURNING HOUSE

SHOPKEEPER

A fourth line had been written and crossed out so hard I could not read
it.

I pointed.

"What was that?"

"No."

"What?"

"Exactly."

"Am I Shopkeeper?"

"Yes."

"Anything else?"

"Ghost."

"I'm not the ghost."

"You're carrying it."

"That's not acting."

Teren looked at me.

"I didn't say it was."

Good.

I carried the tube.

We reset it for the evening.

This time Nessa made me place the outlet.

I put it where she had put it.

She looked.

"Too low."

"It was there."

"No."

"It was."

"The hanging moved."

I looked.

Someone had retied it two fingers higher.

"That's ridiculous."

"Fix it."

I moved the outlet.

"Too far."

I moved it back.

"Stop moving it like you're angry."

"I am angry."

"At leather?"

"Currently."

She crouched, touched the outlet, and rotated it slightly toward the
plaster side wall.

"Why?"

"Because that wall gives some back."

"The room."

"What?"

"Nothing."

Nessa looked at me.

"Varo?"

"Yes."

"She charges too much."

"One silver six."

"For a basic throw?"

"Yes."

"Too much."

"Pell said it was fair."

"Pell buys broken hinges."

"I heard that," Pell said.

He was under the stage.

"Good."

The doors opened.

The audience came in.

Not huge.

East Market Hall was not new to them.

They talked.

Found seats.

Moved seats.

One man objected to a post and then sat behind it anyway.

A child touched the painted wall until her mother stopped her.

The first piece began.

I was crew.

That felt different from waiting.

During King, I moved the ghost tube farther from a costume basket
because someone had put the basket on it.

Then I held a door flat while a hinge pin went back in.

Then I was supposed to be ready at the tube for Mourning House.

I was not the voice.

Pell was.

I only had to make sure nothing crushed it.

That was easy.

Until the lamp-oil boy came back.

He put two cans exactly where the tube crossed behind the wall.

I moved them.

He moved them back.

"No."

He looked at me.

"What?"

"Not there."

"Why?"

"Ghost."

He looked at the floor.

"Where?"

"Exactly."

He picked up the cans.

I liked him.

Mourning House began.

Marek died well.

Too well.

He added a hand twitch after the fall.

Veya stepped on it.

The audience laughed.

He stayed dead.

Professional.

Then the house became quiet.

Not actually quiet.

Someone coughed.

A bench creaked.

A child whispered.

But the piece wanted quiet, so the room began cooperating.

Veya sat.

Folded the cloth.

Stopped.

Pell spoke.

"Veya."

The voice came from the dark side.

I knew where he was.

I knew the tube path.

I knew the outlet.

I had adjusted it.

A woman in the third row turned toward the hanging.

Then the man beside her did.

Veya's hands stopped.

Her eyes moved.

The empty place filled.

Not with a ghost.

With everyone's attention.

That was enough.

"Uncle?"

Pell waited.

The pause held.

Then his second line came from the same place.

I watched the audience instead of the hanging.

That was my mistake.

Nessa hit my shoulder.

"Tube."

The costume basket had shifted again.

I caught it before it settled onto the leather.

Work.

The ghost continued without me.

After Mourning House, I had eight minutes to become Shopkeeper.

I used six.

The coat button was wrong.

Not broken.

Wrong hole.

I fixed it.

Then I heard applause and thought I had more time than I did.

Teren appeared.

"Go."

"What?"

"Go."

I grabbed the ledger.

My entrance was late.

Not terribly.

Enough.

Marek was already onstage waiting.

He covered.

"At last."

I came in.

"You say that like I wanted to see you."

The audience laughed.

That line was not supposed to be there.

Marek looked pleased.

I hated him.

The rest went fine.

Better than yesterday's first half.

Worse than yesterday's second.

I lost one word and replaced it with another that meant almost the same
thing.

Teren noticed.

Nobody else seemed to.

At the debt turn, a man near the aisle said, "Don't lend him anything."

I looked at Marek.

"Even he understands."

That got more than it deserved.

Afterward, Teren said, "Late."

"I know."

"Why?"

"Ghost."

He looked at me.

"Did it work?"

"Yes."

"Then next time be late for a worse reason."

I laughed.

He didn't.

Maybe a little.

Rinna paid me two copper for the night.

Four copper across two local shows.

That felt small after company silver and large after buying bread.

Money was becoming irritating that way.

Pell got paid too.

Then Rinna took one copper back for the latch.

He protested for three minutes.

Nobody helped him.

I helped Nessa coil the ghost.

That was harder than installing it.

Leather remembered every wrong bend.

"Loose," she said.

"I am loose."

"The coil."

"I know."

"You don't."

I loosened it.

She took the first loop, showed me once, and handed it back.

No speech.

I did the rest.

When we finished, the ghost looked like rope.

That was disappointing.

"Where does it live?" I asked.

"Wall chest."

"With magic?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it's a tube."

"Good."

She looked at me.

"What are you doing?"

"Nothing."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"Thinking at props."

"I don't think at props."

"You bought broken magic."

"Pell told you."

"Pell tells everyone everything."

From across the room Pell said, "No I don't."

Nessa ignored him.

I said, "Hessa wants me to write a question."

"Who?"

"Guild."

"The woman?"

"Yes."

"What question?"

"Prepared structure."

Nessa put the ghost into the wall chest.

"Ask someone who makes it."

"I did."

"Then why ask Guild?"

"Different question."

She closed the chest.

"Good."

That was all I got.

Outside, it was properly dark.

The street lamps were lit.

One of the prepared globes near the market had gone green again before
settling yellow. A lamp tender stood beneath it with a long pole and
swore at the fixture.

I walked toward Lyssa's.

She was not there.

The woman downstairs was.

"Road actor."

"Greg."

"Same."

"She's out."

"Where?"

The woman looked at me.

"I don't know."

That answer felt healthy.

"When back?"

"Didn't ask."

Also healthy.

I went upstairs.

Lyssa had told me I could.

That still felt strange.

I ate the end of yesterday's bread.

Then I found paper.

Hessa wanted a question.

I wrote:

HOW MUCH OF THE EFFECT HAS TO BE MINE?

I stared at it.

Wrong.

The blue lamp had gotten into the question again.

I crossed it out.

I wrote:

WHAT IS THE SMALLEST THING I CAN MOVE?

That sounded like a child challenging furniture.

Crossed out.

I wrote:

CAN I PRODUCE A REPEATABLE EXTERNAL CHANGE SMALL ENOUGH TO ISOLATE?

I hated the word isolate.

It sounded like Hessa.

I left it.

Then, underneath:

IF YES, HOW LITTLE OF IT HAS TO CHANGE BEFORE IT COUNTS?

That was worse.

Maybe better.

I did not cross it out.

The door opened downstairs.

Footsteps.

Not Lyssa's.

Someone laughed.

A door closed.

I waited.

Nothing.

I looked at the questions.

Then I remembered Veya's hands stopping around the cloth.

The tube had been real.

Pell's voice had been real.

The dark hanging had been real.

The empty space had contained nothing at all.

I had known that.

I had still looked.

I turned the paper over before I could write something stupid about
ghosts.

Tomorrow I had theatre.

The day after, Hessa had paper.

For now, that was enough.
