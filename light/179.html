TEXT READER · CHAPTER 179

THE TESTER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was gone before I woke.

There was bread on the table, half a pear, and a note under the cup that
had been holding down my question.

PAPER DAY.

Under that:

DON'T BECOME A GOAT.

I ate the pear.

The question went into my coat.

The failed throw blank did not. I had considered bringing it, then
decided I had already shown Hessa the garbage. Carrying the same useless
piece of metal back to her felt less like research and more like needing
somebody to admire a purchase.

The Guild rooms were busy enough that nobody noticed me for several
minutes.

A man I did not know was standing in the front passage with a cracked
white plate held between both hands. A narrow copper line ran through
its center and stopped at the crack.

"It held yesterday," he said.

A clerk behind the desk said, "It is cracked today."

"Yes."

"So it did not hold today."

"I can see that."

"Good."

I liked the Guild.

Hessa's door was open.

She was not waiting for me.

She had three narrow boxes on her table and a stack of papers under one
elbow. One box was sealed with red wax. One had a little brass tag tied
to it. The third was open and contained six black stones arranged in two
rows.

A woman in a gray Guild coat stood beside her.

"No," Hessa said.

The woman tapped the paper.

"It is the same mark."

"It resembles the same mark."

"How many differences do you need?"

"One useful one."

The woman looked toward me.

"Yours?"

"No," Hessa said.

"Good."

She gathered two papers and left.

Hessa watched her go.

Then she looked at me.

"You have the question?"

I took it out.

She held out her hand.

I gave it to her.

She read the first line.

Then the second.

Then the first again.

"Why are there two?"

"The second is part of the first."

"No."

"Related?"

"Yes."

"That's better."

She put the paper flat on the table.

"Can I produce a repeatable external change small enough to isolate,"
she read. "That is a question."

"Good."

"Do not look pleased."

I stopped.

She tapped the second line.

"If yes, how little of it has to change before it counts."

"Also a question."

"A worse one."

"Why?"

"Counts as what?"

"An effect."

"Observable?"

"Yes."

"Repeatable?"

"Yes."

"Caused by you?"

"Yes."

"Magical?"

I hesitated.

"Hopefully."

Hessa looked at me.

"Those are not one threshold."

"Right."

"You keep trying to make one sentence do several jobs."

"I've been acting."

That came out before I meant it to.

Hessa leaned back slightly.

"How long?"

"What?"

"How long were you with the players?"

"Several weeks."

"And now?"

"Still with them."

"I know."

She pushed the paper back toward me.

"You have spent several weeks being rewarded when people reach the
intended conclusion."

"Sometimes."

"Sometimes the intended conclusion is that Marek is dead."

"That happened."

"Was he dead?"

"No."

"Then stop bringing me conclusions."

I almost laughed.

She did not.

"I wasn't."

"You wrote 'counts.'"

"That's not a conclusion."

"It is asking permission for one."

I folded the paper once.

"Sometimes more real makes something worse."

Hessa's eyes narrowed.

I knew immediately that I should have left that at home.

"Worse at what?"

There it was.

"A play."

"Then ask a player."

"No. I mean, if you're making something look right."

"To an audience."

"Yes."

"Then ask a player."

"Fine."

"Good."

She stood.

"Today we are making something boring."

"I expected that."

"Good."

She moved the three boxes to the far shelf.

"Do not touch those."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking."

"I look at things."

"I know."

The apparatus from the last session was not on the table.

Hessa had changed it.

The shallow wooden frame remained, but the two folded paper tabs were
gone. In their place were two narrow paper fins, each folded once at the
bottom so it could stand upright. They sat between paired wooden guides
no wider than my little finger. Each guide had three ink marks along its
edge.

The glass shield was taller.

It covered the whole frame.

Four little wedges held it off the table.

"What happened to the tabs?"

"They were bad."

"I liked them."

"They were bad."

"Why?"

"Too much surface. Uneven folds. One leaned."

"The target?"

"The control."

"Did that matter?"

"It could have."

"So you changed them."

"Yes."

She put a finger on one paper fin.

"These can move only forward or back without hitting the guides."

"How far?"

"If we are lucky, not at all."

"Right."

"Sit."

I sat.

She turned the frame around.

Then again.

"What are you doing?"

"Annoying you."

"Working."

"That too."

She took two small wooden squares from a cup. One had a dot on the
underside.

She mixed them in her hands and placed one beneath each side of the
frame where I could not see.

"Which is target?"

"You tell me."

"I don't know."

"Good."

"How am I supposed to affect it?"

"You aren't yet."

She lowered the glass.

"First, you draw."

That part had become familiar enough to be dangerous.

I put my hand where she indicated.

Feet settled.

Shoulders loose.

No reaching.

No pushing.

No trying to make the room interesting.

I drew.

The mana came cleanly.

Not large.

Never large.

It gathered with the same internal wrongness that had stopped feeling
entirely wrong. I held the smallest useful amount and stopped.

Hessa watched my face, then my hand.

"Good."

Twenty-two.

I did not say it.

She probably had it written somewhere.

"Now shape."

"Toward what?"

"Nothing."

That was irritating.

I shaped anyway.

Deliberately.

The familiar asymmetry appeared. The wrist edge wanted to become more
definite than the other side. I did not chase it. I held the shape as
evenly as I could.

Hessa walked around the table.

"Again."

"I thought that was one."

"It is."

"Then why again?"

"Because I said again."

I adjusted.

She stopped me before I could rebuild the whole thing.

"Better."

Eighteen.

Maybe.

I still did not say it.

Hessa lifted the glass, reached under the frame, and checked the wooden
squares.

Then she turned the entire frame a quarter turn.

She replaced the glass.

"The left fin."

"That's target?"

"Yes."

"Why hide it before?"

"Because you spent the last session watching paper until you nearly
invented a result."

"I didn't invent it."

"You stopped yourself before you did."

"That's different."

"Yes."

I liked that answer less than I expected.

She pointed to the left fin.

"One direction only. Away from you. Do not move it sideways. Do not lift
it. Do not make it tremble. Do not make it interesting."

"How far?"

"One mark would be excellent."

The marks were tiny.

"That's all?"

Hessa looked at me.

"Move it first."

Fair.

I settled again.

The mana was already there.

I shaped toward the paper.

Not the whole paper.

Not a shove.

That was the problem with thinking move. Move was large. Move contained
success.

I tried to make the intention smaller.

Forward.

Barely.

The room was quiet except for somebody dragging a chair in the passage.

The sound stopped.

I waited.

Nothing happened.

The paper stood between the guides.

I kept the shape.

Nothing.

My leg began to ache from how I was sitting.

I adjusted without thinking.

Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped.

"What?"

"You moved."

"Me."

"Yes."

"Not the paper."

"Correct."

"Can we try again?"

"We are still trying."

She inspected both fins without touching them.

Neither had moved.

Then she checked the wedges beneath the glass.

"Again."

I reset.

The second time I did not think about the paper.

That was impossible, obviously.

I thought about the space between its front edge and the first ink mark.

Smaller.

The mana held.

The shaping held.

Forward.

The left fin moved.

I saw it.

It did not jump.

It did not fall.

Its bottom edge slid along the wood with a tiny dry sound.

Less than the width of my smallest fingernail.

But it moved.

I stopped breathing.

Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped everything.

She did not look at me.

She looked at the glass.

Then the right fin.

Then the table.

Then the floor.

"It moved."

Hessa lifted one finger.

I shut up.

She crouched.

Checked the wedges.

Pressed the table with two fingers.

Looked at the paper from the side.

Then she stood.

"Did you touch the table?"

"No."

"Did your knee?"

"No."

"Foot?"

"No."

"I didn't move."

"I know you believe that."

"I know where my foot is."

"Good."

She marked something on the page.

"What did you write?"

"Movement."

"That's it?"

"Yes."

"Target movement?"

"Yes."

"Control?"

"No visible movement."

I felt something open in my chest.

Not mana.

Worse.

Hope.

Hessa saw it.

"Again."

The next attempt did nothing.

Of course.

I held the same shape.

Or I thought I did.

The paper did not move.

The control did not move.

Nothing moved.

"Again," I said.

"No."

"What?"

"Release fully."

"I can do another."

"You can release."

I released.

The mana went.

I hated that.

Hessa waited.

Then she lifted the glass and swapped the fins.

Not the wooden squares.

The fins themselves.

"What are you doing?"

"Seeing if one likes to move."

"Paper doesn't like things."

"Good."

She checked the folds.

One was slightly flatter at the base.

Not much.

Enough that I could see it once she pointed.

"Was that the target?"

"Yes."

My chest closed again.

"So it moved because it was easier."

"I did not say that."

"It could have."

"Yes."

"Then it doesn't count."

Hessa looked at me.

"Counts as what?"

I sighed.

She almost smiled.

Almost.

She turned the frame again.

Then she switched which wooden square sat beneath which side.

I watched.

"Now I know."

"Yes."

"So what's the point?"

"The point is that we are not doing another external attempt."

"What?"

"Not today."

"That was one movement."

"Yes."

"And one failure."

"Yes."

"Which means we need another."

"Which means I need to know whether the apparatus is lying before I ask
you to draw again."

"The apparatus can't lie."

Hessa looked at me.

I thought about painted doors.

"Fine."

"It can mislead."

"Better."

She wrote again.

I leaned.

She covered the page with her hand.

"That's childish."

"Sit back."

I sat back.

"What did you write?"

"Possible directional movement during supervised external attempt. Not
repeated. Apparatus asymmetry identified after attempt."

"Possible."

"Yes."

"It moved."

"Yes."

"Under the glass."

"Yes."

"Control didn't."

"Correct."

"And I was shaping toward it."

"Correct."

"That sounds like magic."

"It sounds like a reason to build a better frame."

I stared at her.

"You hate joy."

"No."

"Evidence?"

"Frequently."

Someone knocked.

Hessa said, "Come."

The woman in the gray coat returned without waiting.

"The mark is on two more."

Hessa stood immediately.

"Where?"

"South storage."

"Both?"

"One crate. Two seals."

"Who opened it?"

"No one."

Hessa looked at me.

"We are done."

"What?"

"You heard her."

"I have theatre later."

"Excellent."

"That's not related."

"I know."

She was already gathering two papers.

"What about next time?"

"Not until I replace the fins."

"When?"

"I will tell you."

"Two days?"

"No."

"Three?"

"Greg."

"Right."

She stopped at the door.

"Do not reproduce this at home."

"I know."

"Do not use different paper."

"I know."

"Do not decide that because one paper moved, you can move paper."

"I wasn't going to."

She waited.

"I was going to a little."

"I know."

The gray-coated woman looked between us.

"Is this the paper man?"

"No," Hessa said.

I said, "Apparently."

Hessa pointed at the door.

I left.

At the front desk, the man with the cracked white plate was gone. The
plate was not. It sat in a shallow tray with a scrap of paper tied to
one corner.

DO NOT USE.

Under that, in different writing:

OBVIOUSLY.

I stopped long enough to read both.

The clerk looked up.

"Yours?"

"No."

"Then keep moving."

I did.

Outside, the day had become offensively normal.

A courier passed with a brass seal at his shoulder that flashed once
when the receiving clerk touched it. A woman was arguing over onions.
Somebody had dropped a basket of laundry in the street.

I had possibly moved paper.

Possibly.

That word was unbearable.

I walked to East Market Hall.

Pell had fixed the latch.

I knew because the door opened.

"Look," he said.

"Congratulations."

"It closes too."

"Magic."

"No."

"Better."

The painted doorway was still there.

The crate was still in front of the seam.

I did not touch it.

Teren had tomorrow's bill half written.

Tonight was mostly rehearsal and a shorter public piece later. I was not
in the public piece.

Good.

Sellen was onstage with The Petition pages.

He saw me.

"Goat."

"Neighbor."

Nessa was not there.

Veya was.

She looked at me.

"You're third again tomorrow."

"Seven lines?"

"Unless you earn fewer."

"Fair."

We ran the entrance.

Veya asked what business justified three grown men shouting in her
office.

First petitioner.

Second petitioner.

Me.

"My neighbor's goat has eaten half my fence and all my patience."

Sellen entered on neighbor.

The reed sounded after fence.

I said nothing else.

Sellen's eyes flicked toward me.

That was all.

The scene continued.

It felt smaller.

It also worked for everyone.

Afterward, Pell asked why I kept staring at my hand.

"I might have moved paper."

He waited.

"With magic."

He waited longer.

"Maybe."

Pell nodded.

"Good."

"That's it?"

"What do you want?"

"I don't know."

"How far?"

I held my fingers close together.

"Less."

He looked at the gap.

"That's bad magic."

"Apparently."

"Still magic."

"Maybe."

He shrugged.

Then he went to get a coil of rope because somebody had called his name.

I stood alone for perhaps five seconds.

Then Veya shoved a stack of pages into my chest.

"Office."

"What?"

"Take those to Teren."

I took them.

Work.

The public piece that night used no ghost, no blue lamp, no painted
room. It was two people, a table, a bad marriage, and a bowl of onions.

I watched from the side.

The audience laughed in the right places.

One woman cried near the end.

Nothing magical happened.

I thought about the paper anyway.

That was probably why Hessa had sent me away.

At home, Lyssa was not there yet.

I put the question paper back on the table.

Under the second line, I wrote:

ONE MOVEMENT.

Then I stared at it.

I added:

NOT REPEATED.

Then:

ONE FIN WAS EASIER TO MOVE.

That looked worse.

More accurate.

Worse.

I left it.

Lyssa came in while I was taking off my boot.

"Paper day?"

"Yes."

"Did anything move?"

I looked at her.

She stopped.

"Something moved."

"Maybe."

"That's annoying."

"Extremely."

She put down a bundle of dark cloth.

"How much?"

I showed her with my fingers.

She leaned closer.

"That's nothing."

"I know."

She smiled.

"You're happy."

"No."

"Greg."

"Possibly."

She laughed and went to wash her hands.

I looked at the three lines again.

ONE MOVEMENT.

NOT REPEATED.

ONE FIN WAS EASIER TO MOVE.

At the theatre, I could have made a room out of less.

Hessa would not even let me make a result.
