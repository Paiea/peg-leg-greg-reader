TEXT READER · CHAPTER 180

THE THIRD MAN

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa had the dark cloth spread across the table when I woke.

It covered breakfast.

"Is there bread under that?"

"No."

"Was there bread under that?"

"Yes."

"What happened to it?"

"I ate it."

I sat up.

Lyssa had pinned one edge of the cloth to itself and was measuring
something with a length of cord. The brass leaves were not in her hair.
They were on the shelf beside the washbasin with two pins through them.

"What is it?"

"Skirt."

"For you?"

"No."

"Marra?"

"No."

"Jessa?"

"No."

She looked at me.

"Am I allowed to know?"

"When I know whether I hate it."

"That seems fair."

She folded the cloth back and uncovered a heel of cheese.

"Breakfast."

"That's not bread."

"You slept."

Apparently that was my fault.

I ate the cheese while she marked the cloth with chalk.

After a while she said, "So paper moved and Hessa says maybe you
didn't."

I stopped chewing.

"That's a hostile summary."

"Is it wrong?"

"The paper definitely moved."

"And maybe you did it."

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"Because yesterday you said maybe seven times."

"I did not."

"You did."

"You counted?"

"No. I got tired."

She folded the cloth again.

"What does Hessa think?"

"That the frame needs fixing."

"So she thinks the frame moved the paper."

"No."

"She thinks you moved it."

"No."

"She thinks something moved it."

"The paper moved."

Lyssa looked at me.

I pointed at her.

"Exactly."

She laughed.

I finished the cheese.

By the time I left, she had the cloth rolled under one arm and was going
south, not north. I did not ask where. She kissed me once at the door,
told me not to become a magician before supper, and walked away before I
could explain that this was precisely the disputed point.

East Market Hall had acquired a sign.

GREG MOVED PAPER.

It was written in charcoal on the wall beside the repaired latch.

I stood there.

Pell came through the door carrying three wooden bowls.

"Morning."

I pointed.

He looked.

"Oh."

"Did you write that?"

"No."

"Who did?"

"Don't know."

"You told someone."

"Yes."

"Who?"

He thought.

"Several people."

"Why?"

"You moved paper."

"Maybe."

"It moved."

"Yes."

"You were doing magic."

"Yes."

"So."

"No."

Pell shifted the bowls into one hand.

"I don't understand what part you don't understand."

"I don't understand what part you understand."

"Paper moved."

"Yes."

"While you were trying to move it."

"Yes."

"With magic."

"Possibly."

He nodded toward the sign.

"That's too long."

Then he went inside.

I rubbed at the charcoal with my thumb.

It smeared.

The words remained readable.

I rubbed harder.

"Leave it."

Rinna was behind me.

I turned.

"Why?"

"Because somebody will write it again and I don't want charcoal
everywhere."

"It's not true."

"Did paper move?"

"Yes."

"Did you move it?"

"Maybe."

"Then it's badly written."

"Thank you."

"Move chairs."

There were twenty-four folding chairs stacked near the side wall.

I moved chairs.

The hall was busier than it had been on the road and less organized than
I remembered. Two actors I had seen before but never traveled with were
arguing over a coat. A woman with paint on both wrists was repainting
the edge of a window flat because somebody had stored it face-down. Veya
was onstage with Sellen and another man, working through the office
scene without us.

Teren was at the front table talking to a baker.

Not about bread.

"Four evenings," the baker said.

"Three."

"Four."

"Three and the afternoon."

"That's four."

"No."

The baker stared at him.

Teren stared back.

I carried chairs around them.

Nobody asked whether I had moved paper.

That lasted six minutes.

A woman I knew only as one of the costume workers caught my sleeve.

"Pell says you finally did magic."

"No."

Her eyebrows rose.

"Paper moved."

"Yes."

"With magic."

"Maybe."

She looked disappointed.

"What did you do before?"

"Nothing."

"That's worse."

She let go and kept walking.

I found Pell backstage.

"You told her I finally did magic."

"I said you moved paper."

"She heard finally did magic."

"That sounds better."

"It isn't better."

"For a story."

I stopped.

Pell was tying a cord around a bundle of wooden spoons.

He did not notice.

Or he did and did not care.

"For a story," I repeated.

"Yes."

"Hessa would hate you."

"Who?"

"Never mind."

Nessa still was not there.

Her absence had become a practical problem rather than a mystery. Two
costume questions were waiting on a bench with nobody answering them. A
torn cuff had a note pinned to it. The painted doorway needed a strip of
cloth along the back edge, and nobody knew where she had put the right
gray.

Veya knew where the gray was.

"Third shelf."

Pell looked.

"Which room?"

Veya closed her eyes.

"Paint."

"Ah."

He left.

The hall survived.

That was useful to know.

Around noon, Teren put me at the front door with Sellen.

"Why?"

"Because you can speak."

"So can he."

"Not to strangers."

Sellen said, "I can."

Teren looked at him.

Sellen said, "I don't want to."

"See?"

We had a board with the evening bill painted on it.

THE PETITION.

THE WIDOW'S ROOM.

A SHORT MUSICAL PIECE I had never seen.

Two copper at the door for the full bill. One for children. The first
twenty people got the better benches, which seemed less like an
advertisement and more like a threat.

A man stopped.

"Is this the one with the ghost?"

"No," I said.

Sellen said, "Tomorrow."

The man looked interested.

"Real ghost?"

"Yes," I said.

Sellen turned his head.

The man said, "Really?"

"Pell."

"What?"

"The ghost is Pell."

The man looked less interested.

Sellen waited until he had walked away.

"You are bad at this."

"I told the truth."

"You told all of it."

"Apparently that's bad."

"At a door? Yes."

A woman with two children stopped.

"Any animals?"

Sellen said, "A goat."

"There is no goat," I said.

"There is a goat."

"There is discussion of a goat."

"That's a goat."

The children wanted to see the goat.

We sold three seats.

I decided not to interfere.

Later, I helped set the table for The Petition.

The papers on Veya's desk were fake.

Mostly.

The top sheet had six actual lines of writing.

The sheets beneath it had marks that became words from a few paces away
and nothing at all up close.

I picked one up.

"Who writes these?"

Sellen said, "Who cares?"

"Somebody."

"Probably Nessa."

"Nessa writes fake petitions?"

"She does everything."

I put the paper down.

One corner had a real wax seal.

Not a prepared seal.

Just wax.

From the house, the seal made the entire stack look official.

I knew the rest was nonsense.

It still looked more official with the seal.

I did not touch it again.

Before the doors opened, we also had to find a missing bench.

Not repair one. Find one.

Rinna's count said twelve. The hall contained eleven.

Pell counted twice and got thirteen the second time.

"How?"

"I counted that one twice."

"Why?"

"It looked useful."

Sellen found the missing bench in the costume room with three baskets of
hats on it. Nobody knew who had put it there. The hats had to go
somewhere else, which meant a shelf had to be cleared, which meant a box
of old hand props came down.

I ended up holding a wooden knife, two false keys, and a loaf of bread
made from painted cloth.

The bread looked terrible in my hand.

From ten paces away, beside a real bowl and a real knife, it looked like
bread.

I squeezed it.

"Don't," Veya said.

"I'm not hurting it."

"You're making it bread-shaped wrong."

"It is already bread-shaped."

"Not if you crush it."

I put it down.

The real knife on the prop table was dull enough to be safe and heavy
enough to make the fake loaf look more convincing.

I hated noticing that.

"Do we ever use real bread?"

"Yes."

"Why not here?"

"Because this loaf has been dinner for six years."

That was a better answer than anything I had.

We found room for the hats. The bench went out. The audience would never
know any of this had happened, which seemed to be the normal condition
of theatre.

Before the doors opened, Pell had amended the charcoal sign.

GREG MAYBE MOVED PAPER.

That was worse.

"Why did you add maybe?"

"You complained."

"I don't know if this is better."

"It's more accurate."

Rinna passed us.

"Move."

We moved.

The first piece went badly for somebody else.

A stool leg cracked during an entrance and the actor sitting on it
managed to remain upright by grabbing the other actor's sleeve. The
audience laughed. The scene was not funny.

Backstage, nobody cared why they had laughed.

The stool went out.

Another came in.

Work.

Then The Petition.

I stood in the wing and listened to the first petitioner complain about
a drainage ditch.

The second had a pig.

Then Veya called for the third.

I entered.

The local audience was quieter than the road crowds. I knew that now. I
did not throw the first line at them.

Veya looked at me as if I were already wasting her afternoon.

"What grievance?"

"My neighbor's goat has eaten half my fence and all my patience."

Sellen entered on neighbor.

The reed sounded after fence.

There was a laugh.

Small.

Smaller than the laugh I had gotten when I called Sellen a goat.

I felt the old line waiting.

And apparently he's followed me.

It would work.

I knew exactly where to put it.

I did not.

Sellen crossed in, offended.

"My father sent me because the goat is innocent."

The audience laughed harder.

Not at me.

Sellen held the pause.

Then he added, perfectly serious, "My father is less certain."

The laugh got bigger.

Veya looked down at her papers so the audience would not see her mouth
move.

I hated him.

The scene worked.

The reed player hit the next cue.

Veya got her business.

I got my next line.

Nobody had to repair anything.

When we came off, Sellen said, "Better."

"Your father line isn't written."

"No."

"You improvised."

"Yes."

"You told me not to."

"I told you not to ruin my entrance."

"That's different?"

"Very."

He walked away.

I considered killing him.

Not seriously.

Mostly.

Teren caught my shoulder before I could leave the wing.

"Good."

"Sellen improvised."

"I heard."

"He got a bigger laugh."

"Yes."

"I hate this job."

"No, you don't."

He was right, which made it worse.

The Widow's Room used the painted doorway.

I was crew for the change.

The crate stayed where it belonged.

I knew that without being told.

The fake stack of petitions went off. A low table came on. Someone
scattered dust across only the audience-facing half of an old coat and
hung it beside the painted doorway.

I looked at the clean back.

"Why only half?"

The woman with paint on her wrists said, "Dust costs time."

That was all.

From the house, the coat looked abandoned.

From backstage, it looked like somebody had gotten tired halfway through
making it dirty.

The scene did not use the ghost tube.

It did not use magic.

An actor stood beside the painted doorway and spoke into the room that
did not exist.

Nobody answered.

She waited.

The audience waited with her.

Then she closed the real doorframe against the painted dark.

It should not have worked.

It did.

After the bill, Rinna paid me one copper.

"Third."

"Thank you."

She was already counting someone else's share.

"Rinna."

"What?"

"Did Pell tell you I did magic?"

"Yes."

"I didn't."

"Good."

"I might have."

"Bad."

"Why?"

"Because if you can do magic, somebody will eventually ask me whether
you can do it in a show."

"I can't."

"Then don't."

"That's what I said."

"Good."

She pushed a chair toward me with her foot.

"Stack."

I stacked.

The charcoal sign lasted through cleanup.

By then it had changed again.

GREG MOVED PAPER WITH MAGIC MAYBE.

I stared at it.

Pell stood beside me.

"That one wasn't me."

"Who writes maybe at the end?"

"Someone careful."

"No."

He grinned.

A young actor passing with two lamp stands read it aloud.

"Oh. You did magic?"

"No."

He kept walking.

"Pell says you did."

"I said maybe."

The actor called back, "Congratulations."

I looked at Pell.

He raised both hands.

"I didn't say congratulations."

Nessa came in during the last half hour.

Nobody announced her.

She had a wrapped parcel under one arm and looked tired.

Veya immediately met her with the torn cuff.

Rinna had two questions.

The paint-wrist woman had three.

Nessa put the parcel down and said, "One at a time."

Nobody asked where she had been.

Neither did I.

She answered the cuff first.

By the time I left, the hall was still working.

Sellen was practicing his father line with Veya.

Pell was trying to convince Rinna the charcoal sign increased morale.

Nessa was cutting gray cloth.

Teren and the baker were still arguing about whether three evenings and
an afternoon were four bookings.

I went home.

Lyssa was there.

The dark cloth was not.

"Did you sell it?"

"No."

"What happened?"

"I took it where it was going."

"Where was it going?"

"West."

I took off my coat.

She was eating an apple.

I looked at it.

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked."

"Marek has damaged me."

"Good."

I put my copper on the table.

"Paid?"

"Third Man."

"Goat?"

"Still innocent."

She bit the apple.

"Did paper move today?"

"No."

"You tried?"

"No."

"Good."

I sat.

She looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"What?"

"Pell told people."

"That paper moved?"

"That I moved paper."

"Did you?"

"Maybe."

She chewed.

"Then he told them the exciting version."

"Yes."

"Do you like the exciting version?"

"No."

She kept looking.

"Maybe."

"Greg."

"I know."

That was the problem.

I liked it.

Not enough to lie.

Enough to understand the temptation.

Hessa had written three ugly lines that made the event smaller every
time they got more accurate.

At the hall, somebody had written six words in charcoal and made me a
magician by moving maybe to the end.

I went to the table.

My question paper was still there.

ONE MOVEMENT.

NOT REPEATED.

ONE FIN WAS EASIER TO MOVE.

I did not add anything.

Lyssa said, "You want supper?"

"Yes."

"I didn't make any."

"Then no."

"We can get something."

I folded the paper and put it away.

We stopped at a street stall two corners from home.

The woman running it had a shallow iron pan over a prepared heat tile.
The tile showed one dull orange line along the rim. She slapped flat
dough onto the pan, turned it, filled it with beans and chopped greens,
and charged us two copper for three.

"Why three?" I asked.

Lyssa said, "Because I know her."

The woman said, "Because the third one tore."

It had.

She folded the torn side inward and handed it to me.

"Still food."

"That's a good business model."

"It is if you stop talking."

We ate standing under the edge of an awning.

Lyssa knew the woman. She also knew a man who stopped to ask whether
Marra still needed the blue thread, and apparently knew that Jessa's
sister had changed her mind about a hem. None of those facts involved
me.

I ate my torn supper and listened.

When the man left, I said, "Blue thread?"

"Tomorrow."

"What happens tomorrow?"

"I carry blue thread."

"Important."

"More useful than moving paper."

"Possibly moving paper."

"Sorry."

She was not sorry.

Outside, somebody shouted at a cart.

A street lamp clicked brighter as the lamplighter touched the prepared
catch at its base.

Lyssa found her shoes.

I found my crutches.

By the time we reached the corner, I had stopped thinking about paper.

Mostly.
