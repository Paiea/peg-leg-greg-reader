LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 182

THE SUBJECT

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Davin had the curtain wheel on the floor.

I stopped in the East Market Hall doorway.

The wheel looked smaller there.

Most things did when somebody took them apart.

Davin sat on an overturned bucket with the wooden wheel between his
knees, working a narrow metal pin out of its center. The curtain cord
hung uselessly from above. A second man I did not know was on a ladder,
scraping old wax from the bracket.

"Today is tomorrow," I said.

Davin looked at me.

"Yes."

"Good."

He went back to the pin.

That concluded our conversation.

Jori was back.

I knew because a thin man with a crooked nose was standing at my low box
with the cue page folded in half.

"Are you Jori?"

"Yes."

"I did your job."

"I heard."

"I was adequate."

He looked at the curtain cord hanging from the ceiling.

"Apparently."

Then he walked away.

I had expected to feel replaced.

Instead I felt relieved.

Rinna caught me before I could steal one of the baker's rolls.

"Third Man tonight."

"My goat?"

"Your goat."

"Tovin said it wrong."

"He says most things wrong."

"Why did you let him have it?"

"You were busy."

That was also apparently the entire theory of casting.

I ate the roll.

Rinna saw me.

She did not stop me.

That worried me more than being caught.

"Guild after midday," I said.

"I know."

"How?"

"You told Teren."

"I did?"

"You tell people things."

I thought of Hessa's note.

DO NOT BRING PELL.

"I should work on that."

"Yes."

She pointed toward a stack of chairs.

I moved chairs until noon.

Lyssa did not appear.

Neither did Pell, which made keeping him away from Hessa considerably
easier.

After midday I went to the Guild.

Hessa was not there.

Her room was open, but a gray-coated man had three wooden boxes on her
table and was comparing seals against a page. I recognized none of the
marks. He did not look at me.

I sat outside.

People went past.

A woman carrying a tray of small prepared glass beads stopped at the
room, saw the boxes, swore quietly, and kept going. Two Guild workers
argued at the end of the corridor about whether something belonged in
south storage or had only been found there. One of them said, "If it was
logged east, it stays east until someone changes the page."

The other said, "The crate is south."

"Then the crate is wrong."

I liked him.

Hessa arrived carrying six paper fins in one hand and looking irritated.

"You're here."

"You told me to be."

"Good."

She looked into her room.

The gray-coated man looked up.

"Five minutes."

"You said that ten minutes ago."

"I wasn't here ten minutes ago."

He considered this.

"Five minutes."

Hessa sat beside me.

I looked at the fins.

They were all the same pale paper.

"New fins."

"Yes."

"Did you make them?"

"No."

"Who did?"

"Someone who folds paper better than you."

"That could be anyone."

"Yes."

She had not brought a book.

She had not brought my old notes.

For five minutes she sat beside me and watched Guild workers go past.

I wanted to ask about the boxes.

I did not.

At six minutes, Hessa stood.

The gray-coated man came out carrying two boxes. He left the third on
her table.

Hessa stopped him.

"No."

He looked at the box.

"I need the table."

He took it.

Hessa went inside.

I followed.

"Do not touch anything."

"I know."

"You touched the glass last time."

"After."

"You touched it."

I sat.

The wooden frame from our last test was gone.

The glass shield remained.

So did the washers.

Hessa brought out a different base. It was a flat board with two shallow
grooves cut parallel into the surface. Each groove had three ink marks
beside it.

"New?"

"Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"The board is old."

"From what?"

"Not relevant."

That meant I would never know.

She set the six fins in a row.

They were small folded pieces of paper, each with a flat foot and an
upright face. The folds looked sharper than the ones we had used before.

Hessa picked up the first.

"Look."

"I am."

"The base."

I looked.

"Flat."

She set it down.

Second.

"Flat."

Third.

"Flat."

By the sixth I understood the activity.

"Flat."

"Good."

"Was that a test?"

"No."

"Good."

She took a narrow piece of wood and pressed it along the fold of each
fin.

"Same edge," she said. "Same paper. Same fold."

"Same person?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

"No."

I leaned back.

Hessa placed two fins into the grooves. One in each.

She slid the glass shield over them.

"Nothing yet."

"I know."

She checked the table with the same small wooden wedges she had used
before. One leg got a thin sliver beneath it.

Then she tapped the table.

Both fins stayed still.

She walked around it.

Both stayed still.

She opened the glass, switched the fins, closed it, and tapped again.

Nothing.

"You're making this very boring."

"That is the intention."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Usually."

She looked at me.

"Why did you write not to bring Pell?"

"Who?"

I stared at her.

Hessa's mouth moved.

Barely.

"You remembered."

"Unfortunately."

"Why?"

"Because you described a man under a stage speaking through leather."

"That is Pell."

"I gathered."

"He would have liked this."

"No."

"He likes garbage."

"No."

"Fair."

Hessa sat opposite me.

"Hands."

I put my hands on the table.

She checked them.

Then my wrist.

Then the old familiar questions.

Pain.

Numbness.

Tingling.

Heat.

Anything unusual since the last session.

No.

No.

No.

No.

No.

"Draw."

I did.

The mana came easier than it had before the road, though I no longer
trusted easy as a useful word. It was familiar. That was different.

I held the minimal draw where she had taught me to hold it.

Hessa watched.

"Stop."

I stopped.

"Again."

I looked at her.

"Again?"

"Baseline."

I drew again.

The second was smaller.

Cleaner.

The old unevenness along the wrist edge was there. Not stronger. Not
gone.

Hessa touched two fingers lightly against the inside of my wrist.

"Stop."

I stopped.

She wrote.

"Two?"

"Two."

"So twenty-four."

"Yes."

I had been keeping count.

Apparently so had she.

"Shape."

"No paper?"

"Not yet."

I shaped the draw without trying to send anything anywhere.

The asymmetry came back almost immediately.

It felt like leaning toward a door that was not there.

I corrected too hard.

Hessa said, "No."

"I know."

"Then stop."

I stopped.

She waited.

"Again."

The second shaping was better.

Not good.

Better.

I held it for three breaths.

"Stop."

I stopped.

Hessa wrote again.

"Twenty."

"Yes."

I flexed my hand.

"So now paper."

"Now paper."

She opened a small cloth bag.

Inside were six little wooden counters.

Three had black dots.

Three were blank.

"What are those?"

"Not magic."

"I assumed."

"You shouldn't."

She shook two counters into her closed hand.

"Choose."

"Why?"

"Choose."

"Left."

She opened her left hand.

Black dot.

She placed the black counter beside the left groove.

"Target?"

"Yes."

"You could have just said left."

"Then you would know I chose left."

"I know now."

"Yes."

I hated this.

She closed the glass.

"One attempt."

"Only one?"

"One, then we inspect."

I settled.

The room was quiet except for voices somewhere in the corridor and the
scratch of somebody dragging something heavy across stone.

I drew.

Not much.

I shaped.

The target fin was on the left.

I tried not to look at its edge.

That was impossible.

I looked at its edge.

I wanted it to move.

I wanted it so badly that I could feel myself beginning to turn wanting
into evidence.

I stopped thinking about the fin.

That did not help.

I shaped toward the space in front of it.

The familiar wrist-edge tendency pulled sideways.

I corrected.

Too much.

Back.

Smaller.

The fin moved.

I stopped breathing.

It had moved.

Not far.

Less than last time, maybe.

Forward.

Across the first ink mark.

The right fin had not moved.

"It moved."

"Stop."

I stopped.

Hessa did not look at me.

She looked at the table.

Then at my hands.

Then at the glass.

Then at the fin.

She walked around the table.

I waited.

She opened the shield.

"Don't touch."

"I wasn't."

She checked the target base.

Then the control.

Then the groove.

Then the ink mark.

She switched the fins.

The moved fin went to the right.

The control went left.

Then she shook the counters again.

"Choose."

"Right."

Blank.

She put the blank counter beside the right groove.

"Target?"

"Right."

"Again."

"Isn't that the same fin?"

"Yes."

"So if it moves again, we know the fin is easier."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because that is not the only explanation."

"Right."

I hated her.

I drew.

The second attempt felt worse immediately.

Not painful.

Crowded.

The shape would not sit where I wanted it.

I tried to force it narrower.

Hessa said, "Don't chase."

I loosened it.

The right fin did not move.

The left fin did not move.

I held.

Nothing.

"Stop."

I stopped.

Hessa wrote.

"That doesn't help."

"It does."

"How?"

"It didn't move."

"That's not helpful."

"It is information."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"No."

She let me rest.

I rubbed my palm.

The cloth I had wrapped around the crutch grip had left a faint line
across the skin.

Hessa noticed.

"What's that?"

"Curtain."

She looked at me.

"That answer is not useful."

"I pulled a curtain."

"Better."

"I called a ghost."

"No."

"Fine."

She waited until I stopped smiling.

Then she changed both fins.

Two unused ones.

She placed them in the grooves.

She checked the bases.

Glass down.

Counters.

"Choose."

"Left."

Blank.

Target right.

I did not like not choosing the target.

That was probably why she was doing it.

Third attempt.

I drew.

The shape came cleaner.

Not easy.

Cleaner.

I found the wrist edge, let it be there, and worked around it instead of
trying to erase it.

Hessa did not speak.

The right fin stood beyond the glass.

I shaped toward it.

Nothing.

I kept the shape small.

Nothing.

Then the fin gave a tiny jump.

Not forward.

Sideways.

Its upright face twisted a fraction toward the groove wall.

I stared.

The left fin did not move.

Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped.

She checked everything again.

"That wasn't forward."

"No."

"Does it count?"

"As movement."

"Caused by me?"

She looked at me.

"Wait."

I waited.

She removed the right fin and set it on the table.

It stood.

She tapped the board.

It trembled but did not twist.

She replaced it.

Then she switched the two fins.

"Again?"

"No."

"What?"

"Rest."

"I can do another."

"I know."

"Then why rest?"

"Because your hand is shaking."

I looked.

It was.

Barely.

Still enough.

I put it in my lap.

Hessa wrote.

I tried to read upside down.

She covered the page with her hand.

"Hostile."

"Rest."

We waited.

The room did not become dramatic.

Someone outside laughed.

A cart rattled in the street.

Hessa got up, went to the shelf, and came back with water.

I drank.

"How many external attempts is that?"

"Three today."

"So eight."

"Yes."

"And two movements."

"Today."

"Three total."

"Observed movements during external attempts, yes."

"That sounds good."

"It sounds like three movements."

"You're impossible."

"Good."

After several minutes she put the fins back.

Not the same pair.

She chose two others.

This time she put the counters in her hands before I could say anything.

"Choose."

"Right."

Black.

Target right.

"Last."

"Only?"

"Last."

I settled again.

My shoulder hurt from yesterday.

My palm hurt.

My wrist did not.

That mattered more.

I drew.

The mana sat where it sat.

I shaped.

The right fin waited.

I did not imagine the audience.

I did not imagine Pell.

I did not imagine a ghost.

I tried to make one stupid piece of paper move a stupidly small distance
while one woman watched and refused to be impressed.

The fin moved forward.

Not much.

Enough to cross the first ink mark.

I let the shape go immediately.

"Stop."

"I stopped."

"Hands away."

I pulled them back.

Hessa checked.

Glass.

Table.

Fin.

Control.

Groove.

Base.

She switched the fins.

She checked the board.

She looked at the wedges.

Then she sat.

I waited.

She wrote for a long time.

"Well?"

"No."

"No what?"

"Do not make me answer while I am writing."

I waited.

She finished.

Then she turned the page toward me.

There were four lines for today.

First: left target, forward movement.

Second: right target, none.

Third: right target, lateral movement.

Fourth: right target, forward movement.

Below that she had written:

THREE DIFFERENT FINS MOVED ACROSS TWO POSITIONS.

I read it twice.

"Three?"

"The first, the third, the fourth."

"The second was the same fin as first."

"Yes. It did not move."

"So three different fins moved."

"Yes."

"While I was shaping."

"Yes."

"Targets."

"Yes."

"Controls didn't."

"No visible control movement."

I looked at her.

"Did I move paper?"

Hessa leaned back.

I could see her deciding how much she hated the question.

"Under this setup," she said, "during supervised deliberate shaping
toward a designated paper target, we have now observed small movement of
multiple target fins without visible movement of the paired controls."

I waited.

"That is a terrible sentence."

"It is an accurate sentence."

"Did I do it?"

"We have removed the easiest explanation from last time."

"That isn't what I asked."

"I know."

"Hessa."

She looked at the page.

Then at the fins.

Then at me.

"Yes."

Nothing happened.

No light.

No sound.

No pressure.

No revelation.

I sat there.

"Yes?"

"Within the limits I just stated."

"I moved paper."

"You produced a repeatable external response under this apparatus."

"Paper."

"Do not make me regret this."

I smiled.

I could not help it.

Hessa pointed at me.

"No."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You are expanding the claim in your head."

"I am not."

"You are."

I was.

I stopped.

Mostly.

"What does it mean?"

"That we have a result."

"What can I do?"

"This."

She pointed at the paper.

"That's pathetic."

"Yes."

I laughed.

She did not.

Then she almost did.

I saw it.

"Can we try something heavier?"

"No."

"Farther?"

"No."

"Different direction?"

"Not today."

"Tomorrow?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you have done two minimal draws, two deliberate shaping
baselines, and four external attempts."

I counted.

"Twenty-four draws. Twenty shaping. Nine external."

"Yes."

"Nine?"

"Four today."

I had forgotten the last one while celebrating it.

Hessa folded the page.

"You are done."

"But now we know."

"Now we know one thing."

"One good thing."

"One small thing."

"Same."

"No."

I looked at the fins.

"What next?"

"Direction."

"Forward?"

"Whether forward is yours or the apparatus."

That took some of the smile out of me.

"How?"

"Different orientation. Different target direction. Same fins. Not
today."

"Because hand shaking."

"Among other things."

She gathered the counters.

"What other things?"

"You have somewhere to be."

I looked toward the window.

The light had shifted.

"Shit."

"Yes."

"Rinna."

"Probably."

I grabbed my crutches.

Hessa said, "Greg."

I stopped.

"Restrictions remain."

"I know."

"Say them."

"No independent draw. No shaping. No testing. No trying paper at home.
No trying anything else because paper worked."

"Good."

"It worked."

"Leave."

I left.

I was halfway down the corridor when I went back.

Hessa looked up.

"What?"

"Can I tell Lyssa I moved paper?"

Hessa stared at me.

"Leave."

I took that as yes.

At East Market Hall, Davin had the curtain wheel back up.

Jori was calling.

Nobody cared that I had returned from the Guild.

Rinna pointed at the coat rack.

"Third Man."

I put on the coat.

Sellen was already waiting.

"You're late."

"I moved paper."

He looked at me.

"Good."

"With magic."

"Better."

"Hessa says under a controlled setup during supervised deliberate
shaping toward a designated paper target, we observed small movement of
multiple target fins without visible movement of paired controls."

Sellen stared.

"Did you move paper?"

"Yes."

"Why didn't you say that?"

I hated everyone.

The Petition began.

I waited for my entrance.

Veya put down the petition.

The reed sounded.

Jori pointed at me.

I went.

"My neighbor's goat has eaten all my patience and half my fence."

Sellen came in exactly where he was supposed to.

The audience laughed.

Not huge.

Enough.

I did not add anything.

Afterward, Rinna paid me one copper.

"Pell says congratulations."

"Where is Pell?"

"Under the stage."

"Of course."

I found Lyssa after the show at home with the blue thread spread into
smaller bundles.

She looked up.

"Paper?"

"Yes."

"Moved?"

"Yes."

"On purpose?"

"Yes."

"More than once?"

I put my crutches against the wall.

"Yes."

Lyssa sat back.

"Oh."

That was better than cheering.

I sat opposite her.

"Hessa said yes."

"Actually yes?"

"With a terrible sentence around it."

"That sounds like Hessa."

"I have a result."

"What can you do?"

"Move very small pieces of paper a very small amount under a glass cover
while Hessa watches."

Lyssa considered this.

"Useful."

"You're mocking me."

"A little."

"I did magic."

"You've been doing magic."

"I did magic outside me."

She stopped sorting thread.

That was the part that reached her.

"Ah."

"Yes."

She looked at my hand.

"Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Good."

Then she picked up the thread again.

"What happens next?"

"Different direction."

"Why?"

"To see if I moved it or the table likes forward."

Lyssa nodded as if this were reasonable.

It was.

I put the copper from the show on the table.

One copper.

One result.

Six bundles of blue thread.

Lyssa had apparently spent the day turning six into twelve.

Tomorrow the hall had another show.

Hessa did not want me tomorrow.

That felt rude now.

I wanted to move something else.

I wanted to move the cup.

The thread.

The copper.

Anything.

I did not.

I sat on my hands for a moment instead.

Lyssa noticed.

"Good."

"Don't."

She smiled.

The paper had moved.

This time, maybe had become yes.

It was a very small yes.

It was enough.
