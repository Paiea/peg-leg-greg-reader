TEXT READER · CHAPTER 183

THE WORKER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I woke up looking at the corner of a piece of paper.

It was sticking out from under Lyssa's book.

I stared at it.

The paper did not move.

That was because I was not doing anything.

I knew this.

I continued staring at it.

Lyssa came in carrying a cup and stopped.

"No."

"I am not."

"You are looking at it."

"Looking is allowed."

"Is it?"

"Yes."

"Did Hessa say?"

I looked at Lyssa.

She drank from the cup.

"Hostile."

"You moved paper yesterday."

"Yes."

"Go to work."

She had already dressed. The blue thread from last night was gone except
for three short lengths on the table, all tied around small folded
scraps of cloth. I did not know what the scraps meant.

"Where is the thread?"

"Mostly not here."

"I can see that."

"Good."

"Where is it?"

"With people who needed blue thread."

That seemed intentionally unhelpful.

Lyssa picked up two of the folded scraps.

"Marra?"

"Some."

"Jessa?"

"One."

"Who are the others?"

She kissed my forehead.

"People."

Then she left.

I sat for another minute.

The paper corner was still there.

So was the cup.

So was one copper from last night.

The copper seemed heavier than it had before.

Not actually heavier.

I knew that too.

I put my hands on my knees, stood, got my crutches, and went to work.

At East Market Hall, Pell was waiting for me beside the repaired latch.

That was suspicious.

"You did magic."

"Yes."

He grinned.

It was the first time I had been able to answer that without immediately
correcting the sentence.

"Do it."

"No."

His grin vanished.

"Bad magic."

"Yes."

"What can you move?"

"Paper."

He picked up a torn corner of an old handbill from the floor and held it
out.

"Move this."

"No."

"You just said paper."

"Under glass."

Pell looked at the handbill.

"Put glass over it."

"No."

"Why?"

"Hessa."

"She isn't here."

"That is the problem."

Pell thought about this for perhaps half a second.

"Bad magic."

"I already agreed."

He dropped the handbill.

"Can you move a feather?"

"I don't know."

"A string?"

"I don't know."

"A fake petition?"

"I don't know."

"Things?"

"No."

"You can move paper but not things."

"Paper is a thing."

"Then you can move things."

"No."

Pell looked genuinely offended by my categories.

I understood Hessa a little better.

Nessa came through carrying a painted wooden bracket.

"Pell."

"Yes?"

"Tube."

He left immediately.

Apparently I ranked below tube.

Rinna arrived with a small wooden box under one arm and a folded sheet
in her hand.

"Afternoon."

"I know what time it is."

"Show."

"Oh."

The baker's afternoon.

I had forgotten.

Rinna handed me the sheet.

It was not a cue page.

It was a bill.

THE LITTLE KING.

THE MILLER'S SON.

THE RED DOOR.

Under that, in smaller writing:

SHORT SONG IF CHILDREN BEHAVE.

I looked at her.

"Do children behave?"

"No."

"Then why write it?"

"Hope."

Teren came out from behind the stage carrying a length of rope.

"Greg."

"Yes?"

"Can you do the paper thing on cue?"

"No."

"Can you do it here?"

"No."

"Can you do it without the Guild woman?"

"No."

"Can you move anything larger than paper?"

"No."

He nodded once.

"Good."

"Good?"

"Then it isn't my problem."

He handed me the rope.

"Doorframe."

That was how my first established external magical effect entered the
theatre.

I carried rope.

The red doorframe had split at the lower joint.

It was not the same painted doorway from The Widow's Room. This one was
narrower, red on the audience side, bare wood on the back, and built to
stand by itself with two feet that stuck out behind it.

One foot had cracked.

The frame leaned.

Nessa was kneeling beside it with the broken foot in her lap.

"Don't lift it."

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

"I was going to look."

"Look lower."

I sat on the floor.

The crack ran through one of the old peg holes.

"Can Davin fix it?"

"Yes."

"Where is Davin?"

"Not here."

"Can you fix it?"

"Enough."

She took the rope from me.

"What do you need me to do?"

"Hold that."

She pointed at the upright.

I held it.

For the next fifteen minutes, my contribution to theatre consisted of
keeping a fake red door vertical while Nessa bound a replacement block
against its foot.

My shoulder still hurt from the curtain work. Holding the frame was not
heavy, but it was awkward. I shifted my grip twice.

"Stop moving."

"My arm hurts."

"Then sit closer."

I moved closer.

That helped.

Nobody congratulated me.

A girl carrying a basket of costume belts stepped over my good leg
without apologizing.

Sellen came past wearing a green coat and stopped.

"I heard you can move objects with magic."

"No."

"Pell says."

"Pell says I moved paper."

"He said objects."

"He's wrong."

Sellen considered the doorframe I was holding.

"Can you move that?"

"No."

"Then that's disappointing."

"Thank you."

He left.

Nessa pulled the rope tighter.

"You told Pell?"

"He already knew."

"How?"

"Rinna."

Nessa looked toward the front.

"Rinna."

"What?"

"Nothing."

The repaired block held when she let go.

She stood.

"Now lift."

I lifted my side.

A man I did not know took the other.

We carried the doorframe six paces to its mark.

The new curtain wheel worked.

I noticed because Jori pulled the curtain while we were setting the
door, and it opened without the old scrape and hitch.

That was all.

Davin had fixed it.

The curtain worked.

Nobody mentioned it.

The afternoon audience began arriving before we were ready.

They were louder than the evening people.

Children came in pairs and groups, some with adults and some with older
siblings who had apparently been told to keep them alive until supper.
Market workers arrived still wearing aprons. One baker came with flour
up both forearms.

Rinna put me at the door because she had not learned.

"Two copper full bill," I said. "One for children."

A woman with three children pointed at the painted sign.

"Magic?"

I looked.

Someone had added a small star beside THE LITTLE KING.

"I don't know why that's there."

The oldest child looked at me.

"Are you the magician?"

"No."

"Pell says you are."

Of course he did.

"I moved paper."

The child waited.

"With magic," I added.

"Can you make something disappear?"

"Yes."

His face changed.

I took the handbill from his hand, put it behind my back, and handed it
to Rinna.

The child stared at me.

Rinna stared at me.

"That's not magic," he said.

"No."

He seemed disappointed.

His mother laughed and paid four copper for all of them.

Rinna waited until they were inside.

"That was terrible."

"They bought tickets."

"Do it again."

"I hate this job."

"No, you don't."

I hated when people knew that.

The afternoon problem was not the children.

It was the lamp.

The Little King used a small prepared lamp with a pale blue glass cap.
Not the same blue-lamp piece I had hung before. This one was supposed to
brighten briefly when the king opened a wooden box.

It did not.

The first test gave a weak gray flicker.

The second gave nothing.

Pell found me.

"Magic."

"No."

"Fix."

"No."

"Bad."

"Find Nessa."

Nessa was already there.

She took the lamp from the boy holding it, checked the worked line under
the cap, then called for Rinna.

Rinna touched the prepared strip with one finger.

Nothing.

"Spent," she said.

"Can it be charged?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Now?"

"No."

Pell looked at me.

I pointed at Rinna.

"See?"

"Your magic is still bad."

"My magic has nothing to do with this."

Nessa removed the blue cap.

"Plain lamp."

Pell said, "But the king's box."

"Plain lamp."

"What about the flash?"

"No flash."

"What if Greg moves paper?"

Nessa looked at him.

Pell left.

The piece ran with a plain lamp.

The king opened the box.

Nothing brightened.

A little girl in the front row still gasped because the actor looked
into the box as if he had seen something wonderful.

I was carrying a stool behind the side cloth and almost stopped to
watch.

Nessa tapped the stool.

"Move."

I moved.

The Miller's Son was next.

I had one line.

Not Third Man.

Not Shopkeeper.

Not anything I had performed before.

I was a clerk.

Teren had given it to me twenty minutes before the audience came in.

My entire job was to enter with a folded page, tell the miller's son
that the north wheel belonged to the widow until the debt was paid, hand
him the page, and leave.

"Which widow?" I had asked.

"The widow."

"From the other piece?"

"No."

"Does she have a name?"

"No."

"Why does she own a wheel?"

"Debt."

That had been enough for me.

I liked the clerk.

He knew what he was doing.

I entered.

The miller's son was played by Hara.

She looked younger than she had yesterday because someone had put her
hair loose and given her a badly fitted brown coat.

I knew she was Hara.

She looked seventeen.

That was annoying.

I gave her the page.

"The north wheel belongs to the widow until your father's debt is paid."

Hara stared at me.

I waited.

She was supposed to say, "My father is dead."

She did not.

A child coughed.

Hara looked down at the page.

Then back at me.

"My father is dead."

Too late.

I had already started turning.

I stopped.

My crutch squeaked on the floor.

A boy laughed.

I turned back.

"That does make collection difficult."

The audience laughed.

Hara's eyes changed.

Not angry.

Interested.

"Will you tell the widow?"

"No."

Bigger laugh.

That was not the scene.

I knew it immediately.

I had done it again.

Hara folded the page.

"Then I'll tell her myself."

That got us back.

I left.

Backstage, Teren was waiting.

"It worked," I said before he could speak.

"For you."

I closed my mouth.

He had not been there for the last version of this conversation, but
apparently theatre had one answer.

Hara came off two minutes later.

"You left."

"You forgot your line."

"I remembered it."

"After I left."

"You were supposed to wait."

"I did wait."

"You turned."

"Because you didn't say anything."

"That was my silence."

"It was a long silence."

"It was my silence."

Teren pointed between us.

"Both."

Hara frowned.

"Both what?"

"Both wrong."

Then he walked away.

I looked at Hara.

She looked at me.

"Your father is dead," I said.

"I know."

We both started laughing.

The Red Door used the repaired frame.

That was its entire reason for being called The Red Door as far as I
could tell.

A woman came home.

The door was locked.

Her brother was inside.

He would not open it.

They argued through an empty frame that had no wall attached to it.

From backstage, it looked ridiculous.

From the side gap, I could see the brother standing three feet behind
the red frame with nothing between him and his sister.

From the audience, the frame and a strip of painted cloth made a house.

The repair held.

Halfway through, one of the children yelled, "Go around!"

The woman onstage stopped.

The brother stopped.

The audience laughed.

She looked at the painted wall.

Then at the child.

"I've tried."

The child accepted this.

The play continued.

The short song happened because the children had not behaved and Teren
apparently wanted them tired.

Afterward, the hall looked like a meal had exploded.

Cups under benches.

A heel of bread behind the third row.

One child's scarf tied around the leg of a chair.

A wooden horse that belonged to nobody in the company.

Rinna held it up.

"Whose?"

Three children shouted, "Mine."

She put it on the ticket table.

"Fight later."

We cleaned.

Pell found the old magic sign stain and added a second tiny paper beside
the first.

This one had legs.

"Why?"

"It can move."

"Paper does not have legs."

"Yours does."

"No."

He added a hat.

"Stop improving it."

"Magician."

"I'm not a magician."

"You did magic."

"Yes."

"You moved a thing."

"No."

He put down the charcoal.

"You moved paper."

"Yes."

"Paper is a thing."

I looked at him.

He smiled.

I did not have Hessa's patience.

Rinna paid me one copper for the afternoon clerk role and crew work.

"One?"

"One."

"I also did door."

"You sold tickets."

"I repaired scenery."

"Nessa repaired scenery."

"I held scenery."

"One."

I took it.

"Fair."

"Yes."

"Are we paid tonight too?"

"If you work tonight."

"What am I?"

"Third Man."

"My goat."

"Your goat."

That made two shows in one day.

The afternoon crowd left.

The hall did not empty completely.

A few company people lay down backstage. Hara ate half an onion with
bread. Sellen disappeared. Jori sat on the low box with his cue page
over his face.

I went outside.

Carrow was still doing everything else.

A fish seller poured meltwater from a shallow cooling tray into the
gutter. A delivery boy pushed a handcart too fast around a corner and
lost three cabbages. Two women argued over a bolt of yellow cloth. A
prepared bakery stone showed a dull orange line beneath a pan of rolls.

Nobody knew I had moved paper.

That was good.

I bought one roll.

The baker did not ask me to move it.

Also good.

When I returned, Teren had changed the evening bill.

The Petition was gone.

Third Man was gone with it.

I stared at the board.

"What happened to my goat?"

Teren did not look up.

"Tomorrow."

"You told me tonight."

"I was wrong."

"What am I doing?"

"House."

"Door?"

"House."

"What does that mean?"

"Seats. Late people. Don't let anyone through the left passage during
the second piece."

"Why?"

"Costume change."

I had become less important in under three minutes.

I worked the house.

It was worse than acting.

People wanted aisle seats after buying center tickets. A man arrived
during the first piece and whispered that he had paid yesterday. A woman
insisted the left passage was where she had entered last week.

"No."

"Yes."

"Not tonight."

"Why?"

"Costume change."

"Whose?"

"I don't know."

That was the correct amount of information.

She went around.

During the second piece, I stood at the back wall and watched a scene I
had never seen before. Sellen played a physician. Veya played a woman
pretending not to know him.

I did not know why.

It was good.

Nobody needed me.

Then a bench leg shifted.

I caught the end before it tipped.

The man sitting on it looked at me.

"Thank you."

"Don't move."

He froze.

I found a folded scrap of wood, shoved it under the short leg, and let
go.

The bench stayed.

The scene continued.

That was my largest practical accomplishment of the day.

No magic.

After the evening show, Rinna gave me another copper.

"House."

"Bench."

"House."

"I saved four people."

"You stopped a bench wobbling."

"It was going."

"Two copper total today."

I put both coins in my pocket.

Pell came past carrying the ghost tube.

"Can you move coins?"

"No."

"Bad magic."

"Go away."

He did.

Lyssa was not home when I got there.

There was a note on the table.

BACK LATE. EAT.

Under it sat half a loaf and a small bowl of beans.

I ate.

Then I found paper.

Not the paper I had stared at that morning.

A different piece.

I wrote:

FORWARD?

Then underneath:

MY FORWARD OR BOARD FORWARD?

I looked at it.

That was ugly enough to be useful.

I did not draw.

I did not shape.

I did not put anything under glass.

I folded the paper and set it beneath Hessa's old note.

Then I put the two copper from the day beside yesterday's copper.

Three.

The coins did not move.

Neither did the paper.

Lyssa came in while I was washing the bowl.

She carried no blue thread.

"Where is it?" I asked.

"Where is what?"

"The thread."

"Gone."

"All of it?"

"Most."

"What happened?"

"People took it."

"Who?"

Lyssa looked at me.

"Still people."

I dried the bowl.

"I worked two shows."

"Good."

"I was a clerk."

"Good."

"I moved no paper."

"Very good."

"I held a door."

"That sounds more useful."

"It was."

She took off her shoes.

"How was the magic?"

"I told you. No paper."

"I mean yesterday."

"Oh."

She smiled.

I leaned against the table.

"It is still good."

"Good."

"Everyone keeps asking what I can do."

"What can you do?"

"Move paper."

"Then tell them."

"I do."

"And?"

"They ask what else."

"What else?"

"Nothing."

Lyssa nodded.

There was no cruelty in it.

That made it worse.

I looked at the folded question.

Tomorrow Hessa did not want me.

Tomorrow the hall did.

I had one small piece of real magic and no use for it.

I had a repaired curtain, a red door that stood if nobody kicked it, two
copper from a day of work, and a question about which direction paper
preferred.

That seemed like enough things to own at once.
