TEXT READER · CHAPTER 184

THE RUNNER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa asked me for a favor before I had finished chewing and put a gray parcel tied with blue thread on the table. For one stupid second I thought she wanted me to move it with magic. She wanted me to carry it to Marra's.

The instructions were simple in the way other people's errands were always simple: give it to Marra, say the left side was marked, do not untie it, and if Jessa happened to be there tell her narrow, not wide. I did not know what was narrow. Lyssa assured me I did not need to.

My right palm was still tender from calling the show, and the scrap cloth around the crutch grip had bunched again. Lyssa told me to replace it. Then she noticed I was still pleased about moving paper and reduced the achievement to its proper dimensions: a very small amount, under glass, while supervised.

She would be west until at least midday. I had the parcel, three copper, a theatre call before noon, and no reason to go near the Guild. It seemed manageable.

That was my second warning.

Marra's was a few streets out of the way, which remained more meaningful on crutches than it sounded. I delivered the parcel to the green door, repeated Lyssa's message about the marked left side, and learned that Jessa was supposedly at Sen's red door two lanes back.

On the way, Nessa intercepted me and added a paper packet of hooks for the hall. Sen told me Jessa had gone to Marra's. I went back. Jessa was standing exactly where I had left the parcel. 'Narrow, not wide,' I told her. She understood, which made one of us.

I escaped before anyone could hand me a third thing. By then the parcel was gone, the message was delivered, the hooks were under my arm, and the day had quietly converted me from a man going to work into a route.

By the time I reached East Market Hall, my palm hurt and I was late enough for Rinna to be waiting in the doorway. I offered the hooks as evidence of productivity. She opened the packet and informed me they were costume hooks, not curtain hooks.

Nessa had given them to me. Nessa was also somewhere else. Rinna sent the packet twelve paces into costumes, where a woman sewing a brown coat pronounced them useful. The wrong hooks had therefore reached the right place by a route nobody had intended.

Teren was onstage.

"Greg."

"Yes."

"Third Man."

"My goat?"

"Your goat."

Finally.

"Then after, take this."

He handed me a folded note.

I did not take it.

"Where?"

"South market. Lamp oil seller beside the cooper."

"Why?"

"Tell him six small tins tomorrow, not four. Rinna will pay on delivery."

"Why me?"

"You're going that way."

"I'm not."

"You are now."

I took the note.

"I have a show."

"Yes."

"When am I supposed to go?"

"After."

"After the show?"

"After your piece."

"There's more show after my piece."

"Correct."

"Do I come back?"

"If you want."

This was not a schedule.

It was weather.

I put the note inside my coat.

The Petition ran that afternoon, and I got my goat back. I waited for the right cue, delivered the familiar argument without adding anything, and the scene worked. Teren said good and immediately gave me set work, which was becoming the more meaningful review.

The repaired red door held. The curtain worked. The fake petition returned to its box. For almost an hour, I forgot I had any magic at all. Then Pell passed with a painted bucket, called me 'Magician,' and accepted 'Props' as my entire answer.

I left for the south market after my piece.

The lamp seller beside the cooper was farther south than Teren had implied.

Everything was farther when somebody else described it.

I had made it halfway when the cloth around my right crutch grip twisted again.

I stopped beside a cobbler's stall and fixed it.

The cobbler watched.

"That's terrible."

"It was free."

"Still terrible."

He had a basket of leather scraps under the bench.

I looked at it.

He looked at me.

"How much for a strip?"

"What size?"

I showed him the grip.

He took my crutch before I could object, turned it once, then pulled a narrow brown strip from the basket.

"This."

"How much?"

"One copper."

"That's too much for scrap."

"It's good scrap."

"It's in a basket on the ground."

"Then use your cloth."

I looked at the cloth.

The cloth looked back.

It was not going to improve.

"Half."

"No."

"Half and I don't tell people you charge full copper for rubbish."

"One and I wrap it."

"Half and you don't."

"One."

I paid one copper.

He wrapped it.

That changed the argument.

The leather went around the grip tight and smooth, with the seam away from my palm. He tied it under itself and cut the end.

I put weight on the crutch.

Better.

Annoyingly better.

"Good scrap," he said.

"I dislike you."

"Walk on it."

"I don't walk on that end."

He laughed.

I left.

The lamp seller was beside the cooper.

Teren had been right about that part.

I gave him the note.

He read it.

"Six?"

"Yes."

"Tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Not four?"

"Apparently this is important."

"It is if he wants six."

"Do you have six?"

"Tomorrow."

"Good."

He folded the note.

"Tell Teren I want the old tins back."

"Why?"

"Because they're mine."

"That seems fair."

"Tell him."

I had become a message with crutches.

On the way back, I passed the Guild street.

I did not need to go there.

I kept going.

Then a boy in a gray Guild vest came out of the side entrance and called, "Greg?"

I stopped.

This was becoming a problem.

He held a folded paper.

"For you."

"From Hessa?"

He looked at the front.

"Yes."

I took it.

"Do I need to go in?"

"No."

"Good."

I opened it on the street.

DAY AFTER TOMORROW. AFTER MIDDAY. BRING THE QUESTION. NO TESTING BEFORE.

Under that, in smaller writing:

THIS INCLUDES PAPER.

I read it twice.

The boy was still there.

"Does she usually add that?"

"I don't know."

"Good answer."

He went back inside.

Two workers were carrying a long shallow crate through the side entrance. One of them complained that it had been marked for east storage. The other said something about south.

I kept walking.

Hessa had scheduled direction.

Not tomorrow.

The day after.

I folded the note and put it with Teren's lamp message, then remembered I had already delivered Teren's lamp message.

My coat was becoming an office.

I reached the hall during the last piece.

Nobody had missed me.

That was useful information.

Rinna was counting coins.

"Six tins tomorrow," I said. "He wants the old tins back."

"Good."

"I bought leather."

"I can see."

"One copper."

"Why are you telling me?"

"I don't know."

She looked at my hand.

"Better?"

"Yes."

"Then stop complaining."

"I wasn't."

"You were preparing."

She was right.

I helped clean.

A costume sleeve had torn under the arm. The woman with the brown coat was already fixing it using one of the hooks Nessa had given me.

So the hooks had not been wrong.

They had merely been wrong for Rinna.

That felt familiar.

Jori rolled his cue page.

Sellen stole the last baker roll.

Hara accused him.

He denied it while chewing.

The hall had another evening show.

I did not.

Rinna told me I was done.

"Paid?"

She gave me one copper.

It replaced the one I had spent on leather.

That felt less like earning money than moving it through myself.

"Tomorrow?"

"Morning setup. Evening maybe."

"Role?"

"Maybe."

"Don't."

She smiled.

I left.

Lyssa was not home.

I was beginning to suspect she had a job.

I put Hessa's note on the table beside my folded question.

FORWARD?

MY FORWARD OR BOARD FORWARD?

The note looked like an answer to a different question.

DAY AFTER TOMORROW.

I had tomorrow first.

There was still one copper from the previous days on the table, plus the copper I had just earned. I had spent one on the grip, so the pile had not changed much.

I touched the new leather.

Worth it.

Then I realized I had forgotten to tell Jessa anything except narrow, not wide.

That was exactly what Lyssa had told me to say.

So perhaps I had succeeded.

Lyssa came home after dark carrying a basket.

"Did you take it?"

"Yes."

"To Marra?"

"Yes."

"Did you tell her left side?"

"Yes."

"Jessa?"

"Eventually."

Lyssa stopped.

"What happened?"

"She wasn't at Marra's."

"She was supposed to be."

"She went to Sen's."

"Why?"

"I don't know. Then she went back to Marra's."

Lyssa closed her eyes.

"I found her."

"Did you tell her?"

"Narrow, not wide."

"Good."

"That is all I know."

"That is all you needed to know."

I pointed at her.

"Exactly."

She put the basket down.

Inside were onions, a small wrapped piece of cheese, and two things that might have been turnips.

"What's that?"

"Food."

"I know what food is."

"Do you?"

"I had beans yesterday."

"That is evidence."

I took the basket to the table.

"Hessa sent a note."

"When?"

"I passed the Guild."

"You weren't going to the Guild."

"I didn't."

"Then how did you get a note?"

"They found me."

Lyssa looked at the paper.

"Day after tomorrow."

"Yes."

"Direction?"

"Probably."

She read my question.

"MY FORWARD OR BOARD FORWARD?"

"Yes."

"That's ugly."

"Thank you."

"What does it mean?"

"I don't know yet."

"Good question."

I looked at her.

She started cutting an onion.

"You sound like Hessa."

"Hostile."

"Accurate."

I sat.

My palm still hurt, but the crutch grip no longer rubbed the sore place when I leaned it against the table.

Lyssa noticed the leather.

"New?"

"One copper."

"Good."

"The man was a thief."

"Is it better?"

"Yes."

"Then maybe not."

"He charged me for scrap."

"He wrapped it."

I hated that everyone understood this except me.

We ate late.

The turnips were turnips.

Neither of us had expected the other to have food ready, so the meal was bread, onion, cheese, boiled turnips, and the last of yesterday's beans.

It was fine.

Lyssa's day had involved three people changing what they wanted after cloth had already been cut.

Mine had involved carrying her parcel to the correct green door, walking to the red door anyway, carrying Nessa's hooks, performing a goat complaint, buying leather, carrying Teren's message south, receiving Hessa's message while not visiting Hessa, and returning to a theatre that had continued perfectly well without me.

"Whose was worse?" I asked.

"Mine."

"You didn't hear mine."

"I lived mine."

"Unfair."

"Yes."

I ate cheese.

Afterward I put tomorrow's theatre time on a scrap beside Hessa's note.

MORNING HALL. EVENING MAYBE.

Under that:

DAY AFTER TOMORROW. HESSA. QUESTION.

I looked at the two pieces of paper.

Neither moved.

I did not ask them to.

Tomorrow I had theatre.

The day after, I had magic.

Tonight I had dishes.

I did those first.
