LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 189

THE SHADOW

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was wrapping her ankle when I woke.

Not because it was worse.

I knew this because she told me before I asked.

"It's better."

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked."

"I have eyes."

"So do I."

She pulled the cloth snug, tied it, stood, and put most of her weight on
the foot before I could object.

I looked again.

She pointed at my crutches.

This argument had developed rules.

I ate bread.

Lyssa drank water and sorted three folded pieces of cloth into a basket.
None were yellow or green. I did not ask whose they were.

This was growth.

She put the basket beside the door.

"If Marra tells you anything today, don't bring it home after dark."

"I wrote it down."

"That wasn't the problem."

"I was working."

"So was Jessa. She still found me."

"Jessa has two legs."

Lyssa stared at me.

"That's what you're using?"

"No."

"Good."

I tore another piece of bread and wrapped it in cloth with half an onion
and a little salt.

Lyssa watched.

"Food?"

"The rolls are gone."

"You knew they would be."

"I knew the agreement ended. I didn't know that meant the food would end
emotionally."

"That's usually what ending means."

I put the food in my bag.

The Hessa note was still under the cup.

DAY AFTER TOMORROW.

AFTER MIDDAY.

AWAY FIRST.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

Except it was not day after tomorrow anymore.

It was tomorrow.

I knew this without touching the paper.

I left it there.

Nothing moved.

Outside, Carrow was already noisy.

A wagon blocked half the street near the cooper. Someone had dropped a
basket of pale roots, and three people were collecting them while a
fourth argued that they were doing it wrong. A boy ran past me with a
long strip of red cloth tied around his shoulders.

I did not think about direction.

I thought the cloth was going to catch on something.

It did.

He stopped hard enough to nearly sit down.

I kept walking.

East Market Hall had both front doors open.

That usually meant work or smoke.

Today it was work.

Nessa was onstage with a coil of cord around one arm. Jori stood in the
house staring upward. Pell was behind a painted wall with his feet
sticking out. Rinna had a chalk board propped on the ticket table.

My name was on it.

Not large.

Not important.

Still there.

GREG: PORTER / REAR.

I read it twice.

Rinna said, "You can read."

"I like confirmation."

"Then confirm the rear window is still on the rear rack."

"Today?"

She looked at me.

"Yes."

I went.

The rear window was not on the rear rack.

This pleased me more than it should have.

I came back.

"It isn't."

Rinna stopped writing.

"Where is it?"

"I don't know."

"Less useful."

"But correct."

"Find it."

I found it behind the left hanging, where Pell had moved it because the
rear rack was needed for two wall flats.

"I moved it," Pell said from under the wall.

"I know."

"How?"

"You move everything."

"Not everything."

"Where's the bad stool?"

"Which?"

I pointed at him.

"Exactly."

He slid farther under the wall.

I carried the window back toward the stage.

It was not really a window.

It was a wooden frame with stretched pale cloth, painted around the
edges to suggest stone. From the front it could be a high window in a
house, a low window in a tower, or possibly a very disappointing
painting depending on what they put around it.

Nessa saw me.

"Not there."

"I was told rear rack."

"Later."

"Where now?"

"Stage left."

"Today?"

"Greg."

"Yes."

I put it stage left.

Teren came through the side door carrying a bundle of pages and a heel
of bread in his mouth.

He pulled the bread out.

"You're Porter."

"I saw."

"Six lines."

"How many things do I carry?"

"One."

"That seems low."

"You complain about carrying things."

"I complain about unpaid carrying."

"Good. This is paid."

He handed me a page.

The piece was called The Borrowed House.

I had not seen it.

My role was a porter delivering a small chest to the wrong house.

This caused a woman to believe her husband had bought jewelry, the
husband to believe the woman had bought poison, and a neighbor to
believe both of them were leaving town.

My six lines were not difficult.

"I was told this house."

"By whom?"

"A man."

"What man?"

"The one who told me this house."

I liked the Porter.

He was an idiot with procedural integrity.

Teren pointed at the right entrance.

"Second piece."

"Which side?"

"Right."

"Today?"

Teren chewed.

"Keep doing that."

I narrowed my eyes.

He walked away.

I rehearsed the lines while carrying the window again because Nessa had
changed her mind.

Or, more accurately, because I had misunderstood "stage left" as its
final location instead of where she wanted it while she measured
something.

She did not explain this distinction.

She just said, "Back."

I carried it back.

Then she gave me a coat on a stick.

I looked at it.

It was an old black coat tied across a short wooden crosspiece. Above
the shoulders, another piece of wood rose into a battered round hat.
There was no head.

One sleeve had been stuffed lightly with cloth. The other hung empty.

I looked at Nessa.

"No."

"What?"

"Whatever this is."

"Man."

I looked at the coat.

"No."

"Tonight."

I looked again.

"That's a man tonight?"

"Window."

She pointed.

The pale-cloth window was now upright behind part of the set for The
Borrowed House.

I understood less.

Pell emerged from behind the wall and took the coat-stick from me.

"He's too close."

"I haven't done anything."

"Not you."

He carried the coat behind the window.

Nessa had placed a plain lamp several feet farther back.

Pell stood between lamp and cloth.

A dark shape appeared on the pale window.

From where I stood beside it, the shape was obviously a coat hanging on
wood.

Nessa walked into the house.

"Back."

Pell stepped backward.

The shape softened.

"More."

He stepped again.

The shoulders changed.

The hat stopped looking like a hat balanced on a stick and became the
top of a head I could not see.

Nessa said, "Lower."

Pell lowered it.

"Stop."

I moved into the house beside her.

There was a man behind the window.

Not a good man.

Not a detailed man.

A man.

I went back.

Coat.

House.

Man.

Back.

Coat.

I did this three times.

Pell said, "Are you broken?"

"Maybe."

Nessa said, "Greg holds it tonight."

"Why?"

"Pell changes coat."

Pell pointed at himself.

"In the scene."

"You are in this?"

"I'm the neighbor."

I looked at the coat.

"Who is he?"

"No one."

"Then why is he there?"

Nessa said, "Because Veya sees him."

That was apparently the entire explanation.

The scene had the husband and wife arguing near the end. Veya, playing
the wife, looked up toward the window and saw someone outside. The
husband turned. The shadow moved away.

That was it.

Four seconds.

Maybe five.

I took the stick.

It was not heavy.

It was awkward.

I could not stand behind the cloth safely while holding it with both
hands because I needed one hand for a crutch. Nessa looked at me, looked
at the space, and dragged a low stool into position.

"Sit."

"I can stand."

"Sit."

I sat.

My crutches went against the rear wall.

With both feet placed badly, I shifted.

Nessa pointed at my good foot.

"Flat."

I put it flat.

The coat-stick rested lightly against the floor beside my knee.

This was much easier.

"Up," Nessa said.

I raised it.

The shadow appeared.

"Back."

I moved the stick backward.

"Not you."

I stopped.

"Move the bottom."

I angled the base away while keeping the shoulders roughly in place.

The shadow changed.

"Too much."

I corrected.

Pell stood in the house.

"Stick."

"What?"

"I can see the stick."

I looked at the shadow.

The lower vertical line showed beneath the coat.

"How do I hide it?"

"Lower coat."

"If I lower the coat, the man gets shorter."

Pell shrugged.

"Then shorter man."

I lowered it.

Nessa said, "Good."

Apparently we had sacrificed height for having legs that were not one
wooden pole.

We ran the scene.

I waited behind the window.

Veya and Hara argued onstage.

I could hear them clearly through the cloth.

Veya said, "Then why was it delivered here?"

Hara said, "Because the porter is an idiot."

I took this personally.

That was not my cue.

I waited.

Veya crossed.

She looked toward the window.

I raised the coat.

Too early.

Her eyes had not landed yet.

The shadow was already standing there when she found it.

Nessa said, "Down."

I lowered it.

Veya stopped.

"Again."

We reset.

"What's the cue?" I asked.

Nessa said, "Her."

"She looked."

"Not enough."

This was becoming familiar.

Veya heard us.

She said, "I turn. I see it. Then you give it to me."

"That's three things."

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"The seeing."

"How do I know when you see something that isn't there?"

Veya smiled.

"You'll know."

I disliked actors.

We ran again.

Veya turned.

Nothing.

Her eyes rose.

Nothing.

Then her body stopped.

Not much.

Just enough.

I lifted the coat.

The shadow appeared in the place she was already looking.

"Oh," I said.

Nessa said, "Don't."

"What?"

"Talk."

"During rehearsal?"

"Practice."

We did it again.

I got the timing right.

Then I moved the shadow away.

Smoothly.

Too smoothly.

Pell, watching from the house, said, "Board."

"What?"

"It slides."

"It is on a stick."

"Man doesn't know that."

"How does a man leave?"

Pell stood and took three steps sideways.

His shoulders moved a little. His height changed a little. His head
shifted.

"Walk it."

I tried.

The coat bounced like a drunk scarecrow.

Pell covered his face.

"Less man."

"You said walk."

"Not a parade."

Again.

Small rise.

Small settle.

Pause.

Then out.

From the house, it was enough.

Not good.

Enough.

I had a role to rehearse too.

The chest was real wood and mostly empty. I carried it onstage with one
hand while using a crutch in the other.

Teren stopped me.

"No."

"What?"

"You're working too hard."

"I'm carrying a chest."

"Porter does this every day."

"He has two legs."

"So?"

I stared at him.

Teren pointed toward the entrance.

"Use both hands. Sellen brings it to the mark."

Sellen was playing a servant in the first scene. He could carry the
chest to just inside the wing before my entrance. I could brace, put
both hands on it, and take the last four steps without a crutch.

I tried.

It worked.

Not elegantly.

Better.

My first line came while I was still holding the chest.

"I was told this house."

Veya said, "By whom?"

"A man."

"What man?"

"The one who told me this house."

She stared at me.

I stared back.

Someone behind the set laughed.

Teren said, "Don't wait for it."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

"I was breathing."

"Faster breathing."

The next run was cleaner.

At midday, I ate my bread and onion sitting on the rear step.

Pell sat beside me with nothing.

I looked at him.

He looked at my bread.

"No."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"I was going to ask what kind."

"Bread."

"What kind?"

"Mine."

He sighed.

"Rolls were better."

"Paid work ended."

"We're still working."

"Different problem."

He looked offended.

I had been waiting to use that.

The afternoon disappeared into small work.

A buckle broke.

Not mine.

A paint pot spilled.

Also not mine.

Jori could not find a cue page because he was holding it.

Hara changed stockings twice.

Sava came in, collected the wrapped prepared strip from yesterday, and
left with it. Nobody asked me to help.

The plain taper remained in use.

The audience began arriving before sunset.

I worked the front for ten minutes, then Teren called me to dress.

Porter had a brown coat with a patched elbow and a cap that smelled like
someone else's hair.

I put it on.

The first piece went without me.

Then The Borrowed House began.

I waited right.

Sellen brought the chest to the mark.

He said, "Yours."

"Today?"

He looked at me.

"Forever."

"That seems unlikely."

My entrance came.

I set the crutch aside where it would not fall, braced, took the chest
with both hands, and walked the four steps.

The audience saw a porter carrying a chest.

They did not see Sellen move it most of the distance.

"I was told this house."

Veya looked at the chest.

"By whom?"

"A man."

"What man?"

"The one who told me this house."

A laugh.

Not huge.

Enough.

I liked it.

The husband entered.

The misunderstanding grew.

I had three more lines, one of which was simply, "I don't know."

That got the best laugh.

I left.

Sellen handed me my crutch.

"Good."

"Which part?"

"You left."

I hated actors.

I stripped the cap off and went rear.

Pell was already changing into the neighbor's coat.

The fake man waited against the wall.

Coat.

Stick.

Hat.

Nothing else.

I sat on the stool.

Crutches against wall.

Good foot flat.

The lamp was lit.

The pale window glowed.

Onstage, Veya and Hara argued about the chest.

The audience laughed.

I held the stick loosely.

Not yet.

Veya crossed.

Not yet.

She turned.

Not yet.

Her eyes rose.

Then she saw him.

I lifted.

Someone in the audience stopped laughing.

A small sound came from the front.

Not quite a gasp.

Close.

Hara turned toward the window.

The shadow stood there.

A man.

I knew there was no man.

I could see the stuffed sleeve hanging six inches from my hand.

Veya stepped backward.

"Who's that?"

I moved the bottom of the stick.

Too much.

The shadow leaned strangely.

I corrected before it became obvious.

Then I walked it away.

Small rise.

Settle.

Pause.

Out.

The audience made another sound.

Gone.

I lowered the coat.

My shoulder immediately complained.

Four seconds.

Maybe five.

Pell came behind the hanging in his neighbor's coat.

"Did it work?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"How do you know?"

"They shut up."

Then he went onstage.

I stayed behind the window and grinned at a coat.

Jori appeared.

"Why are you smiling?"

"Man."

He looked at the coat.

"That's not a man."

"Tonight."

Jori looked toward the stage.

"Fair."

Then he handed me a rope.

"Hold."

I held it.

The rest of the show did not care that I had just been a Porter and a
man who did not exist.

There was a missed entrance in the final piece.

Not mine.

A cup rolled off a table and did not break.

Veya forgot a glove and used the wrong hand for an entire scene.

Nobody died.

The audience laughed.

Afterward, I carried benches.

The fake man came apart.

Hat onto shelf.

Coat into basket.

Crosspiece against wall.

The man disappeared faster than the audience.

Nessa checked the pale cloth for soot.

Pell complained about food again.

Rinna counted money.

She paid me one copper.

"Porter and rear."

"I was also a man."

"One copper."

"I was two visible people."

"One of them was a coat."

"The audience disagreed."

"Ask them for money."

I took the copper.

Tomorrow was Hessa.

The thought arrived without warning.

Not day after tomorrow.

Tomorrow.

Away first.

I looked at the fake man's crosspiece leaning against the wall.

Then at my hand.

Then stopped.

That was not paper.

That was not the apparatus.

That was not anything.

I put the copper in my pocket.

On the walk home, my shoulder hurt more than my palm.

The right grip still did not twist.

Worth one copper.

Possibly the best financial decision I had made in Carrow.

Lyssa was home before me.

Her ankle was unwrapped.

She was standing at the table cutting thread.

I looked down.

She did not look up.

"It's better."

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked."

"You're developing magic."

"Mine works."

I sat.

She glanced at my coat.

"How was theatre?"

"I delivered a chest to the wrong house."

"On purpose?"

"Professionally."

"Good."

"Then I was a man in a window."

Lyssa stopped cutting.

"Were you?"

"No."

She waited.

"It was a coat."

"That sounds less impressive."

"Audience thought it was a man."

"Did you wear the coat?"

"No."

"Was anyone wearing the coat?"

"No."

She looked at me.

"So you were a coat."

"I was operating a man."

"That's worse."

"It worked."

"Did they pay the man?"

"One copper."

"Good for him."

I put the coin on the table.

She slid it back toward me.

"Yours."

"Hessa tomorrow."

"That's not money."

"No."

She looked toward the cup.

The note was still underneath.

I pulled it out.

DAY AFTER TOMORROW.

AFTER MIDDAY.

AWAY FIRST.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

The first line was wrong now.

I found charcoal and crossed out DAY AFTER TOMORROW.

I wrote TOMORROW above it.

Lyssa watched.

"Scientific."

"Very."

"Does Hessa know you changed her note?"

"No."

"Good."

I put it back under the cup.

Tomorrow, Hessa would put paper under glass and ask whether away was
easier.

Tonight there was a wooden crosspiece against a theatre wall that had
been a man for five seconds because a lamp, a coat, Veya's eyes, and my
hand had agreed to lie at the same time.

I did not write that down.

My shoulder hurt.

Lyssa's ankle was better.

There was bread for morning.

That was enough work for one day.
