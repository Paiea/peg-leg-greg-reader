TEXT READER · CHAPTER 190

THE MEASURE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was gone before I finished changing Hessa's note.

Not because she had written it wrong.

Because time kept attacking the accuracy.

TOMORROW.

AFTER MIDDAY.

AWAY FIRST.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

I crossed out TOMORROW and wrote TODAY.

The paper now had three different versions of the first line on it.

This seemed scientifically important.

Lyssa had seen me doing it while tying her shoe.

"Your science keeps changing."

"The science is fine. The calendar is unstable."

"That's worse."

Her ankle was unwrapped. She still favored it for the first few steps,
then less once she had moved around. I noticed without saying anything.

She noticed me noticing without saying anything.

We were improving.

She left with a cloth bag over one shoulder and told me she would
probably be late.

"Where?"

"Out."

"Excellent detail."

"You'll survive."

She kissed my cheek and left.

I ate bread.

The note stayed under the cup.

Nothing moved.

Today it would.

Maybe.

That word had become expensive.

I got to East Market Hall early enough to be useful and late enough that
Rinna did not accuse me of arriving yesterday.

The chalk board had changed.

GREG: SET / FRONT UNTIL MIDDAY.

No role.

I looked at Rinna.

"Paper," she said.

"Today."

"After midday?"

"Yes."

"Then you're mine until food."

This was apparently how scheduling worked when two institutions wanted
pieces of me.

Nessa had the pale window down on the floor. The man from last night was
no longer a man. His coat was in a basket. His hat was on a shelf. The
wooden crosspiece had been tied into a bundle with three other pieces of
scrap wood.

I looked at it once.

Then Nessa handed me a bucket.

"Water."

I got water.

The morning was mostly walls.

One wall went up.

Another came down.

A narrow painted arch had to move from rear storage to the right side
without scraping the fresh edge. Pell and I carried it together.

"Your end up," he said.

"My end is up."

"More up."

"Then yours is down."

"Correct."

We reached the turn.

The arch did not.

We backed out.

"Other way," Pell said.

"You said this way."

"I was wrong."

"That was easy."

"I practice."

We turned the arch and tried again.

It fit.

Jori had me check three stool positions for the afternoon bill, then
changed one after I checked it. I asked whether he wanted me to check
again.

"No."

"How will we know?"

"I moved it."

"That has failed before."

He pointed at the front doors.

"Go sell something."

I worked the front until the smell of somebody's fried onions outside
became stronger than my loyalty.

A man asked whether The Borrowed House was playing again.

"Tomorrow."

"Is the porter the same?"

I paused.

"No."

He looked disappointed.

This was flattering for almost half a breath.

Then he said, "Good."

I sold him a seat anyway.

At midday Rinna pushed a copper-colored token across the ticket table.

"Food."

"What is this?"

"Marker. Bring it back."

"Why?"

"Because it is mine."

I looked at the token.

"Does it buy food?"

"No."

"Then this is a terrible food system."

"It means I know you left."

"Why do you need to know I left?"

"So I know you're gone."

I took the marker.

"Very advanced."

She ignored me.

I ate bread and a small piece of cheese in the lower market, returned
the marker, collected my crutches from behind the ticket table, and left
for the Guild.

Rinna called after me.

"Greg."

I turned.

"Paper."

"Yes."

"Don't be late tomorrow because paper happened."

"I don't know what paper is going to do."

"Neither do I. That's why I'm telling you now."

This was unreasonable and probably useful.

The Guild front clerk saw me and raised one finger before I spoke.

I stopped.

"I hate that."

"Wait."

"I haven't asked anything."

"Good."

I waited.

The front room smelled of dust, old wood, and something sharp from the
corridor to the left. A woman I had seen before was arguing with a clerk
over a bundle of prepared cord.

"I brought six."

"You brought five."

"I tied six."

"There are five."

"Then one is somewhere."

"Yes."

This did not appear to satisfy either of them.

The cracked prepared plate was not on the shelf where I had seen it
before. A chalk mark remained on the wood beneath the empty space.

The note was gone too.

I did not ask.

A gray-coated woman came through carrying two narrow boxes.

"Paper Man."

"No."

She kept walking.

I had lost this fight permanently.

Hessa appeared from the opposite corridor with a folded board under one
arm and a cup in the other.

She looked at me.

"Shoulder?"

I blinked.

"Hello."

"Shoulder."

"Sore."

"Why?"

"I was a man."

She stopped.

"At the theatre."

"That did not improve it."

"I held a coat on a stick behind a window."

Hessa looked at me for a moment.

"Of course you did."

"It worked."

"Your shoulder?"

"The man worked."

"Sit."

This was becoming a theme in my life.

The room was not the same one as last time.

It was smaller and had one high window, two shelves of boxed pieces,
folded cloth, iron weights, and three empty wooden frames stacked
against the wall. The table was broad enough for the apparatus but left
little room around it.

Hessa set down the board.

"Today is not whether you can move paper."

"I can."

"Yes."

I enjoyed that.

She took a drink.

"Today is whether one requested direction produces the response more
reliably than another."

"Which way works."

"If you need to make it worse."

"I do."

"Which way works."

I liked Hessa.

She did not always deserve it.

The apparatus had changed.

The square board was gone. In its place was a long pale board marked
with a center line and three shallow positions. One nearest me. One
farther away. One crossing the center at right angles.

Hessa had paired guides for each position, target and control, with
enough space between them that the fins would not touch.

The glass shield was the same.

Or looked the same.

"Same glass?"

"Yes."

"Today?"

Hessa stared at me.

I smiled.

She checked the table legs, pressed two corners, then wedged the rear
left leg with a thin piece of wood.

"Chair."

I put it where I thought it belonged.

"Back."

I moved it.

"More."

I moved again.

"Stop."

My good foot sat flat. My other leg rested where it did not press the
table. The crutches went against the wall.

Hessa touched my shoulder lightly, then had me lift the arm.

"Pain?"

"Muscle."

"That was not the question."

"Yes. A little."

"Does it change if you hold here?"

I moved.

"Yes."

"Then don't."

She made a mark beside the chair leg.

"You're not bracing on the table."

"I know."

"You're tired."

"I'm always tired."

"Useful."

"How?"

"It means I don't have to ask."

She laid out the fins.

They were small, folded from pale paper, matched as closely as I could
tell. She made me inspect the bases.

Flat.

Edges similar.

No obvious crease differences.

Then she took six little wooden counters from a pouch.

Three had black marks.

Three did not.

"Order?" I asked.

"Mine."

"Why?"

"Because if you choose, you'll choose away."

"That's the point."

"The point is to compare it."

She closed the counters in her hand.

I disliked science again.

First came the draw.

I settled.

Breath.

The familiar inward gathering came slowly, then narrowed.

The same slight wrongness at the wrist edge.

Nothing new.

Nothing dramatic.

Hessa watched my hand.

"Hold."

I held.

"Release."

I released.

She made a mark.

"Twenty-six."

I knew.

I did not say it.

That felt like another kind of progress.

Then shaping without a target.

No paper under glass.

Just the familiar deliberate attempt to make the narrow gathered thing
become something more directed without letting it run away from me.

The wrist edge crowded first.

I wanted to correct harder.

"Don't chase."

I didn't.

The shape held for two breaths, rough but deliberate.

"Release."

I did.

"Twenty-two."

Then the paper came out.

Hessa placed the first pair in the straight guides.

Target assignment by counter.

Left.

She set the black-marked counter beside it.

"Requested direction?"

"Away."

"Say it once."

"Away."

The target fin sat between us and the far end of the board.

I drew.

Shaped.

This time the shape did feel different.

Not stronger.

Cleaner was too generous.

Less crowded, maybe.

Like there was one fewer thing in the way.

I held the idea of away.

The fin moved.

Barely.

A tiny forward slip, less than the width of my smallest fingernail.

The control did not visibly move.

I breathed out.

Hessa's finger rose.

I closed my mouth.

She inspected the glass.

Then the board.

Then the guide.

Then the fin.

She checked the wedge under the table.

"Movement."

"I know."

"Good."

"That sounded almost kind."

"It wasn't."

Attempt fourteen.

Movement.

Away.

The second pair went into the same guides.

New target assignment.

Right.

Hessa drew another counter.

"Toward."

I looked at her.

"You hate me."

"Toward."

I drew.

Shaped.

The feeling was not painful.

Not blocked.

Just worse.

Crowded sooner.

I tried to hold the requested direction without pushing harder.

Nothing moved.

I released.

Hessa inspected anyway.

Attempt fifteen.

No movement.

"Again?" I asked.

"Different."

She moved the guides to the cross position.

Now the target and control sat sideways relative to me.

New fins.

New counter.

Left target.

"Requested direction?"

"Away."

I frowned.

"The guide is sideways."

"Yes."

"So if it moves away, it leaves the guide."

"Possibly."

"That's rude."

"Shape."

I did.

The first breath felt messy.

The second settled.

Away.

The target twisted.

Not much.

Its front edge shifted out of the shallow guide and stopped.

The control stayed where it was.

I looked at Hessa.

Her finger was already up.

I had not even opened my mouth.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were."

She inspected.

The fin had not traveled forward along the guide.

It had moved partly out of it, in the direction farther from me.

Hessa marked the page.

"Movement."

"Away."

"Observation."

"Away."

"Yes."

Attempt sixteen.

Movement.

Away.

Something warm moved through my chest that had nothing to do with mana.

Hessa replaced everything.

"Water."

"I'm fine."

"Water."

I drank.

She waited longer than I wanted.

The room had a high narrow window. Somewhere outside it, someone dropped
metal and swore. A cart rolled over stone. Two people passed the door
arguing about a frame number.

Hessa read something on another sheet while I sat there.

Her day continued even during mine.

"Ready?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Say that after you move your shoulder."

I moved it.

It hurt.

"Less yes."

"I can do another."

"I know."

She set the next pair.

Straight guides.

Target right.

"Requested direction?"

"Sideways. Left."

"That's not away."

"Correct."

I drew.

Shaped.

Tried to keep left.

The shape wanted to smear.

That was the only word I had for it.

I corrected once.

"Don't chase."

I stopped correcting.

The target gave a tiny tremble.

Or I thought it did.

Then one corner shifted.

Not left.

Slightly away.

Hessa leaned closer.

"Release."

I released.

She inspected.

"Movement?"

I asked.

"Maybe."

"Direction?"

"Not left."

"Away."

"Possibly."

She wrote longer this time.

Attempt seventeen.

Possible movement.

Requested sideways left.

Observed displacement too small for clean directional claim, with
possible away component.

I hated the sentence before she finished it.

"That's disgusting."

"It happened."

"Maybe."

"Yes."

"You enjoy this."

"No."

I did not believe her.

Attempt eighteen was toward.

Nothing moved.

Not the target.

Not the control.

The shape felt crowded almost immediately. My shoulder wanted to lift.
Hessa stopped me before I did.

"Release."

I did.

She checked the apparatus.

No movement.

Toward.

Failure.

I stared at the fin.

"I don't like toward."

"Noted."

"That was a joke."

"I know."

"You wrote it."

"I wrote that you reported increased difficulty maintaining the
requested shape."

"That is not what I said."

"It's what I heard."

I considered arguing.

She was right enough to be irritating.

"One more," she said.

"Only one?"

"Yes."

"Away?"

She showed me the counter after drawing it.

"Away."

The final pair went into the far position.

Still along the same line from me, but farther from the edge of the
table than the first away attempt.

I noticed.

"Distance."

"Not today."

"You changed it."

"I know."

"Then it matters."

"It may. Which is why I am recording it."

I wanted to ask three questions.

I asked none.

Draw.

Shape.

Away.

The first breath was bad.

The second was better.

The familiar wrist-edge asymmetry sat there, annoying and known.

I left it alone.

Away.

The target moved.

A tiny slip.

Then stopped.

Not farther than the first.

Maybe less.

Still visible.

Control unchanged.

My whole body wanted to lean toward it.

I did not.

Hessa raised no finger this time.

I stayed still anyway.

She looked at me.

"Good."

That was worse than the finger.

She inspected everything.

Attempt nineteen.

Movement.

Away.

Hessa sat.

I kept looking at the paper.

Three away requests.

Three movements.

Two toward requests.

No movements.

One sideways request.

Something too small and wrong-directed to call cleanly, maybe away.

I knew what I wanted to say.

Hessa knew too.

She started writing before I could.

"So away is easier."

"Probably."

I stopped.

That was not one of her usual words.

"What?"

"Probably."

"You said probably."

"I know."

"Write that."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I'm writing what happened."

"What happened is probably."

"What happened is three requested-away trials produced visible target
movement today. Two requested-toward trials did not. The sideways trial
produced an ambiguous small displacement that did not follow the
requested sideways direction."

"And went away."

"Possibly."

"Mostly."

"Possibly."

I leaned back.

My shoulder objected.

Hessa pointed at it.

"Also that."

"That is theatre."

"That is your body."

"Different problem."

"Not while your body is part of the apparatus."

I hated when she did that.

She finished writing.

I tried to read upside down.

She turned the page.

AWAY RESPONSE REPEATED UNDER MULTIPLE TARGET POSITIONS.

TOWARD RESPONSE NOT OBSERVED TODAY.

DIRECTIONAL PREFERENCE RELATIVE TO GREG'S POSITION SUPPORTED.

NOT GENERALIZED.

I read it twice.

"Supported."

"Yes."

"Not possible."

"It remains possible in the larger sense."

"You changed the word."

"Because we have more evidence."

I looked at the fins again.

They were pathetic.

Tiny pale folded pieces under glass.

Nothing about them looked like an achievement.

"So away is easier."

Hessa rubbed ink from the side of one finger.

"That is currently the better explanation."

I smiled.

"Don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You are."

"Can I practice away?"

"No."

"That was fast."

"No independent draw. No independent shaping. No paper testing. No
coins. No thread. No trying larger objects. No trying to discover
whether every object in your room dislikes you."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

"I was going to think about it."

"You may think."

"Generous."

"Quietly."

I looked at the page again.

"Does this mean the wrist edge..."

"No."

"You let me get farther that time."

"No."

"Everything has a side."

"Everything has many sides if you describe it badly enough."

"That sounds familiar."

"It should."

She pulled a fresh sheet toward herself.

"Next question."

"How hard can I push?"

"No."

"How far?"

"Closer."

I waited.

She drew two short lines on the page.

One near.

One farther.

"Does distance from you change the response if the requested direction
remains away?"

I looked at the far line.

"You already moved the last pair farther."

"Yes."

"And it moved."

"Yes."

"So distance doesn't matter."

Hessa stared at me.

I sighed.

"Fine."

"Good."

"You're going to make me fail at two distances."

"I'm going to compare them."

"Same thing with better handwriting."

She wrote:

AWAY ONLY.

NEAR / FAR.

I pointed at it.

"When?"

"Not tomorrow."

"Of course."

"I have other work."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"People keep saying that."

She gathered the counters.

I looked at the record one more time.

"Twenty-six."

"Yes."

"Twenty-two."

"Yes."

"Nineteen."

"Yes."

"And away is probably easier."

Hessa paused.

"Supported as a directional preference in the current setup."

"Probably."

She gave me the cup.

"Leave."

I drank the last of the water first.

Then I left.

The gray-coated woman was in the front room.

She looked up.

"Paper Man."

"Away."

She frowned.

I kept walking.

This was a victory.

East Market Hall was still open when I returned.

I had not planned to go back.

My feet apparently had.

Rinna saw me.

"Paper?"

"Away."

Pell was carrying a basket behind her.

He immediately took one step backward.

"Working."

I hated him.

Rinna said, "Can you carry chairs?"

"Yes."

"Better."

I carried chairs.

Not with magic.

There were six.

Pell carried four at once and made a point of it.

I carried two.

At home, Lyssa had beaten me back.

She was sitting with one foot up on the other chair, ankle bare.

Better.

Actually better.

I put Hessa's new paper under the cup.

Lyssa watched me.

"Paper?"

"Yes."

"Did it move?"

"Three times."

Her eyebrows went up.

"Away."

"All three?"

"All three away attempts."

"Did the other ways move?"

"Toward didn't. Sideways did something stupid."

"Like you."

"Very much like me."

She held out her hand.

I gave her the sheet.

She read Hessa's wording.

"DIRECTIONAL PREFERENCE RELATIVE TO GREG'S POSITION SUPPORTED," she
said.

"See?"

"That sounds important."

"It sounds terrible."

"Does Hessa agree?"

I sat down.

"More than before."

Lyssa nodded once and gave the paper back.

That landed better than congratulations would have.

I put the sheet under the cup.

Beside it I put Hessa's next note.

AWAY ONLY.

NEAR / FAR.

Not tomorrow.

No date yet.

The room contained bread, a cup, two chairs, a table, thread, my copper,
Lyssa's basket, and several other things that could theoretically be
farther from me than they currently were.

They remained exactly where they were.

I was getting very good at magic.
