TEXT READER · CHAPTER 195

THE CARRIER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa had turned the wooden hoop into cloth.

Not all of it.

The hoop was still wood. The cloth had simply been stretched across it
so tightly that I could no longer understand why the thread had been
necessary.

I stood over her shoulder.

"What is it?"

"You lasted longer yesterday."

"I wasn't awake enough."

"Apparently."

Pale cloth filled the hoop. A torn place near one edge had been drawn
together with stitches so small I had to lean closer to see them.

Lyssa put two fingers against my forehead and pushed me back.

"You're breathing on it."

"I breathe most places."

"Not there."

I sat down.

Morning had gotten farther than usual before either of us left. Light
came through the window. Somebody below us was arguing about a bucket.
The soap sat beside the basin with one corner already rounded from use.

On the table, Hessa's note remained under the cup.

Tomorrow.

After midday.

Away only.

Near / far.

I knew what it said without lifting the cup.

That was convenient.

Lyssa cut the repaired cloth free from the hoop.

"Hold this."

She handed me the hoop.

Then the cloth.

Then a folded dark blue piece from the chair.

"Separate?"

"Yes."

I put the hoop on my lap and the two cloths beside each other.

She handed me a smaller pale bundle.

"Separate."

"From which?"

"Both."

"This system needs labels."

"This system has me."

"That seems fragile."

She ignored me and went to wash.

By the time she came back, I had four things.

"Where are we going?"

"Marra."

"We?"

"You have theatre?"

"Later."

"How much later?"

I had gone to the hall the previous night without asking tomorrow's
time. This had felt like personal growth until now.

"I don't know."

Lyssa stopped tying her hair back.

"You didn't ask?"

"No."

She looked impressed.

I did not like it.

"I can go ask."

"No."

"I need to know."

"You can ask after."

"After what?"

"Marra."

This was how I learned I was going to Marra.

Lyssa took the hoop from me and gave me a larger bundle.

"I thought I was holding that."

"You were."

"What happened?"

"Now you're holding this."

The theatre had prepared me for nothing.

We left after eating the heel of yesterday's bread and one piece of
cheese between us.

Lyssa carried the hoop under one arm. I had the large bundle tucked
against my side, one crutch under the opposite arm, and a smaller folded
piece balanced over my forearm.

Halfway down the stairs, she put another bundle on top.

"How many hands do you think I have?"

"The useful number."

"That's not a number."

"Keep walking."

We walked.

Carrow in the morning had already heated at the edges. Bakers had their
shutters open. A cart loaded with narrow boards blocked half a crossing
while three men discussed where the cart ought to have been. A woman
carrying flowers passed us going the other way, leaving a sharp green
smell behind her.

Lyssa adjusted her pace at the first crowded corner.

I adjusted mine.

Neither of us said anything.

At the next crossing she reached for the top bundle before I needed to
change my grip. I gave it to her, stepped around a basket seller, then
took it back on the other side.

That was apparently how we did streets now.

Marra's place was busy enough that nobody noticed us for almost a
minute.

I liked this.

Jessa stood on a low stool pinning the side of a green dress on a woman
who had both arms lifted and the expression of somebody regretting every
decision that had led to clothing.

Marra was behind the long table cutting dark fabric.

Another woman sat near the wall with a half-finished sleeve in her lap.

Lyssa walked in.

Marra said, "There."

Not hello.

There.

Lyssa put the hoop down.

I waited.

Jessa glanced over.

"You delivered him."

"I carried myself."

"Late?"

"No."

"Then somebody else did it."

The woman in green looked at me.

I looked at Jessa.

"That was one message."

"It was a memorable message."

"It was not."

"You wrote it down and still didn't deliver it."

"I eventually delivered it."

Lyssa put a bundle on the table.

"Hours later."

I looked at her.

"You were there."

"Yes."

"You're supposed to be on my side."

"I am. That's why I remember."

Marra pointed at me without looking up.

"Blue on the chair."

I looked down.

I was holding dark blue, pale, and something brown that had appeared
somewhere between our room and here.

"Which blue?"

"The blue."

"There are two."

Every woman in the room looked at me.

I checked again.

There were apparently not two.

One was blue.

The other was, according to Jessa, "blue-gray."

"That is blue."

"No."

"It contains blue."

"So does a bruise."

"That is not helping."

The woman in green laughed.

Jessa said, "Chair."

I put the actual blue on the chair.

"Other one?"

"Table."

I put the blue-gray on the table.

Lyssa immediately moved it six inches to the left.

"Why?"

"Pins."

There were pins where I had put it.

I had not seen them.

"This room is hostile."

Marra said, "Only to blood."

"That is not reassuring."

She finally looked at me.

"Hold."

A long piece of brown cloth came across the table.

I took it.

"How?"

"Up."

I held it up.

"More."

I raised my arm.

"Not you. Cloth."

I raised the cloth.

"Stop."

I stopped.

Marra and Lyssa looked at it.

Jessa looked at it from the stool.

The woman in green looked too, because she had nothing better to do
while wearing half a dress.

"Too warm," Lyssa said.

Marra nodded.

I looked at the brown cloth.

It was brown.

Jessa said, "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to ask what warm brown means."

"Exactly."

"What does it mean?"

"No."

The woman in green laughed again.

I had become entertainment without pay.

Marra took the cloth.

Then she handed me another.

This one was also brown.

I decided to survive.

For the next half hour, my work consisted of holding things, moving
things, and not placing things where pins lived.

I failed the third one twice.

Lyssa repaired the pale cloth from the hoop at the table beside Marra.
It was not embroidery. It was a torn inner panel from some larger piece,
and the hoop had only kept the fabric stretched while she worked the
tear back together.

This made sense once somebody else explained it to Jessa.

I pretended I had understood already.

A customer came in and greeted Lyssa by name.

Then another.

The second asked whether she had finished "the narrow one."

Lyssa said, "Tomorrow."

Nobody told me what the narrow one was.

Marra sent Jessa into the back for black thread.

Jessa came out with black thread, a red ribbon, and a complaint about a
missing box.

Lyssa knew which shelf the box had been on last time.

Marra knew who had moved it.

The woman with the sleeve knew who had borrowed it.

None of them needed me.

I held brown cloth.

This was peaceful in a different way from the lower market.

Eventually Marra said, "Done."

I lowered my arms.

"With me?"

"With that."

She took the cloth.

Lyssa folded the repaired pale panel and put it with the blue-gray.

I picked up the blue.

"No."

I stopped.

"What?"

"That stays."

"You brought it."

"I did."

"So it goes back."

"No."

I looked at Lyssa.

She pointed at the blue-gray.

"That."

I picked it up.

Then the pale panel.

"No."

I stopped again.

"That stays too."

"Why did I carry things here if I'm not carrying them back?"

"Because that was the job."

"This work is badly designed."

Marra put a different bundle in my arms.

"Take this."

"Where?"

"Home."

"Whose?"

"Lyssa's."

I looked at Lyssa.

She was smiling.

"This is how you get furniture," I said.

"What?"

"You keep putting things on it."

Jessa said, "Furniture doesn't complain."

"Bad furniture."

The woman in green said, "He's more like a shelf."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't praise."

I was beginning to suspect this entire city had agreed on one sentence.

Lyssa had one more stop.

A thread seller two streets over.

I waited outside because the stall was narrow and because I had become a
shelf.

She came out with two small paper-wrapped spools.

"How much?"

"Not yours."

"I can contribute."

"You can carry."

"That's not currency."

"It is today."

We bought food after.

That part I was allowed to understand.

Bread, a small wedge of white cheese, and three carrots.

I paid for the carrots.

Lyssa let me.

This felt suspiciously ceremonial, so I did not mention it.

We ate one carrot raw while walking.

It was not good enough to justify doing this regularly.

At the turn toward East Market, Lyssa stopped.

"I go west."

"I go east."

"I know."

She took the smaller spool bundle from under my arm.

I handed it over before she asked.

She took half the bread too.

"That was mine."

"Was."

"You're becoming Rinna."

"No."

"Marra?"

"Worse."

She bit the carrot.

"See you tonight."

"Probably."

She pointed the carrot at me.

"Don't start."

I went east.

The hall board had my name.

It had apparently survived since last night.

GREG: SET / NEIGHBOR.

I stood in front of it.

Rinna came past with a stack of tickets.

"After fourth bell."

"What?"

"You're early."

"I went to Marra."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

"What's Neighbor?"

She pointed at a rolled page.

"Four lines."

"New?"

"Old."

"Mine?"

"Today."

I picked up the page.

The piece was The Broken Gate, which I had seen but never performed.
Neighbor entered once to complain about a cart blocking the lane, left,
returned near the end to discover the gate had fallen down, and blamed
the wrong person.

Four lines.

I read them.

"That's it?"

Rinna stopped.

"Do you want more?"

"No."

"Good."

This was the most successful negotiation we had ever had.

Marek was near the rear door with a canvas bag, two shirts, and a length
of cord.

"Packing?"

"No."

I looked at the bag.

He looked at the bag.

"Yes."

"Need help?"

"Know where Pell put the short tying cord?"

"Upper shelf. Left of narrow tubes. Not the long narrow one."

Marek stared.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I found the cord.

It was exactly where it should be.

Pell was not there to witness this miracle.

Marek tied the bag.

"Leaving morning?"

"First bell."

"Back when?"

"Depends."

"On baby?"

"On sister."

"Right."

I had learned.

He pulled the knot tight.

"Tell them not to replace me."

"Who?"

"Everyone."

"They already replaced you yesterday."

"Then tell them badly."

"That I can do."

He nodded.

That was goodbye.

No one embraced.

No one promised anything.

He carried the bag out to wherever he was sleeping before morning.

I went to set.

The Broken Gate needed a gate.

We had one.

It was painted wood on a frame with a hinge that had been repaired
enough times to become mostly repair.

Davin was checking the bottom brace.

"Can I step on the floor now?"

He looked at me.

"Yes."

I stepped on yesterday's pale replacement strip.

Nothing happened.

"Good."

Davin said, "Yes."

"Strong day."

He went back to the gate.

Rehearsal for Neighbor took six minutes.

Teren was there.

He gave me an entrance, a place to stop, and one correction.

"Don't stand in the gate."

"I thought I'm complaining about it."

"From beside it."

"Why?"

"Because EvIn needs to come through."

"That seems selfish."

"Again."

We did it again.

I stood beside the gate.

EvIn came through.

Civilization survived.

That was all.

The rest of the afternoon I moved two benches, carried a lamp, fetched a
cup for Nessa, and helped Jori hang a narrow painted strip that had
nothing magical about it and did not become important later.

I appreciated this.

The audience was decent.

The Broken Gate ran second.

My first line was, "Whose cart is blocking my lane?"

EvIn said, "Not mine."

"It has your name on it."

"Then someone has stolen my name."

A laugh.

I waited where I was supposed to wait.

"My wife says move it."

EvIn said, "Tell your wife I am considering it."

"I did. She said consider faster."

A better laugh.

I left.

Later I returned.

The gate was down.

It was supposed to be down.

I looked at it.

Then at Hara.

Then at EvIn.

"I leave for ten minutes."

Hara said, "It was twelve."

"Then this is my fault."

The audience laughed.

I blamed Hara.

Hara blamed EvIn.

EvIn blamed municipal drainage.

The piece ended.

I went backstage.

Nobody corrected me.

Nobody said fine.

I almost went looking for somebody to do it.

Instead I carried the gate frame out of the way for the next piece.

This was healthier.

The last show ran a little late because a strap broke on a costume and
Nessa refused to let the actor go out with it hanging.

She fixed it.

The actor went out.

The audience never knew they had waited for thread.

Afterward, Rinna paid me one copper.

"Neighbor. Set."

"Gate."

"Set."

"Floor testing."

"No."

"Carrots."

She looked at me.

"Unrelated."

"You don't know my accounting."

"I know enough."

"One?"

"One."

I took it.

I did not stay for food.

Pell arrived as I was leaving and asked if I was coming.

"No."

"Why?"

"I have bread."

"That's not a reason."

"It was yesterday."

He accepted this.

Outside, the air had cooled.

I walked home with the remaining bread under one arm and the bundle
Marra had given me under the other.

I had carried the bundle through the entire theatre shift without
opening it.

This felt like evidence of character.

Lyssa was home first.

She had the hoop out again, but no cloth stretched across it.

She looked at the bundle.

"You still have it."

"Nobody told me to lose it."

"Good."

I put it on the table.

"What is it?"

"Mine."

"I know that."

"Then you're improving."

She opened it.

Two finished dark sleeves.

I stared.

"I carried sleeves all day?"

"Yes."

"They weigh nothing."

"Did you want heavier sleeves?"

"I wanted my labor to have shape."

Lyssa held one against her arm.

"It had shape."

"Not enough."

She laughed.

We ate bread, cheese, and cooked carrots because raw carrots had failed.

I told her I had been a Neighbor.

"Whose?"

"Apparently everyone's."

"Did you know them?"

"No."

"Bad neighbor."

"I was excellent."

"Did someone say that?"

"No."

"Then how do you know?"

"I wasn't corrected."

Lyssa considered this.

"Fine."

I pointed my knife at her.

"Don't."

She smiled.

After food, I finally lifted the cup.

Hessa's note was still there.

THREE DAYS.

It no longer meant three days.

Tomorrow.

After midday.

Away only.

Near / far.

Same restrictions.

I read it once.

Lyssa saw.

"Tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Excited?"

I put the paper back.

"A little."

That was true.

"Nervous?"

"A little."

Also true.

"What do you do?"

"Sit where she tells me."

"Good start."

"Then draw if she tells me. Shape if she tells me. Ask the paper to move
away."

"Near and far."

"Yes."

"What does far mean?"

"I don't know."

Lyssa nodded.

"What does near mean?"

"I don't know."

"You know a lot again."

"I was hoping you'd forgotten that."

"No."

I put the cup back over the papers.

Nothing moved.

I did not ask anything to.

Marek would leave north at first bell.

Lyssa had work.

The hall would open whether I was there or not.

After midday, Hessa would put paper somewhere near me and somewhere
farther from me, and we would find out whether distance mattered inside
one very small thing I was actually allowed to claim.

Tonight I had carried cloth, sleeves, a gate, bread, and several objects
nobody had trusted to themselves.

That seemed like enough.
