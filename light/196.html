LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 196

THE SITTER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Marek left north at first bell.

I knew this because the cart made enough noise to announce itself to
three streets.

Lyssa was already dressed when the first wheel struck something outside
and produced a wooden crack followed by a man's opinion of the road.

"That him?" I asked.

"Probably."

I went to the window.

The cart was long, low, and loaded with sacks, two barrels, a wooden
chest, and enough rope to make Pell emotional. Marek sat on the rear
edge with his canvas bag between his boots.

He looked up because I was leaning out the window.

"See your sister," I called.

"That was the plan."

"Don't get replaced."

He lifted one hand.

The cart moved.

That was goodbye.

Lyssa handed me bread.

"You weren't going down?"

"He was already moving."

"You could have."

"I said goodbye yesterday."

"Then why did you hang out the window?"

"Confirmation."

She ate.

I looked at the cup on the table.

She looked at me looking at the cup.

"After midday."

"I know."

"Good."

"I've been very good."

"At not touching paper?"

"At several things."

"Name two."

I ate bread.

Lyssa left before I did.

She had the narrow thing to finish.

I still did not know what the narrow thing was.

This had begun to feel deliberate.

I washed, used the soap, checked my right palm, and left the cup where
it was.

My palm had stopped being interesting. The skin was still newer where
the bad grip had rubbed it, but it no longer complained every time I put
weight through the crutch.

This was excellent.

Body parts should become boring whenever possible.

At the hall, Rinna was counting tickets that did not yet belong to
anyone.

"I have Guild after midday."

She kept counting.

"Fine."

"I don't know how long."

"Less fine."

"Do you need me tonight?"

"Probably."

"Role?"

"Probably."

I waited.

She looked up.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

I had become extremely mature.

The board said:

KING: SELLEN / HARA / EVIN

BROKEN GATE: EVIN / HARA / GREG

WIDOW: VEYA / NESSA / HARA

GREG: NEIGHBOR / SET

Marek's name was gone.

Nobody had written a little memorial around the empty chalk.

Sellen was carrying a crown.

"You're king again."

"I can read."

"How does it feel?"

"Like a hat."

He put the crown on a shelf.

I worked until midday.

Nothing important happened.

This was not entirely true. Davin discovered that one of the rear bench
braces had cracked. Nessa discovered somebody had hung a damp shirt
among dry costumes. Jori discovered the lamp oil count was wrong by one
small tin.

All of these were important to the people holding them.

None of them changed my life.

I helped Davin turn the bench over.

The underside contained dust, a bent ticket, and one dried pea.

Davin tapped the cracked brace.

"Hold."

I held.

He removed two nails, replaced the brace with a shorter piece from
behind the work shelf, and nailed it in.

"Done?"

"Not yet."

He drove another nail.

"Done?"

"Yes."

We turned the bench back.

I sat on it.

Davin looked at me.

"Testing."

"You test everything by sitting?"

"Only things designed for it."

The bench held.

"Excellent."

At midday I ate the rest of the bread with a little cheese.

Rinna came past.

"Guild?"

"After."

"Go."

"I have time."

"Go slowly."

"I don't think that's how appointments work."

"It is if you leave now."

I left.

Hessa's room looked exactly like it had been waiting for me.

The table was clear except for the square board, the paper fins, the
glass covers, two small weights, Hessa's notes, and a piece of chalk.

There was also a chair.

I looked at the chair.

Hessa looked at me.

"Sit."

"I was afraid of that."

"Why?"

"I've developed a reputation."

"For sitting?"

"Apparently."

She pointed.

I sat.

The chair had been placed at one end of the table rather than the side.
A chalk line crossed the tabletop a little more than a forearm from the
edge nearest me. Another line crossed it near the far end.

The board rested beside Hessa, not on either mark.

"Eat?"

"Yes."

"Sleep?"

"Yes."

"Hand?"

"Mostly boring."

"Show me."

I held out my right hand.

She turned it once, pressed the healed skin near the base of my fingers,
then let go.

"Shoulder?"

"Fine."

"Wrist?"

"Fine."

"Any draw since last time?"

"No."

"Shaping?"

"No."

"External attempt?"

"No."

"Paper?"

"No."

"Coin?"

"No."

"Thread?"

"No."

She looked at me.

"I know."

"You asked."

"Good."

She sat opposite the side of the table, where she could reach the board
without standing.

I looked at the chalk lines.

"Near and far."

"Yes."

"Those?"

"Yes."

"Near is that near?"

"Today."

Of course.

Hessa put the board on the first chalk line.

The center of the target fin sat roughly a forearm and a hand beyond the
table edge in front of me. Far enough that I could not accidentally
breathe on it. Close enough that I could see the cut edge of the paper
clearly.

She set the glass cover over it, checked the two small control fins,
then marked something on her page.

Then she moved the entire board to the far line.

It was more than twice as distant. I could still see the target, but the
smallest cut marks disappeared into paper.

"Far," she said.

"That is definitely farther."

"Yes."

"Good experiment."

"Sit back."

I had leaned forward.

I sat back.

She moved the board near again.

"Your position does not change."

"At all?"

"Not intentionally."

"Comforting."

"Feet."

I adjusted my foot.

"Crutch."

I had leaned it against the table.

She pointed to the wall.

I moved it.

"Hands."

"Where?"

"Same place each attempt."

She had me rest my left hand on my thigh and my right hand open on the
other.

This felt like being arrested by furniture.

"Draw first," she said.

I stopped joking.

The familiar part was still not easy.

That bothered me less than it used to.

I settled my breathing and waited until Hessa told me to begin.

The draw came narrow.

Not smooth. Not pleasant. But narrow.

I found the small controlled pull without reaching for more. Heat
gathered under my ribs and behind my sternum, not enough to frighten me,
enough to make my shoulders want to join the work.

I kept them out of it.

"Stop."

I stopped.

Hessa watched my face.

"Again?"

"No."

I blinked.

"That was a question?"

"How do you feel?"

"Oh."

I checked.

"Fine. Warm."

"Where?"

I told her.

She wrote.

That made twenty-seven successful supervised minimal draws.

She waited until my breathing was ordinary again.

"Shape."

The shaping was worse.

It always wanted to become movement somewhere in my body instead of
remaining the small deliberate pressure Hessa had been making me
practice.

My wrist tightened.

"Release."

I did.

"Again?"

"You just said one."

"I asked how you feel."

"You're using the same word for too many things."

"How do you feel?"

"Wrist tight. Shoulder fine. Breathing fine."

She wrote again.

Twenty-three supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Then she put the board on the near line.

My stomach tightened.

That was ridiculous.

It was paper.

Hessa checked the target fin, then the controls. She lifted the glass,
replaced it, and looked at me.

"Away only."

"Away from me."

"Yes."

"Not board-forward."

"Not board-forward."

"Not left, right, north, south, spiritually distant."

She waited.

"Away from me."

"Yes."

I looked at the target.

I did not imagine a hand.

I did not imagine a shove.

I did not imagine the paper flying across the table and proving
something much larger than it had ever proved.

I tried to ask for the same small external change we had already gotten,
in the direction away from where I sat.

The pressure narrowed.

My wrist wanted to tighten.

I let it stay small.

The paper fin moved.

Not much.

The upper edge gave a tiny turn away from me, enough that the cut shadow
beneath it changed.

I saw it.

Hessa did not react.

The control fins did not move.

"Stop."

I stopped.

She leaned in, checked the target, checked both controls, then wrote.

"How much?"

"Less than I'd like."

"Observed."

"Half a fingernail?"

"Less."

"That's insulting."

She ignored that.

External attempt twenty.

Near.

Response observed.

I knew better than to celebrate paper.

I still wanted to.

Hessa gave me water.

We waited.

Then she moved the board to the far line.

The same board.

Same fins.

Same glass.

Same direction relative to me.

The target looked smaller from here.

"Do I lean?"

"No."

"I thought not."

"Same position."

I put my hands where they had been.

Hessa checked them.

"Ready?"

"Yes."

"Away."

I asked.

Nothing happened.

The sensation was there. The small shaped pressure was there. My wrist
tightened a little earlier than it had near.

The paper did nothing.

I held too long.

"Stop."

I stopped.

Hessa checked the target.

Controls unchanged.

Target unchanged.

She wrote.

I stared at the far fin.

It looked smug.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You leaned."

I sat back.

External attempt twenty-one.

Far.

No observed response.

"Rest."

"I'm fine."

"Rest."

I drank more water.

The room had a small high window. Somebody outside passed close enough
that their shadow crossed the lower wall and disappeared.

Hessa turned her page.

I looked at the far chalk line.

"So."

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to."

"This is becoming a city problem."

She waited.

I did not say closer was easier.

This required effort.

After a few minutes she moved the board back to near.

She checked the fins again.

"Same."

"Same."

"Away."

The second near attempt felt worse.

Not dramatically.

Just enough that I noticed the first attempt had been cleaner.

My shoulder tried to lift.

I lowered it.

The pressure narrowed.

The target fin gave a small twitch.

Then, after a moment, a clearer turn.

Away.

Tiny.

Real.

The controls remained still.

Hessa stopped me almost immediately.

She checked everything.

"Observed?"

I asked.

"Yes."

"Good."

She wrote.

External attempt twenty-two.

Near.

Response observed again.

I smiled.

Hessa looked up.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're smiling."

"I'm allowed."

"Yes."

This was generous of her.

She moved the board far.

The smile became less useful.

"Same?"

"Same."

I rested my hands.

The target was far enough away that I had to resist narrowing my eyes at
it.

Hessa waited until I stopped.

"Away."

I tried.

The first few breaths felt wrong.

I released before she told me.

Hessa said nothing.

"Again within the same attempt?"

"No."

I looked at her.

"No?"

"You released."

"I thought that was good."

"It was."

She checked my wrist.

"Pain?"

"No."

"Tight?"

"Yes."

"Shoulder?"

"Starting."

She wrote something.

"Are we done?"

"Not yet."

I looked at the far board.

She waited longer this time.

My shoulder settled.

My wrist did not entirely.

"One more external attempt," she said.

"Far?"

"Far."

"Then we're ending on the one that doesn't work."

"We are ending on the condition we have less evidence for."

"That is a much worse sentence."

"It is more accurate."

I put my hands back.

She checked my position.

The far target waited under glass.

"Away."

I tried again.

Nothing.

Then maybe something.

I stopped breathing for half a second.

The upper edge of the target seemed to tremble.

I could not tell whether it had actually changed position or whether I
had stared at it long enough to invent mercy.

Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped.

She went to the board.

She did not immediately write.

That was worse.

"Did it move?"

"Maybe."

"That's not one of your usual words."

"It is when I am not sure."

She checked the control fins.

Then the target.

Then the glass.

"One control may have shifted also."

I leaned forward.

"How much?"

"Enough."

"So it doesn't count."

"It counts as an attempt."

"Of course it does."

She wrote.

External attempt twenty-three.

Far.

Uncertain target movement. Possible control movement. Not usable as
evidence of response.

I hated this.

It was exactly correct.

I hated it more.

"Again."

"No."

"One clean far."

"No."

"Because of the control?"

"Because your wrist is tightening earlier, your shoulder has joined
twice, and this would be the fifth external request after draw and
shaping."

"I can do one."

"Probably."

"Then."

"No."

I looked at the paper.

Hessa closed her notes.

That was unfairly final.

"Near worked twice."

"Yes."

"Far failed once."

"Yes."

"And far maybe did something useless."

"Yes."

"So near is easier."

"Here."

I looked at her.

"Today."

"Yes."

"In this arrangement."

"Yes."

I sighed.

She opened the notes again.

"I liked it better closed."

She wrote slowly enough that I could read upside down if I leaned.

I did not lean.

She turned the page toward me when she finished.

AWAY RESPONSE OBSERVED TWICE AT NEAR POSITION.

FAR RESPONSE NOT ESTABLISHED.

NEAR RESPONSE MORE RELIABLE THAN FAR RESPONSE TODAY.

POSSIBLE DISTANCE DEPENDENCE IN CURRENT APPARATUS.

NOT A RANGE.

NOT GENERALIZED.

I pointed.

"You wrote the part I was going to say."

"Which?"

"Near is easier."

"I did not write that."

"You wrote it with more words."

"No."

"It is emotionally the same."

"No."

"Fine."

She added beneath it:

REPEAT.

NEAR / FAR.

AWAY ONLY.

No date.

I noticed.

"When?"

"Not today."

"I know that."

"Good."

"When later?"

"I'll tell you."

"Tomorrow?"

"No."

"Day after?"

"I'll tell you."

"You're becoming Rinna."

Hessa stared at me.

"Never mind."

She checked my wrist again.

Then my shoulder.

"Same restrictions."

"All of them?"

"All."

"No paper."

"No."

"No coin."

"No."

"No thread."

"No."

"No external testing."

"No."

"No independent draw."

"No."

"No independent shaping."

"No."

"I remembered."

"I know."

That was almost praise.

I decided not to damage it.

"Twenty-seven draws," I said.

"Successful supervised minimal draws."

"Twenty-three shaping."

"Supervised deliberate shaping attempts."

"Twenty-three external."

"Supervised external-effect attempts."

"You make every number longer."

"That is because you shorten every claim."

I thought about that.

"Fair."

She looked suspicious.

"I can say fair."

"Go home."

"I have theatre."

"Then go there and do not test anything."

"I don't usually test things at theatre."

"Good."

I stood.

My right wrist complained when I picked up the crutch.

Not pain.

Just enough fatigue to make me shift the grip.

Hessa saw.

"Light work."

"Tell Rinna."

"I am telling you."

"Less effective."

"Your problem."

This was also fair.

The hall had moved on without me.

It had done this very successfully.

When I came in, Pell was carrying a stool upside down.

"Guild?"

"Yes."

"Good?"

"Paper."

He nodded as though that answered anything.

Maybe it did.

Rinna saw me.

"You came back."

"You said probably."

"I say many things."

"I've noticed."

She looked at my wrist.

"Hessa say anything?"

"Light work."

"House."

"That's light?"

"Compared to scenery."

I could not argue.

I spent the evening taking tickets, moving one stool, answering where
the privy was, and telling two people The Broken Gate was second.

It became third.

I told the next person third.

The performance happened without me.

This felt slightly strange.

Not bad.

Just strange.

I heard laughs through the door.

At one point I heard my Neighbor line delivered by somebody else.

I could not tell who from the front.

The audience laughed.

I objected privately.

Then a man asked whether he could bring a cup inside.

"No."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

He looked at me.

I looked toward Rinna.

She said, "No cups."

I looked back.

"No cups."

"Why?"

"Authority."

Rinna made a sound.

The man left the cup.

At cleanup she paid me one copper.

"House."

"Guild fatigue."

"Not my expense."

"I returned."

"That is why you got paid."

"Excellent system."

"Go home."

Everyone had become Hessa.

Lyssa was not home when I arrived.

I washed my hands and wrist in cool water.

The soap had become smaller again.

This was the problem with useful things.

I did not lift the cup.

There was no reason.

The old note underneath it was now wrong in every temporal sense and
still correct in every instructional one.

When Lyssa came in, she had no hoop.

She had a small wrapped parcel and a smear of something dark near one
thumb.

"Near or far?" she asked before taking off her shoes.

"Near."

She looked at me.

"Near here."

"Better."

"Far was worse."

"How worse?"

"Nothing once. Maybe something once, but a control may have moved too,
so Hessa refused to count it as evidence."

Lyssa put the parcel on the table.

"Near?"

"Twice."

"Both moved?"

"Yes."

"Far?"

"Not established."

She smiled.

"That sounds like Hessa."

"I said near was easier."

"What did she say?"

"Here."

Lyssa laughed.

"Then today."

"Yes."

"In this arrangement."

"Yes."

She laughed harder.

"I don't see what's funny."

"You."

"That's not useful."

"It is to me."

I sat.

My shoulder felt tired now that nobody needed it.

Lyssa unwrapped the parcel.

Inside was a piece of flatbread with onions folded into it.

I looked at her.

"Two?"

"One."

"For us?"

"Obviously."

That word had also become infected.

She tore it in half.

I ate.

After a while she asked, "Again when?"

"I don't know."

"Hessa doesn't know?"

"She probably knows. She hasn't told me."

"What next?"

"Same thing."

Lyssa paused.

"Again?"

"Near and far. Away only."

"No new thing?"

"No."

"Good."

I looked at her.

"Why good?"

"Because the last thing barely moves paper."

This was difficult to dispute.

I ate more flatbread.

Twenty-seven successful supervised minimal draws.

Twenty-three supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Twenty-three supervised external-effect attempts.

Near had worked twice.

Far had not worked cleanly at all.

That was more than I had known this morning.

It was less than I wanted to know.

Hessa would have liked that sentence.

I did not tell her.
