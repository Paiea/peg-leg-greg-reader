TEXT READER · CHAPTER 197

THE PETITIONER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

My wrist was stiff in the morning. Not hurt. Stiff. I rotated it until Lyssa told me to stop because four checks had already established the point.

She finally identified the mysterious narrow dark-blue cloth as sleeve binding. Three days of mystery had produced an edge treatment. I was not disappointed, exactly. I had simply expected more object.

The old Hessa note remained under the cup and I left it there. Lyssa noticed, kissed the side of my head, and told me to go to work before I could turn restraint into an argument.

On the way I bought one piece of fried dough folded around greens. It cost enough to regret for one bite and tasted good enough to end the regret. Financially dangerous.

The board gave me PETITIONER / SET. Marek was still absent, and nobody had marked the space around his missing name as special.

Rinna clarified that I was not Third Man. Today I was the fence petitioner first, then the same man returning with a drainage complaint. Four lines. Teren confirmed the local version, entrance, and count in roughly the time it took Davin to reject a bent hinge pin.

Nessa put me in a short brown coat that smelled faintly of old smoke. Nearby she was still forcing Marek's broader king coat onto Sellen, who regarded every pin as attempted murder. I briefly became responsible for a piece of cord because that was how theatre objects migrated.

Veya and I ran the petitioner once. I arrived furious that a neighbor kept moving a boundary post. She told me to move it back. I returned later because the lane drain was pouring into my yard. She told me the garden was watered and the chair should go inside.

Teren's only useful correction was not to wait for help that the official had no intention of giving. The man's problems were real. The joke was that government remained perfectly calm about them.

I liked the petitioner.

He had problems.

Nobody respected them.

This was familiar.

The rest of the morning was set work. When Davin needed the repaired door held upright, my wrist reminded me about yesterday, so I asked Pell for the heavier side. He took it without requiring an explanation. Davin fitted the new pin, tested the door twice, and dismissed us when it behaved like a door.

At midday Hara arrived with her hair only half pinned and a paper parcel under one arm.

Rinna said, "You're late."

"I was working."

"Here is work."

"I know."

Hara put the parcel on a bench.

Pell reached toward it.

She slapped his hand without looking.

"Not yours."

"What is it?"

"Food."

"What kind?"

"Mine."

I looked at Sellen.

He looked at me.

This was spreading.

Hara unwrapped two little meat pies.

Pell looked wounded.

"You have two."

"I know."

"That means one is extra."

"No."

"Mathematically."

"No."

He went away.

I ate bread and the last piece of white cheese.

Sellen had nothing.

I watched him not eat.

"Where's your food?"

"Didn't bring any."

"Why?"

"I forgot."

"You forgot food?"

"Apparently."

This was the first truly disturbing thing I had learned about him.

I tore off a piece of bread and held it out.

He looked at it.

"Why?"

"Food."

"I know what bread is."

"Good."

He took it.

"You want money?"

"No."

"Then why are you looking at me?"

"I wasn't."

"You were."

I looked away.

Sellen ate the bread.

After a while he said, "Marek would have brought dried apples."

"That sounds like criticism."

"It is."

"When is he back?"

Sellen shrugged.

"After the baby."

"That's not a time."

"It is to Marek."

Nobody seemed worried.

I was not worried.

Marek had gone north to see his sister have a baby.

The world contained enough dangerous things without making childbirth travel into one from a distance.

Rinna came through with a chalk stub.

"House is decent."

"How decent?" Pell asked.

"Decent."

"Numbers."

"No."

"That's not a number."

"Work."

He did.

The hall filled slowly through the afternoon.

Not full.

Not thin.

People came in warm from the street and brought dust with them.

A woman argued with Rinna about whether her boy counted as a full seat if he spent half the show on her lap.

Rinna said yes.

The woman said he was small.

Rinna said the bench did not charge by weight.

The woman paid.

I loved watching professionals.

Before the first piece, I moved a small wooden writing box from beside the petition table because it was in the path where I entered.

Nessa saw me.

"Put that back."

I stopped.

"It's in my way."

"It's for Widow."

"That's after Petition."

"Yes."

"Why is it here?"

"Because it needs to be."

"For a later piece?"

"Yes."

"Now?"

"Yes."

I put it back.

Then I entered from six inches farther left.

This solved everything.

The King went first.

Sellen wore Marek's coat.

From backstage I could see the folded cuffs.

From the house, nobody cared.

He did not play Marek's king.

I had expected him to, without realizing I expected it.

Marek's king carried irritation like a personal injury. Sellen's seemed to assume everyone else had become stupid while he was asleep.

The audience liked him.

Hara got the first big laugh.

Sellen got the second.

EvIn dropped a rolled petition, kicked it by accident, then incorporated the kick into crossing the room.

Nobody died.

I watched until Pell needed the space.

"Move."

I moved.

The Petition came next.

My coat sat wrong at the neck.

I fixed it.

Nessa fixed what I had fixed.

Then I went on.

"My neighbor moved the boundary post two feet into my garden."

Veya barely looked up.

"Then move it back."

"I did."

"Why are you here?"

"He moved it again."

"Move it again."

I did not wait for help.

The audience laughed.

Not a large laugh.

Enough.

I left.

Backstage, Sellen was waiting for his next entrance.

"Your government is terrible."

"Not mine."

"You're in it."

"That is not how government works."

"That explains a lot."

He went on.

Later I returned.

"The lane drain is pouring into my yard."

Veya said, "Then your garden is watered."

"It has my chair in it."

"Move the chair."

"Where?"

"Inside."

A woman near the middle laughed before anyone else did.

Then several people joined.

I left again.

Four lines.

One coat.

No disaster.

I could have done more.

This was not a complaint.

It was simply true.

Then I had to get the coat off because Nessa wanted it for a different man in Widow.

"Already?"

"Yes."

"I just became attached."

"Don't."

She took it.

The Widow's Room had the writing box I had tried to move.

It turned out to contain three folded letters and a piece of chalk.

None of them mattered to my entrance.

I found this insulting.

During the scene, Veya got the room quiet without asking it.

Nessa sat beside the painted doorway and said almost nothing for long enough that I forgot I was waiting to carry a bench afterward.

Then Hara crossed the false room, opened the writing box, and found the wrong letter.

The audience made one small sound together.

That was the strongest moment of the afternoon.

It had nothing to do with me.

Good.

When the scene ended, Pell said, "Bench."

I remembered my job.

I grabbed one end.

My wrist tightened.

I stopped.

Pell looked at me.

"Other hand?"

"Other person."

He called EvIn.

EvIn took my side.

Nobody gave a speech about responsibility.

I carried the lighter stool instead.

This was irritatingly sensible.

The short piece nearly failed because the spoon was missing again.

This time it was not in the Letter Box cup.

It was in Hara's paper parcel.

She stared at it.

Pell said, "You stole your own spoon."

"I did not."

"It is in your food."

"It wasn't."

"It is now."

Hara looked at Nessa.

Nessa said, "Use it."

Hara wiped meat grease off the spoon with a cloth.

"Disgusting."

Pell said, "The audience won't know."

"I will."

"Then act."

Hara threw the cloth at him.

The short happened.

The audience laughed.

At cleanup I found the wooden writing box on the wrong shelf.

I picked it up.

Then I stopped.

"Nessa."

"What?"

"Where?"

She pointed without looking.

I put it there.

This was safer than memory.

Rinna paid me one copper.

"Petitioner. Set."

"Also restraint."

"No."

"I didn't move a box."

"Congratulations."

"I also didn't lift a bench."

"You're negotiating for things you didn't do now?"

"I am expanding the field."

"One copper."

"Fine."

She handed it over.

I pocketed it.

Outside, the afternoon heat had broken.

Not into coolness.

Carrow did not believe in gifts.

But the sun had moved behind the roofs and the stone no longer reflected it directly into my face.

I stopped at a food stall and bought two little fried cakes with chopped onion and something green inside.

The woman wrapped them in paper.

"Two?" she asked.

"Yes."

"For you?"

"One is."

She looked at me.

"Then two."

This was unnecessary.

I carried them home.

Lyssa was not there.

I put the food on the table.

The cup remained where it had been.

Still no new note.

I washed.

The wrist felt better after cool water.

The shoulder was already ordinary again.

I sat for a while.

Not because I was practicing peace.

Because sitting existed.

Lyssa came in after dark enough that the window had become a black square.

She put her bag down.

"Food?"

"One is yours."

She looked at the two wrapped cakes.

"Which?"

"I don't know."

"Then how do you know?"

"I bought two."

She took the larger one.

"That was mine."

"You didn't know."

"I know now."

We ate.

After a few bites she asked, "Theatre?"

"Petitioner."

"Again?"

"Different one."

"What did you want?"

"A fence."

"What did you get?"

"Water."

She waited.

"I had two complaints."

"Did anyone fix them?"

"No."

"Sounds official."

"It was."

I told her Marek's coat did not fit Sellen.

I told her Nessa made it fit anyway.

I told her Hara had stolen a spoon from herself.

I did not tell her about the writing box until she asked why I was smiling at nothing.

"I moved a box."

"Why?"

"It was in my way."

"Was it yours?"

"No."

"Did you know where it belonged?"

"No."

"Good system."

"I put it back."

"Excellent."

"Then later I asked where it belonged instead of deciding."

Lyssa nodded solemnly.

"Growth."

"Don't."

She ate.

After a while she looked at my wrist.

"Better?"

"Yes."

"Actually?"

"Yes."

I rotated it once.

She looked at me.

I stopped.

"Still no paper?"

"No."

"From Hessa?"

"No."

"I meant you."

I looked at her.

"No."

"Good."

There it was again.

I ate the last bite of my cake.

Twenty-seven draws.

Twenty-three shapings.

Twenty-three external attempts.

Near had worked twice yesterday.

Far had not worked cleanly.

Today I had moved no paper.

I had moved a stool, a coat, part of a door, one petition, one writing box incorrectly, and one writing box correctly.

None of those counted.

This was probably healthy.
