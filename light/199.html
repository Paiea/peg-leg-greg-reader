LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 199

THE ATTENDANT

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was still home when I woke, which meant I knew exactly what time I
was supposed to leave and had no excuse to leave then.

This was a problem.

She was sitting at the table with one of the dark sleeves across her
lap, picking out three stitches she had apparently decided were wrong
after already finishing them.

I ate bread.

She picked out a stitch.

I drank water.

She picked out another.

I looked at the door.

"Third bell," she said.

"I know."

"You looked."

"I am allowed to look at doors."

"Not like that."

"I have a schedule."

"I know."

This was becoming irritating.

The Hessa note remained under the cup. Tomorrow. After midday. Repeat.
Near and far. Away only. Same restrictions.

I did not touch it.

Lyssa pulled out the third stitch, inspected the edge, then began
putting the stitches back in what looked to me like almost exactly the
same place.

"What was wrong with them?"

"They pulled."

"They were holding cloth."

"Wrong kind."

I looked at the sleeve.

It looked like a sleeve.

Lyssa looked at me.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say it looks good."

"No, you weren't."

"I could have."

She bit the thread and set the sleeve aside.

"There is a bowl at Marra's."

"Of course there is."

"Bring it back."

"Why is our bowl at Marra's?"

"Because I brought her soup."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

"You brought someone soup and didn't tell me?"

Lyssa stood.

"I am sorry. Next time I will submit household food movements to the
theatre slate."

"That would help."

She kissed my cheek on the way past.

"Marra. Bowl. Then whatever you do until third bell."

"What do I do until third bell?"

"Anything."

This was not useful.

She left before I did.

I remained at the table.

The room had become offensively quiet.

There were things I could do. I swept. This took less time than expected
because our room was small and most of the dirt had concentrated itself
near the door. I rinsed the basin. I folded the blanket badly, unfolded
it, then folded it in a way Lyssa would probably still redo later.

I checked the leather wrap on my crutch.

Not magically.

It was just leather.

The seam still sat where it should. The lower ferrule had picked up a
thin line of pale grit from the street. I wiped it off with a damp rag.

That still did not make it third bell.

I went to Marra's.

The morning streets had settled into the period after people had decided
where they were going but before they regretted it. Bakers had stopped
shouting about the first loaves. Shop doors stood open. A woman with six
clay cups balanced on a board moved through a knot of men without
slowing down. Two boys were rolling a hoop along the gutter and arguing
about whether touching it with a hand counted if nobody saw.

I knew the route to Marra's without thinking about it now.

This was probably how cities happened to people.

Jessa was at the front table when I came in. She had a strip of red
cloth wrapped around one wrist and four pins between her lips.

I stopped inside the door.

"Bowl."

She pointed with her chin.

There were three bowls on a shelf.

I looked at them.

Jessa removed the pins from her mouth.

"Blue rim."

"That eliminates none of them."

"One has a blue rim."

"All three have blue."

"No."

I looked again.

One was mostly white with a thin blue line. One had little blue leaves.
One had a dark band that I considered blue and Jessa would probably call
storm-gray or dying-fish or some other hostile color.

I pointed to the white bowl.

"That one?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"You've improved."

"At bowls?"

"At asking."

"I hate this city."

Marra called from the back, "No, you don't."

I took the bowl.

"I am being monitored across rooms now."

"You're loud," Jessa said.

There was no reason to remain.

I remained for almost a minute because a customer came in carrying a
green overdress with one sleeve partly detached, and Jessa immediately
stopped talking to me. The woman knew her. Jessa knew the dress. Marra
called a price from the back without coming out.

I had become irrelevant.

This was convenient.

I left with the bowl.

At home I put it on the table.

Then I discovered that completing an errand did not cause time to
accelerate.

I considered going to the hall.

Third bell.

I did not go to the hall.

Instead I bought salt.

We did not desperately need salt, but we had little enough that buying
more could be defended in court.

The seller gave me a small folded paper packet and warned me not to get
it wet.

"I had planned to soak it."

"Then don't."

"Good advice."

I bought a cheap handful of greens from another stall because the bread
at home had begun looking lonely.

That still left time.

I walked past the theatre once.

This did not count.

I stayed on the opposite side of the street.

The front doors were open. Pell came out carrying a rolled hanging over
one shoulder.

He saw me.

I kept walking.

"Greg."

"No."

"What?"

"Third bell."

Pell looked up at the sky as if checking whether I had become religious.

"All right."

"Rinna scheduled me."

"Congratulations."

"It isn't a promotion."

"I didn't say it was."

"Good."

He shifted the hanging.

"Can you hold this?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Third bell."

Pell stared at me.

I stared back.

Then he laughed so hard the hanging slipped down his shoulder.

I kept walking.

This was discipline.

It felt terrible.

By the time third bell was close enough that I could hear the first
strike beginning from two streets over, I had returned the bowl, bought
salt, bought greens, swept the room, cleaned my crutch, walked past my
workplace, refused work, and eaten the heel of yesterday's bread.

I arrived at the hall during the last bell.

Rinna was at the ticket table.

She looked up.

"On time."

"I have a schedule."

"Don't become proud."

Then she handed me a stack of tickets.

That was it.

Apparently the schedule had already become boring.

"Front?"

"Until people come."

"People are why front exists."

"Until more people come."

I sat on the stool behind the little table and counted the tickets.

Forty-three.

I counted again.

Forty-two.

I counted a third time.

Forty-three.

This was concerning.

Rinna came back with a small coin tray.

"How many?"

"Forty-three."

"You sure?"

"No."

She took the stack, split it once, counted faster than I had, and said,
"Forty-three."

"Good."

"You've been here four minutes."

"I have already produced uncertainty."

"Set after."

She put the tickets down.

I looked toward the board.

GREG: FRONT / SET / PORTER

I knew the Porter.

The Borrowed House.

Six lines.

Chest.

Wrong house.

Procedural integrity.

I pointed.

"Porter?"

"Yes."

"Today?"

Rinna looked at me.

"I have to."

"Fine. Today."

"Same local version?"

"Ask Teren."

"Chest?"

"Ask Teren."

"Coat?"

"Nessa."

"Entrance?"

"Greg."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one you already know."

I did know.

This was annoying in a different direction.

Teren confirmed the local version without making me perform the entire
interrogation.

"Same entrance. Same chest. Same six."

"Any cuts?"

"No."

"Any chickens?"

He looked at me.

"No chickens."

"Good."

"Why?"

"Administrative history."

He walked away.

The hall was already in motion around me. Davin and Jori were shifting a
narrow painted wall piece that had nothing to do with Borrowed House.
Nessa had three costumes over one arm and was arguing with Hara about
whether a pale scarf could survive another wash. Sellen, still
temporarily king, stood beside the board eating something from a cup.

Marek's name remained absent.

Nobody had written a note beside it.

North was simply where Marek was.

Nessa threw the Porter's coat at me.

I caught it against my chest.

"Later," she said.

"I know."

She stopped.

"Good."

This was the problem with becoming competent. People stopped explaining
things and then acted pleased when you were not surprised.

I hung the coat where it belonged.

Then I moved two benches.

Then Rinna called me back to front because a family had arrived early.

Then Pell called me to hold the bottom of a rolled backdrop while he
retied the top cord.

Then Rinna called me back because Pell was apparently not authorized to
steal front staff.

"Staff?" I said.

"You."

"I have become plural."

"Sell seats."

The family wanted four.

The father wanted to know whether the little boy needed one.

Rinna, from behind me, said, "Bench still doesn't charge by weight."

The father looked confused.

"I was going to say that."

"No, you weren't."

"I was thinking it."

"That costs nothing."

They bought four.

I marked the little board incorrectly.

Rinna wiped out my mark.

"What did I do?"

"You put them in the second row."

"They asked for the second row."

"That mark is third."

I looked at the marks.

They were tiny scratches beside tiny numbers.

"Your system is bad."

"My system works when I use it."

She moved the family into the second row with one chalk stroke.

I had been employed for less than an hour and had already seated four
people in the wrong place without physically moving them.

This seemed efficient.

By the time the house began filling properly, Rinna took over front and
sent me inside.

The Borrowed House needed very little rehearsal.

I put on the Porter coat. Sellen passed behind me carrying a cup.

"Chest left," he said.

"I know."

"Good."

He kept walking.

I looked after him.

That had apparently been the rehearsal.

The chest was already in the left wing. It was not the heavy one. The
heavy one had metal corners and made my body negotiate with it. This one
was mostly painted boards and confidence.

I set my crutches where I had set them before, close enough to retrieve
after the visible carry. Sellen came back.

"You still know it?"

"Yes."

"Excellent."

"Are you Teren now?"

"No."

"Good."

"Why?"

"I don't know where he is."

Sellen nodded.

"Then he's Teren."

Teren was, in fact, outside talking to a man with a bundle of rolled
papers.

I never learned why.

Food happened in pieces.

I had brought the greens but not the salt, because even I understood
that loose salt was not lunch. Hara had bread and a little soft cheese.
Pell had something wrapped in cloth that turned out to be two cold
potatoes.

Sellen looked into the cloth.

"Why?"

"Food."

"Why cold?"

"They were hot yesterday."

"That is not how today works."

Pell bit one.

"It is now."

EvIn arrived, took one look at the potatoes, and produced a small
sausage from his pocket.

Everyone objected to this for different reasons.

"Why is that in your pocket?" Hara asked.

"Wrapped."

"That doesn't answer her," I said.

"It answers enough."

Sellen pointed at me.

"Government."

I ate my greens.

The first performance began with a decent house and no particular
weather inside it except heat.

The audience shifted on benches. Someone had brought a baby that made
one soft complaint during the opening and then apparently decided the
play was acceptable. A man near the back sneezed three times in exactly
the same place in three different scenes.

I spent the first piece moving things.

The Porter came in Borrowed House.

I put on the coat.

Nessa pulled the back straight as I passed.

"Was it wrong?"

"Now it isn't."

I took my position.

The chest came into my hands.

For the visible part of the entrance, I carried it without the crutches,
the same solution we had already worked out. Four steps. Set it down.
Recover the crutch. Continue.

Nobody applauded the logistics.

This was ideal.

"I was told to bring this here," I said.

Veya looked at the chest.

"Who told you?"

"A man."

"What man?"

"The one who paid me."

"Did he have a name?"

"Probably."

A laugh.

Not a large one.

I kept going.

The role lived in my body enough now that I did not have to think about
where the chest went. I only had to remember that the Porter had
absolute faith in a delivery system nobody else understood.

When Veya told me it was the wrong house, I said, "Then the house is
wrong."

That line got less than it had the first time I played it.

Fine.

Later, "I don't know" got more.

Also fine.

The scene belonged to Veya by the end. She had a stretch where three
people contradicted her at once, and she simply waited until all of them
finished before asking, "Would anyone like to be correct?"

The house laughed harder than it had at anything I said.

I was backstage by then, taking off the coat.

Nessa held out her hand.

I gave it to her.

"Good."

"Me or the coat?"

"The coat."

"Of course."

She was already gone.

Between pieces, I made my ordinary mistake.

Rinna told me to bring the short blue runner from rear storage.

There were two blue runners.

One was short.

One was shorter.

I knew this kind of problem.

I should have asked.

Instead I looked at them and decided the shorter one was obviously what
she meant because the other one was not, in my opinion, short enough to
deserve the name.

I carried it out.

Rinna looked at it.

"No."

I stood there.

"This is short."

"Other one."

"This is shorter."

"Yes."

"Then your words are wrong."

"My words are fine. That is the short blue runner. The other is the
little blue runner."

I looked toward the storage room.

"You have created a hierarchy after the fact."

"Bring the other one."

I brought the other one.

It fit the front of the low platform exactly.

The shorter runner would have left both corners bare.

I hated this.

Pell passed carrying a lamp.

"Wrong blue?"

"Wrong short."

He nodded as if this was a known disease.

The second piece went on.

Then Widow.

Then a short break before the evening bill.

Not a supper break. A sitting-on-crates-and-eating-whatever-remained
break.

Rinna sold enough seats for the evening that she opened another row.
Nobody celebrated. Jori moved the rope. Pell carried a bench. I carried
the other end because my wrist was no longer interesting and the bench
was not absurdly heavy.

My wrist remained boring.

Good.

Hara had to leave after the first evening piece.

She changed clothes quickly, pinned her hair differently, and left
through the rear door with half a piece of bread in her mouth.

"Work?" I asked.

She nodded.

"Where?"

She pointed at the bread with one hand as if this prevented speech.

Then she was gone.

I did not know where.

The bill adjusted.

Nessa took one of Hara's smaller parts in the short piece. A later piece
was dropped. EvIn complained because he had already changed stockings.

Rinna said, "Change back."

"I just changed."

"Then you're practiced."

The evening house was a little louder than the afternoon one. Not rowdy.
Just fed.

Sellen's King got the best moment.

Not a line.

A chair.

The king's chair had been moved a little too far back during the change.
When Sellen sat, one rear leg landed on the edge of the low platform
instead of fully on it.

The chair tipped.

Sellen caught himself with one hand on the arm, froze, and looked slowly
at EvIn.

EvIn had not touched the chair.

The audience already knew this.

Sellen said, "Treason."

The room broke.

EvIn stared at him.

"I was standing here."

"Exactly where a clever man would stand."

Another laugh.

Pell, beside me backstage, whispered, "Chair did it."

"I know."

"Should we arrest it?"

"After."

The scene continued.

The chair remained innocent.

I had no role in The King that evening. Guard was not on the bill for
me. Someone else handled the entrance.

This did not bother me as much as I thought it might.

I was carrying a basket.

The last piece ended later than the afternoon one but earlier than
anyone had feared. The dropped piece bought time. People left in a slow
scrape of benches and shoes. A child had lost a ribbon. An old man had
left a folded paper on a seat. Rinna checked it, decided it was nothing
important, and put it on the front table in case he came back.

Cleanup began.

I returned the short blue runner.

The correct short blue runner.

Before putting it away, I asked Pell, "Where?"

He pointed to the middle shelf.

I put it there.

"The little one?"

"Bottom."

"Good."

"I thought you knew."

"I knew incorrectly."

"That's worse."

"Yes."

We folded the last cloth.

Rinna paid me two copper.

I looked at the coins.

Then at her.

"Two?"

"Two houses."

"I was also front."

"Yes."

"And set."

"Yes."

"And Porter."

"Yes."

"And runner specialist."

"No."

"I handled two different lengths."

"One of them wrong."

"That increased labor."

She held out her hand.

I closed mine.

"Two is correct."

"Thought so."

I had been paid more because I had worked more.

This was suspiciously straightforward.

Then I remembered.

"Guild tomorrow. After midday."

Rinna stopped writing on the slate.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

She looked at me.

"We've discussed the quality of this information."

"I am improving. I know when it starts."

"Morning, then."

I blinked.

"What?"

"Come morning. Set and front. Leave before midday."

"But my schedule says third bell."

"Tomorrow it doesn't."

"You made it yesterday."

"And now it changed."

"This institution has no respect for law."

"Morning."

"What if Hessa keeps me?"

"Then don't come back."

"What if she doesn't?"

"Come back if you can still work."

"That is very flexible."

"It means I don't know either."

I considered this.

"Finally, honesty."

Rinna pointed at the door.

I left before she could alter my pay.

Outside, Carrow had cooled enough that the stone no longer threw the
day's heat directly into my face.

I bought bread.

I bought a small scoop of cooked beans because two copper had made me
temporarily reckless.

I did not spend the second copper.

This was restraint.

I carried the food home.

Lyssa was already there.

She had one shoe off and the other still on, which suggested she had
arrived recently or had become only partly committed to footwear.

"You're late," she said.

"Two houses."

"Two?"

"Apparently I am a professional."

"How much?"

"Two copper."

"Then yes."

I put the bread and beans on the table.

"One is still alive."

"Copper?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"I bought beans."

"Still good."

"You're using that word too freely."

She took the bowl.

We ate.

I told her about the wrong short blue runner.

She said Rinna's naming system sounded perfectly clear.

I accused her of professional bias.

She said there was no profession in which short and little meant the
same thing.

I told her theatre had proved otherwise.

She asked whether I acted.

"Porter."

"Again?"

"Yes."

"Good?"

"I delivered the wrong chest to the wrong house exactly as required."

"So yes."

"Competently incorrect."

"Your specialty."

I ignored this.

After we ate, Lyssa began sorting the three wooden buttons from
yesterday against a strip of dark cloth. She rejected one, accepted two,
then rejected one of the accepted ones after holding it beside the cloth
again.

I watched.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Hessa?"

"Yes."

"After midday."

"And theatre?"

"Morning."

She looked up.

"Morning?"

"Schedule changed."

"Already?"

"Rinna says this is what schedules do."

Lyssa smiled.

"I like her."

"You barely know her."

"I know enough."

I looked at the cup.

The note was still under it.

I did not move the cup.

Tomorrow, near and far again.

That thought sat in the room for a moment.

I was curious.

I was also tired.

Both could exist without fighting.

Lyssa pushed one wooden button toward the rejected side.

"Paper tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Near and far?"

"Yes."

"Away?"

"Only."

She nodded.

Then she went back to the buttons.

That was all.

Twenty-seven draws.

Twenty-three shapings.

Twenty-three external attempts.

Nothing had changed today.

I had obeyed third bell.

I had worked two houses.

I had carried the wrong short blue runner and the correct one.

I had earned two copper and managed not to eat both of them.

Tomorrow I would go to the hall in the morning because my new schedule
had already changed, then leave for the Guild after midday because
another schedule said so.

I had wanted a life with structure.

Apparently structure moved.
