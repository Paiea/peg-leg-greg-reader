TEXT READER · CHAPTER 200

THE RESPONDER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was gone before I woke.

This solved one problem and created another.

I did not have to explain why I was looking at the cup.

There was nobody there to stop me from looking at it.

I ate bread instead.

The note remained under the edge exactly where it had been.

AFTER MIDDAY.

REPEAT.

NEAR / FAR.

AWAY ONLY.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

I knew the words. Reading them again would not improve them.

I read them again anyway.

Administrative.

Then I put the cup back down.

The theatre wanted me in the morning.

The Guild wanted me after midday.

For the first time in my life, two institutions were competing for
portions of a day I had previously been willing to sell to almost anyone
with food.

This felt like progress.

It also meant I had to leave.

The hall was already open when I arrived. Rinna had the front table
turned sideways and was using it to support a stack of folded handbills
while Jori argued with one of the front shutters.

"You said morning," I told her.

Rinna looked up.

"It is."

"Good."

"Why?"

"I wanted confirmation."

"You came here to confirm morning?"

"I came here to work."

"Then do that."

She pointed at the rear.

The short blue runner was rolled beside the little blue runner.

I stopped.

Rinna saw me stop.

"Short."

"Short or little?"

She pointed at the larger roll.

I picked it up.

"Your system remains bad."

"It worked."

"Because I interrogated it."

"Platform."

I carried it to the platform.

That was my morning.

Not entirely.

There were benches to straighten, a front rope to move, two lamp tins to
count, and a chest from Borrowed House that had somehow acquired a
cracked handle between yesterday and today. Davin looked at it, pressed
the split wood once with his thumb, and said, "Other chest."

Pell said, "We used the other chest yesterday."

"Use it again."

"Problem solved," I said.

Davin looked at me.

"You didn't solve it."

"I witnessed the solution."

"Move."

I moved.

Marek remained north.

His absence had become part of the board. Sellen's name sat where
Marek's would have sat in The King, and nobody paused over it anymore.

Sellen came in carrying Marek's coat over one arm and his own breakfast
in the other hand.

"You have food," I said.

"I learned."

"From yesterday?"

"From hunger."

"Stronger teacher."

He held out the coat.

"Hook."

I took it.

There was a hook beside the costume rail.

I hung the coat.

Sellen ate.

No one thanked me.

This was increasingly how employment worked.

Teren arrived, changed two marks on the board, crossed out one short
piece, then disappeared into the rear with Nessa.

I had no acting role that morning.

This was useful because I was leaving before midday and because Rinna
had decided I belonged at front for the first part of the house setup.

There was no performance before I left. Only preparation for one that
would happen without me if Hessa kept me.

That felt stranger than it should have.

At one point Pell asked whether I was coming back.

"If I'm useful."

"Are you?"

"Rinna's standard."

Pell considered this.

"Probably not."

"Then I can go home."

Rinna, from the front, said, "I can hear you."

"That keeps happening," I said.

Before midday I ate a heel of bread, drank water, and told Rinna I was
leaving.

She did not look up from the ticket board.

"Guild?"

"Yes."

"Go."

"Do you need me after?"

"If you're useful."

"We've established uncertainty."

"Then come back and I'll decide."

This was an excellent system because nobody had to know anything.

I left.

The walk to the Guild was familiar enough that I no longer spent the
whole route thinking about the Guild.

A fish seller had moved his table farther into the lane and was being
punished for it by every cart. A woman carried a cage with nothing in
it. Two men were repairing a drain cover while a third man stood over
them explaining drains.

I passed a baker.

I considered buying something.

I had bread in me already.

This was evidence of maturity.

I kept my copper.

The Guild front room smelled like old paper, hot stone, and whatever
sharp oil they used on prepared metal.

A clerk I had seen before was arguing with a man holding five lengths of
cord.

"I brought six."

"You have five."

"I brought six."

"You are holding five."

"I know what I brought."

The clerk looked at the cords.

"Then one left."

The man looked behind himself as if the sixth cord might have become
ashamed.

I went around them.

Hessa was not waiting for me.

Good.

She was in the smaller room with the broad table, writing on a sheet
while a shallow wooden case sat open beside her. The long pale board was
already on the table.

She looked up.

"Eat?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Before I left the hall."

"Water?"

"Yes."

"Wrist?"

"Boring."

"Show me."

I rotated it.

Once.

Hessa watched.

"Again."

I did.

No pain. A little ordinary pull at the far edge of the motion, maybe.
Less than yesterday. Less than I would have noticed if she had not
asked.

"Shoulder?"

"Also boring."

"What did you lift?"

"Runner. Lamp tins. One end of a bench. A coat."

"Chest?"

"No. Davin rejected it."

"Why?"

"Cracked handle."

She nodded as if Davin had personally preserved the experiment.

"Sit."

The chair was not where I remembered it.

I noticed because there was a chalk line on the floor and the front legs
were not on it.

Hessa moved it until they were.

"That line new?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because I was about to accuse my memory."

"Don't use memory when you can use a mark."

I sat.

"That sounds like theatre."

"No."

"All right."

My crutches went against the wall.

Good foot flat.

No bracing against the table.

The board ran away from me across the table. Hessa had marked two
positions on it in chalk. One sat close enough that I could have reached
the paper fin easily if the glass were not there. The other sat much
farther across the table, beyond comfortable reach even if I leaned.

Near and far.

Both guides ran in the same direction.

Away from me.

Beside each target position was a matched control guide.

Hessa set four folded paper fins on a clean cloth.

"Same paper?"

"Same stock."

"Same folds?"

"As close as I can make them."

"You made them?"

"Yes."

"Then better than mine."

"Everything is better than yours if you leave it alone."

I looked at her.

She was already checking the glass.

"That was unnecessary."

"It was accurate."

She set the shield over the board.

I could see the tiny fins through it.

They looked stupid.

I had moved paper before.

Not much.

Enough that Hessa had stopped saying we did not know whether anything
happened at all.

Today we were trying to discover whether the paper cared where it was.

This was an unreasonable amount of attention for something that weighed
less than a bad excuse.

Hessa sat opposite me.

"Baseline first."

I nodded.

She watched my hands.

I let my breathing settle.

The draw was familiar now, which did not make it easy. Familiar was
different. I knew the place where I tended to reach too hard. I knew the
inward gathering. I knew the irritating tendency for the wrist edge to
feel crowded before the rest of the shape had decided what it was doing.

I did not chase any of it.

The draw settled.

Hessa watched for another breath.

"Enough."

I released.

She wrote.

"Twenty-eight," I said.

"Yes."

Successful supervised minimal draws.

Twenty-eight.

The number felt much larger than the thing.

"Shape."

I started again.

The same gathering.

The same wrist-edge crowding.

I held the deliberate shape badly at first, relaxed before it broke, and
tried again within the same supervised attempt.

It steadied.

Not beautiful.

Not useful.

Deliberate.

Hessa counted under her breath.

"Enough."

I let it go.

"Twenty-four."

She wrote that too.

Then she moved the first target into the far position.

Of course she started far.

I had wanted near.

That was probably why she started far.

"You chose that because I wanted the other one."

"I chose it before you arrived."

"Then you anticipated my complaint."

"That was easy."

She checked the target fin.

Then the control.

Both upright.

Both still.

Glass down.

"Requested direction?"

"Away from me."

"Distance?"

"Far."

"Do not change the question."

"I know."

"Say it."

"Away. Far."

Hessa sat back.

I looked at the little fin.

The far position was not dramatically far. It was the other side of a
table.

That made the whole thing worse.

If someone had told old me I would someday sit in a magical guild trying
to move a piece of paper across a table, I would have expected more
fire.

I gathered.

Shaped.

The wrist edge crowded.

I left it alone.

Then I made the request.

Away.

Nothing happened.

The target stayed upright.

The control stayed upright.

I held until Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped.

The silence afterward felt louder than movement would have.

Hessa leaned forward and checked both fins without touching the glass.

"Nothing visible."

"I noticed."

"Twenty-four."

External attempts.

The number had caught the others.

"Far," she said.

"No observed response."

She wrote.

I wanted to argue with the paper.

There was nothing useful to say.

Hessa lifted the glass, removed both fins, and replaced them with the
second pair.

This time she set the target in the near guide.

My attention sharpened before I wanted it to.

Hessa noticed.

"Same effort."

"I know."

"Your face doesn't."

"My face is not part of the apparatus."

"Your body is."

I relaxed my jaw.

"Better?"

"No."

"Good."

Glass down.

Target near.

Control beside it.

"Distance?"

"Near."

"Direction?"

"Away."

I drew.

The familiar shape came with less fighting than the first external
attempt. Not stronger. Cleaner, maybe. Or I wanted it cleaner because
the fin was close.

That was exactly the problem.

I held.

Requested away.

The paper fin moved.

Barely.

Its lower edge slipped along the guide less than the width of my
smallest fingernail. The top leaned, corrected, and stayed in the new
position.

The control did not move.

I stopped breathing for a second.

Hessa did not.

"Stop."

I released.

She checked.

Then she lifted the glass and bent close to the guide.

"Movement away," she said.

"Yes."

"Control unchanged."

"Yes."

"Small."

"Yes."

"Clean."

I looked at her.

She wrote.

"Twenty-five."

External attempt twenty-five.

I smiled.

Hessa saw it.

"You have moved paper before."

"Not today."

"Now you have."

"Near."

"Here."

I stopped smiling slightly.

"Near here."

"Better."

I hated improvement in vocabulary.

Hessa removed the fins.

"Water."

"I am fine."

"That was not a question."

I drank.

She did not immediately set the next pair.

Instead she checked my wrist, had me open and close my hand, then
pressed two fingers lightly along the forearm where I had tightened
after the last session.

"Anything?"

"No."

"Shoulder?"

"No."

"Good."

There it was again.

Nothing hurt and everyone was delighted.

She went back to the board.

The third target went far.

I watched her set it.

"Again?"

"That is what repeat means."

"I thought repeat meant doing the part I liked."

"No."

Glass down.

Far.

Away.

I gathered.

The shape came slower.

I could feel myself wanting to make more of it because the first far
attempt had done nothing.

That was chasing.

I knew it before Hessa said anything.

I backed off.

"Better," she said.

I made the request.

For a moment I thought the paper twitched.

Not moved.

Twitched.

The upper corner changed against the background line, or I thought it
did. The lower edge remained where it had been.

I held.

Nothing else happened.

"Stop."

I released.

Hessa stayed still.

"Did you see that?"

"Maybe."

"That is a terrible answer."

"It is the correct answer."

She moved around the table.

The target fin leaned a fraction differently than I remembered.

The control looked exactly the same.

Hessa checked the guide, then the paper, then the chalk mark.

"Possible displacement."

"Away?"

"Not cleanly."

"It moved."

"Maybe."

"I saw it."

"You saw something."

"That sounds intentionally worse."

"It is intentionally narrower."

She wrote.

I tried to read upside down.

She covered the line with her hand.

"Rude."

"Rest."

External attempt twenty-six.

Far.

Ambiguous.

Not cleanly away.

That was not nothing.

It was also not useful enough to become something.

I drank again.

Outside the room, somebody dropped metal.

There was a sharp clatter, a curse, then another voice saying, "Don't
pick that up."

Hessa did not react.

The Guild continued existing around us.

A clerk passed the open doorway carrying three narrow wooden cases.
Somewhere farther down the corridor, the man with the cords was still
arguing. I heard "six" again.

This comforted me.

Eventually Hessa put the fourth pair into place.

Near.

I looked at it.

She looked at me.

"Don't."

"I didn't do anything."

"You were about to become hopeful."

"I am allowed."

"After."

This was unfair.

Glass down.

Near target.

Near control.

Both still.

"Direction?"

"Away."

"Distance?"

"Near."

I settled.

The first draw had been clean enough.

The shaping was tired now, but not collapsing. The wrist edge crowded. I
let it. The center held.

I asked for almost nothing.

Away.

The fin stayed still.

One breath.

Two.

Then the bottom edge gave a tiny slip.

Less than the first near movement.

Still away.

The control remained exactly where it was.

My whole body wanted to lean forward.

I did not.

Hessa said, "Stop."

I stopped.

She checked the glass.

The guide.

The control.

The target.

Then she sat down and wrote.

I waited.

She kept writing.

"That is too many words."

"No."

"How many?"

"Enough."

"Did it count?"

"Yes."

I exhaled.

External attempt twenty-seven.

Near.

Movement away.

Control unchanged.

Hessa finished the line and turned the sheet toward herself rather than
me.

"Read it."

"I can't."

"Then move your chair."

"My chair is marked."

"The test is over."

I moved the chair.

This felt illegal.

I went around the table and read over her shoulder.

AWAY RESPONSE REPEATED AT NEAR POSITION IN SECOND SESSION.

FAR RESPONSE NOT REPEATED CLEANLY.

NEAR RESPONSE MORE RELIABLE THAN FAR ACROSS CURRENT SESSIONS.

DISTANCE DEPENDENCE SUPPORTED IN CURRENT APPARATUS.

NO RANGE ESTABLISHED.

NOT GENERALIZED.

I read it twice.

"Distance dependence supported."

"Yes."

"So near is easier."

"In this apparatus, under these conditions, near has produced a response
more reliably than far."

"That is a very long way to say near is easier."

"It is a shorter way to avoid saying something we have not shown."

"Far doesn't work."

Hessa looked at me.

I corrected myself.

"Far has not worked reliably."

"Good."

"I resent you."

"No."

"You don't know."

"I have evidence."

I looked back at the paper.

Twenty-eight successful supervised minimal draws.

Twenty-four supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Twenty-seven supervised external-effect attempts.

Two sessions now where near had behaved better than far.

The magic had not become larger.

Nothing had become useful.

The far paper might have moved a little. The near paper had definitely
moved a little.

This was, somehow, exciting.

"What next?"

Hessa pulled another sheet toward herself.

"Not today."

"I know."

"You asked."

"I can ask without doing it."

"Good."

She drew three small marks in a line.

One close.

One far.

One between them.

I looked at the middle mark.

"Middle."

"Intermediate."

"Middle."

"Fine."

"Next time?"

"Possibly."

"That is not a date."

"No."

I waited.

She wrote:

NEXT QUESTION: INTERMEDIATE POSITION. AWAY ONLY. SAME APPARATUS IF
POSSIBLE. NO OTHER CHANGE.

Then below it:

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

I pointed.

"Still?"

"Yes."

"We repeated it."

"Yes."

"And got the same pattern."

"Broadly."

"So I have earned what?"

"Better evidence."

"I cannot eat that."

"You have theatre."

"That pays in copper."

"Then eat copper."

"That seems medically unsound."

"It is."

She gathered the fins.

"Do not test at home."

"I know."

"Do not test at the theatre."

"I know."

"Not with paper."

"I know."

"Not with anything else because you think it is technically not paper."

"I know."

"No coins."

"I know."

"No thread."

"I know."

"No larger objects."

"I know."

"No practice."

"I know."

She looked at me.

"I know," I said again.

"Good."

I hated that word.

She checked my wrist once more before letting me stand.

Still boring.

My forearm felt worked, not hurt. The distinction mattered enough that I
did not complain.

When I left the Guild, the man with the cords was gone.

There were five cords on the clerk's table.

I never learned whether there had been six.

The day was still alive outside.

I could go home.

I could also go back to the hall.

Rinna had told me to come back if I was useful.

This created a test with much poorer controls.

I went to the hall.

The afternoon performance had already started.

Rinna saw me come through the front.

"Useful?"

"Probably."

She pointed toward rear.

"Cloth basket."

That was all.

I went rear.

Pell was crouched beside a stack of stools.

"Paper?"

"Near."

He looked at me.

"Near what?"

"Exactly."

"Did it work?"

"Some of it."

"Good."

"Stop saying that."

He handed me a folded cloth.

"Costumes."

I carried it to Nessa.

She took it without asking about magic.

Better.

I spent the rest of the performance doing almost nothing important.

A stool moved.

A coat needed hanging.

Someone had left a cup behind the painted wall and I retrieved it after
the scene.

Sellen came offstage sweating in Marek's coat and said, "Water."

I handed him water.

He drank half.

"Guild?"

"Yes."

"Successful?"

"I moved paper almost nowhere."

"Excellent."

"Everyone here is broken."

"Probably."

He went back out.

I was not paid for a second house because I had arrived after most of it
and Rinna had apparently retained a functioning sense of arithmetic.

She did give me half a heel of bread left from the rear table.

I accepted this as institutional recognition.

At home, Lyssa was later than me.

I washed.

I ate the bread.

I looked at Hessa's new sheet, which I had carried folded inside my
coat.

I did not put anything near it.

Eventually Lyssa came in carrying a bundle of pale cloth and smelling
faintly of hot street dust.

She put the bundle down.

"Near or far?"

"Near."

She waited.

I sighed.

"Near here."

Lyssa smiled.

"That sounds like Hessa."

"It is contagious."

She sat.

"Far?"

"Nothing clean. Maybe a tiny movement once. Hessa won't call it."

"Near?"

"Twice."

"Again?"

"Again."

She took off one shoe.

"So you learned something."

"I learned that paper has preferences."

"No."

"I learned that paper near me has preferences."

"No."

"I learned that under one specific arrangement, tiny folded paper near
me is more cooperative than tiny folded paper farther from me."

Lyssa took off the other shoe.

"That definitely sounds like Hessa."

"I hate this."

"You look pleased."

"I can do both."

She nodded.

I showed her the sheet.

She read the conclusion without trying to improve it.

Then she saw the three marks.

"Middle?"

"Intermediate."

She looked at me.

"Hessa?"

"Yes."

"Thought so."

"No date."

"Good."

"There it is again."

Lyssa gave the paper back.

I put it under the cup with the others.

Twenty-eight draws.

Twenty-four shapings.

Twenty-seven external attempts.

Near had worked again.

Far had not worked cleanly again.

I still could not practice.

I still could not move anything useful.

Tomorrow the theatre wanted me at third bell unless Rinna changed her
mind, sent for me, forgot, or decided schedules were decorative.

Hessa wanted me at some future time she had not named so we could move
the paper to the middle of the table and ask it to do almost nothing
there.

This was progress.

Annoyingly, it was also evidence.
