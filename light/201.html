LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 201

THE CLERK

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The cup had nothing under it.

I knew because I looked.

This was not testing.

It was checking the mail.

Lyssa was sitting at the table with a dark sleeve turned inside out over
one hand. The pale binding I had spent several days refusing to
understand was now sewn along the edge. It looked like a sleeve.

This was disappointing only because it was exactly what she had said.

"No note?" she asked.

"No."

"Good."

I looked at her.

She did not look up.

"You're doing it deliberately now."

"Yes."

I ate bread.

The third bell was still far enough away that going to the theatre would
have been a violation of a system I had personally complained about
until someone created it.

I had no Guild work.

No errands.

No bowl.

No salt emergency.

This was dangerous.

Lyssa turned the sleeve right side out, checked the seam, then folded it
with another piece of dark cloth.

"Customer?" I asked.

"Yes."

"That's it?"

"That's what?"

"The narrow thing."

"One of them."

I pointed at her.

"No."

She smiled.

There were apparently infinite narrow things.

I washed the cup, which had done nothing wrong, and went outside before
I learned about another one.

Carrow was already moving.

The morning had warmed the stone but not yet the walls. A cart with
green onions passed me. A boy carried a stack of flat baskets taller
than his head and navigated entirely by looking around one side. Two
women stood in a doorway arguing about whether somebody named Orin had
paid for lamp oil. I did not know Orin.

This was healthy.

I bought nothing.

This was less healthy but financially responsible.

The third bell began while I was still half a street from the hall.

I arrived before the last strike finished.

Rinna was not at the front table.

This immediately made the entire schedule feel suspicious.

Pell was.

He had both hands inside a sack.

"Where's Rinna?"

"Rear."

"What are you doing?"

"Looking."

"For?"

He stopped.

"Don't know yet."

"Good system."

"Found it."

He pulled out a wooden mallet.

"Did you know that was in there?"

"No."

"Then you weren't looking for it."

"I am now."

I left him.

The board was crowded.

Someone had erased Sellen's name from The King and written MAREK over
it.

I read it twice.

Not because I was uncertain.

Because Marek was standing under the costume rail eating dried apples.

"You came back," I said.

Marek chewed.

"Apparently."

He held out the little paper twist.

I took one.

This was the correct form of reunion.

"Baby?"

"Yes."

"Good?"

"Yes."

"Sister?"

"Fine."

"Good."

Marek looked at me.

"You ask fewer questions now."

"I have evidence."

"What?"

"Nothing."

I ate the apple.

Sellen was standing three feet away in his shirt while Nessa attacked
Marek's King coat with pins.

More accurately, she was removing the attack she had previously
committed.

The folded cuffs had been opened. One shoulder pin was already between
Nessa's lips. The tie at the back hung loose.

Marek looked at the coat.

"What did you do to it?"

"Made it fit," Nessa said.

Sellen said, "Barely."

"It fit."

"I could not breathe."

"You didn't need to breathe in that scene."

"I had lines."

"Bad planning."

Marek took another dried apple.

"Did he ruin the King?"

Sellen answered before I could.

"Improved him."

Marek nodded.

"Then I'll ruin him back."

There was no ceremony.

No one asked Marek about the road.

No one asked whether he had missed us.

Nessa pulled the last temporary pin and handed him the coat.

"Try."

He put it on.

The shoulders sat where they were supposed to.

The sleeves ended at his wrists.

Marek rolled one shoulder.

"Better."

Sellen looked offended.

Nessa looked at him.

"Your body was wrong."

"Temporarily."

"Still."

Rinna came out of the rear carrying the small slate.

She saw me.

"Third bell."

"I know."

"Good."

"I don't like when you say that."

"Clerk."

"What?"

She pointed at the board.

My name was written beside THE DEBTOR'S UNCLE.

GREG: CLERK / SET.

The Clerk.

I had done it once when Evin was late. Eight lines then, if I remembered
correctly.

I did not trust remembering correctly.

"Today?"

Rinna sighed.

"Teren."

Teren was somewhere behind the painted wall.

I found him crouched over a stack of pages with one knee on the floor.

"Clerk."

"Yes."

"Same version?"

"Local."

"Eight lines?"

"Seven."

"Why seven?"

"Road cut."

"Which line?"

He held out his hand.

I gave him the page Rinna had shoved at me without my noticing she had
done it.

Teren crossed out a sentence near the bottom.

"There."

"What happened to it?"

"Nothing. We don't use it."

"That is a better answer than the chicken."

Teren looked up.

"What chicken?"

"Exactly."

He went back to the pages.

I had seven lines.

I knew six of them already.

The seventh was not new. It had merely survived a cut I had forgotten.

This was becoming the main hazard of repertoire.

Not learning.

Remembering which version of what I had learned was currently alive.

I went back to the board.

The King had changed around Marek's return. Sellen was back in Petition.
Evin had shifted one mark lower. Hara remained where she had been. The
short piece was back because Hara apparently did not have outside work
today.

Nobody explained this.

The board did.

Mostly.

Pell came up beside me.

"Marek brought apples."

"I know."

"You took one."

"I know."

"He only gave me half."

"Did you ask for two?"

"No."

"Then current evidence suggests he gave you half."

Pell stared at me.

"What happened to you?"

"Hessa."

"Can she fix it?"

"No."

I regretted that immediately.

One contamination of ordinary speech was enough.

I went to work.

The Clerk coat was gray.

Not Witness gray.

Different gray.

This was information I had not wanted.

Nessa handed it to me.

I started toward the hook where I had hung the Witness coat last time.

"Other."

I stopped.

"Of course."

"Second peg."

I hung it on the second peg.

"Why are there this many gray coats?"

"Because people own gray."

"Badly."

Nessa had already turned away.

The hall needed more chairs on the right because Rinna had sold a
cluster of seats together. Jori and Pell moved the heavy ones. I carried
two lighter chairs one at a time because my wrist was boring and I
wanted to keep it that way.

Davin had the repaired Borrowed House chest open on a bench.

"Fixed?"

"Yes."

I reached for the handle.

Davin slapped my hand away.

"I fixed it."

"That usually means testing."

"I tested it."

"How?"

"With the handle."

I withdrew.

"This place has become hostile to inquiry."

"It was always hostile to you."

Fair.

Marek had been gone long enough for one of the rear paths to change.

The narrow painted wall that used to stand flat against the brick was
now stored upright beside a costume basket because the cracked chest had
occupied its old place during repairs.

Marek came around the corner carrying the King ledger and stopped.

"Why is that there?"

Pell, under a bench again, said, "Tuesday."

"What happened Tuesday?"

"A lot."

Marek looked at me.

"Did you do this?"

"No."

"Good."

"Current evidence..."

He walked away.

I stopped.

Hessa was becoming a disease.

The rehearsal for Debtor's Uncle took less than ten minutes.

Evin was there, so I was not replacing him. The Clerk was simply mine
today because the bill had been arranged that way.

This felt different.

Not important.

Different.

The Clerk entered with a folded account, told Veya's uncle that the debt
had been entered twice, then had to explain that entering it twice did
not mean the debtor owed twice.

This should have been simple.

It was not.

"Then why write it twice?" Veya asked in character.

"Because the first copy went to the office."

"And the second?"

"The second proves the first existed."

"To whom?"

"The office."

"Which already has the first."

"Unless it doesn't."

Veya stared at me.

The line was hers.

The logic belonged to government.

Teren said, "Again. Faster."

We did it again.

It was funnier faster because nobody had time to save the system.

That was all the note we got.

No lecture.

No deeper truth.

The Clerk was an idiot in a coat who believed paper could repair paper.

I felt unfairly represented.

At food, Marek's dried apples became public property despite no formal
decision.

He had also brought a small hard cheese wrapped in cloth.

Sellen took a piece.

Marek slapped his hand.

"You ate while I was gone."

"I suffered."

"You had beans."

"Exactly."

Hara had a boiled egg and bread.

Pell had nothing.

This lasted until Rinna walked by, saw him looking at everyone else's
food, and threw him half a heel without stopping.

Pell caught it against his chest.

"She loves me."

Rinna, already ten feet away, said, "No."

I had bread from home and another dried apple.

Marek sat on an overturned crate.

"Girl," he said.

"What?" Hara asked.

"The baby."

"Good?"

"Yes."

"Your sister?"

"Fine."

Sellen said, "Name?"

Marek told him.

I forgot it almost immediately because someone at the front dropped a
stack of wooden seat markers.

No one repeated it for my benefit.

Marek had gone north.

His sister had a daughter.

He had seen her.

Now he was back eating cheese and complaining that Sellen had stretched
his coat, which was not physically possible.

This was enough information.

The afternoon house was normal.

Not full.

Not thin.

The front row had a woman with two girls who whispered until the first
scene started and then became silent. A man near the aisle had a cough
that sounded expensive. Someone in the rear smelled strongly of onions.

The King went first.

Marek walked on wearing his coat as if it had never belonged to anyone
else.

Sellen's King had spent several days looking at the kingdom like
everyone in it had become stupid overnight.

Marek's King took the first petition as a personal insult.

"Your road is flooded?" he demanded.

Hara said, "Yes, Majesty."

"And you brought the water here?"

The laugh came before Hara answered.

"No, Majesty."

"Then why have you brought me the problem?"

Different.

Not better.

The play had changed back without becoming the same.

Marek reached for the ledger on his right.

It was not there.

The ledger had been moved to the left during Sellen's run because
Sellen's entrance came from a slightly different angle.

Marek's hand found air.

For half a breath he looked at it.

Then he looked at Evin.

"You have moved my government."

Evin, who had not moved anything, said, "It was heavy."

The audience laughed.

Pell made a strangled sound backstage.

I looked at him.

"Did he know?"

"No."

Marek took the ledger from the left and continued.

Later, Hara got the strongest moment.

She had a petition about a wall built across a shared lane. Marek kept
interrupting before she could finish.

The first two times she tried to speak over him.

The third time she stopped.

She folded the petition.

Put it away.

Waited.

Marek finished a long complaint about citizens refusing to state simple
problems.

Hara said, "Are you ready?"

The room broke.

Marek stared at her.

"No."

She nodded.

"I'll come back."

Bigger laugh.

She turned.

Marek lunged half out of the chair.

"Wait."

That was the largest laugh of the piece.

I was backstage holding a stool.

Excellent work.

Debtor's Uncle came later.

My Clerk coat fit badly at the neck.

I ignored it.

The folded account was on the correct table.

I entered when I was supposed to.

The first two lines got nothing.

The duplicate-account exchange got a laugh.

I did not expect it.

I kept going.

Veya asked why the second copy proved the first existed.

I said, "Because the office has it."

She said, "The first?"

"The second."

"And the first?"

"That is why we made the second."

A better laugh.

This was scripted.

I took full credit internally.

Then I made my mistake.

I left the folded account on the table when I exited.

It belonged with me.

I knew that.

I had carried it out in rehearsal.

I had also spent enough time around Letter Box that a folded paper left
on a table looked completely reasonable.

Backstage, Teren held out his hand.

I looked at it.

"What?"

"Account."

I looked through the gap in the flat.

The account sat onstage.

Veya was still there.

"Ah."

"Yes."

"Do we need it?"

"Next entrance."

"I can get it."

"Not now."

So I waited.

Veya crossed the room during the scene, saw the folded account where it
should not be, picked it up as part of clearing the table, and carried
it toward the side.

She never looked at me.

At the edge, she let her hand fall behind the flat.

I took the account.

She continued speaking.

The audience never knew.

Teren looked at me.

"Thank you," I whispered toward Veya.

From onstage, without turning, she said her next line louder.

I took that as forgiveness.

My next entrance happened with the account.

No disaster.

No lesson.

Afterward Veya came off.

"You left it."

"I recovered it."

"I recovered it."

"I participated."

"You held your hand out."

"Critical infrastructure."

She drank water.

I carried the stool away.

The short piece used the apple.

A different apple.

Not Marek's dried apples.

I checked.

No one ate it early.

This represented institutional improvement.

Cleanup was louder than the show.

One bench leg had started squeaking. Pell insisted it had not squeaked
before. Davin said wood did not care about his testimony. Hara left with
someone I did not know, still arguing about whether she had time to eat
before wherever she was going next. Sellen changed out of a brown coat
and immediately put on his own brown coat, which made costume work feel
insulting.

Marek found the painted wall still in the wrong place.

"Does this live here now?"

Jori said, "For now."

"Why?"

"Chest."

"The chest is fixed."

Davin, across the room, said, "Tomorrow."

Marek accepted this.

He had been gone several days and had already learned the correct
answer.

Rinna paid me one copper.

"Clerk. Set."

"Account recovery."

"No."

"That required cooperation."

"Veya should charge you."

"Then don't tell her."

Veya, behind me, said, "I heard."

Rinna handed her nothing.

"Tomorrow, third bell," Rinna said.

"No Guild."

"I didn't ask."

"No Hessa note."

"I still didn't ask."

"I'm providing useful scheduling information."

Rinna looked at me.

"Third bell."

"Excellent."

"Go home."

I bought greens on the way.

Not because I had to.

Because bread had begun to feel like a personal failure.

The woman selling them added one small bruised onion after I paid.

"For soup."

"I didn't buy enough for soup."

"Then buy more tomorrow."

This was aggressive salesmanship.

I took the onion.

Lyssa was home before me.

The finished dark sleeve was gone.

So was the pale binding.

A different piece of cloth sat on the table.

I refused to ask whether it was narrow.

"Marek came back," I said.

Lyssa looked up.

"Baby?"

"Girl."

"Good?"

"Yes."

"Sister?"

"Fine."

She smiled.

"You got the whole report."

"I asked fewer questions."

"Growth."

"Don't."

I put the greens down.

"And Sellen?"

"Demoted by childbirth."

"Was he upset?"

"No. Nessa gave Marek his lungs back."

Lyssa waited.

I explained the coat.

She laughed.

We made something that was not soup with the greens, onion, bread, and
the last piece of cheese.

It became soup after enough water.

The seller had won.

I told Lyssa about Marek reaching for the ledger in the wrong place.

"And?"

"He accused Evin of moving the government."

"Did Evin?"

"No."

"Good."

"Current evidence suggests..."

Lyssa pointed the spoon at me.

"No."

I stopped.

"Thank you."

"Hessa is ruining you."

"I said that."

"No note?"

"No."

"Good."

I stared at her.

She ate.

The intermediate position could wait.

The paper could wait.

Hessa could decide when she wanted to put the middle of the table under
investigation.

Today Marek had gone north and come back.

Sellen had stopped being king because the other king returned.

I had been a Clerk because Rinna wrote it on a board.

I had forgotten an account onstage and Veya had quietly saved me.

One copper had become greens and a bruised onion.

Tomorrow I was expected at third bell.

No one had written anything under the cup.

For once, the schedule had stayed where we put it.
