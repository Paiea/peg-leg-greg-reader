TEXT READER · CHAPTER 204

THE KEEPER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Four copper looked smaller after I slept.

This was probably healthy.

It had spent part of the night on the table because I had been tired
enough to consider the table a financial institution. In the morning I
put it away before Lyssa could point out that tables did not have locks.

She was already dressed.

The last half of the Havel pastry was gone.

I looked at the empty wrapping.

Lyssa looked at me.

"You brought it home."

"I did."

"That was generous."

"I had one."

"Then we're both alive."

I sat down.

My shoulders felt like I had carried a theatre across Carrow, which was
unfair because I had carried mostly cloth, small props, and one lamp
tin. My good leg was tired. My hands were dirty in places I had
apparently failed to wash the night before. My wrist moved normally
until I bent it as far as it would go.

There, at the very end, it was a little stiff.

I stopped.

Lyssa noticed.

"Done?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"I checked once."

"I counted once."

"That's invasive."

She tied a narrow bundle.

I looked at it.

She looked at me.

I did not ask.

This was growth, and I resented it.

There was nothing under the cup.

I checked.

Mail.

Still mail.

Lyssa left before me with the bundle under one arm. She said she might
be late. I asked where.

"Work."

"Specific."

"You'll survive."

She kissed my cheek on the way past.

Then I had a room, four new copper somewhere not on the table, a
third-bell schedule, and enough time to make poor financial decisions.

I made practical ones instead.

It was terrible.

The lamp oil had been low for several nights. Not empty. Low enough that
we had both started trimming the wick as if precision could manufacture
fuel.

I took the little household oil flask with me.

Carrow was already moving.

Yesterday I had crossed half the city with actors and a handcart. Today
nobody cared.

A woman was washing the front stones of a bakery. Two boys carried a
plank between them and argued about which one was walking crooked. A man
with a basket of onions had stopped in the middle of the lane to
negotiate with someone in an upstairs window.

I went to the oil seller.

He took our flask, held it to the light, and said, "Small."

"It fits the lamp."

"Still small."

"I don't need to light the city."

He filled it and set it down beside a twist of coarse salt and two
little packets of lamp wick.

"One copper."

"For oil?"

"For oil and wick."

"I didn't ask for wick."

"You need wick."

I looked at our old wick.

I had brought it because I had apparently become the kind of person who
carried evidence to an oil seller.

He looked at it.

"You need wick."

"Fine."

One Havel copper disappeared.

Not all of it.

Just one.

This was how civilization worked.

I bought food next.

Not much.

A small piece of meat with more bone than ambition, a bunch of greens,
and two onions. The seller wanted one copper for the lot.

I stared.

"You want it?"

"Yes."

"Then one."

"I worked very hard yesterday."

"Good."

"That should make your food cheaper."

"It makes you able to buy it."

I paid.

Two Havel copper were now oil, wick, meat, greens, and onions.

This felt less like spending and more like being attacked by a
household.

I carried everything home first.

The oil flask went beside the lamp.

The wick went beside it.

The food went where Lyssa would find it.

I left the meat wrapped.

Then I still had time before third bell.

I sat.

That was getting easier.

Not emotionally.

Physically.

Third bell eventually arrived because bells were more dependable than
actors.

I reached the hall while the last sound was still hanging over the
roofs.

Rinna was inside.

She looked at me.

"Third bell."

"I know."

"I was confirming."

"No."

Yesterday's private engagement was spread across the hall like the
remains of a minor military campaign.

The handcart stood near the rear door. One wheel had dried mud along the
rim. Two lamp tins sat on the floor instead of their shelf. The painted
post from Broken Gate leaned against a wall with its traveling base
still tied on. Costume cloth hung over three chair backs. A coil of rope
had become something between a circle and an accusation.

Pell was inside the costume basket.

Not entirely.

His upper half.

"Why?"

"My knife."

"Did the basket take it?"

"I put it somewhere safe."

"Then this seems deserved."

Nessa came through carrying the little blue bundle.

"Blue."

"Little?"

"Open."

She handed it to me.

I untied it.

Inside were folded cloths.

Blue cloth outside.

Gray and brown cloth inside.

Nothing else.

I looked at Nessa.

"I carried cloth across half Carrow to protect other cloth."

"Yes."

"And lamps."

"Some."

"This is just cloth."

"Most cloth is."

I unfolded one.

It had soot on one edge.

Another had a pale grease mark.

Protective wraps.

For lamp parts, buckles, anything that should not rub against anything
else in the cart.

I had moved them out of a kitchen passage repeatedly.

Nessa pointed.

"Gray shelf. Brown basket. Blue back."

I started sorting.

Pell emerged from the costume basket holding his knife.

"Found it."

"Where?"

"Safe."

"Good."

He left before I could ask what that meant.

I put the gray protective cloths on the gray shelf.

Nessa came back.

"Other gray."

I looked at the shelf.

There were two.

Of course there were.

"Why?"

"One is clean."

I looked at the cloth in my hand.

It had soot on it.

I moved it.

No one learned anything.

The brown wraps went into the brown basket.

The blue outer cloth went back around the clean folded pieces that
stayed together.

The entire mystery was laundry with organizational problems.

I liked it less than I expected.

Davin was working on one of the lamp tins.

The cap had bent during travel.

I picked up the other three and started putting them away.

"Not that one," he said.

I stopped.

"This one?"

"That one."

"The one you're holding?"

"Yes."

"Then why did you say not that one?"

"Because you were looking at it."

I looked at the tin in his hands.

"I was looking at the shelf."

"Good."

I put the three good tins away.

The fourth stayed with Davin.

Rinna passed with a stack of tickets.

"They paid," she said.

"Yes."

"Good."

That was the entire Havel review.

I followed her.

"Yesterday established a rate."

She kept walking.

"For Havel."

"I was the same person yesterday."

"Unfortunately."

"Four copper."

"Yesterday."

"What am I today?"

She stopped at the board.

"House."

I looked.

The board said:

KING: MAREK / HARA / EVIN

PETITION: VEYA / SELLEN / EVIN

WIDOW: VEYA / NESSA / HARA

SHORT: SELLEN / MAREK

GREG: HOUSE / SET

"No role."

"No."

"One copper?"

"If you work."

"Yesterday I worked."

"Then yesterday's money was very lucky."

She handed me the tickets.

"Front until the first piece starts. Then rear. Set between."

"Four copper."

"One."

"Current evidence..."

Rinna raised one finger.

I stopped.

"Hessa has damaged negotiations."

"One."

I took the tickets.

The hall smelled like yesterday again.

Not Havel yesterday.

Our yesterday.

Old boards, dust, lamp oil, cloth, dried paint, people.

The private courtyard had left mud on the cart wheel and soot on a
protective wrap. That was most of what remained.

Nessa had Hara's Widow shawl over one arm and was brushing something
from the hem.

"What is that?"

"Dirt."

"From Havel?"

"Probably."

"So we brought some of their courtyard home."

Nessa looked at me.

"Brush."

I took the brush.

Apparently I was now helping dirt leave.

At the front, Rinna showed me the seating rows for the afternoon.

Normal arrangement.

No private tables.

No expensive chairs.

No fish dictating the start time.

The first audience arrived while I was still counting tickets.

A woman with two children.

Then an older man who wanted the aisle.

Then three workers who smelled like cut wood and argued over which piece
they had seen before.

I counted once.

Then again because the first count was wrong.

Rinna held out her hand.

"How many?"

"Eleven."

"Tickets?"

"Ten."

I looked at the people.

One of the children was sitting on the other child's lap.

"Eleven people."

"Ten seats."

"That feels like fraud."

"Child's small."

The mother heard me.

"He is."

The child looked offended.

I gave Rinna ten.

She gave me the look that meant I had turned arithmetic into a social
problem.

I returned to the door.

By the time the first piece began, the house was not full.

It was not empty either.

It was a local afternoon.

People sat where they wanted until Rinna told them otherwise.

Someone coughed before the first line.

A bench creaked.

Pell whispered from behind the rear flat, "Davin."

Davin whispered, "Later."

Marek went on as King.

I went to the rear.

No role.

This felt strange for almost a minute.

Then Jori handed me a stool.

"Left."

I moved it left.

A folded petition needed to be on the table.

I put it there.

Evin needed the small brown cup.

I found the correct one on the second try.

The King ran.

I heard Hara get the first laugh.

Marek got another.

A man in the third row sneezed during a pause and said, "Sorry."

Marek looked toward him.

"So am I."

The audience laughed.

Marek continued.

Not the strongest moment.

That came in Petition.

Veya was arguing with Sellen over a request to repair a public well that
apparently belonged to three different offices and no one.

Sellen, playing the clerk who did not want the problem, kept trying to
hand the petition back.

Veya refused to take it.

He put it on the table.

She pushed it toward him.

He pushed it back.

The paper slid off the edge.

Neither of them looked down.

Sellen said, "You have dropped your complaint."

Veya said, "No. The town has."

The house laughed.

Sellen looked at the floor.

Then at her.

"Then the town may pick it up."

Veya folded her hands.

"No authority."

That got the biggest laugh of the afternoon.

Backstage, Pell made a noise into his sleeve.

I did not think about paper moving.

It had been pushed by a hand.

This was a normal thing paper did.

I was proud of myself for not needing a meeting about it.

Between Petition and Widow, I moved the wrong stool.

Not the Havel stool.

The normal hall stool.

There were two normal hall stools.

One had a slightly shorter leg and belonged behind the Widow table
because the uneven floor there made it sit level.

I carried the level stool instead.

Nessa saw it.

"Other."

I stopped.

"Why?"

"Floor."

I looked at the floor.

It was the same floor it had been for weeks.

I had apparently never needed to know this.

I exchanged stools.

"How many secret systems does this building have?"

Nessa was tying her landlady apron.

"All of them."

Then she went onstage.

Widow happened.

The chipped brown cup from yesterday's hall show was back where it
belonged.

No one ate anything meant to be a prop.

No one moved a piece of expensive furniture because we did not own any.

I worked rear.

At one point a lamp wick guttered.

Jori fixed it.

I held nothing.

This was also work.

The short piece went on last.

Sellen and Marek had their hats.

Both hats.

Pell checked twice.

I asked why.

"Yesterday."

"The hats didn't go to Havel."

"Exactly."

I did not understand.

He seemed satisfied.

The show ended.

People left.

One of the wood-smelling men told Rinna the well piece was good.

Rinna said, "Yes."

He waited.

She waited.

He left.

I swept under the third row.

There were seed shells, one broken bit of string, and a little wooden
bead.

The bead went into the lost-things cup.

The string went away.

Pell claimed the seed shells were not his.

"No one accused you."

"I know."

"Then why?"

"Evidence."

I pointed the broom at him.

"No."

He grinned.

The Havel cart rope finally got coiled properly.

Jori did it.

The painted post lost its traveling base and went back with the rest of
Broken Gate.

Davin set the bent lamp cap aside for tomorrow.

Nessa finished restoring the costume basket.

By the time Rinna paid me, the hall mostly looked like itself again.

One copper.

I held it.

"Yesterday..."

"No."

"I hadn't started."

"You had."

"One copper for house and set."

"Yes."

"Ticket arithmetic."

"Included."

"Stool correction."

"That was your mistake."

"Then correction should be extra."

"Do you want less?"

"No."

"Good."

She turned away.

"Third bell tomorrow."

"I know."

"Good."

This was getting oppressive.

I went home with one new copper after having spent two of yesterday's.

This was not a ledger.

I refused to calculate further.

Lyssa was home before me.

The lamp was burning.

Brighter.

I stopped in the doorway.

She looked at me.

"Oil."

"Yes."

"You bought oil."

"I did."

"And wick."

"I was forced."

"By?"

"An expert."

She looked toward the food.

The meat was no longer wrapped.

"Meat."

"Also forced."

"Same expert?"

"Different criminal."

Lyssa smiled.

"Four copper lasted well."

"Some of it still exists."

"I didn't ask."

"Good."

A folded note sat under the cup.

I saw it.

Lyssa saw me see it.

"That came after I got home."

"You didn't read it?"

"It has your name."

Hessa did not usually need my name.

Apparently the cup had become enough of an address.

I lifted it.

The note was short.

TWO DAYS.

AFTER MIDDAY.

INTERMEDIATE.

AWAY ONLY.

SAME APPARATUS.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

I read it once.

Then put it down.

Lyssa waited.

"Two days."

"I can read."

"That was rude."

"You were going to read it to me."

"I was sharing."

"Paper?"

"Intermediate."

"Which means?"

"Between near and far."

"That seems suspiciously like what the word means."

"Hessa is very technical."

Lyssa put the meat into a small pot with one onion and some of the
greens.

I added water.

She added less water.

Apparently accidental soup had rules now.

"Tomorrow?" she asked.

"Theatre. Third bell."

"And the day after?"

"Theatre until I tell Rinna otherwise. Hessa after midday."

"So tell Rinna tomorrow."

"That system is becoming annoyingly functional."

"Good."

I looked at the note again.

Intermediate.

Away only.

Same apparatus.

Same restrictions.

No new question hiding inside it.

Just the next position between two positions we already knew.

I did not ask anything to move.

I did not need to.

The lamp burned steadily with oil I had paid for.

The meat smelled better than our usual dinner.

There was still bread.

Yesterday had been four copper.

Today had been one.

Some of yesterday had become light and food before I reached the hall.

Tomorrow I was expected at third bell.

In two days Hessa expected me after midday.

The schedule had moved again.

The room had not.
