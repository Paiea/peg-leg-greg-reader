LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 209

THE MESSENGER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

There was still no note under the cup.

I checked because that was what the cup was for now.

Lyssa looked over from the table.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

"Could be bad."

"It could also be nothing."

I put the cup back.

She had three narrow strips of dark cloth laid beside a pale sleeve. I
knew enough to understand that asking what they were for would produce
an answer involving a customer I had never met and a problem I could not
solve.

So I ate bread instead.

"Third bell?" she asked.

"Third bell."

"Men?"

"Unknown."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the most accurate answer available."

She looked at me.

"You're getting worse."

"I've had professional help."

She tied the sleeve and strips into a bundle.

Her work was taking her south of Marra's today. Mine was taking me to
the hall. We did not overlap, so we left at different times.

This felt less dramatic than walking together.

I preferred it.

Carrow had apparently survived another night without asking me.

A fish cart blocked half a crossing. A woman carrying two baskets
blocked the other half. Both parties believed this was the other's
fault.

I went around.

No one followed me.

No one asked if I was Guild.

A man with a gray coat passed on the opposite side of the street.

I noticed the coat.

Then I noticed he had two legs.

Progress.

At the hall, the front door was latched again.

I knocked.

"Who?"

"Greg."

The door opened.

Pell looked disappointed.

"Too easy."

"I considered lying."

"You should have."

"I did yesterday."

"That was better."

He let me in.

The hall smelled like dust, old wood, and something fried.

Nessa was already there.

That was unusual enough that I looked at the bell light through the
front shutter to make sure I had not arrived late.

"Third bell," Rinna said from the table.

"Good."

Nessa had a bundle open across two chairs. Not company coats. These were
finer, dark green with pale lining, and none belonged to our repertory.

I pointed.

"How much?"

"No."

"I was checking whether yesterday changed you."

"It didn't."

She folded one coat.

Then she said, "The man might be called Bren."

I stopped.

Pell stopped too.

Rinna did not.

She was writing.

"Who?" I asked.

"The one who came here."

"Heavy coat?"

"Maybe."

"Close hair?"

"Maybe."

"Left glove?"

Nessa looked at me.

"I said he might be called Bren."

"That's fewer details than I wanted."

"You wanted a biography?"

"Yes."

"No."

She tied the coat bundle.

I waited.

Nessa sighed.

"One of the women at the house yesterday asked why I work here."

"Reasonable question."

"She heard about the men."

"Less reasonable."

"Her brother works at a cart yard near Mason's Cut. Someone came there
last month asking for a small share to keep wheels from breaking."

Rinna stopped writing.

That mattered more than the name.

"Same words?" she asked.

Nessa shrugged.

"Small share. Wheels. More later if they refused. That's what she
remembered."

"Did they pay?"

"Didn't ask."

I stared at her.

"You had one job."

"I had three coats."

"Your priorities are terrible."

"My priorities paid me."

Rinna said, "Name."

"The woman said her brother called the man Bren. She didn't see him. I
didn't see her brother. I don't know if it's our man."

Good.

Annoying.

Useful.

Rinna wrote BRENN? on the edge of a scrap, then crossed out one N.

"Why one?" I asked.

"Because she said Bren."

"Could still have two."

Rinna looked at me.

"Do you want me to add three?"

"No."

"Then stop."

I leaned on the table.

"So where does Bren stay?"

"No," Rinna said.

"I was asking."

"No."

"I didn't say I was going."

"You were about to."

"I was about to ask."

"You did."

"That proves nothing."

"It proves enough."

Nessa picked up her bundle.

"Also, someone at the fitting asked if our Guild man was handsome."

Pell made a noise.

I said, "What did you say?"

"No."

"You didn't answer?"

"I had three coats."

"Again."

"Yes."

She left for the north side with her bundle.

Her life continued to be badly organized around my information needs.

Rinna folded the scrap with Bren's possible name and put it beneath the
edge of her ledger.

"That is all we know."

"Another cart yard."

"Maybe."

"Same phrase."

"Maybe."

"Mason's Cut."

"The cart yard is near Mason's Cut."

"Which is where he came through."

"Which you guessed from stone on his boots."

"I did not guess."

"You observed grit and named a road."

"Correctly."

"That is still not the same as knowing where he lives."

I looked toward the door.

"I know."

"Good."

Pell said, "I know where I live."

Nobody asked.

The board was already up.

KING: MAREK / HARA / EVIN

LETTER BOX: VEYA / SELLEN / GREG

SHORT: PELL / EVIN

GREG: MESSENGER / SET

I read it twice.

"Messenger?"

Teren was behind me.

"Five lines."

"That's a lot of messages."

"One message."

"Five lines for one message seems inefficient."

"It's a play."

"Right."

The Letter Box had existed around me for months in different shapes.
Witness. Clerk. People carrying letters badly. People denying letters
existed. People reading things they should not.

Apparently it had a Messenger too.

Teren handed me a folded paper.

"Blue fold."

I looked at it.

It was not blue.

"The string."

A faded blue string held it closed.

"That is dishonest naming."

"Messenger enters after Veya asks whether anyone came."

"From which side?"

"Rear."

"Which rear?"

Teren pointed.

I nodded.

"Five lines?"

"Four if Sellen cuts you off correctly."

"Does he?"

"Sometimes."

This was reassuring.

I went to put the message on the prop shelf.

There were two folded papers there.

Both had blue string.

I stared at them.

One was smaller.

One had a dark wax mark.

I picked the smaller.

Veya said, "No."

She was passing with a chair.

"How do you know?"

"That's Widow."

"Why does Widow have a blue message?"

"It doesn't."

She took it from me and turned it over.

The string was green.

Old green.

I looked at my message.

Blue.

"Color is failing us."

"Your eyes are failing you."

"That seems more serious."

She kept walking.

No lesson occurred.

Before house, a man came to the door carrying a small brass lamp in both
hands.

Not a company lamp.

Household size.

The metal around its top had blackened.

He looked at me, then at the hall.

"You the Guild fellow?"

"No."

He hesitated.

"I heard there was one here."

"There isn't."

"You work there."

"I go there."

Rinna, from inside, said, "Greg."

I stopped.

The man lifted the lamp.

"It won't hold."

I looked at it.

"Hold what?"

He looked surprised.

"Light."

"Right."

The blackened top looked like every broken magical thing I did not
understand.

Maybe it was not magical.

That was worse.

"I can't fix that."

"You don't know?"

"No."

"But you're Guild."

"No."

"You said you go there."

"I do."

Rinna said, "Greg."

"I'm stopping."

The man frowned at the lamp.

"Know anyone who can?"

I thought of Varo.

Then stopped.

I did not know whether Varo repaired this kind of lamp. I knew he made
and repaired particular prepared things. That was not permission to
redirect every broken object in Carrow toward him.

"Ask the lamp seller on Cooper's Row," Rinna said.

The man nodded.

"Thanks."

He left.

I watched him go.

Pell appeared at my shoulder.

"You could have charged him."

"No."

"One copper."

"No."

"Half copper."

"We don't have half copper."

"Then one."

"No."

Rinna said, "Good."

I looked at her.

"Was that praise?"

"No."

"Very close."

"It was relief."

"I'll take it."

The rumor had acquired another shape.

Not protection.

Competence.

That was more dangerous in a quieter way.

A frightened man could leave.

A person with a broken lamp might hand me the lamp.

I did not want the lamp.

House came normally.

The possible Bren did not appear.

Neither did his lookout.

The onion woman did not appear.

A pair of older women did, arguing about whether one of them had already
seen The King.

A carpenter came in with sawdust still on one sleeve.

Three young men paid together and immediately separated to different
benches.

The broad man from yesterday did not return.

I sold tickets until Rinna took over, then went rear.

Marek wore the King hat on his head.

This was where it belonged.

Pell looked at it once.

Marek saw him.

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"No."

The King opened.

I had nothing to do in it except move a low stool before the first line
and take it away afterward.

The audience laughed in the usual places and one unusual place when Evin
coughed during a silence.

Marek waited.

Evin coughed again.

Marek said, "Finished?"

Evin nodded.

"Good. Then the kingdom may continue."

The laugh was larger than the cough deserved.

Evin bowed from where he stood.

Marek refused to acknowledge him.

That was probably the strongest moment in King.

Letter Box came next.

I put on a plain brown outer coat.

No gray.

No mystery.

The blue-string message went into my hand.

Teren pointed at it before I entered.

"Blue."

"Actually blue."

"Go."

I went.

Veya was already arguing with Sellen over whether a letter had been
received.

"It was not delivered," Sellen said.

"You opened it."

"That is not delivery."

"It has my seal broken."

"Evidence of opening."

"By whom?"

"Unknown."

That got a small laugh.

Veya turned toward my entrance.

"Did anyone come?"

I entered.

"The guest sent this."

Sellen looked at me.

"To whom?"

I looked at Veya.

That was not my line.

My line was to say, "To this house."

I knew that.

Unfortunately, I had already looked at Veya.

Sellen saw it.

"Excellent," he said. "The messenger has chosen."

The audience laughed.

I said, "To this house."

Veya held out her hand.

Sellen held out his.

I stopped between them.

My next line was, "I was told to place it in the box."

The box was behind Sellen.

I said the line.

Both turned.

The box was not there.

For half a second I thought someone had moved it.

Then I remembered.

Wrong side.

The courtyard version of nothing had infected me even though we were not
in a courtyard.

I had put the letter box on the opposite side during set.

Veya looked toward where it should have been.

Then at me.

I could not fix it from the stage without crossing directly through
their argument.

Sellen said, "You were told poorly."

Veya said, "He was told by you."

"I deny it."

"You deny everything."

"I am consistent."

The audience laughed.

Pell, backstage, slid the box into the edge of the scene from the other
side with his foot.

Veya saw it first.

"There."

Sellen turned.

"I have never seen that box."

"Of course not."

That laugh was better.

I put the message inside.

My remaining lines happened.

One got nothing.

One got a small laugh.

Sellen cut off the fifth.

Teren had been right.

Backstage, I whispered to Pell, "Thank you."

He said, "One copper."

"No."

"Half."

"We still don't have half."

"Then one."

"No."

The short belonged to Pell and Evin.

No hats.

No shadows.

Pell played a minor official trying to collect a fee from a man who kept
producing the wrong document.

Evin had three folded papers.

The first was too old.

The second belonged to his brother.

The third was blank.

Pell stared at it.

"This says nothing."

Evin looked over his shoulder.

"Then it cannot be wrong."

Pell considered.

"That is not how records work."

"Then write something."

"What?"

"That I came."

"You are here."

"Good. We agree."

The audience laughed.

Pell looked toward the audience as if asking for help.

An older woman near the front said, "Charge him."

Pell pointed at her.

"Finally."

Evin turned.

"She's not an official."

Pell said, "Neither am I after today."

That was the strongest moment.

The older woman clapped once.

Greg the Guild man did not matter.

Bren did not matter.

For several minutes, a blank paper mattered because Evin was holding it
and Pell wanted money.

That felt healthy.

After the show, I moved the letter box back to its normal shelf.

The correct side.

Teren watched me.

I said, "Nothing happened."

"I know."

"Good."

Rinna paid me one copper.

Messenger and set.

No special rumor money.

No danger money.

No lamp-repair money.

I accepted it.

The hall emptied slowly.

The carpenter stayed long enough to tell Veya that Letter Box had been
better than last time.

Veya asked when he had seen it last.

He could not remember.

This weakened the review.

Rinna latched the front door after him.

No hard knock followed.

Nobody demanded a share.

Nobody broke a lamp.

Davin had checked the cart earlier and did not check it again.

This was progress of some kind.

Rinna took the folded scrap from beneath her ledger.

BREN.

She looked at it.

Then at me.

"Maybe."

"Maybe."

"Cart yard near Mason's Cut."

"Maybe."

"Same words."

"According to someone who heard them from someone."

"Yes."

I thought about the boot grit.

The glove.

The man looking at his own hand.

The second man outside.

The missing third man who had never existed except as a sentence.

A name was better than a shadow.

It was also less useful than I wanted.

"Do you know the cart yard?" I asked.

Rinna said, "No."

"Should we?"

"No."

"Should someone?"

"Maybe."

"Who?"

"Not you."

"That answer is becoming repetitive."

"Good."

She folded the scrap again.

"I'll ask people I know."

"Who?"

"People I know."

"Names?"

"No."

"This is very difficult for me."

"I know."

She put the paper away.

That was the difference between Rinna and me.

I saw a loose thread and wanted to pull it.

Rinna saw a loose thread and first checked whether it was holding up the
curtain.

We left in a group of four.

Me, Pell, Veya, and Sellen.

At the second crossing, Sellen went west.

At the third, Veya left us.

Pell and I walked another block.

He said, "Bren."

"Maybe."

"Sounds criminal."

"It's four letters."

"Exactly."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

At his turn, Pell stopped.

"You want me to walk you home?"

"No."

"You sure?"

"Yes."

He nodded.

Then, because he was Pell, he said, "I could bring the broom."

"Go home."

He went.

I walked the rest alone.

Nothing followed.

No cat this time.

At home, Lyssa was not there.

Her bundle was.

A different one sat on the table, tied badly enough that I knew she had
not tied it.

Customer work.

I put my copper away.

The cup was empty.

Still no Hessa note.

Lyssa came in later carrying a paper packet that smelled like beans.

"Trade," she said.

"For what?"

"Work."

"Good work?"

"Finished work."

"Better."

She put the beans between us.

"They come back?"

"No."

"What happened?"

"We might have a name."

She sat.

"Might?"

"Bren."

"Who says?"

"Nessa heard it from a woman whose brother works at a cart yard near
Mason's Cut."

Lyssa waited.

"The cart yard got the same small-share approach last month."

"Same man?"

"Maybe."

"Same words?"

"Apparently."

"Did Nessa see him?"

"No."

"Did the woman?"

"No."

"So you don't know."

"No."

That was cleaner than trying to improve it.

We ate beans with bread.

I told her about the broken lamp.

She looked at me.

"You didn't touch it?"

"No."

"You didn't tell him you could fix it?"

"No."

"You didn't take money?"

"No."

"Good."

"Everyone is very relieved when I don't commit fraud."

"You should notice that."

"I have."

I told her about the letter box being on the wrong side.

She cared less.

Correctly.

The lamp burned.

The cup remained empty.

Bren might be Bren.

A cart yard might have heard the same words.

The man with the glove had still promised to return.

We knew more than yesterday.

Not much.

But it was real.

For now, that was enough.
