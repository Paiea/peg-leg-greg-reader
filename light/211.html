TEXT READER · CHAPTER 211

THE NEPHEW

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

There was still nothing under the cup.

I checked.

Lyssa watched me from the table.

"Anything?"

"No."

"Good."

"You've started doing that deliberately."

"Doing what?"

"Calling nothing good."

She pulled thread through a dark cuff.

"Nothing exploded."

"Nothing usually explodes."

"Then most mornings are good."

This was unreasonable optimism.

I ate bread.

The bread was yesterday's. The edge had gone hard enough that dipping it
in water became less an option and more a structural requirement.

Lyssa had two cuffs today.

Same dark cloth.

Different stitching.

"Same customer?"

"No."

I waited.

She did not continue.

I had become excellent at not asking about cloth.

I was less excellent at not thinking about a missing cart pin.

The men had threatened a wheel.

Someone had pulled a wheel pin.

Another cart yard had heard the same demand.

Bren might be Bren.

That was everything.

Anything beyond that belonged to the part of my brain that liked turning
three stones into a fortress.

I finished the bread.

"Third bell," Lyssa said.

"Yes."

"You're going."

"That is generally how employment works."

"Sometimes you go before they ask."

"Initiative."

"Annoying."

"Also true."

She tied the cuffs into a bundle.

We left within a few minutes of each other, then separated at the second
crossing because her work went south and mine went west.

Carrow was busy enough to be insulting.

A fish seller argued with a woman over whether a fish had been larger
before it died.

Two boys carried a chair between them and nearly took out a basket of
onions.

A cart passed with both wheel pins visibly present.

I noticed.

Then got annoyed that I noticed.

By third bell, the theatre cart was still inside.

The chalk line Jori had drawn across the replacement pin and axle
remained straight.

I looked once.

Then stopped.

Progress.

Rinna was at the front table with a woman I did not know.

The woman was perhaps forty, maybe older, with broad hands, sun-dark
skin, and a leather apron rolled under one arm. Dust had settled into
the seams of her boots. Not pale Mason's Cut grit. Darker road dirt and
wood dust.

She looked like she had somewhere else to be.

Good.

People with nowhere else to be were dangerous to conversations.

Rinna saw me.

"Greg."

The woman looked at my crutches.

Then my leg.

Then my face.

"That's him?"

I stopped.

"Depends."

Rinna said, "Come here."

That was less encouraging.

I crossed the hall.

Pell was carrying a stool behind them and slowed down enough to become
furniture.

Rinna pointed.

"Out."

Pell kept walking.

The woman put her apron on the table.

"Name's Talla."

"Greg."

"I know."

"Apparently."

She looked at Rinna.

"This the Guild one?"

Rinna said, "No."

I said, "Technically complicated."

Rinna looked at me.

"Not complicated."

"I go there."

"Not helping."

Talla's mouth moved slightly.

Not quite a smile.

"Thought you had Guild."

"We don't," Rinna said.

There.

Clean.

No gray coat.

No paper.

No shadow.

No broom wearing royal clothing.

Talla nodded.

"Fine."

Rinna said, "Talla works at the cart yard."

"The one near Mason's Cut?"

"Yes."

Talla looked at me more carefully.

"You know it?"

"I know the road."

"Bad road."

"Yes."

"Good."

Apparently road criticism was trust.

Rinna said, "Tell him what you told me."

Talla looked at her.

"I already came here."

"You said you were passing."

"I am."

"Then pass information."

Talla sighed.

"Man came last month. Said yard was doing well. Said good runs meant
good coin. Wanted a small share."

Exact enough that my shoulders tightened.

"What happened?" I asked.

"We paid."

Rinna said, "Once?"

"Once."

"And then?"

Talla looked at me.

"More."

There it was.

Not sophisticated.

Not hidden.

The first payment did not buy safety.

It bought the next invoice.

"How much more?" I asked.

Rinna said, "Doesn't matter."

Talla said, "Enough."

Also true.

"Same threat?" I asked.

"Wheel first. Then axle. Then somebody might start finding our customers
before we did."

That was new.

Not a gang map.

Not a boss.

Just pressure moving from objects to business.

Rinna's face did not change.

"Did you see him?"

"Yes."

"Description."

Talla shrugged.

"Big enough. Heavy coat. Hair cut close."

My attention narrowed.

"Glove?"

Talla looked at me.

"What?"

"Did he wear a glove?"

She thought.

"One."

"Which hand?"

"Left."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Pell dropped something backstage.

A wooden knock.

Ordinary.

Talla glanced toward it.

I did not.

That felt strange now.

"Left," I repeated.

"Pretty sure."

"Pretty sure?"

"He kept that hand close. Other one did the talking."

"Did you see what was wrong with it?"

"No."

Good.

Not good.

Useful.

I had not known anything about his hand.

I still did not.

The glove was real.

The reaction had been real.

Whatever conclusion he had drawn from my sentence remained his.

Rinna said, "Name?"

Talla scratched the side of her nose.

"Other man called him Bren."

"Called him?"

"Once."

"Bren what?"

"Didn't ask."

"Could be nickname?"

"Could be his mother's name for all I know."

I liked Talla.

Rinna looked at me before I could speak.

"No."

"I didn't ask anything."

"You were about to."

"I was going to ask where he stays."

"No."

Talla looked between us.

"You always do that?"

"Apparently."

"Yes," Rinna said.

Talla picked up her apron.

I asked, "Did the same lookout come back the second time?"

Rinna's expression sharpened.

One question too many.

Talla answered anyway.

"Didn't notice."

Good.

Unknown.

"How many men?"

"Two first time. Three second."

That mattered less than my brain wanted it to.

Different day.

Different purpose.

Maybe same people.

Maybe not.

I kept my mouth shut.

Rinna said, "You paid the first time. What happened after the second?"

"We didn't."

"And?"

Talla tied the apron around her waist.

"Nothing for four days. Then one of our drivers got offered the same run
by another yard for less. Could be them. Could be business."

"Wheel?"

"One cracked spoke two weeks later."

"Cut?"

"No."

"Broken?"

"Broken."

"Then not evidence."

Talla looked at me.

"You're annoying."

"Yes."

"Good."

Rinna's mouth moved.

Definitely a smile this time.

Talla continued.

"We started bringing the carts in. Told the drivers not to talk coin in
taverns. Told the next yard over what happened."

"Did it stop?"

"Don't know yet."

There.

No solution.

No heroic resistance.

No secret militia.

A yard paid once, refused once, changed some habits, and kept working.

Talla had probably come because Rinna asked through somebody ordinary
and because small businesses survived partly by warning other small
businesses when somebody started charging for air.

She picked up her apron.

"I've got a wheel waiting."

"Broken?" I asked.

"Actually broken."

"Good."

She stared at me.

"Sorry."

"You're strange."

"I've been told."

At the door she stopped.

"Rinna."

"Yes?"

"If he comes back, don't tell him I came."

Rinna nodded.

"Wasn't planning to."

Talla left.

The door closed.

I looked at Rinna.

"Bren."

"Probably."

That was an upgrade.

"Same man."

"Probably."

"Left glove."

"Probably."

"He was at the yard."

"Probably."

"He asked them for a small share."

"Yes."

"They paid once."

"Yes."

"He came back for more."

"Yes."

I nodded.

Rinna said, "You're enjoying this."

"No."

"You look interested."

"That's different."

"Make it less different."

I looked toward the door.

"Where does he stay?"

"No."

"I knew you'd say that."

"Then why ask?"

"Hope."

"No."

"Do we know who he works for?"

"No."

"How many?"

"No."

"Territory?"

"No."

"Why Havel?"

"No."

"Whether Havel mattered specifically or we were just visible?"

"No."

"Whether he has an injured hand?"

"No."

Rinna waited.

I sighed.

"Fine."

"Good."

"Everybody uses that word too much."

She pointed at the board.

"Work."

The board said:

DEBTOR'S UNCLE: SELLEN / VEYA / EVIN / GREG

WIDOW: VEYA / NESSA / HARA

SHORT: MAREK / PELL

GREG: NEPHEW / SET

I stared.

"Nephew."

Teren was behind me.

"Five lines."

"Whose nephew?"

"Evin's."

"Is he the debtor?"

"No."

"Am I?"

"No."

"Then why am I here?"

"Family."

"Terrible reason."

"That's the piece."

He handed me a folded cap.

The Nephew existed mostly so the Uncle could try to transfer
responsibility for a debt to someone younger and less responsible.

I understood the social structure immediately.

Teren walked me through the entrance.

Left side today.

Not right.

"Why?"

"Table."

I looked.

The table was on the other side.

"That seems like the table's fault."

"Left."

"Fine."

My first line was:

"I was told my uncle required family."

Second:

"He usually requires money."

Third:

"I have less family than money."

That line did not make sense to me.

Sellen said, "It makes sense in the scene."

"It does not make sense in language."

"Then say it convincingly."

I hated actors.

Before the house opened, I bought a small onion from the woman two doors
down.

Not our onion seller.

A different onion seller.

Carrow had enough onions to support competing institutions.

I put it in my coat pocket and immediately regretted the shape.

Pell saw it.

"Lunch?"

"Dinner."

"You carry dinner?"

"I transport assets."

"Guild assets?"

I looked at him.

He grinned.

"No."

"Good."

The onion stayed in my pocket through setup and made sitting
uncomfortable. This was entirely my own fault and therefore one of the
day's better problems.

House opened with the cart still inside.

Jori checked the chalk line once.

Straight.

No missing pin.

No Bren.

No lookout.

No mysterious glove.

A man bought two tickets and complained that the bench nearest the aisle
was already occupied.

A child asked if there would be a ghost.

I said no.

Nessa said, "Maybe."

The child was happier with Nessa.

We had twenty-three by the time Debtor's Uncle started.

I was backstage with the folded cap on.

The cart-yard conversation had settled into a smaller shape.

Bren was probably Bren.

Probably the same man.

Left glove probably real across both encounters.

He had used the same language.

The yard paid once.

He asked for more.

That was enough.

I did not need his address before my entrance.

This was obvious.

I still almost entered from the right.

Veya caught the back of my coat.

"Other."

I stopped.

Looked.

Left.

"Yes."

I crossed behind the panel and entered correctly.

Small failure.

No one died.

Sellen, as the creditor, was already arguing with Evin.

"Your brother owes twelve."

"My brother owes everyone twelve."

"Then he is consistent."

Veya said, "Consistency is a virtue."

"Not in debt."

That got a laugh.

My cue came.

I stepped in.

"I was told my uncle required family."

Evin looked at me.

"I require assistance."

"He usually requires money."

Laugh.

Sellen turned.

"Are you the nephew?"

"Unfortunately."

"Can you pay?"

"I have less family than money."

Nothing.

Of course.

The line remained nonsense.

I kept going.

Veya said, "How much money?"

"Also less."

That got the laugh the written line had failed to earn.

Sellen looked at Evin.

"Your family is insolvent in several directions."

"That is why we stay close."

Better laugh.

The piece moved.

My final line was just:

"I'll come back tomorrow."

Sellen said, "Bring money."

I said, "Then I won't."

Normal laugh.

Exit.

Done.

Backstage, Teren said nothing.

Good.

Widow followed.

Veya and Nessa had a bowl, a shawl, two chairs, and a letter.

Hara entered carrying the wrong basket.

I knew it was wrong because the correct basket had a broken handle.

Hara knew too.

She looked down at it.

Then at Veya.

Veya said, "You brought everything?"

Hara looked inside.

"No."

"What did you forget?"

"The basket."

Audience laughed.

Nessa looked at the basket in Hara's hands.

"That is a basket."

"Not ours."

"Whose is it?"

Hara looked toward the wings.

I stepped backward.

"Apparently his."

The audience laughed harder.

I had not said anything.

This was good.

Hara put the wrong basket down carefully.

Veya opened it.

Inside were Pell's gloves for the short.

She lifted one.

"Your husband left these?"

Hara said, "He had private interests."

Nessa covered her mouth.

The audience broke.

That was the strongest moment.

Pell, beside me, whispered, "Those are mine."

"I know."

"Get them."

"No."

"I need them."

"After."

"What if she throws them?"

"Then adapt."

"You've changed."

"No. I'm enjoying this."

He glared.

The gloves survived.

The short happened.

Pell had his gloves.

Marek had both shoes.

Nobody stole anything.

Nobody threatened anyone.

The audience laughed at a man pretending not to recognize his own hat.

For almost twenty minutes, Bren ceased to exist.

Afterward, he returned only because Rinna checked the rear lane before
we put the cart away.

Empty.

Jori checked the chalk.

Straight.

Davin was not there today, which meant nobody was allowed to improve the
pin.

I returned the Nephew cap.

Nessa took it from me.

"Inside out."

I looked.

It was.

"I didn't do that."

"You wore it."

"That is not the same thing."

She fixed it.

Rinna paid me one copper.

I took it.

No hazard pay.

No information pay.

No reward for correctly identifying a glove.

Employment remained stubbornly local.

Talla had been gone for hours.

Nothing else happened.

At close, Rinna said, "Third bell tomorrow."

I nodded.

Then she handed me a folded scrap.

"For you."

I looked at it.

"From?"

"Boy brought it while you were on."

I opened it.

Hessa.

TWO DAYS.

AFTER MIDDAY.

INTERMEDIATE / FAR.

AWAY ONLY.

SAME APPARATUS.

SAME RESTRICTIONS.

I read it twice.

There.

The other life.

Not separate.

Not connected either.

Just another appointment.

"Guild?" Rinna asked.

"Two days. After midday."

"Tell me tomorrow."

"I'm telling you now."

"Tell me tomorrow when I have tomorrow's board."

"Efficient."

"Go home."

We left.

I walked partway with Pell and Evin.

Pell said, "Bren."

"Probably."

"Left glove."

"Probably."

"Same man."

"Probably."

"You've become boring."

"Evidence."

He looked at me.

"Don't."

Good.

At home, Lyssa had beans again.

Different beans.

I could tell because these were darker and slightly less disappointing.

"Trade?" I asked.

"Part."

"For cloth?"

"For work."

I sat.

She looked at me.

"They come back?"

"No."

"Cart?"

"Still has both wheels."

"Good."

I let it go.

"We know more."

"Do you?"

"Probably."

She waited.

I told her about Talla.

Not every line.

The useful parts.

Cart yard paid once.

Bren returned for more.

Heavy coat.

Close hair.

Left glove.

Another man called him Bren.

"Same man?" Lyssa asked.

"Probably."

"Probably Bren?"

"Yes."

"Hand?"

"Still don't know."

"Who he works for?"

"No."

"Where he stays?"

"No."

"What happens next?"

"No."

She put beans in my bowl.

"So you know his name."

"Probably."

"And he has a glove."

"Probably."

"And he asks for more after people pay."

"Yes."

"That's useful."

"Yes."

"Not much."

"No."

She nodded.

That was becoming our preferred amount of certainty.

I put Hessa's note on the table.

Lyssa read it.

"Far."

"Intermediate and far."

"Two days."

"Yes."

"Rinna know?"

"Yes."

"Good."

I pointed at her.

"There."

"What?"

"Again."

She ate a bean.

I ate mine.

Bren was probably Bren.

The glove was probably the same glove.

The cart-yard payment had bought another demand.

Two days from now, Hessa wanted paper at two positions.

Tomorrow, Rinna wanted me at third bell.

None of those things explained the others.

For once, I let them remain separate.
