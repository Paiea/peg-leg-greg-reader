LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 214

THE BROTHER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

My forearm had become boring again.

This was good.

It was also rude after yesterday.

One piece of paper had moved at the far mark. Once. Hessa had spent
several lines making sure that one movement remained exactly one
movement, and my body had responded by becoming completely ordinary
overnight.

No ache in the wrist.

No shoulder complaint.

No mysterious new ability.

Nothing under the cup.

I checked once.

Lyssa was tying a narrow roll of pale cloth with blue thread.

"Nothing?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"You sound disappointed."

"I enjoy receiving instructions."

"No, you don't."

"Sometimes they prove people remember me."

She looked at me.

"Vale remembered you."

"That was debt."

"Still remembered."

I considered this.

"I preferred Hessa."

"Because she doesn't charge you?"

"She charges me dignity."

Lyssa put the cloth roll into her bag.

She had work near the west market, then somewhere beyond it. She did not
explain where. I did not ask.

This was becoming a skill.

She tore the last heel of bread in half and gave me one piece.

"Third bell?"

"Third bell."

"Bren?"

"Not scheduled."

"Good."

That was all.

She left.

I washed the cup.

I did not touch the paper under it.

At third bell, the theatre cart still had both wheels.

This was becoming how I greeted the building.

The cart sat farther inside than it used to, between the rear wall and a
stack of painted flats. Jori's chalk line across the replacement pin and
axle remained straight.

I looked.

I did not touch.

Pell was already beside me.

"Still there."

"Yes."

"I checked before you."

"Congratulations."

"I also checked the other wheel."

"Did it become dangerous?"

"No."

"Good work."

He nodded as if I meant it.

Marek came past carrying his King hat.

He held it with both hands.

Pell watched.

Marek stopped.

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were thinking."

"I do that."

"Not with the hat."

Marek carried it backstage.

The King hat had apparently survived crime and become more valuable.

Rinna's board had three pieces.

EMPTY TABLE: VEYA / SELLEN / EVIN / GREG

WIDOW: HARA / NESSA / VEYA

SHORT: MAREK / PELL

GREG: BROTHER / SET

I pointed at Brother.

"Whose?"

Rinna looked up from a list.

"Veya's."

"In life?"

"In the play."

"Good. I wasn't prepared."

"You have six lines."

"Less prepared."

The Brother belonged to The Empty Table.

I had seen the role before, though not recently. He came in late,
complained that Veya's character had sent for him without saying why,
discovered that the family argument had already moved beyond him, and
left with somebody else's scarf.

The scarf was important.

Not emotionally.

It was simply the thing I had to remember.

Teren walked me through the entrance once.

"Door side. Not kitchen side."

"We don't have a kitchen side here."

"You still tried it last time."

"Memory is powerful."

"Use it on the correct entrance."

Nessa handed me a dull brown coat with a loose right cuff.

"This one."

I checked the label.

GREG.

"Mine."

"For today."

"Still counts."

She gave me the scarf.

Blue with a thin cream edge.

"Veya's chair. Then your hand. Then off."

"Why does he steal it?"

"He thinks it's his."

"Is it?"

"No."

"Good. Character depth."

Nessa left me.

The hall was normal.

Davin was checking a lamp bracket.

Jori was cutting two wicks to the same length and becoming angry that
one was not the same length.

Sellen and Evin were arguing over whether a written pause was actually
written.

Hara had brought a small paper packet of roasted seeds and was refusing
Pell access.

Nobody had been stabbed.

Nobody had burned anything.

Nobody had returned.

I was starting to believe the day might have poor dramatic instincts.

The knock came while I was moving the Empty Table table.

Not hard.

Three quick taps.

Rinna looked at the door.

So did everyone else.

That was new.

Before Bren, a knock had meant somebody wanted the door.

Now a knock had acquired options.

Rinna went herself.

She opened it only far enough to see out.

A boy stood there.

Maybe fourteen.

Maybe younger.

Thin arms. Dusty knees. A cord around one wrist. He held a folded piece
of rough cloth in one hand.

He looked past Rinna.

"Greg?"

Nobody moved.

The boy looked around again.

"One leg?"

Pell said, "That's him."

Rinna turned her head.

Pell looked offended.

"What? It is."

The boy held out the bundle.

"Man paid me to bring this."

Rinna did not take it.

"What man?"

"Said Bren."

The room got quieter.

Not silent.

Jori's knife still scraped once against wick.

Then stopped.

Rinna said, "You know him?"

The boy shook his head.

"Where did he find you?"

"South market."

"What did he look like?"

The boy shrugged.

"Man."

"Large?"

"Large enough."

"Coat?"

"Had one."

"Glove?"

The boy looked annoyed now.

"I didn't count his clothes."

Fair.

Rinna pointed at the bundle.

"What is it?"

"He didn't tell me."

"Did you open it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"He paid me not to."

This was the first thing about the boy that made complete sense.

"How much?" Pell asked.

Rinna looked at him.

"What?"

Pell said, "Information."

The boy said, "One copper."

Pell looked impressed.

I was too.

The boy extended the bundle again.

"For Greg."

Rinna said, "No."

The boy stared at her.

"I have to give it."

"You can put it down."

"That's giving it."

"Fine."

He crouched and set it on the floor outside the door.

Then he remembered something.

"Bren says the share is still small."

Nobody answered.

The boy waited.

Rinna said, "Anything else?"

"No."

"Did he tell you to bring an answer?"

"No."

"Good."

She closed the door.

The boy's footsteps went away.

Pell moved first.

"One copper."

"Don't," Rinna said.

"I wasn't touching it."

"You were thinking."

Marek, from backstage, said, "That defense has failed today."

Rinna waited a few breaths before opening the door again.

The bundle remained on the ground.

The boy was gone.

No one else stood immediately outside.

Rinna picked it up.

She did not hand it to me.

This seemed correct.

She put it on the front table and unfolded the cloth.

The iron pin inside was dirty.

Small.

Ordinary.

I knew what it was before Davin came over.

So did Jori.

Pell said, "That's ours."

"Probably," I said.

"He kept it."

"Apparently."

"That's rude."

Davin picked up the pin.

He turned it once.

There was a flattened mark along one side.

Jori leaned over.

"Nick."

Davin nodded.

I asked, "Certain?"

Davin looked at me.

"I made it."

"The first one?"

"Yes."

He set it beside the edge of the table.

Jori pointed to the nick.

"From the old axle edge. Before I cleaned it."

"So ours," Rinna said.

Davin nodded.

"Ours."

That changed the shape of things.

Not everything.

The boy had said a man calling himself Bren had paid him.

The boy had brought our missing pin.

The message had been about the share.

That was considerably less maybe than yesterday.

It was still not a surname.

It was not an organization.

It was not proof that Bren himself had pulled the pin with his own left
hand.

But somebody who wanted us to believe the message came from Bren had
possession of the pin.

That was enough to make my stomach feel colder.

Pell looked at the pin.

"He could have kept it."

"Yes," I said.

"Why give it back?"

Because the pin was not the point.

The point was that he had touched the cart.

The point was that he could prove it.

The point was that returning a cheap piece of iron cost him almost
nothing and made us imagine the next thing he might choose not to
return.

I did not say all of that.

Rinna folded the cloth over the pin.

"What do we send back?" I asked.

"Nothing."

I looked at her.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing."

"He addressed it to me."

"I noticed."

"That feels like it deserves something."

"No."

Pell said, "We could send his copper back."

"We don't have his copper," Rinna said.

"The boy does."

"Then especially no."

She carried the bundle to the small shelf near her papers.

The threat note was still there.

BREN?

LEFT GLOVE.

CART YARD.

PAID ONCE. MORE.

Rinna added one line beneath it.

PIN RETURNED.

MESSAGE IN BREN'S NAME.

She did not write BREN PULLED PIN.

Good.

I said, "Vale told me to tell him if I got the other half of the name."

Rinna looked at me.

"Did you?"

"No."

"Then don't."

"Bren returned a pin."

"Vale asked for a name."

"Yes."

"Good."

I disliked when everyone became Hessa.

Rinna turned away.

Then turned back.

"You made yourself part of this."

"I was already standing there."

"You made him think you were more than standing there."

I had no useful answer.

She was right.

The bluff had bought us time.

It had also apparently changed who Bren thought he was negotiating with.

I had wanted one man to hesitate.

I had not intended to receive mail.

Rinna pointed toward the stage.

"Table."

Right.

Theatre.

The Empty Table table was still halfway between the wall and where it
belonged.

I moved it.

Not alone.

Davin took the heavier end because Hessa had said no lifting yesterday
and because apparently I was now surrounded by people who listened when
told things.

We set it.

Teren checked the spacing.

"Too far left."

Of course.

We moved it six inches.

Crime could not protect me from furniture.

By the time the doors opened, the returned pin was off the front table.

The replacement pin remained in the cart.

The chalk remained straight.

The audience knew none of this.

They came in wanting seats.

One woman wanted to know whether Widow was playing.

A man with a red nose wanted the aisle.

Two boys argued over who had paid last time.

An older woman brought her own cushion and treated our bench as a
hostile surface she had prepared to defeat.

I worked front until Rinna sent me backstage.

My brown Brother coat waited on the hook.

The blue scarf waited on Veya's chair.

The pin waited somewhere near Rinna's papers.

I tried not to think about any of them.

The Empty Table began.

Veya and Sellen had the first exchange.

Evin came in with the letter.

The house was not large, but it was awake.

That mattered.

I stood in the wing.

Door side.

Correct side.

Teren touched two fingers together.

My cue.

I entered.

"You sent for me."

Veya looked at me.

"I sent for family."

"You should have been more specific."

A small laugh.

Good.

Sellen turned.

"Are you her brother?"

"Only when required."

Another laugh.

The written line after that was about the table.

I knew it.

I had known it ten minutes earlier.

Then I looked toward Veya's chair.

The scarf was there.

Blue.

Cream edge.

And for half a second the folded shape reminded me of the cloth around
the pin.

My line disappeared.

Not dramatically.

It simply ceased to exist.

Veya waited exactly long enough to make the audience think the pause
belonged to us.

Then she said, "My brother has always needed instructions."

There it was.

"Only the useful ones," I said.

Not the line.

Close enough to the scene.

Sellen said, "Then sit."

That got me back.

I sat.

The next written line arrived where it belonged.

No disaster.

No breakthrough.

Backstage, Teren did not say anything.

That was worse.

I remembered the scarf.

At least I did that.

Near the end, Veya threw it over the chair while arguing with Evin.

I picked it up.

"This is mine."

Veya said, "It is not."

"Then why do I recognize it?"

"Because you've stolen it before."

That was written.

I left with the scarf.

The audience laughed.

Backstage, Nessa took it from me.

"You missed."

"One line."

"I know."

"Teren knows."

"I know."

"Everyone knows."

"Probably."

Comforting.

Widow went next.

The strongest moment had nothing to do with us.

A little girl in the second row did it.

Hara stood near the front and asked Veya, "Do you expect me to believe
that?"

The line was rhetorical.

The girl answered.

"No."

The audience laughed before any actor did.

Hara stopped.

Looked at the girl.

Then back at Veya.

"Good. We have found one sensible person."

Veya turned toward the child.

"She's too young to understand."

The girl said, "No."

That was worse.

Hara put a hand to her chest.

Veya stared at the ceiling.

Nessa, who was supposed to cross behind them with a folded shawl,
stopped halfway and said, "Do you want her removed?"

The girl's mother was already laughing too hard to object.

Veya said, "No. She has earned her seat."

The whole house went.

The girl sat straighter.

Hara nearly lost the next line.

She got it back.

The scene continued.

Nobody backstage could do anything except wait until the laughter came
down.

Pell whispered, "Hire her."

Marek said, "No."

"Why?"

"She'll take my hat."

Pell considered this a serious concern.

Widow finished strong.

Short followed.

Marek and Pell did their usual work without a child taking over the
government.

Between pieces I passed Rinna.

"What was the line?" she asked.

I told her.

"Why'd you lose it?"

I looked at the shelf where the pin was no longer visible.

"Thinking."

"Do less."

"People keep recommending that."

"Listen."

Then she was gone.

Short got its laughs.

Pell missed one of his own cues because he was looking at the wrong side
of the room.

This improved my mood.

At close, the audience left with no idea that a piece of cart hardware
had occupied more of my attention than six written lines.

We swept.

We stacked.

Jori checked the replacement pin.

Chalk straight.

Davin checked the rear door.

Normal.

Nobody had taken a lamp.

Nobody had blocked the entrance.

No boy returned.

No Bren.

Rinna paid me one copper.

I looked at it.

"Still one."

"You forgot a line."

"I recovered."

"Veya recovered."

"Teamwork."

"One."

I took it.

Pell was still thinking about the returned pin.

"He paid a copper to return our own property."

"Yes."

"That's bad business."

"It wasn't a sale."

"He could have just brought it himself."

"No," Rinna said.

Pell looked at her.

She tied the small bundle closed again.

"He could have."

That landed differently.

Bren had chosen not to come through the door.

Maybe because he believed something about me.

Maybe because he doubted it.

Maybe because he wanted to see what we did.

Maybe because he had something else to do.

Those were all stories.

The only fact was that he had sent a boy.

Rinna put the pin away.

"We leave."

We did.

Not in one frightened knot.

Not alone either.

The company naturally broke into overlapping directions.

Marek and Pell went east for part of the route.

Veya and Nessa south.

I went west with Sellen until his turn.

No one followed that I could see.

I did not keep looking after every corner.

That was how you supplied your own extra men.

At home, Lyssa was later than me.

I had time to cut the remaining onion before she arrived.

This was dangerous because I was not good at cutting onions evenly.

She came in carrying an empty cloth bag and looked at the pieces.

"What happened?"

"To the onion?"

"To you."

Apparently my face remained a security failure.

"Bren sent the cart pin back."

She stopped.

"The missing one?"

"Davin says yes."

"Did Bren bring it?"

"No."

"Who did?"

"Boy. Runner, I think."

"He knows Bren?"

"Says a man called Bren paid him one copper in the south market."

"What did he say?"

"'The share is still small.'"

Lyssa put the bag down.

"To Rinna?"

"To me."

Her eyes came back to my face.

"Why you?"

"I don't know."

"You know some possibilities."

"Yes."

"Those aren't the same."

Everyone had become Hessa.

I put the onion in the pot.

"Rinna sent nothing back."

"Good."

"I asked."

"Of course you did."

"I thought maybe silence looked weak."

"Does it?"

"I don't know."

That was the problem.

I knew what I had said to Bren the first time.

I knew what Pell had put behind the panel.

I knew what Bren had seen.

I did not know what he had built from it.

Now he had sent something specifically to Greg.

Not the Guild man.

Not the gray coat.

Not Rinna.

Greg.

Maybe that meant he had learned enough to doubt the rest.

Maybe it meant he believed it more.

Maybe he wanted me to wonder.

Lyssa added beans to the onion.

"Vale?"

"No."

"You didn't tell him?"

"He asked for the other half of Bren's name."

"And?"

"I have a pin."

"Not a name."

"Exactly."

She nodded.

No Hessa note waited under the cup.

I did not check twice.

The counts remained thirty, twenty-six, thirty-four.

One far response remained one far response.

The theatre had both wheels.

The door still opened.

The lamps were still ours.

Bren had spent one copper to return a piece of iron worth less than the
message attached to it.

I had made him uncertain.

Now he was making sure I knew he was still there.
