TEXT READER · CHAPTER 216

THE RELATIVE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I counted my money before breakfast.

Not all of it.

That would have required knowing exactly where all of it was, which was
a level of organization I had not achieved.

I counted what I had in the little cloth fold Lyssa had made me stop
calling a purse because, according to her, it was not a purse.

"It holds money."

"So does a mouth."

"Not well."

"Exactly."

She was tying back her hair while I put yesterday's copper with the
others.

The two copper Antonius had credited against my debt were not there.

That was the point.

I had worked for an hour and become two copper less poor without
becoming one copper richer.

In my old life, I had spent years thinking about money as a number that
got larger if I did things correctly.

Here it had become more physical.

Bread.

Oil.

Carrots.

A repaired grip.

A theatre copper.

A private-job copper.

A debt copper that never touched my hand.

And somewhere in Antonius Vale's ledger was a number I still did not
know exactly, becoming smaller slowly enough to remain important.

I folded the cloth.

Lyssa watched me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You were making the money face."

"I don't have a money face."

"You do."

"What does it look like?"

"Like you're trying to argue with arithmetic."

"I usually win."

"How much today?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know what they're paying?"

"Rinna knows."

"Good."

"You said that yesterday."

"It stayed good."

I put the money away.

The Copper Kettle job might pay me two copper.

It might pay one.

It might pay three, although that seemed optimistic enough to become
religion.

Whatever it paid, some part of me had already started dividing it.

Food.

House.

Keep.

Debt.

Except the debt did not work like that. Antonius did not want me
arriving with random coppers whenever guilt became numerically
inconvenient. He called labor when he had labor that fit me, recorded
credit, and continued owning the problem.

It was an unpleasantly efficient relationship.

Lyssa picked up her work bag.

"You meeting them before midday?"

"Yes."

"Then don't be late."

"I'm never late."

She looked at me.

"I have been late in several very specific ways."

"That is still late."

She left.

There was nothing under the cup.

I checked.

No note.

No Hessa.

No magic.

Today I was going to a wedding.

That felt safer until I remembered weddings involved families.

At the theatre, everyone had arrived early enough to make the room look
as if it were being robbed by professionals.

One costume box sat near the front.

One prop basket sat beside it.

Nothing else was allowed to travel.

Nessa stood over the costume box with the expression of a woman being
forced to choose which children survived winter.

"I need the blue wrap."

Rinna said, "Then take out something else."

"I need everything else."

"Then you don't need the blue wrap."

"This is why art dies."

Pell carried the prop basket.

"Art is heavy."

"Put that down."

"I was helping."

"You were bending the handle."

He put it down.

The cart remained farther inside.

Jori's chalk line was straight.

I looked once.

He saw me.

"Good?"

"Good."

That was all.

Rinna gave assignments.

Davin and Jori took the costume box between them for the longer
stretches.

Pell and I shared the prop basket, which meant Pell carried it whenever
the street was smooth and handed it to me whenever he wanted to point at
food.

Teren carried rolled cloth.

Nessa carried a smaller personal bundle she had somehow produced after
Rinna said one costume box.

Rinna stared at it.

"Personal."

"That word does work," Rinna said.

We left together.

Not in formation.

Not as a defensive column.

A theatre company walking through Carrow with too much cloth.

The north market was busy enough that we became ordinary almost
immediately.

A fish seller shouted at a cart driver.

Two boys ran between us and nearly took Pell with them.

Someone was unloading sacks of grain.

A Vale cart crossed the next street ahead of us, brown side mark visible
for a moment before it turned.

I watched it go.

Antonius had two copper more of me than he had three days ago.

Or two copper less of my debt.

Both were true.

Neither helped carry the basket.

Pell handed it to me.

"Important question."

"No."

"You don't know it."

"Still no."

"Do you think wedding food is better than funeral food?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"People are happier."

"That doesn't affect stew."

"It affects portions."

He considered this.

"That's good."

The Copper Kettle had copper-colored paint on its hanging sign and no
actual copper kettle visible from the street.

Fraud.

The covered yard was already crowded.

Venn had underestimated the number of cousins who became real.

Tables filled the long space. One fixed table ran along the wall. Two
others had been shifted toward the open side. The support post stood
almost exactly where any sensible actor would want to stand.

Children occupied the floor as if they had been assigned to prevent
movement.

The bride wore a pale green dress with stitched yellow flowers at the
cuffs. The groom was broad through the shoulders and had the forearms of
a man who made barrels and expected them to remain barrels.

Neither looked miserable.

Promising.

Venn found Rinna.

"Fifty-three."

Rinna looked at the room.

"That post moved?"

"No."

"Shame."

"We tried asking."

"Didn't listen?"

"Old timber."

Teren walked the space.

Not metaphorically.

He actually walked it.

From the wall.

Around the post.

To the open lane.

Back.

He pointed at one table.

"That moves."

Venn said, "After the bread."

"Why?"

"Grandmother."

I looked.

An elderly woman sat at the table with both hands around a cup.

She looked capable of defeating us.

Teren nodded.

"After the bread."

We had no wings.

We had a kitchen passage, a narrow strip behind the wall-side table, and
the open lane.

The lane could work as an entrance if no cart came through at the wrong
time.

The kitchen passage could work if servers did not need it.

They did.

Constantly.

Nessa said, "We're changing there?"

Venn pointed to a back room.

Nessa inspected it.

"Fine."

This was high praise.

King went first.

I had a small Relative role.

Apparently weddings had infected casting.

The Relative belonged to Hara's side of the court, entered once to
complain that the King had ignored a family obligation, stayed for six
lines, and returned near the end to discover the obligation had been
assigned to someone else.

I had seen Sellen do it.

Teren had given me the role because Sellen was needed elsewhere around
the room.

"Entrance from lane," Teren said.

"Through actual outside?"

"Yes."

"What if someone is there?"

"Go around them."

Advanced stagecraft.

We tested it.

I came in from the lane.

Half the audience area could see me.

The other half got the support post.

Teren moved me two steps farther before my first line.

"Here."

"That's almost in the audience."

"Yes."

"Good."

"No."

I had become comfortable enough with theatre to know that "stage" meant
whatever patch of floor everyone had agreed not to eat on.

The problem was getting everyone else to agree.

Before the first piece, Venn's people cleared two tables.

Grandmother remained.

Teren built the action around her.

The King chair was a tavern chair with a folded cloth over the back.

Marek sat in it.

Pell whispered, "Reduced throne."

Marek heard him.

"Reduced Pell."

Then we started.

Wedding audiences were terrible.

They were also excellent.

They talked.

They drank.

They recognized one another inside jokes that were not about them.

A man near the wall repeated one of Marek's lines a breath before Marek
said it because he had apparently seen King before.

Marek stopped.

Looked at him.

The man grinned.

Marek said, "Would you like the chair?"

The man shook his head.

"Then allow me the burden."

That got the room.

The man stopped helping.

Hara came in hard enough to pull the attention back.

Evin worked wider than he did at the hall, turning his body around the
support post so each side of the yard got pieces of him.

I watched.

My cue came.

I went through the lane.

A server carrying three cups came through at the same time.

We both stopped.

She moved left.

I moved left.

She moved right.

I moved right.

The nearest table laughed.

I said, "Family first."

She said, "Beer first."

The whole near side laughed.

I stepped back.

She passed.

Then I entered.

"Your Majesty."

Marek looked at me.

"Late."

"Delayed by a stronger authority."

Another laugh.

Not huge.

Mine.

I liked it.

Then I made the mistake.

I planted where Teren had told me during the empty-space test.

Except the guests had shifted.

The bride's uncle had moved his chair.

A child sat on the floor.

The support post now hid me from almost an entire table.

I delivered two lines to people who could hear a Relative but could not
see one.

Hara crossed behind me and touched my shoulder as part of the scene.

She kept the hand there half a beat longer.

Pressure.

Move.

I moved two steps.

Faces appeared around the post.

Right.

I finished.

No one died.

The strongest moment came later.

A guest raised his cup during Hara's accusation.

Not to interrupt.

He was trying to toast the bride from the side of the yard.

"To Lessa!"

Half the room answered.

The play stopped because fifty-three people had decided it did.

Hara looked at the bride.

Then at the groom.

Then at Marek.

Marek lifted the King's cup.

"Apparently the court recognizes a higher marriage."

The groom laughed.

Hara took the cup from Marek.

"Then the court can pay for it."

She handed it to the groom.

The groom said, "I already did."

That destroyed the room.

Even Rinna laughed.

Marek leaned back in his tavern throne and waited until they returned
the play to him.

Experienced people made interruption look planned.

I made it look survivable.

Different skill levels.

After King, we ate.

Pell had been correct.

Wedding stew was better.

There was pork in it.

Actual pieces.

Bread still warm enough to tear instead of negotiate with.

Pickled greens.

A little dish of onions with vinegar.

We sat against the wall and ate quickly while guests argued about
whether the groom's brother had cheated at some game I did not
understand.

I took a second piece of bread.

Then stopped.

Not because I was full.

Because I had started thinking about what bread cost.

This was irritating.

Two copper debt credit.

One copper yesterday.

Whatever today paid.

Bren wanted a share.

Antonius already had one, except his was legal, older, and attached to a
ledger I had agreed to while having more legs.

The comparison was unfair to Antonius.

Which was a sentence I disliked.

He had loaned me value.

I owed him value back.

Bren had loaned us nothing.

That difference mattered.

The amount could be small and still be theft.

Maybe especially then.

A small demand was designed to make refusal feel more expensive than
surrender.

Antonius had said something close to that.

I ate the bread.

Pell looked at me.

"You done?"

"No."

"Good."

"Why?"

"I was going to ask for it."

Widow started after the tables were pushed back again.

I had no role.

I helped Nessa with the change, carried the prop basket behind the wall
table, then stayed near the open side where I could move a stool if
someone blocked Veya's path.

The wedding crowd was louder now.

Fed.

Some drinking.

Children more certain they owned the building.

Veya handled them like weather.

Nessa came in with the widow's things.

Hara followed.

A boy crawled under the wall table during a quiet line.

Veya saw him.

She did not look down.

She shifted one foot so he could pass.

Nessa nearly stepped on him, saw him at the last instant, and turned the
near-stumble into a sharp little spin toward Hara.

The audience laughed.

Hara had no idea why for half a second.

Then the boy emerged behind her.

She looked down.

He froze.

Hara said, "Are you mine?"

The boy shook his head.

"Excellent."

She pointed toward the audience.

"Return to whoever failed to keep you."

The boy ran.

His mother laughed harder than anyone.

The rest of Widow went clean.

Not quiet.

Clean enough.

When it ended, the bride hugged Hara.

The groom shook Marek's hand.

Pell accepted congratulations despite not having been in either piece.

We packed.

One costume box.

One prop basket.

The missing blue wrap had not killed art.

Venn found Rinna near the front room.

The owner came with her.

Payment changed hands discreetly.

I did not see how much.

Good.

I was getting better at not knowing things.

Then Venn said, "I thought you'd have a cart."

Rinna stopped folding the client paper.

"Why?"

Venn shrugged.

"Man asked this morning."

The room around us remained a wedding.

Someone shouted for more beer.

A child cried because another child had taken something.

Pell was trying to acquire wrapped bread.

Rinna said, "What did he ask?"

"If the theatre people were coming by cart."

"Those words?"

"Near enough."

"Did he give a name?"

"No."

"Know him?"

"No."

"What did he look like?"

Venn thought.

"Man."

Rinna waited.

"Brown coat. Maybe. Hair."

"Helpful."

"I keep accounts. I don't paint portraits."

"Anything else?"

"He asked. I said I didn't know."

"Then?"

"He bought beer."

That complicated criminality.

"Which hand?" I asked.

Rinna looked at me.

Venn looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I stopped.

I had promised myself not to stare at everyone's hands.

Now I was interviewing beverages.

Rinna said, "Did he say why?"

"No."

That was all.

Unknown man.

Asked about cart.

Bought beer.

Could be Bren.

Could be someone who liked carts.

Could be a man whose entire morning had been destroyed by curiosity.

We did not know.

Rinna said, "All right."

Venn looked at her.

"Is that bad?"

"Don't know."

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"I have a wedding."

Fair.

We left together.

The walk back felt different because now I knew someone had asked.

That did not make the streets different.

A man with a sack remained a man with a sack.

Two women arguing over cloth remained two women arguing over cloth.

A brown coat became irritating for half a block until the person wearing
it turned and proved to be an old woman carrying radishes.

I stopped trying.

At the theatre, the cart was where we had left it.

Jori went to it first.

Replacement pin installed.

Chalk straight.

Other wheel fine.

Rear door fine.

Nothing missing.

The original pin remained wherever Rinna had stored it.

Nobody celebrated.

Davin put away the prop basket.

Nessa unpacked the costume box because apparently cloth suffocated
overnight if left folded incorrectly.

Rinna paid us.

My share was two copper.

I held the two coins.

Two.

Not debt credit.

Mine.

That distinction felt larger than it should have.

Yesterday I had earned one copper locally.

Three days ago Antonius had written two copper against debt.

Today I had two copper in my hand because fifty-three people had wanted
a King, a Widow, and enough distraction to make a wedding louder.

Pell looked over.

"Two?"

"Yes."

"Me too."

"Then stop looking at mine."

"I was checking fairness."

"Check yours."

I put one copper into the inner fold of my coat.

The other I kept in my fingers for a moment.

Bread.

Oil.

Meat if I was stupid.

Debt if Antonius asked for labor, which was not how this coin worked.

That was the strange part.

I could not throw it at the ledger from across Carrow and make the
number smaller.

Money had lanes here.

Theatre money fed us.

Vale labor reduced Vale.

Guild work mostly cost me time and gave me something stranger than
money.

Bren wanted to cut a lane into all of it and call the cut small.

I closed my hand.

Rinna said, "Greg."

"Yes?"

"You're making a face."

"Apparently I have one."

"Go home."

We left.

No one followed us that I could establish.

No one stopped us.

No Bren.

No runner.

No threat.

At home, Lyssa had eaten.

She had left beans covered for me.

I put the two copper on the table before I sat.

Her eyes went to them.

"Two?"

"Two."

"Good."

"Food too."

"Better."

I ate.

She asked about the show.

I told her about the support post.

The server.

The groom accidentally improving Marek's scene.

The boy under the table.

Pell trying to steal my bread through anticipated charity.

Then I told her about Venn.

"Someone asked if we were bringing the cart."

"Who?"

"Unknown man."

"Did you see him?"

"No."

"Did Venn know him?"

"No."

"Name?"

"No."

"Description?"

"Man."

Lyssa waited.

"Possibly brown."

"Terrifying."

"He bought beer."

"Monster."

"So we don't know it was Bren."

"No."

"Cart?"

"Fine when we got back. Pin there. Chalk straight."

She nodded.

I ate another spoonful.

Then she touched one of the copper pieces.

"Keeping both?"

The question should not have been difficult.

"I owe Vale."

"Yes."

"He doesn't take random payment."

"I know."

"I could ask."

"You could."

I looked at the coin.

Antonius had a ledger.

Bren had a share.

Rinna had wages.

Hessa had counts.

Everyone in Carrow seemed determined to turn my life into arithmetic.

"I think I remembered the debt more when I wasn't earning," I said.

Lyssa looked at me.

"Why?"

"Because it was impossible."

"And now?"

"Now it looks payable."

Not soon.

Not easily.

Not by throwing two copper at Antonius after a wedding.

But payable was different from impossible.

That changed the shape of it.

It also changed the shape of Bren.

A copper was not just a copper anymore.

It was bread I did not need to borrow.

Oil.

A little meat.

A piece of debt if Antonius had work.

A day where Lyssa did not have to cover something because I could not.

Bren's share could stay small forever and still take pieces out of those
things.

I put the two coins away.

Nothing under the cup.

No note.

No magic.

Thirty draws.

Twenty-six shaping attempts.

Thirty-four external attempts.

One far response that did not yet mean far worked.

Tomorrow had not been assigned yet.

For once, neither had my money.

I liked that.

I also understood it better than I had yesterday.
