LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 217

THE DEBTOR

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

I moved one copper.

That was the entire financial revolution.

It went from the cloth fold with the other money into a smaller fold
Lyssa used for buttons.

The button fold had no buttons in it.

This made it available for promotion.

Lyssa watched from the other side of the table while eating the end of
yesterday's bread.

"What is that?"

"Debt."

"That is a copper."

"It can become debt."

"It is already money."

"You're making this difficult."

"No. Antonius did."

Fair.

I had woken with the Copper Kettle coins still feeling different from
ordinary theatre pay.

Two copper was not wealth.

It was also not nothing.

Yesterday it had been stew, fifty-three wedding guests, a support post,
two plays, and several hours of carrying cloth through Carrow. Now it
was two small pieces of metal on a table.

I had decided one of them belonged to Future Greg.

Future Greg had debts.

Present Greg had breakfast.

This seemed balanced.

Lyssa tapped the little fold.

"Did Vale ask for it?"

"No."

"Can you pay him cash?"

"I don't know."

"Then it's still yours."

"I know."

"You look offended."

"I preferred impossible debt."

She chewed.

"Why?"

"It required less administration."

That got a small smile.

I picked up a scrap of paper.

The question took longer to write than it should have.

ANTONIUS,

WHAT DO I CURRENTLY OWE?

CAN I PAY CASH AGAINST IT, OR DO YOU PREFER WORK CREDIT?

GREG

I read it.

Lyssa held out her hand.

I gave it to her.

She read it too.

"That's almost normal."

"I can make it worse."

"Don't."

"Should I mention the copper?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you don't know if he wants it."

I took the note back.

There was another reason not to mention Bren.

Antonius had asked for the other half of Bren's name.

I did not have it.

I had an iron pin, a verbal message, and a man in a possibly brown coat
asking a tavern about a cart.

None of those were surnames.

This note was mine.

My debt.

My question.

That distinction felt worth preserving.

I folded it.

Nothing waited under the cup.

No Hessa note.

Thirty, twenty-six, thirty-four remained exactly where they had been.

One far response remained one far response.

Paper could wait.

Debt had apparently become interesting.

I left with Lyssa because she was headed toward the market again. Her
cuff customer had become satisfied enough to recommend another customer,
which meant yesterday's argument had somehow reproduced.

"Good?"

"Maybe."

"More cuffs?"

"Shoulder seam."

"Same length?"

She looked at me.

"I'll go now."

We separated two streets later.

I went toward Vale.

Not the main office.

That would have been dramatic for a note containing two questions.

One of Antonius's commercial yards had a receiving clerk near the east
market. I knew it because I had once spent half a morning waiting there
while three men disagreed about whether wet rope counted as damaged
rope.

The clerk was not one of those men.

She was older, narrow-faced, and busy making marks beside six stacked
crates.

"Vale?"

She looked at the side of the building.

"Usually."

"I have a note for Antonius."

"Name?"

"Greg."

"His or yours?"

"Mine."

That earned me a look.

"Gregory?"

"Unfortunately."

She held out her hand.

I gave her the note.

"Does this need an answer today?"

"No."

"Good."

"That sounded personal."

"It sounded like work."

Also fair.

I left.

That was it.

I had taken action on my debt and nothing exploded.

Disappointing.

By third bell the theatre smelled like damp wood, lamp oil, and
yesterday's costumes.

Nessa had already found a crease in one of the Copper Kettle dresses and
blamed travel.

"We walked."

"Exactly."

I did not ask.

The cart sat inside.

Replacement pin installed.

Chalk straight.

I checked once.

Pell was holding the King hat.

Marek entered from the rear.

Pell put it down.

Marek stopped.

"You touched it."

"I moved it."

"With what?"

Pell considered lying.

"Hands."

"Those are touching tools."

Pell looked at me.

"He's become unreasonable."

"You put it on a broom during an extortion attempt."

"One time."

"That's a sentence that doesn't improve with repetition."

Rinna came from the front with a folded page.

"Greg."

"Yes?"

"House and set today."

"No role?"

"No role."

I waited.

She waited.

"Good," I said.

"That sounded injured."

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain one leg and too many opinions. Move the front bench."

I moved the front bench.

Not far.

The house was smaller today.

Normal smaller.

People came in ones and twos. An older couple sat near the wall. Three
workers arrived together and argued about which one had promised to pay.
A woman brought a boy old enough to insist he did not need to sit beside
her, then sat beside her.

We were setting Debtor's Uncle and the Short.

I had just finished moving a stool when someone knocked.

Not three careful knocks.

Two fast ones.

Every head still changed direction.

That was the problem now.

Rinna opened the door.

A Copper Kettle apron stood outside.

Not Venn.

A woman I recognized from carrying bowls yesterday.

She held no package.

No pin.

No weapon.

Just irritation.

"Venn sent me."

Rinna opened the door farther.

"Problem?"

"Maybe. She said tell you."

The woman looked past Rinna at all of us.

Rinna said, "Tell me."

"That man came back."

The room got quieter.

I did not ask which man.

Rinna did.

"The one who asked about the cart?"

"Yes."

"You know it was the same one?"

"I served him both times."

Better than coat color.

Rinna nodded.

"What did he ask?"

The woman frowned, remembering.

"Asked if you got paid."

Nobody moved.

"Those words?"

"Asked if the players got their money."

"What did Venn say?"

"That wasn't his business."

I liked Venn more.

The woman continued.

"Then he asked how much."

"Answer?"

"No."

"Then?"

"He asked if you took it back here."

Rinna's face did not change much.

"Here?"

"The theatre. Said, 'They carry it back to their hall?' Something like
that."

"Did Venn answer?"

"No."

"Name?"

"No."

"Did he say why he wanted to know?"

"No."

"Did he threaten anyone?"

"No."

"Touch anyone?"

"No."

"Follow you here?"

The woman turned and looked into the street.

"I don't think so."

Rinna looked too.

Nobody useful appeared.

"What did he look like?"

"Brown coat. Thin face. Dark hair. Not old."

That was more than Venn had remembered.

Not enough.

"Gloves?" I asked.

Rinna looked at me.

The woman thought.

"Don't know."

I nodded.

Good.

I had asked once.

The world had survived.

Rinna said, "Anything else?"

"He drank another beer."

"Same table?"

"No. Front."

"Did he ask who paid us?"

"No."

"Did he ask where we live?"

"No."

"Any names?"

"No."

That mattered too.

The woman shifted her weight.

"Venn said you'd want the exact questions."

"We do."

"She also said if he comes back she's charging him more for beer."

Pell said from behind me, "That's good policy."

Rinna ignored him.

"Thank her. And you."

The woman left.

Rinna shut the door.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then Pell said, "So."

Rinna turned.

"No."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"I might have had a different question."

"No."

Pell looked offended by predictive management.

I said, "That's not just the cart."

"No," Rinna said.

"He wanted the money."

"He asked about the money."

"Fine."

Rinna pointed at me.

"Keep that difference."

I leaned against the end of the bench.

Unknown man.

Same unknown man, according to someone who had served him twice.

Cart.

Payment.

Amount.

Whether the money returned to the hall.

That was a pattern.

It was not a name.

It was not Bren.

It was also getting harder to call curiosity.

"We can't keep waiting for him to decide what happens," I said.

Rinna looked at me.

"Which him?"

Annoying.

"Whoever is asking."

"Good correction."

"That wasn't an answer."

"No."

"What do we do?"

"We stop carrying private-job money back here when we don't need to."

I looked around.

"Did we?"

"No."

Right.

Rinna had paid us after we returned, but that did not mean the client's
payment had traveled in one obvious purse. I had not watched.

Apparently not knowing things remained useful.

She continued.

"Next outside job, shares get handled before we leave or somewhere else
if that works. Client payment stays quiet. We don't tell strangers what
work pays. Which was already the rule."

"So our response is more accounting."

"Our response is not helping a stranger count our money."

"What if he isn't Bren?"

"Then we've inconvenienced a nosy man."

"What if he is?"

"Then we've inconvenienced a nosy man."

I hated how often Rinna's plans were boring.

They kept working.

"We could ask around the Copper Kettle."

"No."

"I didn't say I would."

"You were thinking it with your face."

Apparently I had several.

"I don't like waiting."

"Neither do I."

That surprised me.

Rinna picked up the folded page she had been carrying before the knock.

"I also don't like sending one-legged actors into markets to ask thin
men in brown coats whether they're extortionists."

"I'm not an actor today."

"Excellent. Then you're even less qualified."

Pell laughed.

I threw a rag at him.

Teren called from the rear, "If everyone is finished solving crime, the
stool is wrong."

The stool was wrong.

Work resumed.

That was not emotionally satisfying.

It was physically useful.

I moved the stool.

Davin adjusted the table.

Nessa changed a cuff.

The audience did not know that someone had been asking where our money
went.

They had paid to watch Veya insult Evin.

That was more reasonable.

Debtor's Uncle started.

I stayed offstage.

House work had its own timing.

Close the door after late arrivals.

Move along the wall without becoming scenery.

Keep the boy from touching the lamp stand.

Fail to keep the boy from touching the lamp stand.

Move him with words instead.

"Hot."

He pulled his hand back.

It was not hot yet.

Truth adjacent.

I felt mildly guilty.

Then the lamp was lit.

Future truth.

The strongest moment came from Sellen.

A woman arrived late exactly as Evin said, "Anyone who arrives after the
debt is due deserves the consequence."

The door opened.

Everyone looked.

Sellen looked at the woman.

The woman froze.

Sellen said, "Apparently consequences have arrived."

She bowed.

"I was looking for a seat."

Veya pointed toward the empty chair beside her.

"Sit near him. He owes more."

The woman sat.

The audience laughed.

Evin stared at Sellen.

"You've invited the consequence inside."

Sellen said, "It paid."

That got them.

I shut the door.

No one outside was watching that I could prove.

The Short followed.

Pell forgot where Nessa had hidden the wooden cup.

Nessa did not.

She made him search three wrong places while the audience watched his
confidence die.

When he finally found it under his own folded cloth, the laugh was
better than the written one.

Backstage he accused her of moving it.

"I put it there before house."

"You knew I'd forget."

"Yes."

"Entrapment."

"Repertory."

Normal.

Good.

Afterward, Rinna paid me one copper for house and set.

I put it with the money I had not assigned to Vale.

That was immediately confusing.

Was the morning copper still the Vale copper?

Yes.

Could I change which physical copper represented the debt?

Obviously.

Did that matter?

No.

Did my brain care?

Apparently.

I was standing near the front trying to determine whether a copper had
an identity when a Vale runner walked in.

Not Antonius.

The young woman with the narrow ledger case.

I knew her.

She knew me.

"Gregory."

"Greg."

"Still."

"Mr. Vale has an answer."

Rinna looked over from the benches.

I almost said this was personal.

Then remembered debt was not private if a creditor sent a clerk into
your workplace.

The runner opened her case.

"Your request was received."

"Excellent system."

"He says the current balance requires the ledger."

"Doesn't he have the ledger?"

"He has several."

Of course he did.

"He wants you at the west office tomorrow, first bell after midday, if
available. Twenty minutes. No labor credit for reviewing your own debt."

"Cruel."

"Accurate."

"Can I pay cash?"

"Yes."

That stopped me.

"Just yes?"

"Cash reduces the debt at recorded value."

"Any minimum?"

She checked the page.

"Mr. Vale says he does not want a runner carrying one copper across
Carrow every time you feel responsible."

Rinna made a noise that might have been a laugh.

I looked at her.

She became very interested in a bench.

The runner continued.

"You may pay at a Vale office against the account. It will be recorded.
He recommends you know the balance before developing a repayment
strategy."

"That sounds almost sensible."

"It was written more rudely."

"Can I see that version?"

"No."

I liked her less.

"Tomorrow. First bell after midday."

"Are you available?"

I looked at Rinna.

She said, "Third bell tomorrow."

There was enough space.

"Yes."

The runner marked it.

"Transport?"

"No."

She looked at my leg.

Not pity.

Calculation.

"Mr. Vale's note says transport is provided for requested debt labor.
This is your requested account review."

"Then no."

"Correct."

Commercial consistency.

She closed the case.

"Anything else?"

I thought about Bren.

Half a name.

No surname.

Unknown man at the Kettle.

No.

"Nothing."

She left.

Rinna watched the door close.

"You didn't tell her."

"I noticed."

"Good."

"I can learn."

"Slowly."

We finished putting the room away.

Jori checked the cart.

Straight chalk.

Replacement pin.

Nothing missing.

Again.

At the market I spent one copper.

Not the Vale copper.

I decided that before I handed it over.

Beans and a small piece of hard cheese.

The cheese was unnecessary.

This made it important.

I carried both home.

Lyssa was not there.

Her work bag was gone from its hook, which meant her day had continued
beyond whatever I had imagined for it.

Good.

I put the food away.

The little fold marked by no actual mark sat where I had left it.

One copper inside.

Vale.

Not paid.

Not gone.

Mine until I chose otherwise.

Lyssa came in later with thread stuck to her sleeve and irritation in
her mouth.

"Bad seam?"

"Customer's daughter changed the dress after I measured it."

"That seems unfair."

"It is."

"Did you tell them?"

"Yes."

"Did they become reasonable?"

"No."

"Good. I was worried the world changed."

She saw the cheese.

"You bought cheese."

"Financial planning."

"That's not what that means."

"I planned to eat cheese."

She sat.

I told her about the Copper Kettle woman first.

Same unknown man had returned.

Asked if we got paid.

Asked how much.

Asked whether we carried the money back to the hall.

Lyssa stopped unwinding thread.

"Same man?"

"The worker says yes. She served him both times."

"Do you know it was Bren?"

"No."

"Do you think it was?"

"More than yesterday."

"That's not the same."

"No."

I told her Rinna's response.

No private pay talk.

No obvious money movement when avoidable.

No investigating.

Lyssa nodded.

"What did you want to do?"

"Something."

"Specific."

"That was the problem."

"Good."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Maybe listen."

Then I told her about Vale.

Tomorrow.

Twenty minutes.

Actual ledger.

Cash accepted.

No random one-copper courier confessions.

She looked toward the little fold.

"You paying it tomorrow?"

"I don't know."

"Good."

I stared at her.

She smiled.

"You hate that word now."

"I have developed associations."

She tore the cheese in half.

"You still eat while owing money."

"I know."

"You still need oil."

"I know."

"You still need things that break."

"I know."

"So find out the number first."

That had been my idea.

It sounded better when she said it, which was offensive.

We ate beans with cheese.

One copper waited in the fold.

Tomorrow I would learn what Antonius's ledger said I owed.

Today I knew someone had asked where the theatre's money went.

Those were separate problems.

I kept them separate.

That took more effort than it should have.

Outside, a cart went past.

I listened until the wheel noise faded.

Then I ate the rest of my cheese.
