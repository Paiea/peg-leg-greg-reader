LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 218

THE PAYER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The copper was still in the fold.

I checked.

This was unnecessary.

I checked anyway.

Lyssa was standing with one arm inside a dark green sleeve that did not
belong to her. The rest of the dress lay across the table.

"You think it escaped?"

"No."

"Then why are you checking?"

"I have an appointment."

"With the copper?"

"Antonius."

"Does Antonius know?"

"About the appointment?"

"The copper."

"No."

She pulled the sleeve through and inspected the seam.

"Then the copper is having a more stressful morning than Antonius."

Probably.

Nothing waited under the cup.

No Hessa note.

My forearm was boring. My shoulder was boring. My wrist was boring.

Thirty, twenty-six, thirty-four.

Paper remained paper.

Money had become the strange thing.

I ate bread.

Lyssa left before me because the shoulder-seam problem had turned into a
second fitting. I had no idea whether this meant the first fitting had
failed or succeeded.

"Which?"

"Both."

"Useful."

"Customer work."

She left.

I waited until first bell after midday because arriving early to inspect
my own debt felt too much like enthusiasm.

The west Vale office occupied the front corner of a warehouse that
smelled of dust, rope, old wood, and whatever had leaked into the floor
over several years and become part of the building.

Two carts stood outside.

One Vale side mark.

One without.

Men were moving sacks through the larger door. A clerk argued with a
driver about six barrels that were physically present and apparently
administratively absent.

Commerce.

I went through the smaller door.

The young woman with the narrow ledger case sat at a side desk.

"Gregory."

"Greg."

"Still."

"Apparently."

She pointed toward the inner room.

"He's waiting."

"That sounds threatening."

"He has another appointment."

Worse.

Antonius sat behind a table with three ledgers open.

Not one.

Three.

I stopped in the doorway.

"You've made my debt plural."

"No."

"Then why are there three books?"

"Because you made your debt inconvenient."

"That sounds like blame."

"It is accounting."

He pointed to the chair.

I sat.

The chair had a straight back and one leg slightly shorter than the
others. I discovered this immediately.

Antonius watched me rock once.

"Don't."

"Fix your chair."

"It's a guest chair."

"That explains your business relationships."

He slid the first ledger toward himself.

I could see my name.

GREGORY.

"Greg."

"The debt is Gregory's."

"I am Gregory."

"Then we've resolved something."

I disliked him.

Good.

The page was older than I expected.

Not ancient.

Just handled.

Marks had been added in different inks. Dates. Credits. Charges.
Cross-references to other pages. Several entries pointed into the second
ledger.

I leaned closer.

"How much?"

Antonius looked at me.

"That was fast."

"I have theatre."

"Of course you do."

He put one finger on the first line.

"Your original loan."

The amount was there.

I stared.

Then the next line.

Additional financing.

Then another.

A fee.

Another advance.

A correction.

Gambling stake.

Materials.

Something I had apparently agreed to while believing future income would
solve present arithmetic.

That version of me had been very optimistic.

"Why isn't there one number?"

"There is."

"Where?"

"At the end."

"Then why are we doing this?"

"Because you asked what you owe."

"I meant the number."

"No. You asked what you currently owe. If you only wanted the number, my
clerk could have copied it."

I looked at him.

He was right in the most annoying possible way.

I went back to the page.

Thirty-five percent.

Eight days.

The original terms were still there.

Then later financing with different conditions attached.

First refusal language.

Labor conversion after default.

Credits for work.

Charges I remembered.

Charges I did not.

I pointed at one.

"What's that?"

"Additional advance."

"For?"

"You were short."

"That describes a period."

Antonius opened the second ledger.

There.

A date.

A note.

I remembered.

"Oh."

"Useful?"

"No."

"It cost money."

"Apparently."

The problem became clear after several minutes.

The ledger was real.

The debt was real.

The exact current balance was also in the ledger.

But the manuscript of my own life had been financially incompetent.

Not literally.

The problem was that several old advances and gambling periods had been
recorded through linked entries, and I did not remember the numbers well
enough to pretend I had always carried the total in my head.

Antonius did.

Of course.

He tapped the current balance.

I looked at it.

Large.

Not impossible.

Those were different things.

I could not reduce it to normal theatre days cleanly because it crossed
denominations and my world had never bothered to give me a comforting
conversion table.

That was probably healthier.

I could still understand scale.

One copper for an ordinary theatre day.

Two copper from the Copper Kettle.

Two copper of debt credit for an hour of useful Vale account work.

Silver had mattered enough that six of them once covered two days of
Pell's time.

Gold belonged to another altitude entirely.

My debt had pieces from the period when I had treated future success
like a currency.

Now I was earning copper.

Regularly.

That was better than earning theories.

It was also slower.

"Well?" Antonius asked.

"I liked it better when it was abstract."

"Most debtors do."

"Do they start theatre companies?"

"You did not start the theatre company."

"Good. I was worried the ledger had added that."

He leaned back.

"You've begun earning."

"Some."

"Regularly."

"Mostly."

"And now the debt bothers you."

"It bothered me before."

"No. Before, it offended you."

I looked at him.

He continued.

"You treated it as an obstacle."

"It is."

"Now you're treating it as an account."

I hated that more because it sounded accurate.

"Do you rehearse these?"

"No."

"That was too neat."

"You are not complicated."

"Fuck you."

"There."

Normal.

I looked down again.

There were the two recent credits.

The earlier post-injury invoice comparison.

Two copper.

The delivery-account reconciliation.

Two copper.

Separate lines.

Small.

Real.

I touched the second one.

"You actually wrote it."

Antonius stared at me.

"What did you think credit meant?"

"I knew."

"Apparently not."

"I knew conceptually."

"Excellent. My favorite kind of repayment."

I took the little cloth fold from inside my shirt.

Antonius looked at it.

I put one copper on the table.

He looked at the copper.

Then at me.

"One."

"Yes."

"This is not a strategy."

"No. It's a copper."

His mouth moved slightly.

Not a smile.

Accounting amusement.

"Do you intend to do this every day?"

"No."

"Good."

"Can I?"

"Yes."

"Do you want me to?"

"I want the debt repaid."

"Helpful."

"I do not care whether a given copper came from theatre, a private
performance, or you selling your remaining leg, provided the transaction
is lawful and you remain capable of producing more value."

I looked at him.

"That became dark."

"Don't sell the leg."

"Thank you."

"It would damage repayment."

There he was.

He pulled the copper toward the ledger.

The clerk entered without knocking, carrying a small ink pot and a
narrow receipt strip.

Apparently one-copper payments had infrastructure.

Antonius made the entry.

Cash.

One copper.

Date.

Account.

The clerk copied it.

Antonius signed.

She handed me the strip.

I stared at it.

"That's it?"

"What were you expecting?"

"Nothing."

"Then you're satisfied."

I folded the receipt.

The current balance had changed by one copper.

Not emotionally.

Mathematically.

That was enough.

"What should I pay?" I asked.

Antonius closed one ledger.

"Whatever does not make you useless."

"Very precise."

"You have variable income."

"Yes."

"You have household expenses."

"Yes."

"Your theatre work may disappear."

"Optimistic."

"Private work may increase."

"Maybe."

"Your Guild work currently produces no meaningful cash."

"Correct."

"Your labor for me can reduce the account when I have work that suits
you."

"Yes."

"Then why would I invent a fixed payment that either becomes trivial
when you earn more or cripples you when you earn less?"

I stared at him.

"You're refusing to budget for me."

"I would rather die."

"Good."

"Pay when you have surplus. Work when I have suitable work. Do not
starve yourself to improve my ledger."

"Lyssa said that."

"She is protecting you."

"You're not?"

"I'm protecting the ledger."

Distinct.

Useful.

I folded the receipt smaller.

"Anything else?"

Antonius looked at the clock on the wall.

"You have theatre."

"That's not an answer."

"It is twenty-two minutes."

"You scheduled twenty."

"You spent two arguing with a chair."

"The chair started it."

He stood.

Meeting over.

I stood too.

At the door he said, "Greg."

I turned.

"If you want the figure again, ask for a copy of the balance. Do not
schedule me to discover that numbers remain numbers."

"You're welcome."

"For?"

"The copper."

"Get out."

I did.

I walked to the theatre with a receipt in my pocket.

The debt had not become small.

It had become finite.

That was worse in one way.

Better in another.

Impossible things did not require plans.

Finite things did.

Third bell was already approaching when I reached the hall.

The cart sat inside.

Chalk straight.

Replacement pin installed.

I checked once.

Rinna was moving names on the board.

"Vale?"

"Still rich."

"You?"

"Less indebted."

"How much?"

"One copper."

She stopped.

"You went there to pay one copper?"

"I went there to learn the account."

"And paid one."

"Yes."

"Did he laugh?"

"Not visibly."

"Strong restraint."

I looked at the board.

KING.

EMPTY TABLE.

SHORT.

GREG: SERVANT / SET.

I pointed.

"I've been Servant."

"So has Evin."

"That isn't how titles work."

"What titles?"

"Nothing."

Nessa threw the dull brown coat at me.

I caught it badly.

"Servant."

"Apparently."

The role was the same small role from before.

Four lines.

Carry a folded cloth.

Answer one question.

Leave.

Normal.

I liked normal.

We were halfway through setting the King table when the front door
opened.

Not knocked.

Opened.

Jori had not latched it yet.

The close-haired man in the heavy coat stepped inside.

Bren.

Probably Bren.

Same spokesman from the first night.

Left glove.

Same way of holding that hand closer to his body.

One man remained outside.

Not the narrow man from the Copper Kettle as far as I could know.

I had never seen that man.

This one I knew.

My stomach dropped before my thoughts caught up.

Nobody screamed.

Pell stopped carrying a stool.

Marek's King hat was in his hands.

He did not put it on a broom.

Progress.

Bren looked at me first.

"Greg."

Rinna stepped between the front table and the open floor.

"You're talking to me."

Bren's eyes moved to her.

Then back to me.

Then her.

"Wedding went well?"

Nobody answered.

That landed harder than a threat.

Copper Kettle.

He knew.

Not necessarily how.

Not necessarily from the thin man.

But he knew.

Rinna said, "What do you want?"

"The same thing."

"We answered."

"You didn't."

"We did."

Bren looked around the hall.

Not theatrically.

Counting.

People.

Doors.

Work.

Maybe value.

"The share is still small."

Rinna waited.

Bren said, "One copper."

I almost laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because I had just put one copper on Antonius Vale's table.

One copper.

Antonius had given me money in the past.

Loaned it.

Advanced it.

Attached terms.

Recorded labor.

Recorded credit.

I owed him because value had moved toward me first.

Bren wanted the same coin for having shown up twice and stolen a pin.

The coin did not care.

I did.

Rinna said, "No."

That was it.

No speech.

Bren looked at her.

"One."

"No."

"Cheaper than a wheel."

Davin's face changed.

Rinna's did not.

"You returned the pin."

Bren smiled slightly.

"Did I?"

Evidence.

There.

He did not confirm he pulled it.

Did not confirm who did.

Only that he knew what she meant.

Rinna said, "We're not paying you."

Bren looked at me.

"You agree?"

Rinna said, "You're talking to me."

He ignored her for one second too long.

That was probably deliberate.

I could feel the old impulse.

Say something strange.

Use the glove.

Use Mason's Cut.

Use Guild.

Use the receipt in my pocket.

Make him build a larger story.

I had no better truth than last time.

So I did not.

"I don't own the theatre," I said.

Bren studied me.

That was true.

Boring.

Useful.

"Didn't stop you before."

"No."

Rinna said, "It stops now."

Bren looked at her again.

Good.

The room had changed.

The first night I had made myself larger because we needed thirty
seconds of uncertainty.

Now that false size had become a business problem.

Rinna was cutting it back down.

Bren said, "You took the Kettle job."

"Yes."

"You'll take others."

"If they pay."

Pell shifted.

Rinna did not look at him.

Bren said, "Then one copper is small."

Rinna said, "No."

His jaw tightened.

Not much.

There.

He had expected negotiation.

Maybe.

Inference.

He glanced toward the rear.

Jori stood there.

Davin near the scenery.

Veya had come from behind the hanging cloth.

Hara beside her.

Nobody had weapons.

Nobody needed to pretend.

Bren said, "Next time won't be one."

Rinna answered, "Then this was the cheap time to say no."

That was almost clever enough to sound rehearsed.

It probably was not.

Bren looked at her.

Then me.

"Greg."

I said nothing.

He waited.

I still said nothing.

That felt harder than the first bluff.

Finally he turned.

At the door he stopped.

"Good crowd at the Kettle."

Then he left.

The man outside went with him.

No attack.

No broken lamp.

No hand on a knife.

Just street noise swallowing two men.

Rinna shut the door.

Jori latched it.

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Pell said, "I liked him better as a runner."

Rinna turned.

"He was never the runner."

"You know what I mean."

"I do. That's why I'm correcting you."

I sat on the nearest bench.

My leg was fine.

My good hand was not shaking much.

Progress.

Or delayed shaking.

Teren came from the rear.

"Are we canceling?"

Rinna looked at the door.

Then at the board.

"No."

Teren nodded.

"Then set."

That was theatre.

We set.

King went first.

I was not in it.

Marek missed a word because Pell had moved the King's cup six inches.

Marek found the cup, stared at Pell from the stage, and changed his line
to, "Even my servants now tax me."

Pell whispered from behind the cloth, "That's not me."

Hara heard him.

"It speaks."

The audience laughed.

Marek said, "Worse."

That beat belonged to them.

Good.

Empty Table followed.

My Servant role came after.

Four lines.

Brown coat.

Folded cloth.

I entered on the correct side.

Veya asked, "Did you bring what I asked?"

I held up the cloth.

"I brought what I was given."

"Is it what I asked?"

"I was not given that information."

Small laugh.

Written.

Useful.

No one in the audience knew a man had demanded one copper before house.

I did not miss a line.

I did not improve one.

I did the job.

After the Short, Rinna paid me one copper.

I looked at it.

She saw.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You have the money face."

"Apparently everyone knows it."

"Keep that one."

"Why?"

"You paid Vale?"

"Yes."

"This one feeds you."

That was not an order.

It sounded like one.

I put it away.

Jori checked the cart before we left.

Straight.

Davin checked the rear.

Fine.

Nothing missing.

Bren had come.

Bren had left.

The building remained a building.

We went home in overlapping groups anyway.

Lyssa was not home when I arrived.

I put the Vale receipt on the table.

Then moved it because oil and receipts seemed incompatible.

She came in later carrying a paper-wrapped bundle and looking pleased.

"Good day?"

"Paid."

"For?"

"Two alterations and a rush repair."

"How much?"

She looked at me.

"Money face."

"Sorry."

She put the bundle down.

"Your day?"

I showed her the receipt.

She read it.

"One copper."

"Yes."

"You paid it."

"Yes."

"How much do you owe?"

I told her what the ledger had shown me.

Not because the exact figure suddenly fixed anything.

Because now I knew.

Her expression changed slightly.

"That's a lot."

"Yes."

"Payable?"

I thought.

"Yes."

Not quickly.

Not cleanly.

Not without food and oil and broken things and days when theatre paid
nothing.

But yes.

Then I told her Bren came back.

She stopped unwrapping the bundle.

"Actually Bren?"

"The man from the first night. Same close hair. Same left glove. He
answered to Bren before. I still don't have a surname."

"What did he want?"

"One copper."

Lyssa looked at the receipt.

Then at me.

"You paid Vale one."

"Yes."

"And Bren wanted one."

"Yes."

"Different."

"Very."

"What did Rinna say?"

"No."

"Then?"

"He said next time won't be one."

Lyssa was quiet.

"Did he touch anyone?"

"No."

"Break anything?"

"No."

"Follow you?"

"Not that I saw."

"Did you do the thing again?"

"What thing?"

She looked at me.

"The stupid impressive thing."

"No."

That hurt.

"Good."

"I told him I don't own the theatre."

"True."

"Painfully."

She finished opening the bundle.

Inside were two small rolls and a piece of smoked meat.

I stared.

"Paid and fed?"

"Customer's mother."

"Your work is improving."

"My customers are."

We ate.

The Vale receipt stayed on the table.

One copper less debt.

One copper Bren had not received.

Same amount.

Different arithmetic.

Outside, somebody laughed in the street.

A cart rattled past.

Nothing happened.

For tonight, that was enough.
