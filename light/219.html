LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 219

THE PURCHASER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The receipt survived the night.

So did I.

Both seemed worth noting.

I put the little strip of Vale paper inside the box where Lyssa kept
needles, two buttons that did not match anything we owned, and a piece
of blue thread she claimed was too useful to throw away.

"Why there?" she asked.

"Because you know where it is."

"I know where most things are."

"Exactly."

She was tying back her hair with a strip of cloth while looking at a
pale sleeve on the table.

"That is not a filing system."

"It is now."

She gave me the expression that meant she had decided not to improve me
before breakfast.

Nothing waited under the cup.

No Hessa note.

My arm was normal.

My shoulder was normal.

My wrist was normal.

Thirty, twenty-six, thirty-four.

I ate the last of one roll with a little smoked meat and tried not to
think about money.

This lasted almost a full bite.

One copper to Vale.

One copper Bren had wanted.

One copper Rinna had paid me after the show.

I still had to eat.

Lyssa had been correct.

Antonius had also been correct, which was more irritating.

"You have the face again," Lyssa said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're trying to spend money without moving."

"I'm eating."

"Poorly."

"I paid a debt yesterday."

"You paid one copper."

"That is how paying works."

"Yes. Slowly."

She picked up the sleeve.

"I have to take this back before the woman decides her shoulder changed
overnight."

"Can shoulders do that?"

"Customers can."

She left first.

I went to theatre at third bell.

The front door was open.

That was normal.

The shouting was not.

Not dangerous shouting.

Rinna shouting.

Different category.

"I ordered two tins."

A boy stood just inside the door with empty hands.

He looked approximately thirteen and deeply interested in being
somewhere else.

"I know."

"And wicks."

"I know."

"Where are they?"

The boy glanced at me as if I might be able to explain adulthood.

I could not.

"Master Olin said not to bring them."

Rinna stared.

Behind her, Jori had three lamps on the worktable with their caps off.

One was nearly empty.

Another had enough oil for a show if we did not believe in long endings.

The third had oil but a wick that looked like it had lost faith.

Nessa was sorting costumes.

Pell was eating something.

Of course.

I checked the cart once.

Replacement pin installed.

Chalk straight.

Then I went back to the argument.

Rinna said, "I ordered them yesterday morning."

"Yes."

"Cash on delivery."

"Yes."

"I have cash."

The boy looked miserable.

"I know."

"Then what changed?"

"Master Olin said not to bring them."

Rinna took a breath.

"What did Master Olin say?"

"That was it."

"Did he say why?"

The boy looked toward the street.

"Someone told him you weren't good for it."

Rinna became still.

Not frightened.

Still.

"Good for what?"

"Payment."

"I pay on delivery."

"I know."

"Does Olin?"

"Yes."

"Then what exactly was he told?"

The boy swallowed.

"That your money was spoken for. That there was trouble over it. Master
Olin said he wasn't getting in the middle."

Nobody said Bren.

That mattered.

I thought Bren.

That mattered less.

Rinna said, "Who told him?"

"I don't know."

"Did you see?"

"No."

"Did Olin?"

"I think."

"Think?"

"He talked to him."

"Today?"

"Early."

"At the shop?"

"Yes."

Rinna looked at Jori.

"How much do we have?"

Jori pointed.

"Enough for King if we cut the front lamps and don't burn the rear one
until house. Not enough for both pieces unless we get more."

"What are we playing?"

Teren answered from the board.

"King. Letter Box."

Rinna looked at the boy.

"Tell Olin I am coming to speak with him."

The boy's relief was immediate.

"Yes."

He left.

Rinna turned to me.

"You're coming."

"Why?"

"You walk."

"Questionable."

"You also heard what he said."

"Better."

She looked at the board, then at Jori.

"Can you hold setup?"

"Yes."

"Nessa?"

"Yes."

"Pell?"

Pell swallowed.

"Probably."

"Don't."

"I didn't do anything."

"Keep doing that."

We left.

The oil shop was not far.

Far enough to make the missing tins annoying.

It sat on a narrow market street with a painted yellow drop above the
door and a smell that made every spark in Carrow feel personally
interested.

Tins lined the rear wall.

Wicks hung in tied bundles.

A squat man with gray in his beard was arguing with a woman about lamp
smoke.

He saw Rinna.

His expression changed.

Not fear.

Regret.

Good.

"Rinna."

"Olin."

The woman looked between them, decided she had purchased enough lamp
smoke for one morning, and left.

Olin wiped his hands on his apron.

"I was going to send word."

"You did."

"I mean proper word."

"You sent a boy with no oil."

"I didn't want him carrying it if there was going to be trouble."

Rinna put both hands on the counter.

"There is no trouble between you and me."

"I know."

"Then why don't I have my oil?"

Olin looked at me.

"Who's this?"

"Greg."

His eyes went down.

Leg.

Back up.

Recognition, maybe.

Not personal.

Rumor had done work before.

Rinna said, "He heard your boy."

Olin sighed.

"A man came in."

"What man?"

"I don't know."

"Name?"

"No."

"What did he say?"

"Asked if I supplied the theatre."

Rinna waited.

Olin continued.

"I said sometimes. Oil. Wicks. Small things. He said I should be careful
taking promises from you because other people had a claim on the money."

"We don't buy on promise."

"I know."

"Did you tell him that?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

Olin rubbed his beard.

"That cash can disappear before it reaches a counter."

I felt something cold settle under my ribs.

Rinna did not move.

"Description."

"Thin fellow. Brown coat. Dark hair."

I looked at Rinna.

She looked at me.

Neither of us said Copper Kettle.

Olin noticed.

"You know him?"

"No," Rinna said.

Correct.

"Have you seen him before?"

"No."

Also correct.

The Copper Kettle worker had.

Not us.

Rinna said, "Anything else?"

"Not old. Narrow face. Talks quiet."

"Gloves?"

I asked.

Rinna turned her head.

I stopped.

Olin thought anyway.

"Don't remember."

Fine.

Rinna said, "Did he threaten you?"

"No."

"Ask you not to sell to us?"

"Not exactly."

"What exactly?"

"He said if I wanted to keep my money, I shouldn't have goods walking
toward people whose money belonged somewhere else."

"That's not exactly subtle."

Olin shrugged.

"I don't know your business."

"You know how I pay you."

"Yes."

"Have I failed to pay you?"

"No."

"Then you do know that part of my business."

Olin's mouth tightened.

"I don't need trouble for two tins of oil."

There it was.

Not loyalty.

Not betrayal.

Two tins of oil.

A merchant did not need to love Bren to become useful to him.

He only needed to dislike uncertainty.

Rinna asked, "Will you sell me the order now?"

Olin hesitated.

"Cash now."

"It was cash on delivery."

"Cash here."

"Same price?"

Another hesitation.

That one irritated me more.

"Olin."

"I had to send the boy twice."

"You sent him once."

"He came back."

"From my theatre."

"That's twice walking."

Rinna stared.

Olin sighed.

"Same price."

Good.

He brought out two tins and a tied bundle of wicks.

Rinna counted the money onto the counter.

I watched.

I could not help it.

Coins had become louder.

Olin took them.

Then Rinna said, "We need another small tin."

Jori had not asked for another.

Olin knew it.

"So you don't have to come back tomorrow?"

"So I don't have to find out tomorrow whether someone else has told you
my money belongs to him."

Olin looked uncomfortable.

"That costs more."

"Yes."

He named the amount.

Rinna looked at me.

"How much do you have?"

I stared.

"This feels like a trap."

"How much?"

I had the copper from yesterday.

Other money existed.

I was not reconstructing my purse in a lamp-oil shop.

"Enough for the small tin."

"Buy it."

"Company expense?"

"Yes."

"Recorded?"

She looked at me.

"Vale ruined you in one meeting."

"Record it."

"I will reimburse you."

Good.

I put my copper on Olin's counter.

Not the Vale copper.

That one was already gone.

This one had been theatre pay for less than a day.

Now it was lamp oil.

Olin pushed the small tin toward me.

Money became objects very quickly when allowed.

Rinna took the wick bundle.

I carried the small tin.

She carried one larger tin.

Olin came around the counter with the other.

"I'll send this."

Rinna looked at him.

"With who?"

"My boy."

"Fine."

Olin paused.

"If the man comes back?"

"Tell him nothing about what I buy, what I pay, or when."

"That's easy."

"And send me word."

Less easy.

Olin frowned.

"I don't want in this."

"You're already in it enough to cancel my delivery."

That landed.

He looked at the tins.

Then at Rinna.

"If he comes back, I'll send word."

"Good."

We left.

On the street I said, "Thin face. Brown coat."

"Yes."

"Dark hair."

"Yes."

"Same description as the Kettle."

"Yes."

"So."

"So the descriptions match."

I waited.

Rinna kept walking.

"That's all?"

"That's what we know."

"He asked about the cart. Then the money. Now he talks to Olin."

"If it's the same man."

"Olin's description matches."

"Yes."

"You're exhausting."

"Carry the oil."

I did.

At the theatre, Jori received the tins like we had returned from war
carrying medicine.

"How bad?"

Rinna handed him the wicks.

"Annoying."

"Cost?"

"Normal order plus another tin."

Jori looked at me.

"Why are you holding it like it insulted you?"

"I paid for this one."

Rinna said, "Company did."

"Eventually."

She reached into her purse and gave me a copper.

I looked at it.

"That was fast."

"I don't want you developing a heroic relationship with lamp oil."

"Too late."

I put the copper away.

The transaction had lasted less than half an hour.

My money had left.

Company money had returned.

Oil remained.

Accounting.

I understood Antonius slightly better and resented him for it.

Rinna gathered Teren, Jori, Nessa, Veya, and Davin near the worktable.

Not a council.

They were the people standing closest who mattered.

She told them exactly what Olin had said.

No embellishment.

Pell drifted closer.

Rinna pointed at him.

"You can listen if you don't improve it."

"I never improve anything."

Marek, from behind him, said, "Correct."

Rinna ignored both.

"For now, if a supplier gets told we don't pay, they check with me
before changing an order. If somebody asks about private jobs, payment,
carts, or what we carry, they get nothing useful. I'll tell Venn. Talla
already knows enough to send word if she hears the same thing."

Greg, I thought.

Do something.

This was something.

Small.

Boring.

Useful.

I said, "What about other suppliers?"

"I'll speak to the ones we actually use."

"Today?"

"The ones I can."

"I can carry notes."

Rinna considered.

"After set."

Good.

Not investigation.

Work.

We set.

King first.

The extra oil meant the lamps burned normally.

Nobody in the audience knew they had almost been darker because a thin
man in a brown coat had said something in an oil shop.

That was probably how most functioning things worked.

A dozen small failures prevented before anyone noticed.

I had no role in King.

I worked the rear cloth and moved one stool.

Letter Box gave me a tiny part Teren had added to the board while I was
gone.

MESSENGER.

Three lines.

Of course.

I had already been Messenger once.

The universe lacked title discipline.

I complained.

Teren said, "Then don't put it on a chapter."

I stared at him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

I wore no coat.

I carried a folded letter.

My job was to enter, hand it to Sellen, say the recipient was not home,
answer one question, and leave.

Before that, the King had its best moment.

One of the front benches had developed a squeak.

Not a dangerous squeak.

A conversational one.

Every time a broad man near the end shifted his weight, the bench
answered.

Marek was in the middle of accusing Hara's petitioner of changing her
story.

"You said there were three."

Squeak.

Hara looked toward the bench.

"There were."

"You said four yesterday."

Squeak.

Hara said, "My witness has arrived."

The broad man froze.

The audience laughed.

Marek looked at him.

"Does he always testify like that?"

The man carefully shifted.

Squeak.

Hara folded her hands.

"Only under pressure."

That got them.

Marek let the laugh finish.

Then he said, "Remove the witness."

The broad man lifted both feet slightly off the floor.

The bench went silent.

Hara said, "Intimidation."

Bigger laugh.

Marek lost the corner of his mouth for half a second.

Strongest moment.

Not mine.

Good.

Letter Box started after a short reset.

My entrance came clean.

I handed Sellen the folded letter.

"The house was empty."

"Did you knock?"

"Yes."

"How many times?"

"Enough to become suspicious."

Small laugh.

Written.

"Did anyone answer?"

"No."

"Then why are you suspicious?"

"Because I was still there."

Another small laugh.

I left.

Adequate.

No miracle.

No Bren.

No brown coat in the house that I could prove mattered.

Afterward, while Nessa packed costumes, Rinna gave me three folded
notes.

One for a cloth seller.

One for the woman who occasionally repaired lamp glass.

One for Venn at the Copper Kettle.

"What do they say?"

"None of your business."

"I'm carrying them."

"Then carry them."

"Are they threatening?"

"No."

"Poetry?"

"No."

"Debt confessions?"

"Greg."

I took the notes.

The cloth seller was closest.

She read Rinna's note, snorted, and said, "You pay me."

"Apparently that is now news."

"If someone tells me otherwise, I'll ask."

Good.

The lamp-glass woman was not there.

Her son took the note.

I did not interrogate him about thin men.

Growth.

The Copper Kettle was busier.

Venn stood near the front counting mugs.

She read the note.

Her face tightened.

"Same man?"

"Maybe."

"What happened?"

I told her the narrow version.

Oil delivery canceled.

Olin described thin face, brown coat, dark hair.

Venn nodded slowly.

"That sounds like him."

"Sounds."

"Yes."

Good.

Everyone had become Hessa.

Terrible.

Venn folded the note.

"If he comes back, I'll send someone."

"Rinna said don't tell him anything useful."

"I already didn't."

"Continue being excellent."

She looked at me.

"Do you want a beer?"

"Is it free?"

"No."

"Then I want it less."

I left.

Back at the theatre, Rinna was still there.

So were Jori and Davin.

The cart chalk remained straight.

The replacement pin remained installed.

No one had broken anything while I carried notes.

I reported.

Cloth seller would check.

Lamp-glass note delivered to son.

Venn said Olin's description sounded like the same man she had seen.

Rinna wrote one line on her small paper.

KETTLE / OLIN DESCRIPTION MATCHES.

Then another.

SUPPLIERS CHECK WITH US.

That was all.

No: BREN'S MAN.

No: LOOKOUT.

No: GANG.

I pointed.

"You could write 'probably.'"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the paper survives us being stupid."

That was annoyingly good.

"Did Hessa teach you that?"

"No."

"She'd like you."

"Don't threaten me."

We closed.

I earned one copper for the show and the work around it.

Rinna did not pay extra for walking oil through Carrow.

I considered filing a complaint.

Then remembered she had reimbursed the oil.

At home, Lyssa was trimming loose thread from a shoulder seam.

Different dress.

Different shoulder.

Apparently the city was coming apart one upper arm at a time.

"How was theatre?" she asked.

"Expensive."

She looked up.

I told her about the canceled oil delivery.

The man.

Olin.

The description.

The extra tin.

The notes.

Lyssa listened.

"Did Bren tell Olin not to sell to you?"

"No."

"Did the man say Bren?"

"No."

"Same man as the Kettle?"

"Venn thinks the description sounds like him. Olin says thin face, brown
coat, dark hair."

"So maybe."

"Yes."

She cut a thread.

"How much did it cost?"

"More than one copper would have."

"That doesn't make paying Bren cheaper."

"I know."

I did.

Talla's yard had paid once.

Then more.

One copper was not the price.

It was the first answer.

Still, I hated that refusing it had already cost real money.

Oil.

Time.

Walking.

Attention.

Three notes.

A supplier who had become uncertain enough to cancel an order he knew we
paid for in cash.

Bren had not needed to break anything today.

Maybe Bren had not done anything today.

That distinction remained.

I hated that too.

Lyssa put the dress aside.

"What are you going to do?"

"Rinna already did something."

"What?"

"She told the businesses we actually use to check with her instead of
listening to strangers."

"Good."

"I carried notes."

"Heroic."

"I carried oil too."

"Then they'll sing about you."

"They should."

She smiled.

I ate bread.

No note came from Hessa.

No runner came from Bren.

No one knocked.

The theatre had spent more than one copper refusing a one-copper demand.

It had also opened, performed, paid people, bought oil, and told its
neighbors how to reach it.

That was not winning.

It was work.

For now, work was action.
