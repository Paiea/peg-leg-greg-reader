TEXT READER · CHAPTER 220

THE LANDLORD

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I bought bread on the way to the theatre.

This should not have required a decision.

It did.

The baker named the price. I paid it. He gave me bread. Nobody congratulated me for preserving my ability to remain alive while owing Antonius Vale money.

Progress.

I also bought a small cake of soap because ours had become a translucent insult beside the basin. That hurt more.

Bread was food. Food was defensible.

Soap felt optional right up until I remembered Lyssa leaning away from my shirt the previous evening and saying, "You work in a theatre full of people."

She had not actually said I smelled.

She had created a structure in which I could reach the intended conclusion.

Theatre was corrupting everyone.

I put the soap in my coat, hung the bread from one crutch in a cloth bag, and continued west.

No Hessa note had been under the cup that morning. No magic. No symptoms. Nothing new in my right arm, hand, shoulder, residual limb, or phantom foot. Thirty draws. Twenty-six deliberate shaping attempts. Thirty-four supervised external attempts. One far response observed once and not established as anything more useful than that.

The numbers stayed where they were.

Good.

The theatre did not.

A boy was waiting outside when I arrived.

Not one of ours.

Olin's delivery boy.

He recognized me first.

"You're the oil man."

I stopped.

"I carried one tin."

"Oil man."

"I have done other things."

He shrugged.

I looked at the open front door.

"Why are you outside?"

"Woman's busy."

That could describe Rinna at every hour I had known her.

Inside, someone was dragging a bench across the floor. Jori was on a ladder adjusting a lamp bracket. Nessa had a green coat spread over two chairs and was arguing with Pell about whether a tear had existed yesterday. Marek stood onstage holding a wooden cane and looking deeply pleased with himself.

Normal.

Good.

The boy said, "Master Olin sent me."

That changed the morning.

I looked at him again.

"The man came back?"

"Yes."

"Brown coat?"

"Yes."

"Thin face?"

"Yes."

"Dark hair?"

"Yes."

"Quiet?"

The boy thought.

"Not loud."

Better answer.

"Did Olin tell you to tell me?"

"He said tell the woman."

"Excellent."

I moved aside and pointed through the door.

"Let's find the woman."

Rinna was in the rear passage counting folded handbills while Davin tried to convince her that twelve and fourteen were different numbers for a reason.

"They are both enough," she said.

"For different streets."

"People can walk."

"That is not how handbills work."

"Then make more."

Davin looked at me.

"Help."

"No."

"Coward."

"Olin sent word."

Rinna stopped counting.

The boy appeared behind me.

She put the handbills down.

"What happened?"

The boy told it badly.

That was not criticism. He was thirteen and had apparently been sent across Carrow because an adult merchant did not want to leave his shop. He started with the man coming in, skipped to Olin getting angry, returned to a question about oil, forgot whether the man had touched the counter, remembered he had not, and then explained that Master Olin had said not to answer anything.

Rinna did not interrupt until he finished.

Then she asked, "What did he ask? Exact if you can."

The boy looked at the ceiling.

"If you bought the oil."

"How?"

"He said, 'She bought it anyway?'"

Rinna nodded once.

"Then?"

"Master Olin said he doesn't talk about customers."

"Good."

The boy looked surprised by the praise.

"Then he asked if you paid cash."

"Exact?"

"I think, 'She paid cash for it?'"

"What did Olin say?"

"Same thing. Customers."

Rinna glanced at me.

I said nothing.

The boy continued.

"Then he asked if you were still doing shows. Master Olin said if he wanted a handbill he could get one somewhere else."

Davin made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Rinna did not.

"Anything else?"

The boy hesitated.

"He asked where you keep it."

Rinna's face changed.

Not fear.

Attention.

"Keep what?"

"Money."

"Exact words."

The boy squeezed his eyes shut.

"Does the woman keep the money at the hall?"

Nobody spoke for a moment.

From the stage, Marek shouted, "I am not limping. The character is limping."

Nessa shouted back, "The character is doing it badly."

The boy looked toward the noise.

Rinna said, "What did Olin answer?"

"He told him to buy oil or leave."

"Did he buy oil?"

"No."

"Did he leave?"

"Yes."

"Did he threaten Olin?"

"No."

"Did he say Bren?"

"No."

"Did he say theatre money was spoken for again?"

"No."

"Did he ask about carts?"

"No."

"Did he ask about Greg?"

The boy looked at me.

"Oil man?"

I closed my eyes.

"Greg," Rinna said.

"No."

Good.

Rinna took a scrap of paper from the shelf and wrote while the boy watched.

MAN RETURNED TO OLIN.

ASKED:
SHE BOUGHT IT ANYWAY?
SHE PAID CASH FOR IT?
STILL DOING SHOWS?
DOES THE WOMAN KEEP THE MONEY AT THE HALL?

OLIN GAVE NO ANSWERS.

She looked at the boy.

"Anything wrong?"

He read slowly.

"He said 'she bought it anyway,' not 'did she.'"

Rinna scratched out the first line and rewrote it.

I liked him.

"Anything else?" she asked.

"No."

"Good. Tell Olin thank you."

The boy did not leave.

Rinna waited.

He shifted his feet.

"I saw him later."

There it was.

I felt the old part of my mind stand up too quickly.

Rinna said, "Where?"

"I had the glass order for the woman near South Steps. He was ahead of me after I came off Narrow Street."

"Doing what?"

"Walking."

"With anyone?"

"No."

"Following anyone?"

"Don't know."

"Which way?"

"Toward Mason's Cut."

My hands tightened on the crutch grips.

Mason's Cut.

Grit on Bren's boot.

A detail I had used before I understood whether it mattered.

Now the same place-name appeared again, carried by a boy who sold lamp oil and had no reason to care about my theories.

I wanted to ask how far.

Which entrance.

Whether the man turned.

Whether he met anyone.

Whether he went into a building.

Whether the boy saw the left side of his coat.

Whether there was mud on his boots.

Rinna said, "You saw him walking toward Mason's Cut."

"Yes."

"Did you see him enter Mason's Cut?"

"No. I turned for South Steps."

"Good."

I hated the word good.

It was correct.

Rinna added:

BOY LATER SAW HIM WALKING TOWARD MASON'S CUT. DID NOT SEE HIM ENTER.

Then she folded the paper once.

"Thank Olin for me."

The boy nodded.

I said, "Wait."

Rinna looked at me.

I knew what she thought I was about to do.

Possibly because I was thinking about doing it.

"I'm not following him."

"Excellent."

"I was going to give the boy bread."

The boy looked interested.

Rinna looked at my bag.

"Your bread?"

I reconsidered the entire moral structure of generosity.

I tore off a piece.

The boy accepted it.

"Thank you, oil man."

"Leave."

He did.

Rinna picked up her handbills again.

I stared at her.

"That's it?"

"No."

"Good."

"You're Landlord today."

I stared harder.

"That is not what I meant."

"I know."

She handed me three pages.

LANDLORD.

Nine lines.

I looked toward the stage where Marek was still practicing the bad limp.

"Which play?"

"The Cousin."

"I haven't done Landlord."

"You have watched it."

"That is not the same thing."

"Correct. Teren wants you in ten minutes."

"What are we doing about the man?"

"Talking to two more people we actually do business with. I am doing that. You are being a landlord."

"He asked where you keep the money."

"Yes."

"That is more specific."

"Yes."

"Mason's Cut."

"He walked toward it."

I exhaled.

"You enjoy this."

"No. I enjoy you not making my paper worse."

Fair.

The Cousin was a stupid play.

This was not criticism either. It knew.

A man pretended to be his own wealthy cousin to avoid paying three separate people. The lie became difficult because the cousin had supposedly rented a room from my character, the Landlord, who had never seen him. Marek played the liar. Hara played his sister. Pell played a creditor with an alarming hat.

My job was mostly to want rent.

I had experience.

Teren put me beside the stage door and ran the scene once.

I entered too early.

"Again," he said.

"I thought the knock was mine."

"It was the creditor."

"There are two knocks."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Doors."

Terrible explanation.

Again.

This time I waited for the second knock and entered correctly.

Marek turned toward me with the broad, desperate smile of a man whose entire life had become paperwork.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

Teren said, "No."

I stopped.

"That is my line."

"You're waiting for it to be funny."

I looked at him.

"It is one word."

"Exactly."

"How do I not wait with one word?"

"Want the rent."

I hated theatre.

Again.

Marek smiled.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

Better.

Not because I changed the word.

Because I interrupted the smile.

Marek reacted faster.

"Of course. My cousin has it."

"Then I want your cousin."

"He is away."

"Then I want the rent."

Teren pointed at me.

"Keep that."

"It is written."

"I know."

Excellent profession.

We worked for another twenty minutes.

I missed a chair on the first turn and clipped it with one crutch. Nessa moved it half a foot and told me not to pretend the chair had attacked.

"It was in my route."

"Your route was wrong."

"Hostile furniture."

"Wrong route."

She won.

By midday Rinna had returned from somewhere with dust on her hem and two fewer handbills.

She did not announce a breakthrough.

She told Jori that the lamp-glass woman would send word if anyone asked about theatre purchases. She told Davin that the cart repair man had been told the same thing. Then she put the remaining handbills back on the shelf and asked whether the front hinge was still sticking.

It was.

We worked on the hinge.

That was the strange part.

The man had asked where the money was kept.

The hinge still needed oil.

Both facts occupied the same building.

By afternoon the house began filling.

The Cousin was paired with a shorter piece I had seen but never performed in. I only had Landlord. Good.

My nine lines did not deserve panic.

I panicked modestly anyway.

The first scenes went cleanly.

Hara got the first real laugh by entering with a basket, taking one look at Marek's fake mustache, and saying nothing for so long that he finally touched it to check whether it had fallen off.

It had not.

The audience understood her entire opinion before she spoke.

When she finally said, "Cousin," the room broke.

Marek almost lost his next line.

Almost.

He recovered by bowing too deeply, which made the mustache lift at one corner.

Hara watched it happen.

Then looked at the audience.

Bigger laugh.

Nothing I did came close.

Good.

My entrance came after the creditor's second knock.

I heard the first.

Waited.

Heard the second.

Entered.

Marek smiled.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

The audience laughed.

Not much.

Enough.

I continued.

Competent.

Ordinary.

Then I made the mistake.

Marek said his cousin had gone to visit an aunt.

My line was, "Then your aunt may pay me."

I said, "Then your cousin may pay me."

Which was the entire problem.

Marek stared at me.

I stared back.

The audience waited.

He said, "He would, if he were not visiting my aunt."

Right.

I could have tried to repair it cleverly.

I did not.

"Then your aunt may pay me."

A small laugh.

The scene continued.

Backstage, Teren caught my sleeve as I passed.

"You repeated him."

"I know."

"Listen."

"I was listening."

"To yourself."

Annoying.

Accurate.

Then he let go because another scene was happening and my personal development was not the theatre's emergency.

Excellent.

The final Landlord scene went better.

No invention.

No rescue.

I entered on the correct knock, wanted rent, did not collide with furniture, and left when the scene ended.

After curtain, the audience became coats and voices and feet moving toward the street.

The company became work again.

Benches shifted.

Props returned to boxes.

Marek peeled off the mustache and stuck it to Pell's sleeve.

Pell did not notice for almost a minute.

Hara did.

She told no one.

I respected her enormously.

Rinna paid me the normal copper.

I put it away.

I did not immediately imagine it crossing Antonius Vale's counter.

Not because the debt had stopped existing.

Because bread existed too.

Soap existed.

Oil existed.

Crutch tips wore down.

Clothes tore.

People who planned to repay debts still had to remain people long enough to do it.

This was obvious.

I had apparently needed several adults to explain it.

Rinna was at the rear table with the folded paper from that morning.

I stopped beside her.

"Any more word?"

"No."

"Did anyone else get asked?"

"Not that I know."

"You're going to keep talking to suppliers?"

"People we actually use."

"Not everyone."

"I don't have time to create a citywide society for people annoyed by strangers."

"Could charge dues."

"Go home."

I looked at the paper.

"The money question matters."

"Yes."

"More than the oil question."

"Maybe."

I smiled.

She saw it.

"Don't."

"You said maybe."

"About importance, not identity."

"The paper survives us being stupid."

"I regret teaching you anything."

"You didn't teach me."

"Worse."

I went home.

Lyssa was not there.

That was becoming normal enough that I did not sit around inventing explanations.

I washed.

The new soap worked offensively well.

I ate some bread.

I considered the copper from the show.

Then put it with the rest of my money.

Not Vale money.

Not food money.

Money.

I could decide what it became when I needed to.

Lyssa arrived near dusk carrying a narrow wrapped bundle under one arm and a small packet in the other.

She put both on the table.

"You bought soap."

"I have been financially reckless."

She looked at me.

"Use more next time."

Cruel.

She unwrapped the smaller packet. Dried fruit.

I forgave her.

"Your day?" she asked.

I told her.

Olin's man returned.

Olin gave him nothing.

The questions.

Oil.

Cash.

Shows.

Where Rinna kept the money.

The boy later saw him walking toward Mason's Cut.

Did not see him enter.

No Bren name.

No threat.

No direct connection proven.

Lyssa ate one piece of fruit.

"And you didn't follow him."

"No."

She looked suspicious.

"I considered it professionally."

"What profession?"

"Landlord."

"What?"

I explained the play.

She laughed at the wrong part, which was apparently me saying the cousin should pay when the entire scene already depended on the cousin not being there.

"It was one line."

"It was the important line."

"There were nine."

"Eight, then."

I took dried fruit before she could become worse.

After a moment she said, "You heard about him before he cost you something this time."

I stopped chewing.

That was true.

Not victory.

Not safety.

Olin had simply done what Rinna asked.

A merchant had refused to answer questions about a customer. A boy had carried a message. Rinna had written down the words. Two more businesses knew how to reach her.

Small things.

Ordinary things.

Useful things.

"Yes," I said.

Lyssa nodded and went back to opening her larger bundle.

Inside was a dark sleeve with three chalk marks near the cuff.

Her work continued too.

No Hessa note came that night.

No one knocked.

Somewhere in Carrow, a thin-faced man in a brown coat had asked where the theatre kept its money.

I did not know where he slept.

I did not know who paid him, if anyone did.

I did not know whether Mason's Cut meant anything beyond the direction he had walked.

But for the first time, he had come looking for information and left without getting it.

We had gotten his questions instead.
