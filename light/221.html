TEXT READER · CHAPTER 221

THE PARTICIPANT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The note was under the cup.

Lyssa found it before I did because she had moved the cup to get at the last heel of bread.

"Your physician has learned table manners," she said.

"She isn't my physician."

"Your terrifying woman from the Guild has learned table manners."

"Better."

Lyssa handed me the folded paper and took the bread.

It contained six words.

AFTER MIDDAY. SAME ROOM. EAT FIRST.

Under that, smaller:

NO PRACTICE.

I turned the note over.

Nothing.

"Romantic," Lyssa said.

"She knows what I like."

"Restrictions?"

"Food."

"Cruel."

She was already dressed to go out, a wrapped bundle of finished work tied under one arm and another smaller packet on the table. I had learned not to ask whether the second packet was coming or going unless I wanted an explanation involving three customers, two sets of measurements, a woman who changed her mind about cuffs, and a button seller who apparently lied as a profession.

I ate.

That was easier.

Thirty successful supervised minimal draws. Twenty-six deliberate shaping attempts. Thirty-four supervised external-effect attempts. Intermediate response repeated. Far response observed once. Far response not established. No range. No independent anything.

The numbers had been sitting still long enough that I had stopped carrying them in the front of my head.

Hessa had not.

Lyssa tied her hair back, checked the bundle, then looked at me.

"Theatre first?"

"Until midday."

"Then Guild?"

"After midday."

"That is what I asked."

"Then yes."

She took the smaller packet too.

"I'm late because of you."

"I have done nothing."

"You spoke."

"Unreasonable standard."

She kissed my cheek on the way past.

"Eat again before you go."

"Hessa wrote that."

"Then two women agree. Be afraid."

The door shut behind her.

I was.

Not about the experiment.

Mostly.

The theatre had no messages waiting when I arrived.

I checked without pretending I had not checked.

Rinna was at the front table with a stack of tickets, a chipped cup, and the folded paper from Olin's boy tucked beneath a ledger. Nobody from the lamp-glass woman. Nobody from the cart repair man. Nobody from Olin.

No new warning.

No brown coat.

No Bren.

The building had survived the night despite my absence from Mason's Cut.

I tried not to take that personally.

Rinna looked up.

"You're here."

"Usually."

"Until?"

"Midday. Hessa wants me after."

"Magic?"

"Probably."

"Useful magic?"

"Absolutely not."

"Good."

She pointed toward the stage.

"Jori needs hands."

I lifted both crutches.

"I have made a terrible scheduling error."

"He knows which ones."

Jori needed me to sit at the edge of the stage and hold the lower end of a painted wall flat while he replaced two leather ties along the back. This was excellent work because it required almost no walking and allowed me to criticize him continuously.

"That knot is ugly."

"You're holding canvas."

"I can see."

"Then look somewhere else."

"It remains ugly when I do that."

He pulled the tie tight.

The wall was part of a room that had been a rich man's parlor, a magistrate's office, a widow's dining room, and once, through desperation, the interior of a ship.

The painted window had survived all of them.

The ship had been difficult.

"Higher," Jori said.

I lifted my end.

"Not you. The canvas."

"We are currently connected."

"Less."

I lowered it.

"There."

For the next hour I was a clamp with opinions.

It was restful.

Marek crossed behind us carrying three hats and wearing none of them. Nessa chased him down because one had a pin still in it. Hara sat in the first row going through pages with Teren. Someone in the rear passage dropped a box and swore with enough conviction that I briefly considered applauding.

Theatre.

Rinna passed once.

I said, "Anything?"

She knew what I meant.

"No."

Then she kept walking.

That was all the threat received that morning.

Good.

At midday Jori released me from service after deciding the wall could once again survive being furniture.

Rinna gave me half a heel of cheese from a paper wrapping and pointed at Hessa's note when I tried to refuse.

"How do you know what that says?"

"You told me she said eat first."

"I regret sharing information."

"Learn from Olin."

I ate the cheese.

Then I went to the Guild.

The same room was waiting.

Hessa had meant that literally.

Same narrow table. Same board. Same chair. Same glass shield. Same paper-fin assembly. Same control beside it. The far position had been marked on the floor with two short chalk lines that I remembered from the last session.

I stopped in the doorway.

"You preserved it."

Hessa looked up from her notes.

"Yes."

"Nobody needed the room?"

"They did."

"And?"

"They used another room."

I considered the institutional power required to make that sentence happen.

"I want to become you."

"Sit down."

Apparently training had begun.

I put my crutches where she indicated, not where I remembered putting them. She checked the chair position against a mark on the floor, then checked my right foot, the angle of my knee, the position of my hips, and the distance from my right forearm to the table edge.

"Same shirt?" I asked.

She looked at me.

"No."

"Then the experiment is ruined."

"If your shirt moves the paper through glass, I will revise several things."

Fair.

She checked my right hand.

"Anything since last session?"

"No."

"Thumb?"

"Normal."

"Wrist?"

"Normal."

"Forearm?"

"Normal."

"Shoulder?"

"Normal."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

"Phantom?"

I waited a moment before answering because it was there once I looked for it, the ordinary absent foot occupying its impossible position without pain.

"Present. Quiet."

She wrote that down.

"Sleep?"

"Enough."

"Food?"

"Bread. Cheese."

"Water?"

"Yes."

She slid a cup toward me anyway.

I drank.

"Question?" she asked.

"Can far repeat."

"Under what conditions?"

I looked at the apparatus.

"These. Away only. Same position."

"Good."

I waited.

"That's all?"

"That is the question."

"No intermediate first?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I am not asking whether intermediate still works today."

I looked at the far chalk marks.

"No closer control?"

"The control is there."

She pointed to the second paper assembly beside the target.

"You mean distance control."

"Yes."

"No."

I liked experiments more when I was allowed to add things to them.

That was probably why Hessa ran them.

The minimal draw came first.

Same as always now, which was a dangerous phrase to think about anything involving mana.

Hessa gave the instruction. I settled, found the familiar internal route, and drew the smallest amount I could manage under supervision.

The sensation arrived cleanly enough to recognize and small enough not to become interesting.

Hessa watched my hand, then my face.

"Return."

I returned it.

No lingering thumb sensation. No unexpected spread. No catch in the wrist.

Thirty-one successful supervised minimal draws.

She waited.

I waited.

Nothing developed.

Then deliberate shaping.

Not a new shape. Not a new direction. Not an attempt to make anything stronger.

She gave me the same narrow setup we had been using before external work, and I followed it as carefully as I could.

The problem with doing something twenty-seven times was that familiarity started pretending to be precision.

Hessa stopped me halfway through.

"Again."

I let the shaping collapse under instruction.

"What?"

"Your shoulder moved."

"My shoulder is attached."

"It lifted before the change."

I thought back.

She was right.

I had braced without noticing.

"Does that count?"

"No."

"Then I'm still at twenty-six."

"Yes."

That annoyed me more than it should have.

She knew.

"Do you want a larger number or a usable record?"

"Both."

"Choose."

"Usable record."

"Again."

The second shaping sequence was cleaner. Right hand quiet. Wrist quiet. Forearm working but not straining. Shoulder down. Hessa watched the whole thing, then had me return.

"Count it," she said.

Twenty-seven supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Then the glass went between me and the paper.

Far position.

Away only.

The paper fin looked ridiculous from there.

That was not scientifically relevant.

It was still true.

Hessa checked the target, then the control. She closed the door. Waited for footsteps in the corridor to pass. Checked the glass edge. Checked the paper again.

"Attempt thirty-five," she said. "Far. Away only. Begin when ready."

I settled.

The first difficulty was not mana.

It was wanting the paper to move.

That sounded stupid because moving the paper was the point.

But wanting it to move made me watch for movement, and watching for movement made every tiny shift in shadow feel important. I had learned enough to distrust enthusiasm.

I looked where Hessa had told me to look.

I drew under supervision.

Shaped.

Held the requested direction.

Away.

Nothing.

The target did not move.

The control did not move.

My right forearm tightened near the end, but not enough for Hessa to stop me before the interval finished.

"Return."

I returned.

She checked both fins.

"No observed response," she said.

I nodded.

Attempt thirty-five.

Far.

No observed response.

Control unchanged.

She gave me a longer rest than I wanted.

I did not complain because complaining sometimes made rests longer.

I flexed my right fingers once when she told me to. Rolled the shoulder once. Drank water.

"Forearm?"

"Worked. Fine now."

"Shoulder?"

"Fine."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

"Phantom?"

I checked.

"Same."

She wrote.

The second external attempt began with less hope.

This was probably useful.

Attempt thirty-six.

Far.

Away only.

I drew.

Shaped.

Held.

For several seconds nothing happened.

Then the target fin turned.

Not much.

A small rotation, the leading edge shifting away from me while the rear edge followed less cleanly. It was not the straight little displacement I wanted. It was not dramatic. I almost doubted it had happened until Hessa said, "Return."

I returned immediately.

The control had not moved.

Hessa came around the table without speaking. She crouched beside the board, looked through the glass from one side, then the other, checked the paper's position against the marks, and wrote for a long time.

I kept my mouth shut for almost all of it.

"Observed?" I asked.

"Yes."

That was enough to make my chest tighten.

"Away?"

"Net away. With rotation."

"Like thirty-three?"

She looked at the earlier page.

"Similar category. Not identical movement."

I wanted to ask whether that meant far worked.

I had known Hessa too long.

Instead I asked, "What does it establish?"

"Nothing yet that the third attempt cannot make less simple."

There she was.

Attempt thirty-six.

Far.

Small rotational response with net displacement away.

Control unchanged.

Far response observed again.

Across more than one session now.

I could feel the sentence trying to grow teeth.

Hessa did not feed it.

She made me rest.

This time my forearm stayed faintly warm after I opened my hand. Not pain. Not tingling. Work.

She pressed along the muscle and asked whether anything changed.

"No."

"Shoulder?"

"Fine."

"Hand?"

"Fine."

"Thumb?"

"Fine."

She waited another minute anyway.

"Third?" I asked.

"If the forearm returns to baseline."

I sat there and attempted to make my forearm less interesting through moral character.

It did not work.

Time did.

The warmth faded.

Hessa checked again.

"Third. Then stop."

"Even if nothing happens?"

"Yes."

"Even if something happens?"

"Yes."

"You remove all incentive."

"Good."

Attempt thirty-seven.

Far.

Away only.

The room had become too familiar by then. Glass. Paper. Chalk. Board. My hand on the same part of the table. Hessa standing at the same angle. A cart rattling somewhere outside the Guild building and fading down the street.

I drew.

Shaped.

Away.

Nothing.

I held until Hessa's interval ended.

Nothing.

The control remained still.

The target remained still.

My shoulder started to rise near the end.

I noticed it this time and lowered it.

Hessa noticed me noticing.

That did not make the paper move either.

"Return."

I returned.

She checked the apparatus.

"No observed response."

"One of three."

"Today."

"Two far responses total."

"Observed in separate sessions under the current apparatus."

"That sounds longer."

"It is more accurate."

I leaned back carefully.

"So far works."

"No."

Worth trying.

"Far has repeated."

She considered that.

"A far response has now been observed in more than one session under the current apparatus."

"That is what I said with fewer words."

"No. You removed response, observed, session, and apparatus."

"They were implied."

"That is where bad records breed."

I looked at the paper fin.

It had moved once today.

Once out of three far attempts today. Once before. Failures around both.

Intermediate had behaved better when we tested it.

The far position had not become a range. It had become a place where something had happened twice.

Annoyingly, those were different statements.

Hessa wrote the session summary while I drank the rest of the water.

I could read part of it upside down.

FAR RESPONSE OBSERVED ACROSS MORE THAN ONE SESSION UNDER CURRENT APPARATUS.

FAR RELIABILITY NOT ESTABLISHED.

INTERMEDIATE REMAINS MORE RELIABLE ON CURRENT EVIDENCE.

NO RANGE ESTABLISHED.

NOT GENERALIZED.

I pointed.

"You could have written 'Greg is magnificent.'"

"Unsupported."

"Cruel."

"Also irrelevant."

She checked my hand again before letting me stand.

The session ended at thirty-one successful supervised minimal draws, twenty-seven supervised deliberate shaping attempts, and thirty-seven supervised external-effect attempts.

Restrictions did not change.

I asked because apparently humiliation was part of the apparatus.

"Independent draw?"

"No."

"Paper?"

"No."

"Coins?"

"No."

"Thread?"

"No."

"Larger object?"

"No."

"Practice?"

"No."

"One tiny celebration?"

"You may eat."

"Again?"

"Frequently, if possible."

I took my crutches.

"Next question?"

"Not today."

"You have one."

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"Not today."

There were times I suspected Hessa had entered medicine only because interrogation was not respectable enough.

Outside, Carrow had continued without waiting for my evidence.

A wagon blocked half the street. Two apprentices were arguing over a coil of rope. A woman with a basket shouted at a man who had apparently stepped in front of her and then committed the greater crime of defending himself.

I went back toward the theatre because I still had part of the day.

Rinna was at the same front table.

The paper from Olin's boy was gone from beneath the ledger.

I noticed.

She noticed me noticing.

"Put away," she said.

"Anything new?"

"No."

Good.

She looked at me.

"Useful magic?"

I considered Hessa's page.

"One piece of paper moved a little."

"Can you do it again?"

"Sometimes. Under glass. In one room. With Hessa watching. At a marked distance. After being fed."

Rinna nodded.

"So no."

"I dislike how quickly everyone understands this."

"Can you carry pages?"

"Yes."

"Useful."

She handed me a stack.

I carried pages.

Not many. From front table to Teren, then from Teren to Hara because he had given me the wrong stack, then back to Teren because Hara already had hers and wanted to know why he was creating paper migration as a profession.

I had no role that evening.

I stayed through the first half of rehearsal anyway, sitting near the side while Teren worked a scene between Marek and Hara. My right forearm felt normal. My shoulder felt normal. The phantom foot remained quietly where it could not be.

Nobody asked me to move anything with my mind.

Excellent workplace.

By the time I left, the lamps were being lit for the evening show.

I was not in it.

That was fine too.

At home Lyssa had beaten me back by enough time to occupy most of the table with cloth.

I put Hessa's note beside the basin.

She looked up.

"Well?"

"Paper moved."

"Good."

"Once."

"Less good?"

"Still good. Just specifically good."

She threaded a needle.

"Did you hurt yourself?"

"No."

"Did she change the rules?"

"No."

"Then specifically good."

I sat across from her.

"It happened at far again."

"Far from what?"

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

"The paper."

"How far?"

"A marked place in Hessa's room."

Lyssa looked at me.

I pointed at her.

"Exactly. This is why she won't call it a range."

"I didn't ask about a range."

"You accidentally became scientific."

"Terrible."

She went back to the seam.

I watched her make three small stitches.

"The man didn't come back," I said.

"Which man?"

"Brown coat. Nobody sent word."

"Good."

"Rinna said the same thing."

"She's learning."

I smiled.

Lyssa worked. I sat. The room smelled faintly of the soap I had bought and the onions she had started before I came in.

Two far responses existed now.

Not a range.

Not reliability.

Not a weapon.

Not a solution to anything happening at the theatre.

A paper fin had moved a little, twice, on different days, under glass, while Hessa watched and wrote down everything I wanted to shorten.

That was more than once.

For tonight, more than once was enough.
