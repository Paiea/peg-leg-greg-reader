TEXT READER · CHAPTER 222

THE PROPERTY MAN

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was gone before I finished tying my boot.

This was not unusual anymore. It still produced a brief, stupid moment in which I looked at the room as if it had misplaced her.

On the table she had left half an onion, two pins stuck through a folded scrap of blue cloth, and a note that said:

BUY THREAD IF YOU PASS MAREN'S.

No color.

No amount.

No indication of whether Maren was a person, a shop, or a threat.

I turned the note over.

Nothing.

Apparently Hessa had started a school.

I ate the rest of yesterday's bread, checked the crutch tips because the left one had begun to feel slightly softer on wet stone, and left the thread problem for Future Greg. The morning was clear enough that the street had already collected carts, apprentices, dogs, two women arguing over cabbages, and a man carrying a door by himself for reasons I could not improve by knowing.

At the theatre, nobody was interested in my magical accomplishments.

This was comforting because neither was I.

The front door stood open. Rinna's table had moved a little farther back from it than usual, enough that someone passing on the street could see her but not the ledger beside her. The cash box was not there.

I noticed.

Rinna noticed me noticing.

"No," she said.

"I didn't ask."

"You were about to."

"I was going to compliment the table."

"Do that silently."

I looked toward the stage instead.

Three people were looking for the same umbrella.

It was not an umbrella anyone sane would use outdoors. The handle curved like a bird's neck. The canopy was striped yellow and green, one rib had been repaired with wire, and somebody had painted tiny red flowers along the edge. I had seen it in at least four plays, none of which required the same kind of weather.

Nessa stood beside an open property chest with both hands on her hips. Marek was holding a sword, a basket, and a hat that did not belong to him. Hara had one shoe on.

"The umbrella was here," Nessa said.

Marek said, "It remains a compelling theory."

"You used it yesterday."

"In rehearsal."

"That was yesterday."

"Time does work that way."

Hara, from the floor, said, "If he has hidden it in the sword chest again, stab him with whatever you find first."

I stopped just inside the aisle.

Nobody told me where to stand.

I did not need them to.

The left side of the stage was clogged with a bench, two rolled backdrops, and Jori's ladder. I went right, passed behind the front row, and set my crutches against the property table where they would not catch anyone moving from the rear passage.

Nessa looked at me.

"Did you see the umbrella?"

"Not today."

"Yesterday."

"Stage left after The Letter Box. Davin carried it toward the rear because the handle caught his sleeve."

Davin shouted from somewhere behind the flats, "It attacked me."

Nessa pointed.

"Find him."

"I am not an umbrella dog."

"You remembered where it went."

That was how employment happened in theatre.

Davin had not hidden the umbrella. He had put it on a hook beside the costume racks because the curved handle fit there and because nobody had told him that the hook belonged to belts.

Nessa found me standing beneath it.

"You passed it twice," I said.

"I was looking for an umbrella."

"It remains one."

"It was with belts."

"Disguise."

She took it down and shoved it into my free hand.

"Property table. Do not give it to Marek."

Marek appeared in the rear passage.

"I heard my name."

"No umbrella," Nessa said.

"I have no interest in umbrellas."

Hara called, "Liar."

Marek looked offended.

"Not in this play."

That distinction apparently mattered.

By the time the company stopped misplacing things, I had acquired a job.

Not a role.

A job.

The afternoon pairing was The Widow's Guest and The Honest Clerk. I had a small speaking part in the second piece, a delivery man with four lines and a parcel. Before that, Nessa wanted me at the property table because Davin had to help Jori with a wall brace and the usual property runner had not come in.

"What does the property runner do?" I asked.

Nessa stared at me.

"Runs properties."

"I regret asking."

"You know most of the pieces."

"I know enough to know Marek steals hats."

"I borrow hats."

Marek had returned.

Nessa did not look at him.

"You also know where people enter now," she continued. "Keep the table clear. Put things back where they belong. If someone asks for a prop, give them the one for their scene, not the one that looks funniest."

"That feels targeted."

"It is."

She walked away.

Marek reached for the striped umbrella.

I moved it behind me.

He smiled.

I did not.

He left.

I had become authority.

Limited authority. Umbrella authority.

Still.

The property table was a long scarred board on two trestles just behind the right wing. Everything needed for the first piece had been arranged in rough scene order: a letter, a small purse with nothing in it, the ugly umbrella, a wooden cup, a shawl pin, two folded handkerchiefs, and a silver-painted key that opened nothing.

The key was familiar. I had carried it as a servant weeks ago and dropped it once behind a chair.

It still had the nick along one edge from landing on the stage.

I picked it up, then put it back before nostalgia became another form of theft.

Hara came through fastening the second shoe.

"Key after the letter," she said.

"I know."

"Good."

She took three steps, stopped, came back, and moved the key two inches farther from the purse.

"Why?"

"Marek sweeps with his sleeve."

"I have never swept anything in my life," Marek said from the other side of the flat.

Hara raised her voice.

"Exactly."

She left.

I looked at the table.

Then at Marek's sleeve when he came through five minutes later.

Wide cuff.

Loose stitching.

He reached for the purse during the run.

The cuff brushed the place where the key had been.

Hara had been right.

I disliked evidence that benefited other people.

The morning run was not really a rehearsal. Everyone already knew The Widow's Guest. Teren stopped only when something physical had changed enough to matter.

The bench was six inches farther downstage because one leg had begun to wobble and Jori wanted it on a flatter board. The moved bench changed Hara's first cross. She adjusted once. Marek forgot, nearly met her shoulder to shoulder, and corrected without anyone saying anything.

I watched from the property table while sorting the handkerchiefs.

One had a red stitched border.

One did not.

The red one belonged to the second scene because the widow recognized it later.

I knew that.

Nobody had told me today.

A month ago I would have been pleased enough with that fact to repeat it to myself.

Today I put the red one where it belonged and kept watching.

Teren pointed at me.

"Parcel."

I looked at the table.

My parcel was not there.

That was because I had not put it there.

I had been so busy becoming master of other people's objects that I had forgotten the one I actually needed.

"Excellent," I said.

"What?" Nessa asked.

"My role is improving."

She followed my eyes to the empty place.

Then she laughed once and went to find the parcel.

It was under the front table, wrapped in brown cloth and tied with twine. Rinna had used it to flatten a stack of handbills.

"Why is my parcel doing office work?"

Rinna looked down.

"Because it was heavy."

"It contains wool."

"Heavy wool."

"It is fake wool."

"Still worked."

I carried it back.

Nessa marked a square on the property table with chalk.

"Parcel lives here."

"I can remember."

"Apparently not."

Fair.

We ran my scene once before midday.

I entered through the right wing after the clerk's second line, crossed to the desk, put down the parcel, said four lines, took a receipt, and left.

The first time, I reached for the receipt too early.

Not dramatically.

My hand simply moved toward it while Pell, playing the clerk, was still asking whether the parcel had come from North Street.

Teren stopped us.

"Greg."

"I know the street."

"Your hand."

I looked at it. It was already halfway to the receipt.

"What about it?"

"You know you're getting the receipt. The delivery man doesn't yet."

I put my hand back.

"Again."

That was all he gave me. We reset.

Pell asked, "North Street?"

"South."

"You were told North."

"I was told wrong."

He frowned at the parcel.

"Then I cannot take it."

"Then I will carry it back."

Only after he sighed, took the parcel, and pushed the little wooden receipt across the desk did I reach for it.

Better.

The difference was tiny.

It was also visible.

Theatre had an irritating amount of work hidden inside things that looked like nothing.

At midday, Davin brought meat pies from the corner stall and nearly caused a riot by failing to buy enough.

There were eight pies and more than eight people.

Rinna said, "Half."

Marek said, "Some of us are larger."

Hara said, "Some of us are louder."

Nessa cut the pies.

Marek received half.

Justice returned to Carrow.

I ate sitting on the edge of the stage with my crutches laid beside me. Jori had already shifted one of the narrow stools out of the path behind the wing without asking. He had been doing that for several days now, not always the same stool, just whatever object would catch a crutch or force me into somebody else's lane.

The first time, people had discussed where I could pass.

Now they moved things and argued about umbrellas instead.

Better use of human attention.

Rinna came through carrying a bundle of tickets.

"Any word?" I asked.

"No."

That was the entire conversation.

She kept walking.

I finished my half pie.

The FAR result from yesterday crossed my mind once when a scrap of paper near the open door lifted in a draft and skittered beneath a bench.

I watched it go.

Then Davin made me crawl under the bench for it because apparently I was closest.

Magic remained useless.

After midday the theatre changed from rehearsal mess to performance mess.

Different mess.

Costumes came off hooks. Hair was pinned. Props moved from storage to tables. Jori checked the brace behind the widow's room wall. Rinna counted tickets at the front. Teren stopped answering questions that had answers written on pages.

I stayed at the property table through The Widow's Guest.

That turned out to be harder than my own four-line role.

People arrived from the dark and expected objects.

Hara's letter.

Pell's key.

Marek's cup.

Hara's handkerchief.

Nessa's pin.

The striped umbrella went out and came back wet because Marek had dipped one edge into a stage bucket for no reason I could determine.

Nessa saw it.

"Marek."

"It was raining."

"There is no rain."

"There is emotionally."

She took the umbrella from him and handed it to me.

"Dry it."

"How?"

"With a cloth."

I found a cloth.

This was apparently stagecraft.

The strongest moment of the piece had nothing to do with any of us getting the right objects.

Marek entered in the second scene supposed to be a nervous guest hiding a letter from the widow. The audience already knew he had put it in his coat.

Hara, as the widow, asked whether he was warm.

He said no.

She looked at the coat.

Then at the fireplace.

Then at the audience.

Marek took one careful step away from the fire.

Hara took one careful step toward him.

The audience started laughing before either of them said another word.

He moved again.

She followed.

By the third step they were circling the room without acknowledging it, Hara calm and Marek increasingly desperate, until he finally said, "It is a very comfortable coat."

The room broke.

I nearly missed the cup handoff because I was watching.

Nearly.

Pell came offstage with his hand already out.

I saw him in time and put the cup into it without looking down.

He went back on.

Nobody died.

Progress.

Between pieces, the property table changed.

Objects from The Widow's Guest went left into return baskets. The Honest Clerk pieces came forward.

Parcel in the chalk square.

Receipt at the desk.

A quill.

Two folded invoices.

A sealing block.

A hat for Pell that Marek was forbidden to touch.

He touched it.

Nessa slapped his hand.

"Hostile workplace," he said.

"Document it."

"I need a clerk."

"You are surrounded by actors."

"None literate enough."

Pell, already wearing the hat, said, "I can write your complaint."

Marek considered him.

"Conflict of interest."

Then the curtain call from the first piece ended and nobody had time to continue.

My four lines were coming.

I did not rehearse them under my breath.

That surprised me only after I noticed I was not doing it.

The words were there.

More importantly, I knew where the parcel was, where Pell would stand, which side of the desk kept my crutch clear, and when the previous actor would leave the lane.

Teren passed behind me.

"Wait for him to finish the question."

"I know."

He looked at my hand.

I held both palms up.

"No receipt."

"Good."

He left.

My cue came while Nessa was still tying back the first play's shawl beside me.

I heard it.

Picked up the parcel.

Went.

The entrance was ordinary.

That might have been the best part.

Pell looked up from the desk.

"North Street?"

"South."

"You were told North."

"I was told wrong."

A laugh, small and immediate.

Not because I had done anything special. Pell had spent the previous scene establishing that the clerk believed every written label more than any living person.

He looked at the parcel.

"Then I cannot take it."

"Then I will carry it back."

"That would be inefficient."

"I have carried worse."

Another small laugh.

Pell took the parcel.

He pushed the receipt toward me.

I waited until it moved.

Then reached.

Correct.

My third line was, "Sign it properly."

I said it.

My fourth was, "I have been blamed for better handwriting."

That got a better laugh than it had in rehearsal, mostly because Pell looked genuinely offended.

I left on time.

Backstage, Nessa handed me the striped umbrella.

I stared at her.

"Wrong play."

"I know. Hold it."

"Why?"

"Marek is looking for it."

I held it behind the flat.

Marek came through thirty seconds later.

"Have you seen the umbrella?"

"No."

Nessa looked at me.

I looked at Nessa.

Marek narrowed his eyes.

"You are both terrible actors."

"Go," Teren said.

Marek went.

The rest of the show did not need me onstage.

It needed me at the table.

A quill disappeared and was found behind the desk.

The sealing block came back in Pell's pocket.

One invoice tore because Hara stepped on it during an exit.

Davin tried to replace it with a handbill folded to the same size.

I stopped him.

"Wrong paper."

"It looks right."

"It says tomorrow's show on the back."

"Nobody sees the back."

"Until Marek unfolds it."

Davin considered the possibility.

"Fine."

We found another invoice.

I was getting disturbingly good at preventing theatre from becoming visibly stupid.

Privately stupid remained beyond repair.

After the final curtain, the company dissolved into labor.

The audience moved out. Benches scraped. Costumes came off. The property table became three piles: return, repair, and somehow wet.

Rinna came behind the front table only after the doors were nearly shut. She brought the cash box with her from somewhere else in the building.

I noticed that too.

This time I did not say anything.

She set it beside the ledger, counted with Davin standing on the other side of the table, then closed it again before the front doors reopened for anyone carrying benches.

No announcement.

No guards.

No dramatic secrecy.

A slightly different routine.

That was enough.

Rinna paid me one copper for the day's work and performance.

I put it away.

Nessa came past with the repaired umbrella.

"Property man tomorrow?"

"Was I property man today?"

"You kept Marek from taking the hat."

"Heroic."

"You also almost gave Pell the cup late."

"I did not."

"Almost counts backstage."

"That is not how Hessa counts."

"I don't know who that is."

"Better life."

She left.

Jori called from the stage, "Greg."

"What?"

"Your lane."

I looked.

Someone had shoved the narrow stool back behind the wing during cleanup.

Right where I would pass.

Before I moved, Davin hooked it with his foot and dragged it aside without interrupting whatever he was saying to Pell.

Nobody looked at me.

Good.

I collected my crutches and went home.

Maren turned out to be a shop.

I learned this because I passed it.

The sign had a spool painted on it, which seemed unfairly helpful.

I stood outside, took Lyssa's note from my pocket, and read it again.

BUY THREAD IF YOU PASS MAREN'S.

No color.

I went inside.

The woman behind the counter asked, "What kind?"

"My partner left me a note designed to test the strength of our relationship."

She did not smile.

Professional.

I showed her the scrap of blue cloth with the pins still through it.

She looked at the cloth.

"Matching?"

"I would like to survive."

She found a spool close enough that I could not see the difference.

I bought it.

At home, Lyssa had not returned yet.

I put the spool beside her cloth, washed, ate the last piece of onion with cheese, and occupied the only clear corner of the table with absolutely nothing.

She arrived after dusk carrying no bundle at all.

That was unusual enough that I looked at her hands.

"Finished?"

"Delivered."

"Paid?"

She looked at me.

"Do you ask Rinna that?"

"Frequently."

"Then stop."

She saw the thread.

Picked it up.

Held it beside the cloth.

"Good."

I felt an unreasonable amount of pride.

"Maren helped."

"Maren usually does."

"So the note was actually for Maren."

"The note was for you."

"Debatable."

She sat, leaned back, and closed her eyes for a moment.

I did not give her the theatre report.

She did not ask for it.

After a while she opened one eye.

"Did the paper move today?"

"No paper."

"Did the dangerous man appear?"

"No dangerous man."

"Did you work?"

"Extensively."

"Doing what?"

I considered the day.

"Protecting an umbrella."

She closed her eye again.

"Important."

"Finally, someone understands."

I put the copper from the theatre with the rest of my money.

Lyssa nudged the spool of thread toward her side of the table, then reached across and took my hand.

We sat there without solving anything.

Tomorrow the umbrella would probably disappear again.

That seemed like enough trouble to prepare for.
