TEXT READER · CHAPTER 223

THE MAINTENANCE MAN

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was gone before I finished tying my boot.

This was normal now. It still produced a brief, stupid moment in which I looked at the room as if it had misplaced her.

On the table she had left half an onion, two pins through a folded scrap of blue cloth, and a note.

BUY THREAD IF YOU PASS MAREN'S.

No color. No amount. No indication whether Maren was a person, a shop, or a threat.

Apparently Hessa had started a school.

I ate yesterday's bread, noticed the left crutch tip felt a little softer than the right, and left Future Greg both problems.

At the theatre, Rinna's front table had moved far enough back that the street could see her without seeing the ledger.

The cash box was gone.

I noticed.

"Cash box moved."

"Yes."

That was all.

Three people were looking for the same umbrella.

It was striped yellow and green, repaired with wire, painted with tiny red flowers, and had appeared in enough unrelated plays to qualify as repertory staff.

Nessa stood beside an open property chest. Marek held a sword, a basket, and a hat that did not belong to him. Hara had one shoe on.

"The umbrella was here," Nessa said.

"It remains a compelling theory," Marek said.

Davin had put it on a hook beside the belts because the curved handle fit there.

Nessa found me under it.

"You passed it twice," I said.

"I was looking for an umbrella."

"It remains one."

"It was with belts."

"Disguise."

She shoved it into my hand.

"Property table. Do not give it to Marek."

Marek appeared behind her.

"I heard my name."

"No umbrella."

"I have no interest in umbrellas."

Hara called, "Liar."

Marek looked offended.

"Not in this play."

That distinction apparently mattered.

By the time everyone stopped misplacing things, I had acquired a job.

Not a role. A job.

The usual property runner was absent. Nessa wanted me behind the right wing keeping scene objects in order.

"What does the property runner do?"

She stared at me.

"Runs properties."

"I regret asking."

"You know most of the pieces now. Keep the table clear. Put things back. Give people the prop for their scene, not the one that looks funniest."

"That feels targeted."

"It is."

Marek reached for the umbrella.

I moved it behind me.

I had become authority.

Limited authority. Umbrella authority.

Still.

The table held a letter, an empty purse, a wooden cup, two handkerchiefs, a shawl pin, the umbrella, and a silver-painted key that opened nothing.

I knew the key. Weeks earlier I had carried it as a servant and dropped it behind a chair. The nick was still along one edge.

A month ago I would have been pleased enough by recognizing every object to make the recognition into an event.

Today I put the red-bordered handkerchief in the second-scene position because that was where it belonged.

Nobody had to tell me.

That felt better.

I still forgot my own parcel.

Teren pointed toward the empty square where it should have been.

"Greg."

"Excellent."

"What?" Nessa asked.

"My role is improving."

The parcel was under Rinna's front table flattening handbills.

"Why is my parcel doing office work?"

"Because it was heavy," Rinna said.

"It contains fake wool."

"Still worked."

Nessa chalked a square on the property table.

"Parcel lives here."

"I can remember."

"Apparently not."

Fair.

My four-line delivery scene produced one useful correction. I reached for the receipt before Pell's clerk had decided to accept the parcel.

Teren stopped us.

"You know you're getting it. He doesn't."

We ran it again. I waited until Pell took the parcel and pushed the receipt across the desk.

The difference was tiny.

It was also visible.

Theatre had an irritating amount of work hidden inside things that looked like nothing.

At midday Davin brought too few meat pies. Nessa cut them in half.

Marek objected on the grounds that some of us were larger.

Hara answered that some of us were louder.

Marek received half.

Justice returned to Carrow.

I ate on the stage edge. Jori moved a stool out of the crutch path while talking to Pell and never looked at me.

The first time I worked there, people discussed where I could pass.

Now they moved things and argued about umbrellas.

Better use of human attention.

During the performance, people came out of the dark and expected the right objects.

Letter. Key. Cup. Handkerchief. Pin.

The umbrella returned wet.

Nessa stared at Marek.

"It was raining," he said.

"There is no rain."

"There is emotionally."

She handed it to me.

"Dry it."

I found a cloth.

This was apparently stagecraft.

My own delivery scene took less than two minutes. I waited for the receipt this time.

Nobody congratulated me.

Good.

At the end of the night a stool landed in the aisle where my crutch would have caught it. Davin hooked it aside with his foot without interrupting his conversation.

Nobody looked at me.

Good again.

Maren turned out to be a shop. The painted spool on the sign was unfairly helpful.

I showed the note to the woman behind the counter.

"What color?" she asked.

"The note is aggressively silent."

She looked at the scrap of blue cloth Lyssa had pinned to it, chose a spool, and charged me for it.

At home Lyssa held the thread against the cloth.

"Good."

I felt an unreasonable amount of pride.

"Maren helped."

"Maren usually does."

"So the note was actually for Maren."

"The note was for you."

"Debatable."

She sat down and closed her eyes for a moment.

After a while she opened one.

"Did the paper move today?"

"No paper."

"Did the dangerous man appear?"

"No dangerous man."

"Did you work?"

"Extensively."

"Doing what?"

I considered the day.

"Protecting an umbrella."

She closed her eye again.

"Important work."

The left crutch tip was worse the next morning.

Lyssa was already using perhaps four inches of the thread I had heroically transported.

I pressed the rubber tip with my thumb.

"That the soft one?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Replace it."

"I was going to assess it."

"Replace it after assessing it."

"Very controlling."

She tied off a stitch.

"You fall on that theatre floor and somebody will make a speech."

That was convincing.

The crutch repairer remembered me well enough to point at the left crutch before I finished explaining.

"That one's wearing faster."

"That is what I came to tell you."

"Good. Saves time."

He showed me the uneven wear.

"You load the left harder when you turn. Could be streets. Could be you. Could be both."

That was less satisfying than discovering a conspiracy in my walking.

He replaced one tip, made me test it, adjusted the fit once, and took his copper.

The coin left my hand without producing a moral crisis.

Very mature.

Outside, the new rubber felt almost annoyingly firm.

I reached the theatre to find Jori under the cart.

Not near it.

Under it.

Davin stood over the visible boots holding a mallet and offering advice with the confidence of a man not trapped beneath timber.

"Tell him leverage matters," Davin said.

"Leverage matters."

"Thank you."

Jori's voice came from below.

"Not to the cracked brace."

A short diagonal brace beneath the cart bed had split near an old bolt hole. The axle was fine. The cart was not collapsing. It was also not going anywhere useful until the brace was replaced.

"Did this happen today?" I asked.

"Found it today," Davin said.

"Different statement."

Jori slid far enough out to glare at me.

"Do not encourage him."

"I was encouraging evidence."

"Go inside."

Nessa immediately handed me six fake pastries.

Apparently I remained employed.

The umbrella square was still chalked on the property table.

The umbrella was not in it.

"Marek?" I asked.

"Marek," Nessa confirmed.

Continuity.

Near midday the cart repair man came through the front door and asked for Rinna.

He had black grease permanently worked into his hands and the expression of someone who had come because not coming would bother him more.

"A man came by yesterday," he said. "Asked after your cart."

The theatre kept moving around us. Nessa sorted pastries. Teren argued with Pell. Something wooden hit the floor backstage.

Rinna asked, "What did he ask?"

"If I still worked on it. Then when you usually bring it in."

"Anything else?"

"Asked if you take it out after the late show. Asked if the same man drives it."

There it was.

"Did you answer?"

"Told him I fix carts. Don't keep theatre hours."

"Good."

"Good?"

"Good that you didn't answer."

Rinna asked for the description.

Brown coat. Dark hair. Not old. Narrow face, maybe. Quiet voice.

My stomach tightened.

Not proof.

Still.

Rinna wrote only what she had.

ASKED CART REPAIRER:
THEY TAKE IT OUT AFTER THE LATE SHOW?
SAME MAN DRIVES IT? [WORDING UNCERTAIN]

DESCRIPTION CONSISTENT WITH OLIN REPORT.

I read it upside down.

"Consistent."

"Yes."

"Not same man."

"Correct."

The repairer asked what she wanted him to do.

"If somebody asks again, don't answer theatre routines. Send word."

"That's what I did."

"Then keep doing it."

He left looking almost disappointed there was no heroic second step.

Davin pointed toward the yard.

"They're asking when the cart leaves."

"Someone is," Rinna said.

I said, "First where the money sits. Now how things move."

Rinna looked at me.

"Yes."

That was the first time she agreed without trimming the sentence.

I felt worse, not better.

"It fits."

"It fits a concern," she said. "It does not tell us what he plans."

"If he plans anything."

"Correct."

Marek had wandered close enough to listen while still holding the striped umbrella.

I looked at it.

He looked at me.

"Not now," I said.

He slowly lowered it.

Progress everywhere.

Rinna called Jori in and gave him the narrow version. No late cart without telling her. No predictable money movement. If the cart had to move after a show, the route and driver were not anyone else's business.

Then everyone went back to work.

That mattered.

The brace still had to be replaced. The fake pastries still had to be returned. Somebody still had to find the umbrella when Marek eventually abandoned it somewhere impossible.

A threat did not become less real because ordinary work continued around it.

Ordinary work did not become less real because a threat existed.

By evening the new crutch tip had stopped feeling new.

I had spent money that morning so the problem would remain boring.

Possibly the finest use of money available.

Lyssa was home before me, the blue cloth across her lap and my thread moving through it in small dark stitches.

"You used it," I said.

"That is what thread is for."

I told her about the cart repair man.

Her needle stopped for only a moment.

"Who?"

"He doesn't know. Description sounds like the other one. Could be same. Not proven."

"What did he ask?"

"When the cart goes out after the late show. Whether the same man drives."

She resumed stitching.

"Did the repair man tell him?"

"No."

"Then your people heard again before it became anything."

That was true.

"They asked where the money sits. Now they're asking how things move."

Lyssa tied off one stitch.

"Then keep making it hard to learn."

Simple.

Not easy.

The new crutch tip held. The cart would get a new brace. The cash box had moved. The suppliers were learning not to answer questions.

Maintenance, apparently, was mostly preventing interesting things from happening.

I was beginning to respect it.
