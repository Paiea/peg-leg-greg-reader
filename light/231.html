LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 231

THE MAGISTRATE

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The brown cloth was still on the table in the morning.

Three pins through one corner. Narrow fold. No note.

I looked at it long enough to confirm it had not become my business overnight, then ate the last turnip cold because I had ruined the hot version yesterday and consequences deserved continuity.

Lyssa had already gone. My shirt cuff had three loose threads, and I did not touch them. This was restraint of a kind Hessa would never properly appreciate because it involved no apparatus and very poor sewing.

I had no note from her anyway. More importantly, Jori had said tomorrow maybe two days ago, which had become today only through the ordinary cruelty of calendars.

I put on the damaged shirt, took my crutches, and went to see whether maybe had survived.

The side door at the theatre was open.

Inside, the magistrate's room had acquired windows.

Not real windows. That would have been reckless.

The painted wall from two days ago stood onstage with Jori's trim fixed around a broad blue rectangle. From the audience it looked like a respectable office window with pale light beyond it. From the wing I could see cream paint along one edge, green on another, and a narrow stripe of older blue at the back where the same timber had lived several previous lives.

The piece I had bumped against the wall carried a small cream mark near the bottom.

Nobody had repaired it.

The audience would never see it.

I found this deeply offensive.

Davin was kneeling beside a low rail with three short boards across his lap. Jori stood over him holding a longer one.

"That one fits," Davin said.

"It fits because I cut it."

"You cut it because I found the gap."

"You made the gap."

"History is written by carpenters."

Jori saw me.

"You're late."

I looked toward the empty house.

"For what?"

He pointed toward the stage.

"Magistrate."

I waited.

Davin looked up.

"Congratulations."

"On what?"

"Corruption."

That was how I learned I had a role.

Nessa found me before Teren did. She came out of the rear passage carrying a black coat and a bundle of pages.

"No flowers," she said.

"I wasn't worried."

"You should be. Marek knows where they are."

She handed me the coat.

It was shorter than the steward's coat from The Second Toast and heavier through the shoulders. The hem stopped high enough that it would not wrap around a crutch tip when I stood. Nessa checked that before I finished putting it on.

"Good."

"Did you choose this for my dignity?"

"For your legs."

"Less romantic."

"Also the other one smells like Pell."

From the stage Pell shouted, "I can hear you."

Nessa raised her voice.

"Then wash it."

Pell said something I could not make out.

Marek laughed.

The company was already busy enough that nobody explained why.

Teren arrived with a pencil tucked behind one ear and pointed to the pages in my hand.

"The Crooked Fence."

I looked down.

The cast list was short.

WIDOW: HARA

NEIGHBOR: MAREK

CLERK: PELL

MAGISTRATE: GREG

Below that, fifteen lines.

"Fifteen."

"Yes."

"I am advancing."

"You're sitting."

I looked at the stage.

A broad desk had been placed beneath the painted window. Behind it, one chair.

"Ah."

"You stand once."

"Demotion."

Teren ignored that.

"The play opens with you already there. Pell enters, then Hara. Marek comes after. You do not need to make the magistrate important."

"I am the magistrate."

"That's the trap."

He took the pages back long enough to tap one line near the middle.

"Listen to the dispute. Don't play the joke before they give it to you."

"Do I know what the dispute is?"

"You read the title."

"Crooked fence."

"Excellent scholarship."

He gave the pages back.

"Stage."

I had played men who delivered things, carried things, served things, borrowed things, guarded doors, stood in rooms, and once represented the entire concept of being a neighbor.

Magistrate felt suspiciously like employment with furniture.

For once, the job description appealed to me.

The set had more pieces than the painted wall suggested.

Desk. Chair. Clerk's stool. A narrow witness rail Davin and Jori were still arguing about. One rolled map. A wooden seal block that did not seal anything. Three folded petitions. A length of rope. A short painted fence panel that looked as though it had lost an argument with weather.

Nessa put the seal block on the desk.

"Do not hit the desk with it."

I looked at the block.

"Why would I?"

"Because every man who plays this part does."

"Tradition."

"Damage."

She placed a small square of dark leather beside my right hand.

"Hit that."

"How often?"

"Twice."

"Only twice?"

"In the play."

"After?"

"No."

Marek appeared beside us and reached for the block.

Nessa moved it without looking at him.

He stopped.

"I was inspecting justice."

"You can inspect it from there."

He leaned toward me.

"Corrupt."

"Apparently."

He smiled and went to find his fence.

I sat behind the desk.

The chair was solid and slightly too low.

My right foot rested flat. Left knee bent comfortably. I could keep both crutches behind the desk to my right without their tops showing above it if I angled them toward the wall. Standing would require one extra beat to reach them, but the script had enough room before the magistrate crossed to the witness rail.

Jori watched me try it once.

"Rail farther left."

Davin said, "It was farther left."

"It was crooked."

"It's a crooked fence play."

"The fence is crooked."

"Theme."

Jori moved the rail.

Not much. Maybe three inches.

That gave my right crutch a clean plant when I stood.

Teren did not comment.

We began.

Pell entered first as the Clerk carrying the petitions and a face suggesting he had already heard too much from everyone involved.

"Morning docket," he said.

My first line:

"How bad?"

Pell looked at the pages.

"Neighbors."

I looked up.

The company laughed.

Teren said, "Again."

"I did not write it."

"You enjoyed it."

"It is enjoyable."

"Again."

Second time I asked, "How bad?" like a man whose morning could still conceivably be saved.

Pell looked at the pages.

"Neighbors."

Better.

Hara entered before the laugh finished.

Her Widow carried the short fence panel under one arm.

Not a piece of paper.

The fence.

She dropped it in front of the desk.

"Evidence."

I looked at Teren.

He said, "Not you."

Right.

The Magistrate looked at the fence.

"That is a fence."

"It was."

"Has its condition changed?"

"It has moved."

Marek entered through the opposite side.

"It was moved."

Hara turned.

"By you."

"By weather."

"Weather does not own a hammer."

Marek held up both hands.

"Neither do I."

From backstage Davin said, "He does."

Teren turned his head.

Davin disappeared.

We kept going.

The dispute was simple enough to survive fifteen lines and complicated enough to occupy everybody else.

Hara's Widow claimed the fence between their yards had moved two feet into her side.

Marek's Neighbor claimed a storm had shifted the posts.

Hara claimed the posts had moved uphill.

Marek claimed water did strange things.

Pell's Clerk kept producing older descriptions of the property that contradicted both of them because the lane had once been wider, then narrower, then apparently measured by a man who wrote one number differently every time.

The Magistrate wanted one fact that stayed in place.

I understood the role.

At the first run, I did too much with that understanding.

I leaned forward through every ridiculous answer. Watched Hara. Watched Marek. Looked at Pell's papers as though I could force them into coherence through judicial attention.

Teren stopped us after five minutes.

"Greg."

"What?"

"Sit back."

I did.

"You're chasing them."

"I am hearing a case."

"The case comes to you."

I looked at the desk.

"That feels administratively optimistic."

Hara said, "He's right."

Teren pointed at her.

"Do not help him."

"I wasn't helping. The magistrate is desperate."

Marek leaned on the witness rail.

"It is his best quality."

The rail shifted.

Jori said, "Off."

Marek stepped away before Jori reached him, which saved everybody time.

Teren looked back at me.

"You don't need to catch every line. Let them bring the mess."

That was specific enough to use.

We reset.

The second run felt easier once I stopped trying to prove the Magistrate was listening.

Pell gave me the petitions.

I read one.

Hara placed the fence.

Marek blamed weather.

I let the argument happen in front of me instead of leaning into it.

The desk became useful.

It gave me somewhere to put my hands. Somewhere to look when two adults were shouting about an object shorter than my leg.

Pell's Clerk became funnier too, because I could glance at him only when the contradictions reached a level requiring professional support.

He never gave it.

At one point Hara said, "The fence moved uphill."

Marek said, "Water rises."

Pell looked at him.

I looked at Pell.

Pell looked back at his paper.

Nobody helped Marek.

The company laughed hard enough that Teren let the silence sit.

That was not my joke.

It worked partly because I did nothing.

I was beginning to understand why theatre kept assigning jobs to absence.

By midday, we had the routes.

The fence panel entered with Hara and left with nobody until the end.

The petitions stayed with Pell.

The rope appeared only in the second half, when Marek attempted to demonstrate how the storm had "pulled" the fence.

The demonstration did not help him.

The seal block stayed on its leather square unless I needed it.

Twice.

Nessa made me prove I remembered that.

"First?"

"After both start talking over Pell."

"Second?"

"Before judgment."

"Desk?"

"Never."

"Good."

She took the block and gave it to Marek.

He brightened.

"Justice."

"Take it to the table."

His face fell.

Temporary authority transferred.

Lunch happened in the aisle because the stage was still full of legal property.

Rinna had bread. Hara had two boiled eggs. Pell had something wrapped in leaves that smelled much better than my bread. Marek claimed he had already eaten and then stole half of Davin's apple.

Davin noticed after two bites were gone.

"That's mine."

Marek looked at the apple.

"Evidence unclear."

I held out my hand.

"Bring it before the court."

Davin gave me the apple.

Marek said, "You cannot be impartial. You know the victim."

I took a bite.

"Case dismissed."

Hara laughed into her egg.

Teren, from three rows away, said, "Do not improve the play."

I swallowed.

"Rehearsal never stops."

Rinna passed behind us carrying a small stack of tickets, stopped long enough to steal a piece of Hara's bread, and kept moving before Hara noticed which piece she had taken.

Hara noticed.

"That was the good side."

Rinna bit it.

"Still is."

She kept walking.

After lunch, Jori and Davin replaced the witness rail with a different one because the first had developed a wobble that was not funny enough to justify keeping.

Davin carried it away.

"I said it was crooked."

Jori said, "It was loose."

"Crooked things become loose."

"That is not carpentry."

"It is philosophy."

"Take it outside."

Davin did.

I watched the new rail go down exactly three inches farther left than the first.

No one measured from my crutch.

They measured from the desk.

The result still worked.

That was better.

The afternoon run was cleaner.

My one standing cross worked without thought. Left crutch, right crutch, right foot, up. Around the desk. Two plants to the rail. Stop. The black coat stayed clear. Nessa had been right about the length.

Hara changed one small thing during the run.

When she said the fence had moved uphill, she stopped pointing at Marek and pointed instead at the fence itself, as though the fence had participated in the crime.

Teren let her keep it.

Marek changed nothing.

Pell changed even less.

That was another thing I was learning. Experienced actors did not always decorate a scene when they found something that worked. Sometimes they protected it.

The house filled for the early performance.

Not full.

Enough.

Voices became a low uneven layer beyond the curtain. Someone coughed repeatedly in the third row. A child was told twice not to climb on a bench. Rinna moved through the front with tickets. Backstage, Nessa checked the fence panel for a loose nail that was paint, checked Marek's rope, checked Pell's petitions, then came to me.

"Block?"

"Leather."

"Twice?"

"Twice."

"Desk?"

"Never."

She nodded and moved on.

I was already seated when the curtain opened.

That was strange.

No entrance.

No moment of stepping from backstage into light.

One second I was Greg behind a desk listening to the audience settle.

Then the curtain moved and I was a magistrate who had already been at work before any of them arrived.

Pell came in.

"Morning docket."

"How bad?"

He looked at the petitions.

"Neighbors."

The laugh was better with people.

Not huge.

Warm.

Hara entered carrying the fence.

She dropped it.

"Evidence."

The audience laughed again because apparently bringing an entire fence into a magistrate's room was funny even before anyone explained it.

Good play.

We moved.

The first half went cleanly.

Marek's weather defense got a laugh.

Hara's hammer answer got a larger one.

Pell read three measurements from three years and made each sound more exhausted than the last.

I sat back.

Let them bring the mess.

When both Hara and Marek began talking over Pell, I picked up the seal block and struck the leather square once.

A hard wooden knock.

They stopped.

Correct.

"One at a time."

Written line.

We continued.

Then came the rope.

Marek tied one end around the fence panel and held the other.

"The wind catches here."

Hara said, "Wind has hands now?"

"Enough wind does."

"Then arrest the wind."

A laugh.

Marek pulled the rope.

The fence panel slid six inches across the floor.

He stopped.

Hara looked at it.

The audience understood before she spoke.

"So it can be moved."

That laugh was bigger.

Marek stared at the fence as though betrayed by physical law.

Pell lowered his petitions.

I had a line.

"Your demonstration has become hostile."

I got it out without smiling.

Small laugh.

The play rolled forward.

Then I made the mistake.

It happened because Pell began a line with the same words he used later.

"Before judgment..."

I knew the second strike came before judgment.

My hand moved.

The block hit leather.

Knock.

Pell stopped.

Not because he was supposed to.

Because I had just cut his sentence in half.

For less than a second the stage became very clear.

My hand on the block.

Pell looking at me.

Hara looking at Pell.

Marek still holding the rope.

The audience waiting.

I had ended something early.

Hara turned to me.

Her Widow said, perfectly calm, "If judgment has arrived before the clerk finishes, I would like to know whether I am required to stay for it."

The audience laughed.

Not enormous.

Enough to reopen the room.

Pell looked down at his petition.

"Apparently not."

Another laugh.

Teren would murder us both later.

Marek lifted the rope.

"Can I go too?"

Hara snapped, "You can take your weather."

Bigger.

The scene was alive again.

Pell restarted from the part I had murdered.

"Before judgment, the court has one older survey."

This time I left the block alone.

The Magistrate listened.

The survey described the property line using a pear tree that no longer existed.

Hara said it had died.

Marek said she had cut it down.

Hara said it was her tree.

Marek said that was exactly the question.

The audience liked that.

I did too.

I kept my face.

Then came the strongest beat.

Pell read the final note.

"The disputed strip includes responsibility for the drainage ditch along the north wall."

Silence.

Hara's Widow had spent the entire play fighting for two feet of land.

She looked at Pell.

Then at the fence.

Then at Marek.

"His."

The audience broke.

Marek said, "You cannot surrender it now."

Hara picked up her end of the fence.

"I have spent an hour proving your ownership."

Another wave.

Pell had to wait.

Hara did not smile.

She turned to me.

"Magistrate?"

My written line was, "You are withdrawing the claim?"

She said, "In the interest of justice."

That finished the room.

I waited.

Not because Teren had told me to.

Because I could not speak over them.

When the laughter fell enough, I said, "Remarkable how quickly justice improves drainage."

A decent laugh.

Not Hara's.

Good.

I struck the block a second time.

Correct time.

The leather square did its job.

"Claim withdrawn."

Marek objected.

Pell collected papers.

Hara carried the crooked fence out with the satisfied speed of a woman abandoning property she had spent twenty minutes demanding.

My standing cross came near the end.

I reached the crutches, stood, moved to the rail, and delivered the final exchange without catching coat, desk, stool, rope, Marek, or justice.

The curtain came down.

Backstage noise arrived immediately.

Nessa took the block from me.

"Two."

I looked at her.

"Three."

"Yes."

"Teren?"

"Later."

She went after the fence.

Pell came through behind her still holding the petitions.

"You killed me."

"I heard 'before judgment.'"

"You heard half a sentence."

"Apparently."

Hara passed us.

"Efficient court."

"I apologize."

"No."

She kept walking.

I looked at Pell.

"Was that no to the apology?"

"Probably."

Teren found me after the second short piece.

I had no role in it. I spent the time helping Nessa reset the magistrate room and then watching from the wing while Hara played a woman trying to return a horse she had never purchased.

The horse was represented by a halter and the confidence of the cast.

It worked disturbingly well.

When the audience left, the company became labor again.

Teren waited until I had carried the clerk's stool toward storage.

"Greg."

I stopped.

"Block."

"Early."

"Why?"

"Pell said 'before judgment.'"

"Which was not your cue."

"No."

"Your cue was after the survey."

"Yes."

He nodded.

That was apparently the whole note.

I said, "Hara fixed it."

"Hara continued."

Important distinction.

I accepted it.

"Again tomorrow?" I asked.

"The play?"

"Magistrate."

Teren looked toward Rinna's table.

"Maybe."

Jori had infected everyone.

"Specific."

"If we pair it again."

That was not a promise.

Good.

He moved on to tell Marek that the rope belonged in the rope box, not around the witness rail.

Marek said he was preserving the evidence.

Nobody cared.

Rinna paid me one copper.

Normal theatre money.

I put it away.

The stage was already changing before I left. Jori and Davin took the magistrate desk toward the rear. The blue-trimmed wall stayed because tomorrow's first piece apparently used the same room as a merchant's office.

Davin pointed at the window.

"Versatile."

Jori said, "It's a window."

"Exactly."

The little cream mark I had made two days earlier disappeared into the wing.

Still unrepaired.

Still irrelevant.

I stopped being offended.

Mostly.

Outside, evening had settled over Carrow with the smell of cooking oil, wet stone, and somebody burning something that had probably once been dinner.

My shoulders felt ordinary.

Right hand fine.

The one standing cross had cost less than carrying flowers all day.

The Magistrate had been the easiest physical role I had played in weeks and one of the harder ones to leave alone.

I had hit the block early.

Pell had survived.

Hara had made the room hers.

Theatre.

At home, the brown cloth was still near the wall.

Three pins.

Same fold.

I did not inspect it.

Lyssa was sitting at the table eating bread with something spread on it that smelled sharp enough to improve the room.

She looked at the black coat folded over my arm.

"Role?"

"Magistrate."

She chewed.

"Did they know?"

"Eventually."

"How many people did you punish?"

"None."

"Poor magistrate."

"I did interrupt the clerk."

"On purpose?"

"No."

"Better."

I hung the coat over the chair so I would remember to return it tomorrow.

Lyssa looked at it.

"That yours?"

"Theatre."

"Then don't forget it."

"I was hoping to begin a private legal practice."

"In that?"

She looked at the coat again.

"No."

There was no argument available against the tone.

I sat.

She pushed the bread toward me.

I took some.

After a while she asked, "Good show?"

"Hara won two feet of land."

Lyssa waited.

"Then learned it came with a drainage ditch and gave it back."

She laughed once.

"Smart."

"Her character spent the whole play proving it belonged to her."

"Then smarter late."

I considered explaining the audience timing.

I did not.

The brown cloth stayed folded near the wall, and my cuff still had three loose threads.

Lyssa took the better piece of bread before I could reach it.

I started to object.

She had already bitten it.

I took the other piece.

It had more of the sharp spread on it anyway.
