LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 238

THE SEALER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The pages for The Missing Key were still on the table in the morning.

That did not make them a schedule.

I knew this because Teren had said, "Again tomorrow if it stays paired," which contained both tomorrow and if, and only one of those words was dangerous.

Lyssa was already gone.

The small paper-wrapped thing from last night was gone too. I noticed because it had been beside her elbow, then stopped thinking about it because objects were allowed to leave our apartment without giving testimony.

The brown cloth had not exercised that right.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf with three loose threads.

At least somebody respected continuity.

I ate bread, drank water, and read the Messenger pages once.

No knock came.

I waited until the bread was gone.

Still no knock.

Theatre had taught me many things, most of them about waiting in the correct place until somebody wanted me. Debt had taught me the opposite.

I folded the pages and left them on the table.

If the play stayed paired, Rinna knew where I lived.

If it did not, I had saved myself a walk.

Either way, Antonius Vale still existed.

The morning was colder than yesterday and brighter. Carrow had the hard clean light that made chipped plaster and old paint look newly accused. A cart of cabbages blocked half the lower lane. The driver was asleep on the seat while three people moved around him with the patient hatred of people who had decided waking him was somehow more trouble.

I went east.

My body had recovered normally from the theatre day. Right hand fine. Shoulders fine. Right leg fine. Residual limb comfortable. Left crutch tip dull and trustworthy.

The Vale office was already open.

The ink-fingered clerk looked up when I came in.

"Bundles?" I asked.

"Later."

"Acknowledgments?"

"Done."

"Excellent. I have successfully arrived for nothing."

He pointed to the far table.

"Seals."

I looked.

A shallow tray held folded outer wrappers, a squat wax pot near a little lamp, two brass stamps, a stack of account copies, and enough string to hang a very small criminal.

"What am I sealing?"

"Outgoing account copies."

"Why?"

He stared at me.

"Because open paper is less private than closed paper."

"Strong system."

"Sit."

I sat.

The chair was the same one I had used before, which meant I already knew which rear leg complained and how far my crutches could lean without falling into the passage.

The clerk put six packets in front of me.

"Not all of them."

"Good."

"Practice row."

"Less good."

He showed me the work.

Each packet already had the account copy inside its outer wrapper. My job was not to decide the account, correct the amount, or interpret any mark I did not know. I checked the outer name and house mark against the top line visible before the final fold. If they matched, I folded the wrapper closed along an existing crease, tied it once around with string, then waited.

The waiting mattered.

The clerk checked the face of the packet and put a short ink stroke beside the house mark.

Only then did I seal the string knot with wax and press the plain office stamp.

"Not the other stamp," he said.

I looked at the second brass stamp.

It had a deeper cut and a small edge mark.

"What does that one do?"

"Not your work."

"Efficient training."

"Good."

I looked at him.

He did not care.

The first packet went badly.

Not catastrophically.

That would have been interesting.

I used too much wax.

The drop spread under the stamp, squeezed past the knot, and made a red-brown lump with one recognizable edge and the general dignity of stepped-on fruit.

The clerk looked at it.

I looked at it.

"Private," I said.

"Ugly."

"Still private."

He took a narrow knife, lifted the wax before it fully hardened, and handed the packet back.

"Less."

"Technical language remains strong here."

"Less."

I used less.

The second seal looked like a seal.

The third looked almost competent.

On the fourth packet I got ahead of myself.

Name matched.

House mark matched.

Fold.

String.

Wax.

The clerk caught my wrist before the stamp came down.

Not hard.

Enough.

"No stroke."

I looked at the packet.

He had not checked it yet.

"Right."

"Why?"

"Because your check and your authority are different things."

He took his hand away.

I disliked how cleanly that sentence fit the office.

"I matched it."

"You did."

"And I still wait."

"Yes."

He checked the face, added the ink stroke, and pushed it back.

I stamped.

No lesson music played.

The packet joined the others.

By the end of six, the seals were all recognizable and none of them had become attached to the table.

The clerk gave me twelve more.

Apparently I had been promoted from dangerous to useful enough.

The office filled around the work.

A woman came in with two account slips and argued about a date. Not loudly. She had the controlled anger of somebody who had already explained herself somewhere else and resented having to own the story again.

The ink-fingered clerk listened, compared her slips to a ledger, then called another clerk over.

Neither asked me.

Good.

A man carrying a narrow crate wanted someone named Sel.

Nobody named Sel was there.

He left the crate anyway.

A boy came for three sealed packets and was told two.

He took two.

Another man stood near Antonius's door for almost ten minutes before being admitted and spent the whole time pretending not to look at anyone else.

Commerce.

I kept tying string.

The work had a rhythm once my hands stopped trying to improve it.

Check name.

Check house mark.

Fold.

Tie.

Wait.

Ink stroke.

Wax.

Stamp.

Set aside.

The clerk checked faster than I worked. That meant I never had to wait long enough to become philosophical.

Excellent design.

I was on the second stack when I found a problem.

The outer wrapper said MARRIS HOUSE, SOUTH WALL.

The visible top line inside said MARRIS HOUSE, SOUTH WALK.

One letter.

Different mark below it too.

I stopped.

The names matched.

The amount was none of my business.

The house did not.

I turned the packet sideways.

The clerk came over.

"What?"

I pointed.

He read it.

Then pulled the account copy free.

"Wrong wrapper."

"Do I win?"

"No."

"Do I at least avoid losing?"

"Continue."

He found the correct wrapper in the stack below.

No hidden conspiracy.

No meaningful connection.

Somebody had put one paper inside another paper.

The world survived.

I tied the correct packet.

Near midday, a boy arrived asking for me.

This one I knew.

Not his name.

His function.

Rinna's handbill boy from yesterday.

He stood in the office doorway looking uncomfortable with the amount of paper surrounding him.

The ink-fingered clerk looked at him.

"Business?"

"Theatre."

The clerk looked at me.

I closed my eyes briefly.

Of course.

The boy said, "Rinna says Teren kept the piece. If you're coming, come now."

There it was.

The if had changed.

Not completely.

Enough.

I looked at the stack in front of me.

Seven packets left in the row.

Another stack behind it.

The wax pot warm.

My hands already in the work.

"Performance?"

The boy shrugged.

"Rehearsal now. Show later."

"Messenger?"

"She said same."

The ink-fingered clerk had gone back to his ledger.

I said, "Can I leave this and come back?"

He looked at the wax.

Then at the packets.

"Today?"

"Yes."

"No."

Not cruel.

Not threatening.

The answer of a man with paper that had to leave today.

"If I go?"

"Someone else finishes."

"Credit?"

He looked at me.

I regretted asking it that way.

Not because money was shameful.

Because I had accidentally asked him to invent a rule for my feelings.

"Antonius credits work completed," he said.

There it was.

No speech.

No punishment.

No promise that my partial stack was worthless either.

The theatre boy shifted in the doorway.

I looked at him.

"Tell Rinna I'm working."

"You aren't coming?"

I looked at the seven packets.

Then at the wax.

Then at the boy.

"No."

He nodded.

No disappointment.

No argument.

He left.

The door closed.

I sat still for one breath longer than necessary.

The clerk said, "Wax."

I looked down.

The drop on my knife was cooling.

"Right."

I reheated it.

That was the choice.

Not dramatic enough for a play.

Debt credit instead of eight lines and three entrances.

I expected to feel more certain after making it.

I did not.

Good.

People who felt certain every time they chose between two useful things were probably lying or rich.

I continued.

The next packet had a house mark I recognized from an earlier acknowledgment stack.

That recognition was almost useful.

My hand started toward the string before I had checked the name.

I stopped it.

Different family.

Same house mark.

I read the line again.

The clerk saw me pause.

"Problem?"

"No."

He waited.

"I knew the mark and almost let that do the rest."

He nodded once.

Not praise.

Not correction either.

I finished the comparison properly and folded the packet.

A few minutes later the boy who had come for two sealed packets returned with one of them.

He held it by the edge.

"Cracked."

The wax had split across the center.

My seal.

I knew it because the stamp sat slightly off level.

The clerk took it, bent the wrapper gently, and the wax opened along the crack.

"Bad?"

I asked.

"Broken."

"Cause?"

He looked at the thin seal.

"Too little."

I stared at him.

"This morning you said less."

"Less than too much."

The office had found a philosophical position after all.

He scraped the old wax away, handed me the packet, and watched while I resealed it with a slightly larger drop.

"That?"

"Yes."

The boy took it.

No one recorded my journey from too much wax to not enough wax and back toward mediocrity.

Civilization would have to survive without the chart.

The woman with the date problem left with one corrected slip and the same expression she had entered with.

The crate for Sel disappeared while I was looking at wax.

Antonius came out once, saw me, saw the sealed packets, and went back into his room.

I received no medal.

By early afternoon my right hand had begun to feel the fine repetitive work differently from crutch use. Thumb and forefinger warm from string. A small wax smear dried near one nail. Shoulders fine because I had been seated. Right leg stiff enough that I stood during a natural break and walked the length of the room twice.

The clerk did not interpret this.

Neither did I.

I drank water.

Sat again.

Finished the row.

Then the stack.

The last packet had a thick fold and fought the string.

I pulled harder.

The knot shifted sideways.

The clerk looked over.

"Again."

"It is tied."

"Badly."

"It has commitment."

"Again."

I untied it.

The second knot sat flat.

Wax.

Stamp.

Done.

The clerk counted the finished packets without asking me to count with him.

Then carried them to Antonius.

I cleaned the knife and put the string ends into the scrap bowl because leaving them everywhere was apparently how offices became theatres.

Antonius came out with the account book.

He opened it to my line.

I saw the current figure before he wrote the new credit.

Still not small.

Smaller after.

He wrote.

Closed the book.

"Useful?"

I asked.

He looked at the sealed packets.

"They are leaving."

This family of answers had infected the city.

"Credit recorded?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Antonius's eyebrow moved.

I regretted nothing.

Outside, afternoon had already started bending toward evening.

I could have gone home.

Instead I went to the theatre.

Not because I thought they needed me now.

That possibility had already been answered by time.

I wanted to know.

The front doors were open and people were coming out.

So the performance had happened.

That answered one question before I entered.

Inside, the house was becoming a room again.

Audience voices thinning.

Benches visible.

Rinna at the side with coins and a small ledger.

Nessa carrying two costume pieces toward the rear passage.

Jori lifting the brown door frame with Davin.

The Missing Key had survived.

I had not been required.

For one stupid second that hurt.

Then Marek came offstage with the brass-colored key in one hand.

He saw me.

"Dead."

"What?"

"You died."

"I was at Vale."

"Debt death."

"Was the play good?"

He considered.

"Hara was."

Accurate.

"Messenger?"

Marek pointed with the key.

"Venn."

Good.

Venn existed.

The world had not waited for my body to become available.

Nessa reached Marek and held out her hand.

He put the key into it immediately.

She looked at me.

"You missed coat."

"I noticed."

"Venn fit."

I looked at the green coat over her arm.

"Better?"

"Different."

That was probably the kindest answer available.

Teren came down the house aisle.

He saw me.

"Vale?"

"Yes."

"Fine."

I waited.

He kept walking.

I turned.

"That's it?"

He stopped.

"What do you want?"

"I don't know."

"Then yes."

Fair.

"Did Venn do Messenger?"

"Yes."

"Good?"

"He left."

That landed exactly where he intended it to.

I smiled despite myself.

"Excellent."

"Tomorrow, different pair."

"So The Missing Key is done?"

"For now."

No punishment.

No reclaimed role.

No schedule.

It had run because it was useful today.

I had not been there.

Somebody else had done the work.

That was theatre too.

Rinna finished with the ledger and came over.

"The boy found you."

"Yes."

"You stayed."

"Yes."

"Vale?"

"Credit."

She nodded.

"Good."

I pointed at her.

"No."

She almost smiled.

No theatre pay waited for me because I had not done theatre work.

This also seemed offensively fair.

I helped nobody with strike.

Not because I was angry.

Because the workday I had actually chosen had already used my hands, and Jori and Davin had the frame.

I said goodbye to nobody formally.

Marek was arguing with Pell about whether a key could be considered missing if the wrong key had been found.

Hara had taken off the repaired shoe and was rubbing one heel through her stocking.

Nessa had the green coat.

Teren had another set of pages.

The theatre continued.

I left.

On the way home I bought a small hot pastry filled with mashed roots.

I paid for it.

No debt arithmetic.

I had just spent the day reducing the debt and was still allowed to be hungry.

The pastry burned my tongue.

Justice.

At home, Lyssa was there before me.

The brown cloth remained near the wall.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf.

Three loose threads.

The paper-wrapped item was not on the table.

The beans were still wherever beans went when they acquired plans.

Lyssa looked at the empty space in my hands.

"Theatre?"

"Yes."

"Show?"

"Yes."

She waited.

"Without me."

That got her attention.

"Why?"

"Vale had work."

"Good?"

I sat down.

"Depends who you ask."

"Who?"

"Vale credited me."

She nodded.

"Theatre replaced me."

Another nod.

Neither answer seemed to trouble her.

I took bread.

"Venn did it."

"Did what?"

"Messenger."

She had already lost interest in the administrative structure of my disappointment.

Probably healthy.

I ate.

After a while she said, "You chose."

"Yes."

"Want different?"

I thought about the green coat.

The warm wax.

Teren saying fine.

The account figure getting smaller.

Hara probably being good without me.

"No."

Lyssa nodded.

Then reached for my pastry.

I moved it.

Her hand followed.

"You ate," I said.

"So did you."

"Earlier."

She looked at me.

I had made a tactical error.

I tore the pastry in half.

She took the larger piece.

Of course.

I ate mine before the office could send anyone to seal it.
