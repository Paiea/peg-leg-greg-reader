LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 239

THE WAITER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa was still home when I woke, which meant I had misunderstood the sound of the door.

She was standing beside the table with her hair half tied back and one shoe on, looking through the small jar where we kept salt.

This was not promising.

"How bad?"

She tipped the jar toward me.

Enough white crystals slid down the side to insult us.

"Technical answer?"

"No."

"Low."

"Good."

"That was not good."

"I know."

The brown cloth remained near the wall with the same narrow fold and the same three pins. My damaged shirt remained on the shelf with three loose threads. The beans were still absent. The paper-wrapped thing from two nights ago was still absent. Nothing had returned merely because I was awake enough to notice it.

Lyssa put the salt jar down.

"I'm going north."

"Work?"

She looked at me.

"Right. Stupid question."

"Yes."

I tore yesterday's bread in half. It resisted enough that I had to brace it against the table.

Lyssa watched.

"Buy bread."

"I had identified that."

"Salt."

"I had not identified that."

"Now you have."

She finished tying her hair.

"Anything else?"

"No."

That should have been manageable.

Bread.

Salt.

Two objects.

Neither magical.

Neither theatrical.

Neither attached to Antonius Vale.

I had recently completed work involving names, house marks, string, wax, authorization, and enough procedure to make two household purchases feel offensively easy.

Lyssa took the smaller half of the bread.

I stared.

"You assigned me an errand and taxed the starting provisions."

"You'll buy bread."

"With what strength?"

She bit into it.

"Old bread."

Then she left.

No Hessa note appeared under the door.

No theatre boy knocked.

Nobody from Vale arrived carrying a warm pot of wax and a threat about incomplete packets.

The morning remained mine.

This felt suspicious for almost five minutes.

Then I ate the other half of the bread, washed, checked my left crutch tip because it had become boring enough to deserve respect, and went west.

The air was colder than the day before, but the sky had lost the hard brightness. Clouds flattened the light over Carrow and made the painted signs look older. The stones near the drain channels were damp without being wet enough to justify caution, which was the worst kind of damp.

My shoulders felt normal after yesterday's seated work. Right hand normal. Thumb and forefinger no longer warm from string. Residual limb comfortable. Right leg fine. The left crutch tip held.

I bought bread first.

This was important because bread had witnesses.

The baker gave me a loaf with a split along the top and named the price. I paid it. He handed it across.

One task completed.

I put the loaf into a narrow cloth bag and hooked the bag high against my right side where it would not swing into the crutch.

Salt next.

I did not buy salt next.

This was Hara's fault.

I saw her outside a cobbler near the west steps with one shoe on and the other in her hand.

Technically, I could have continued walking.

Socially, she saw me.

"Greg."

I stopped.

"That sounded accusatory."

"It is."

She held up the shoe.

I recognized it.

Left shoe. The sole had separated near the toe a few days ago. She had complained about it over soup while everyone else offered competing cobblers and Marek offered a crime against leather.

The sole was attached now.

The heel apparently had declared independence.

"What happened?"

"The repair worked."

"That sounds terrible."

"It is."

She turned the shoe and pressed one thumb inside near the heel.

"I wore it yesterday."

"I saw."

"You saw my shoe?"

"I saw you take it off after the show."

"Strange."

"I was in the room."

"Still strange."

Fair.

The cobbler appeared in the doorway behind her. He was narrow, gray-haired, and had a leather apron with enough cut marks to count as writing.

"It is not the sole," he said.

Hara turned.

"I know."

"You brought it back like the sole failed."

"I brought it back because the shoe hurts."

"That is a different complaint."

"I have one foot in it. I know."

The cobbler looked at me.

I raised one hand.

"Not involved."

"Good."

Hara said, "Coward."

"I learned from professionals."

The cobbler took the shoe back from her and pressed the heel between both thumbs.

"The new stitch pulled this side tighter."

"You stitched it."

"Yes."

"So fix it."

"I am."

"Excellent."

He disappeared inside.

Hara looked down at her stockinged left foot.

Then at me.

Then at the street.

"How long?"

The cobbler called from inside, "Half an hour."

Hara closed her eyes.

I should have left.

I still needed salt.

Half an hour was not an obligation.

Then Hara said, "Sit."

I looked at the bench beside the cobbler's door.

"Was that to me?"

"You're the only person here with two crutches."

"That does not establish authority."

"Sit."

I sat.

Hara sat beside me and stretched her left foot out so the stocking did not touch the damp stone.

For several seconds neither of us spoke.

This was new.

At theatre, silence usually meant somebody had forgotten a cue or Teren was about to make the silence our fault.

Here it meant a cobbler had Hara's shoe.

A cart rolled down the west steps too fast and received three separate insults from people who had to move. A woman carrying bundled reeds stopped at the corner to argue with a man selling onions. Someone above us shook a rug from a window and immediately apologized to nobody.

Hara said, "You missed yesterday."

There it was.

Not accusation.

Fact.

"Vale had work."

"I know."

"How?"

"Rinna said."

"Of course she did."

"Venn survived."

Hara looked at me.

"Did you think he wouldn't?"

"No."

I paused.

"Not exactly."

She nodded.

No rescue.

No reassurance.

Good.

I looked toward the cobbler's open door.

"Did the play survive?"

"Yes."

"Was Marek still innocent?"

"No."

"He wasn't innocent when I was there."

"He disagreed."

"Of course."

Hara watched a man carry two baskets down the steps.

"Venn left after his lines."

I looked at her.

She did not smile.

"Everyone is becoming hostile."

"Maybe you should learn."

"I was told not to improve it."

"Then don't."

That was all the theatre talk we managed.

The cobbler remained inside.

I remembered salt.

"I have an errand."

Hara looked at my bread bag.

"You bought bread."

"One of two."

"Salt?"

I stared at her.

She smiled.

"Lyssa?"

"Yes."

"Go."

"You ordered me to sit."

"I changed my mind."

"Strong leadership."

I stood.

Hara stood too, then looked down at her stockinged foot.

"No."

I looked at her.

She sat again.

"Correct."

The salt stall was visible from the top of the next lane if I took the shorter way around the dye carts. I could have gone, bought salt, returned, and still spent most of the cobbler's half hour listening to Hara insult leather.

Instead I said, "I'll get yours if you need anything."

She looked at me.

I regretted the sentence because it sounded more useful than intended.

"Salt?"

"No."

"Bread?"

"No."

"Then I have successfully offered nothing."

"Wait."

I waited.

She dug into the small purse at her belt and held out a copper.

"Roasted peas. Stall by the red awning."

I looked down the lane.

"Why?"

"Because I'm hungry."

"Excellent reason."

"Small bag."

"How small?"

She pointed at the coin.

"This small."

I took it.

Now I had three tasks.

Dangerous.

The salt seller was where I remembered. I bought a small paper twist of coarse salt and put it carefully beside the loaf where bread could not become seasoned through structural failure.

Two tasks completed.

The roasted-pea stall had a red awning and a woman with one arm elbow-deep in a basket of dried pods.

"Small bag," I said.

She looked at the copper.

"For Hara?"

I stopped.

"That was fast."

She pointed past me.

Hara was visible at the cobbler's bench.

"Less impressive."

The woman scooped roasted peas into a folded paper cone.

"She usually buys two."

"She gave me one copper."

"Then she gets one."

"No loyalty discount?"

"No."

"Healthy business."

She handed me the cone.

I brought it back.

Hara took it and immediately ate three peas.

"You got salt."

"Yes."

"Good."

"Stop saying that."

She held out the cone.

I took one pea.

This was apparently my courier fee.

The cobbler came out ten minutes later with Hara's shoe and a strip of dark leather folded over one finger.

He handed her the shoe.

"Try."

Hara put it on.

Her face changed.

Not much.

Enough.

"Different."

"Better?"

"Different."

I looked at her.

She noticed.

"Don't."

I said nothing.

The cobbler crouched in front of her and pressed the heel while she stood.

"Walk."

Hara took six steps along the flat section beside the shops, turned, and came back.

"Less."

"Good."

She looked at me.

I pointed at the cobbler.

"His word."

"Still hate it."

"Now you understand."

The cobbler ignored us.

"Walk farther. Come back if it bites."

"Bites?"

"You know."

"I know."

Hara paid him.

Then she looked down the street.

I had bread.

I had salt.

My errands were complete.

I could go home.

Hara said, "Walk."

I stared.

"You have both shoes now."

"Yes."

"So why am I involved?"

"If it starts hurting again, I need a witness."

"To what?"

"My murder."

"Of the cobbler?"

"He'll know."

The cobbler, still in the doorway, said, "Go away."

We went away.

Hara set the pace without asking whether I could match it. She already knew how I moved from months of stages, passages, streets after work, and one cookshop where everyone had paid for their own bowl. She did not slow dramatically or take my bag. She simply did not choose the broken edge of the lane when the middle was clear.

We walked south from the west steps, then turned toward the lower market.

"Still?"

I asked after one street.

"Fine."

"That sounded disappointed."

"I paid for a complaint."

"You paid for less pain."

"Less interesting."

A child ran between us chasing a hoop.

Hara moved left.

I moved right.

The hoop survived.

Nobody learned anything.

At the lower market, Hara stopped at a vegetable stall.

I stopped because she did.

The seller was an older man with a nose like a badly carved handle and hands blackened with soil.

He looked at Hara.

"Late."

"I was buying my own foot back."

He looked down.

"Again?"

"Different problem."

"Same shoe."

Hara pointed at him.

"Don't."

He grinned and started gathering greens without asking what she wanted.

I watched.

"Regular?"

Hara looked at me.

"No. Hostage."

The seller put two bundles on the cloth.

"One."

Hara pushed one back.

"Two."

"One."

"You always take two."

"Not today."

He looked at me.

"She always difficult?"

"About vegetables?"

"Everything."

Hara stepped on my right crutch tip.

Not hard.

Enough.

I leaned away.

"Violence."

"Accuracy."

The seller laughed and took her coin.

He did not ask who I was.

Healthy.

Hara tucked the greens under one arm.

"Still fine?"

I asked.

She took two steps.

"Yes."

We kept walking.

The roasted peas did not survive to the next corner.

Hara folded the empty paper cone into a small square and put it in her pocket.

"You keep trash?"

"Until I see a rubbish barrel."

"Responsible."

"You sound surprised."

"I have seen Marek."

"Low standard."

At a cookshop near the lower lane, steam rolled through the open front and smelled like onions, beans, and something peppered enough to make my nose hurt pleasantly.

Hara stopped.

"Food."

"I have bread."

"That is an object."

"It is food."

"Later."

I could have gone home.

Again.

I did not.

Inside, the room was narrow and already half full. Hara took a table near the wall after a man lifted his coat from the second stool without being asked.

She sat.

I put my crutches against the wall where they would not trip the server.

No one moved a chair for me.

Also good.

A woman came over.

Hara said, "Bean."

The woman looked at me.

"Same."

Hara looked at me.

"You don't know what it costs."

"I have money."

"Vale ruined you."

"Rinna said that first."

"Then listen."

The bowls came with beans, chopped greens, and a piece of dark bread soaked at one edge.

I paid for mine.

Hara paid for hers.

The day remained financially complicated enough without merging lunch.

For a while we ate.

Hara was different when nobody was waiting for her to speak.

Less still than onstage, somehow.

At theatre, she could hold a room by doing almost nothing because everyone was watching. Here nobody watched. She bent over the bowl and ate like somebody who had spent the morning arguing with a shoe.

A man at the next table recognized her.

Not me.

"Hara?"

She looked up.

He frowned as if checking his own memory.

"The widow."

"The one with the ditch?"

He smiled.

Hara closed her eyes.

"Yes."

"Good show."

"Thank you."

He went back to his bowl.

I waited.

Hara looked at me.

I smiled.

"No."

"I said nothing."

"You were about to."

"I was going to ask whether justice improved drainage."

She pointed her spoon at me.

"Don't."

I ate beans.

Her face slowly betrayed her.

She laughed once.

Quiet.

Then went back to eating.

That was enough.

He did not ask her to repeat the line or perform anything for him. He went back to lunch.

Outside, we walked another two streets because Hara wanted to test the heel before going wherever she was going next.

I did not ask where.

This showed significant growth.

The shoe stayed on.

The heel did not bite.

At the crossing near South Steps, Hara stopped.

"This is me."

I looked down both streets.

"Which?"

"Not your business."

"Excellent."

She adjusted the greens under her arm.

"Salt."

I looked at my bread bag.

The paper twist was still there.

"Yes."

"Good."

I pointed at her.

She walked away before I could improve the argument.

By the time I reached home, my shoulders felt used and my right palm was warm from the longer walk. Right leg ordinarily tired. Residual limb comfortable. Nothing injured. Nothing new.

The room was empty.

Brown cloth.

Three pins.

Same fold.

Damaged shirt.

Three threads.

Same shelf.

I put the bread on the table.

Then the salt beside it.

Two tasks completed despite social interference.

I considered this an excellent record and did not write it down.

Lyssa came back late enough that I had already cut the loaf.

She carried one small apple and a wedge of cheese wrapped in cloth.

She looked at the table.

"Bread."

"Yes."

"Salt."

I put one hand over my heart.

"Both."

"Good."

I stared at her.

She bit the apple.

"You've had a difficult day."

"You have no idea."

She looked at my face, then at the bread.

"Theatre?"

"No."

"Vale?"

"No."

"Magic?"

"No."

That exhausted the usual categories.

Lyssa waited.

"Hara's shoe."

She chewed.

"What?"

"Exactly."

I told her the sole repair had tightened the heel, the cobbler had adjusted it, and Hara had made me walk until the shoe failed to murder her.

Lyssa listened to maybe half.

"Did it?"

"Did what?"

"Murder her."

"No."

"Good."

I pointed at her again.

She took another bite of apple.

I gave up.

We ate bread with the cheese and split the apple badly enough that Lyssa got more apple.

The brown cloth remained near the wall.

The shirt remained on the shelf.

No one asked anything of me for the rest of the night.

It was almost enough to make waiting feel like a job.
