LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 244

THE SCRAPER

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

There was blue paint on the boy's sleeve.

That was how I knew the theatre wanted labor before he said anything.

He stood outside our door with one hand on the frame and the expression of someone who had already climbed too many stairs for other people's employment.

"Rinna," he said.

"Of course."

"Before midday if you're free."

I looked at his sleeve.

"Blue?"

He looked too.

"Davin."

That explained less than he thought.

"What happened?"

"Wall."

"Better."

He shrugged.

"Bring clothes you don't care about."

Then he left.

I closed the door.

Lyssa had already gone. No note. No customer. No explanation.

The brown cloth remained where it had been.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf with three loose threads.

That created a problem.

I had just been told to wear clothes I did not care about, and the shirt I cared about least was also the shirt most likely to become a pile if I gave it additional work.

I put on the other one.

The Hessa note from yesterday still sat on the table. FAR ONLY. EAT FIRST.

Done.

The counts in my head were thirty-five, thirty-one, fifty-three.

They could stay there.

One clean far response out of four had not changed any rule. It had not created practice. It had not created range. It had not created a reason to spend the morning staring at paper.

Theatre had created a reason to leave.

I ate.

Outside, Carrow was gray without committing to rain. The stones still held yesterday's dust, but the air had the wet smell that meant somebody, somewhere, was about to blame the sky for something.

My body was ordinary after the Hessa session. Right forearm normal again. Shoulders fine. Right leg fine. Residual limb comfortable. Left crutch tip still boring.

I took the lower route because the upper lane had two wagons trying to occupy the same width of street.

They were discussing geometry loudly.

I let them solve it without me.

The theatre's front doors were open, but the stage was dark.

I heard scraping before I saw anybody.

Not rehearsal scraping.

Real scraping.

A dry, ugly rhythm came from the rear yard.

Shhk.

Shhk.

Pause.

Shhk.

I went around.

Two wall flats stood on trestles in the courtyard with their painted faces turned toward the light. One had been part of the blue-trimmed merchant room. The other had a pale cream surface with a brown rectangle painted where a door had once pretended to exist.

Both looked diseased.

Paint had blistered in thin scales across the lower halves. One blue strip had lifted enough that I could see another color beneath it.

Green.

Davin sat on an upturned bucket with a broad dull scraper in one hand.

Jori stood at the far end marking short chalk lines around sections of paint.

Rinna was at the rear door with a ledger under one arm.

She looked at me.

"Free?"

"Currently."

"Good."

I hated the word now.

She pointed at the flats.

"Those need to be usable tomorrow."

"As walls?"

"That is the ambition."

"What are they tomorrow?"

"Kitchen."

I looked at the blue trim.

"Blue kitchen."

"Not after Davin."

Davin raised the scraper.

"I improve."

Jori said, "He removes."

"Improvement by subtraction."

Rinna looked at me.

"You know how to scrape paint?"

"Yes."

Jori said, "No."

I looked at him.

"I know the concept."

"That's different."

Fair.

Rinna went back inside before the argument could become billable.

Jori handed me a second scraper.

It had a wooden handle polished dark by hands and a blade with one corner slightly rounder than the other.

"Loose only."

"I understand loose."

"No, you understand attacking."

"I have never attacked a wall."

Davin looked at the blue flat.

"Give him time."

Jori tapped one of his chalk lines.

"Anything outside the marks comes off if it's lifting. Inside stays unless I say."

"Why?"

"Because we can use some of the old color under the new coat."

I crouched enough to look at the edge.

The blue trim was not one layer.

Blue sat over cream.

Cream sat over something dark red.

At one chipped corner, a thin black line curved under all three.

"How old is this?"

Jori shrugged.

"Wall."

"That's not an age."

"It was here before you."

Davin said, "Older than Greg."

"Most furniture is."

"Not Marek."

"Marek is not furniture."

Jori looked toward the building.

"Debatable."

I sat on a low stool so I could work the lower half without leaning over my crutches. They rested against the courtyard wall where nobody needed the lane.

Jori showed me the angle.

Flatter than I expected.

Not digging.

Slide under what had already lifted and let the bad layer choose to leave.

My first pass produced nothing.

"Good," Jori said.

"I did nothing."

"Exactly."

Hostile trade.

I tried again.

The blade caught a loose edge.

A strip of blue paint lifted, curled, and came away in a piece as long as my thumb.

Green showed underneath.

Davin pointed.

"Wall."

"I had begun to suspect."

We worked.

The sound settled into rhythm.

Shhk.

Tap the blade against the bucket.

Shhk.

Brush flakes aside with a rag.

Jori moved between the two flats, testing edges with his thumb before scraping. Davin worked faster but stopped every few minutes to run his palm over the surface and check whether the lifted area actually ended where it looked like it ended.

I was slower than both.

That was fine.

The lower section in front of me had enough bad paint to make progress visible without requiring skill I did not have yet.

Blue came away in islands.

Cream appeared.

Then green.

At one point a little gold star emerged under the cream.

I stopped.

"Star."

Nobody answered.

I scraped around it carefully.

The whole shape appeared.

Five points.

Badly painted.

Davin looked over.

"Oh."

"What was that?"

"Star."

"Strong historical record."

Jori came around.

He looked at it.

"Old nursery piece."

"You remember?"

"No."

I looked at him.

"Then how do you know?"

He pointed to two more tiny stars hidden farther up the flat.

"Pattern."

Davin said, "Could've been a bedroom."

Jori shrugged.

"Could've."

That was all the theatre archaeology I received.

Nobody named the play.

Nobody told me who had stood in front of the stars.

The wall had been a wall more times than any of us needed to count.

Jori scraped straight through one of the stars.

I stared at him.

"You destroyed history."

"It was loose."

"Monster."

"Scrape."

I scraped.

A little later Venn came into the yard carrying a bucket of water and two rags.

I had seen him yesterday only in other people's sentences.

Venn fit the green coat.

Venn played Messenger.

Venn left.

Today he was a narrow man with dark curls pressed flat on one side of his head and a shirt that had already lost an argument with white paint.

He put the bucket down.

Jori said, "Edges."

Venn nodded.

No introduction.

Apparently we had both worked here enough to skip one.

He started wiping dust from the sections Davin had finished.

I watched for a few seconds.

"Messenger."

Venn looked at me.

"Yesterday."

"Good exit."

He stared.

Behind me, Davin made a noise that might have been a cough.

Venn said, "I walked off."

"Exactly."

He looked at Jori.

"Is he like this all day?"

Jori said, "Sometimes worse."

Excellent.

Venn went back to wiping the wall.

That settled our relationship.

By late morning, my right palm had started to warm where the scraper handle pressed into it.

Not pain.

Different work.

I changed grip and used the rounder corner on a small patch near the bottom.

The problem with simple work was that it became easier exactly when it became dangerous.

At first I had thought about every stroke.

Angle.

Pressure.

Loose edge.

Chalk.

Then my body learned enough of the motion that my mind wandered.

Not far.

Just far enough.

I found a long lifted edge and followed it.

Blue curled away.

Cream beneath.

The strip came beautifully.

Six inches.

Eight.

Ten.

Then Jori said, "Stop."

I stopped.

The blade was two inches inside his chalk line.

I looked down.

The peeled strip crossed cleanly into the section he had told me to preserve.

"Ah."

Davin leaned over from the other flat.

"Future."

I looked at him.

"What?"

"You're scraping tomorrow now."

Jori crouched beside me.

He rubbed the exposed cream with one finger.

It was solid.

Not loose.

I had simply gotten under it because I had been enjoying myself.

"That stays," he said.

"Apparently not all of it."

"No."

I waited for the lecture.

None came.

Jori stood.

"Davin."

Davin looked over.

"Blue."

"Later."

"Mostly him."

"Yes."

I frowned.

"You're not supposed to agree that quickly."

Davin grinned.

Jori handed me a piece of chalk.

"New line."

I looked at the flat.

"Where?"

"Here."

He showed me the edge of the area again.

I redrew the line around the damage I had made.

"Now?"

"Don't cross it."

"I understood that the first time."

"Evidence mixed."

Venn laughed into his rag.

I went back to work.

The mistake cost us exactly what it had cost.

A little more paint later.

No shame ceremony.

No story about learning boundaries.

I just had a chalk line I had personally made and stronger reasons to notice it.

Around midday, the gray sky finally produced rain.

Not much.

Enough to send all four of us into motion.

Jori and Davin lifted the blue flat.

Venn grabbed the rear trestle.

I took my crutches and got out of the lane.

That was my contribution.

The flat went under the rear overhang.

The second followed.

The rain lasted three minutes.

Davin stood under the overhang staring at it.

"Done."

Jori said, "Weather?"

"Yes."

"Good repair."

"Mostly me."

I ate bread against the wall.

Venn had an onion pie that smelled better.

I looked at it.

He looked at me.

"No."

"I said nothing."

"You looked."

Apparently Lyssa had spread.

Rinna came through carrying a stack of folded cloths.

"How much?"

Jori pointed at the flats.

"Enough."

Rinna inspected the blue one.

Her eyes found my pale scrape because of course they did.

"That?"

"Greg," Jori said.

I raised my bread.

"Present."

Rinna looked at the patch.

"Problem?"

"Paint."

"Tomorrow?"

"Fine."

She nodded.

Then she handed the cloths to Venn.

"Front room."

Venn took them.

No argument.

Rinna disappeared again.

I looked at Jori.

"That's it?"

"What?"

"I damaged company property."

Davin said, "You improved by subtraction."

"Do not help."

Jori picked up his scraper.

"Paint isn't company property."

I looked at the wall.

"The wall is."

"Then don't scrape through the wood."

Clear standard.

After bread, we turned the cream flat around.

The back was black.

Not painted black for a scene.

Black in the practical theatre sense, dull and uneven, with old nail holes and two patches where canvas had been repaired.

Nessa appeared in the doorway.

She had a shallow wooden box against one hip.

"Keep that side."

Jori said, "We are."

She pointed at the bottom corner.

"Not that."

I looked.

A little square of canvas had begun separating from the frame.

Davin put his scraper down.

"Glue?"

"Stitch first," Nessa said.

Davin pressed the canvas gently.

"Still good."

"Yes."

She handed him the box.

Inside were heavy thread, needles, two small wood pieces, and things I did not recognize.

Then she left.

Davin changed jobs immediately.

No protest about being the paint man.

No speech about authority.

He sat where I had been, pulled the loose canvas edge into place, and began working the repair with the kind of concentration that made conversation feel rude.

I moved to the other flat.

Jori said, "Top half."

I looked up.

Standing work.

"How long?"

"Until you're tired."

Specific enough.

I planted both crutches, leaned them against the trestle one at a time, and worked a section I could reach without twisting.

The scraper needed both hands only sometimes.

Most of the time one hand did the work while the other steadied me against the trestle or took the crutch again when I changed position.

It was slower.

Jori did not offer to make it fast.

He moved to the opposite side and kept scraping his section.

After ten minutes my right leg began to feel the standing.

After fifteen, my shoulder wanted a different angle.

I sat.

Nobody congratulated me for recognizing fatigue.

Davin had repaired the canvas corner before I noticed he was done.

He handed the box back to Nessa when she returned.

She checked the patch.

Pulled once.

"Good."

Davin looked at me.

I pointed at him.

"Don't."

He smiled.

Nessa took the box inside.

The afternoon became paint.

Old paint.

Bad paint.

Dust in my hair.

Dust on my sleeves.

Dust inside the fold of my collar.

The cream flat surrendered a patch of dark red wide enough to look like a stripe from an older room. Venn returned and wiped the cleaned sections. Jori used a small brush to clear the frame edges. Davin mixed a thin wash in a bucket for the scraped patch I had created.

I watched him.

"Blue?"

"Blue."

"Same blue?"

He looked into the bucket.

"Blue enough."

Healthy artistic standard.

He painted over my mistake.

The wet patch was visibly newer than the rest.

Jori looked at it.

"Dry darker."

Davin said, "Mostly."

"How much mostly?"

"Tomorrow."

There was no answer to that.

By late afternoon, the two flats were not beautiful.

They were usable.

Loose paint gone.

Edges cleaned.

Canvas corner repaired.

My pale mistake blue again.

The old gold stars mostly erased except one high enough that nobody had needed to touch it.

I looked at it.

Jori followed my eyes.

"Loose?"

"No."

"Then it stays."

History survived through structural integrity.

I liked that more than I should have.

Rinna came out with a coin.

One copper.

Normal theatre work.

I took it.

"No rehearsal?"

"No."

"Tomorrow?"

"Not for you yet."

Clear.

"No wall role?"

Rinna looked at the flats.

"You've had enough."

Davin said, "He could stand very still."

"Better than Marek," Venn said.

I looked at him.

"You've been here six hours and already betrayed him."

"I've been here longer than six hours."

Right.

Other people existed before I noticed them.

Annoying feature of the world.

We carried the tools in.

I carried only the bucket of rags because it could hang from two fingers while I used the crutches carefully through the rear passage. Venn took the scraper box. Jori and Davin took the flats once the paint was dry enough to move.

Nobody gave me the awkward wall to prove anything.

Good workplace.

The repaired back latch shut cleanly behind us when Jori tested the door after the last flat came through.

It shut because it had been repaired.

Then he opened it again because Davin was still outside.

Ordinary technology remained complicated.

No one mentioned brown coats.

No one mentioned deliveries.

No one mentioned who opened the door for them.

The latest fact stayed where it was.

Rinna was arguing with a paper at the front table when I left.

Not a person.

The paper appeared to be winning.

I did not volunteer.

Outside, the rain had passed and left the streets darker without making them cleaner.

Blue paint remained under two fingernails despite washing.

My right palm was warm from the scraper. Shoulders used. Right leg mildly tired from the standing section. Residual limb comfortable. No pain. No weakness. No new problem.

I bought nothing on the walk home.

This was less financially noble than it sounded. I had bread waiting.

Lyssa was there before me.

That happened often enough now that neither of us treated it as information.

She was at the table with a dark piece of cloth that was not the brown cloth.

I stopped.

She looked at me.

"Don't."

"I have learned."

"Have you?"

"No."

She went back to the dark cloth.

I put my crutches against the wall.

The brown cloth remained in its old place.

Three pins.

Same fold.

The damaged shirt remained on the shelf.

Three threads.

Same failure.

I washed my hands again.

Blue came off one finger and stayed under another nail.

Lyssa looked over.

"Paint."

"Wall."

"Blue."

"Mostly."

She looked at me.

I pointed.

"Davin."

That explained exactly as much to her as the boy's answer had explained to me.

"Work?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Show?"

"No."

"Role?"

"Scraper."

She nodded.

Apparently valid.

I cut bread.

Lyssa worked for another minute, then said, "You missed."

I looked at the loaf.

"No."

She pointed at my sleeve.

A small blue streak crossed the outside of my forearm.

"That is theatre."

"On your shirt."

"Still theatre."

"Your shirt."

I looked at it.

Not the damaged one.

The better one.

Of course.

I rubbed the streak with my thumb.

It spread.

Lyssa watched.

"Improved."

I stopped touching it.

"Everyone has been saying that today."

She almost smiled.

I ate bread.

After a while she moved the dark cloth farther from my painted sleeve without comment.

That was fair.

"What was the wall before?" she asked.

I thought about the green layer.

The cream.

The dark red.

The little gold star.

"Several things."

"What now?"

"Kitchen."

She accepted that.

No questions about magic.

No questions about Vale.

No questions about the theatre's strangers.

I did not offer any.

The new dark cloth remained whatever it was.

The old brown cloth remained whatever it was.

My shirt had acquired blue paint without solving the three loose threads on the other one.

This felt like progress in the most honest available sense.

Before bed, I washed my hands a third time.

The last blue mark under my nail stayed.

I left it there.
