TEXT READER · CHAPTER 245

THE NAME-TAKER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I met Vale's runner halfway down the stairs.

He was coming up fast enough that we nearly invented a new use for my crutches.

He stopped two steps below me, put one hand on the rail, and said, "Antonius."

I looked past him toward the bottom landing.

"Is he down there?"

"No."

"Good. That would have been strange."

The runner had already decided not to participate.

"Work," he said. "If you want it. Now."

That was specific enough to respect.

Lyssa had left before breakfast. The brown cloth still held the same fold and three pins. My damaged shirt still had three loose threads. The better shirt still carried yesterday's theatre and the older blue paint on one sleeve, so I had put on the damaged one carefully and left the threads alone.

Nothing else in the room had claimed me.

I had been heading downstairs because staying upstairs without a reason had started to feel like a decision.

"Vale office?"

The runner nodded.

"What kind of work?"

He was already turning.

"Ask Vale."

"I appreciate the specialization."

He went down.

I followed more slowly.

Outside, the morning had enough cloud to flatten the street without threatening rain. A cart had stopped crooked near the corner while two men argued about whether the wheel or the street was responsible. A woman carrying a basket stepped around both of them and solved the problem by continuing with her life.

I went to Vale.

The front room usually contained work even when it did not contain work for me.

Today it contained people.

Seven, when I entered.

I counted because the room made counting necessary.

Two sat on the wall bench. Three stood near the front desk. One man had chosen the narrow strip beside the door and was making every arrival negotiate around him. A woman with a little girl held a folded paper in one hand and the child's wrist in the other. The child was trying to reach the inkwell on the clerk's desk with the patience of somebody who expected adults to fail eventually.

The ink-fingered clerk saw me.

"Vale."

"I assumed."

"Back."

I went back.

Antonius stood in the second room with an open ledger and another man I had seen before but never spoken to. They were not waiting for me. The other man had one finger on a line halfway down the page. Antonius finished whatever he was saying before he looked up.

That was healthier than being the center of rooms.

"Greg."

"Runner said work."

"Yes."

"What work?"

Antonius closed the ledger.

"Front."

I looked toward the crowded doorway.

"No."

"Yes."

"I have no money to lend."

"Good."

"I have even less money to collect."

"Better."

He came around the table.

"One clerk is elsewhere. Front room is backed up. You take names, whether they brought a paper, and the order they arrived. Nothing else."

I waited.

"Nothing else sounds important."

"It is."

"Do I read the papers?"

"No."

"Answer questions?"

"No."

"What if I know the answer?"

"You do not."

That seemed unnecessarily broad.

Antonius walked past me.

I followed him into the front room.

The ink-fingered clerk had already cleared a narrow side table beside the wall. A straight chair sat behind it. There was one sheet of rough paper, an inkwell, and a pen.

He pointed.

"Names. Paper or no paper. Order."

"Vale said that."

"Then hear it twice."

I sat.

The chair placed me out of the main traffic if I kept both crutches tight against the wall. The right grip sat where my hand expected when I moved one crutch closer. The repaired wrap did nothing interesting.

The clerk put the pen in front of me.

"If they ask what they owe?"

"Wait."

"If they say they already paid?"

"Wait."

"If they say you know them?"

"Wait."

"If they are bleeding?"

He looked at me.

"Different problem."

"Good system."

"Start."

The man beside the door was first.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

"Name?"

He frowned.

"I was here before you."

"Excellent. Name?"

He gave it.

I wrote it.

"Paper?"

He held up a folded sheet.

I made a short mark beside his name.

"Sit."

"There is nowhere."

I looked at the bench.

There was, technically, room for half a person if everyone already seated disliked each other enough.

"Stand somewhere that isn't the door."

He looked at the clerk.

The clerk did not look back.

The man moved three feet.

Administrative success.

The woman with the little girl came next. She gave me her name, then caught the child's fingers less than an inch from my ink.

"Paper?"

The woman held up the folded sheet.

I marked it.

The girl looked at the pen.

I moved it farther in.

The girl looked at me.

I had made an enemy.

"How long?" the woman asked.

"I don't know."

She looked toward the clerk.

"Can he tell me?"

"Eventually."

The clerk said, without looking up, "Wait."

The woman sat on the edge of the bench. The child immediately found the one loose thread at the hem of her own sleeve and began pulling it.

I looked away.

I had enough damaged clothing in my life.

The third visitor was not a visitor.

He came through the door carrying a tied packet under one arm, walked directly toward the desk, and said, "South route."

I lifted the pen.

"Name?"

He kept moving.

"Not staying."

"That is not a name."

The ink-fingered clerk pointed to a shallow tray at the end of his desk.

"Route packet. There."

The courier dropped it in.

I looked at my list.

"Does he count?"

"No."

"Useful information."

The courier was already gone.

I drew one line through the empty third place.

The clerk looked over.

"Do not leave gaps."

"He arrived third."

"He was not waiting."

"History disagrees."

"List doesn't care."

I looked at the page.

Apparently the page had standards.

I moved the fourth name up when it arrived.

The morning developed a shape.

Name.

Paper or no paper.

Order.

Most people could do all three in less than a minute if they believed those were the only things happening.

Almost nobody believed that.

A gray-haired woman came in with no paper and said, "He knows why."

I looked at Antonius's closed inner door.

"I don't."

"He knows me."

"That seems useful for him."

She stared at me.

"Name?"

She gave it.

"Paper?"

"I said no."

I marked no paper.

"Sit."

"You're new."

"Temporary."

"Good."

I considered objecting to the word and decided I had been given enough authority for one morning.

A young man with a clean folded receipt wanted me to tell him whether the number in the corner included the last payment.

"Wait."

"You can read."

"Yes."

"So read it."

"I was told not to."

"By him?"

He pointed at the clerk.

"Twice, apparently."

The young man looked at the clerk.

The clerk kept writing.

He looked back at me.

"Then why are you here?"

"Names."

He gave me his.

"Paper?"

He waved it in front of my face.

I made the mark.

"Sit."

He did not sit, but he moved away.

That was close enough.

By the ninth person, the room had become less crowded because the clerk and Antonius were taking people through faster than they arrived. The list made the flow visible without making it mine.

That distinction lasted until a woman in a dark wool shawl put a paper on my table and said, "This is wrong."

I looked at the paper.

"Name?"

She gave it.

"Paper?"

She tapped it.

I marked the line.

"This number," she said.

I did not touch the page.

"Wait."

"It was lower."

"Wait."

"Last week."

"Wait."

"I paid him."

"Wait."

She leaned over the table.

"You keep saying that."

"It is the part I was trained for."

She pushed the paper closer.

The top carried one of the account marks I had seen during earlier work. Beneath it were two written lines and a smaller notation near the edge.

I recognized the shape.

Not the account.

The shape.

The clerk was speaking to someone else. Antonius's door was shut.

The woman said, "Look."

So I did.

That was the mistake.

I turned the paper enough to read the top line.

The ink-fingered clerk said, "Greg."

I stopped.

He was still speaking to the man at his desk.

He did not raise his voice.

"List."

I turned the paper back.

"I was looking."

"Yes."

"She asked."

"Yes."

The woman looked from him to me.

The clerk finished with the man in front of him, took the man's paper, and pointed toward the inner room.

Then he looked at the woman.

"You're after him."

She took her page off my table.

"I told you it was wrong."

The clerk said, "Then you tell me."

Not Greg.

Not the temporary name-taker.

Him.

She moved toward his desk.

I looked at my list.

Name.

Paper.

Order.

Nothing else.

The words had not changed because I had recognized a mark.

The gray-haired woman without paper watched me from the bench.

"You lasted a while."

I looked at her.

"Against what?"

"Helping."

I considered the accusation.

"Hostile room."

She smiled and looked away.

The next person gave me a name and no paper.

I wrote both facts.

He asked whether Antonius was angry.

I said, "Wait."

He asked whether that meant yes.

I said, "It means wait."

Better.

Around midday, Antonius came out and looked at the list.

He did not read from the top.

He pointed at a man who had arrived three places earlier than the current call.

"You."

The man stood.

I looked at the list.

"He came after two others."

Antonius looked at me.

"I sent for him."

The man went through the inner door.

I looked at the two names above his.

One of the people attached to them shrugged.

The other looked annoyed.

The list had not failed.

I had simply mistaken it for a law.

The ink-fingered clerk saw my face.

"Order unless called."

"That was omitted."

"You know now."

I added a small dot beside the man's name because I wanted the page to explain itself later.

The clerk saw that too.

"What is the dot?"

"Called early."

"Do I need it?"

I looked at him.

"No."

"Then don't."

I scratched it out.

My administrative innovations were experiencing a difficult day.

A little after midday, the front room emptied enough that I could stand without immediately becoming furniture.

My right leg had gone stiff from the chair. I put weight through both crutches, walked to the back wall and returned. The stiffness eased. Right palm normal. Shoulders normal. Residual limb comfortable.

The writing hand felt more used than the crutch hand for once.

The clerk tore a piece from his bread and ate it over the desk.

I looked at him.

"Do we close?"

"No."

"Do I eat?"

"Do you have food?"

"No."

He looked toward the street.

"Then buy food."

"Is that permission?"

"It's a problem with an obvious solution."

I stood.

"How long?"

"Come back before I dislike you."

"Measured time."

I bought a flat round of bread from a stall half a street away and a spoonful of beans from a pot that charged more for meat than I intended to spend. I ate standing near the wall because all the stools were taken.

Ordinary money left my hand.

The debt remained in Vale's office.

Both facts survived.

When I returned, three people had arrived.

The clerk had written their names at the bottom of my list in handwriting much worse than mine.

I sat.

"That one says Geren?"

"Yes."

"It looks like Goran."

"He knows what it says."

"Who?"

"Me."

The office continued.

The afternoon was quieter but not empty.

One man wanted to argue before he had reached the desk. He gave me his name while explaining that the charge was impossible, the date was wrong, the copy had been sent to the wrong house, and someone in Vale's office had the mind of a boiled root.

I wrote the name.

"Paper?"

"No."

He continued.

I waited.

He eventually stopped because I had not supplied the other half of the argument.

"Well?"

"Sit."

"Did you hear me?"

"Most of it."

"And?"

"Sit."

The gray-haired woman had left hours earlier. I missed her slightly.

The man stared at me, then sat.

When the clerk called him, he restarted the entire complaint from the beginning with renewed energy and finally put the folded paper on the desk.

The clerk listened longer than I had.

Then he looked at the mark at the top of the page.

"Wrong office."

The man stopped.

"What?"

"Not ours."

Silence.

The man looked at the paper.

Then at me.

I had not read it.

That had become the point.

The clerk gave him a short direction to another office two lanes north.

The man left without apologizing to boiled roots.

I looked at the clerk.

He dipped his pen.

"Name was right."

"Office was wrong."

"Yes."

"Could I have known that?"

"Not without doing the job I told you not to do."

I enjoyed that answer more than I should have.

By late afternoon, the second page of names had only six lines.

The clerk took it from me.

"Done."

I looked toward Antonius's room.

"Work done or people done?"

"For you."

That was clear.

Antonius saw me before I reached the door.

He came out with the small account book he used when recording my credit.

No ceremony.

He wrote while I watched. I knew how to read the balance by now.

Smaller.

Still active.

Still mine.

He closed the book.

"Anything else?" I asked.

"No."

He gave me no tomorrow, no maybe, and no promise. I liked the shape of that more than I expected.

The ink-fingered clerk was already dealing with another courier packet when I left. Somebody else would take tomorrow's names if tomorrow had names to take.

Carrow had cooled under the same flat cloud. The crooked cart from morning was gone. The street had survived being blamed.

I walked home without going to the theatre.

Not because I was avoiding it.

Because nobody had asked.

I also did not go to Hessa.

Same reason.

At the apartment, Lyssa was already there. No customer. No new dark cloth. The brown cloth still held three pins in the same fold. My better shirt still had blue paint on the sleeve where I had left it. The damaged shirt was still on me, three threads no worse than morning.

Lyssa looked at my right hand.

I looked too.

A thin line of ink had dried along the side of my middle finger.

"Vale?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Work?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"Names."

She stopped cutting an onion.

"Names."

"And paper."

She waited.

"And order."

"Money?"

"I did not touch money."

"Good."

I looked at her.

She pointed the knife at my hand.

"Safer."

"Hostile household."

She went back to the onion.

I told her about the woman who wanted me to read the wrong number.

"You did?"

"Briefly."

"What happened?"

"The clerk remembered my job."

Lyssa nodded.

I told her about the man who spent several minutes explaining that Vale's office had the mind of a boiled root.

"What was wrong?"

"Wrong office."

She laughed once.

"Did you tell him?"

"No."

"Why?"

"I didn't know."

"Good."

I pointed at her.

She kept cutting.

I gave up before dinner.

We ate onions with bread and the last of the greens from yesterday.

By then the names had already begun to leave my head.

I remembered the woman with the child because the child had wanted my ink. I remembered the gray-haired woman because she had accused me of helping. I remembered the man at the wrong office because he had worked hard for the wrong room.

The rest stayed on two sheets in Antonius's office.

That was where they belonged.
