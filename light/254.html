TEXT READER · CHAPTER 254

THE USHER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The Uncle pages were in my coat.

That was the first thing I checked before leaving.

Not because I needed them.

Because I had decided to return them and had enough experience with theatre to know that deciding to return an object did not guarantee the object would participate.

Lyssa was already gone.

The long brown cloth remained folded near the wall.

Three pins.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf.

Three loose threads.

The pale thing from beside Lyssa's work bag was gone.

This told me exactly nothing except that objects continued having lives when I was not supervising them.

Healthy.

I ate bread.

Not the last bread.

Growth had occurred somewhere.

The better shirt still carried the pale blue streak on one sleeve. In morning light it looked less like paint and more like the memory of paint.

I wore it anyway.

My body had nothing new to report. Shoulders normal. Right hand normal. Right leg normal. Residual limb comfortable. Left crutch tip ordinary. Both grips stayed where grips were supposed to stay.

No Hessa note.

No Vale runner.

No theatre emergency.

Only ordinary call.

That was enough.

Outside, Carrow was cold and bright enough to punish anyone who had believed yesterday's afternoon meant spring. The haircut remained excellent indoors and treacherous in wind.

I turned my collar higher around my neck and went west.

The theatre side door was open.

Nobody was waiting for me.

Good.

I went inside and nearly walked into a stack of benches.

Not because they were moving.

Because I was.

Jori had three of them turned sideways across the aisle while Davin checked the feet.

I stopped.

Davin looked up.

"Road."

"I am in a building."

"Still road."

I went around.

One bench had a chalk mark on the end.

Two short lines.

The other two did not.

I noticed.

I did not ask.

This felt unnatural.

Nessa came through the rear passage carrying two folded coats and a basket of cloth belts.

She saw me.

Then my coat.

Then my face.

"Pages?"

I took the Uncle pages out.

She shifted both coats to one arm and held out her hand.

I gave them to her.

That was it.

No ceremony.

No question about whether I had learned them.

No warning to forget them.

No statement that Pell owned them again.

Nessa folded the pages once along the existing crease and put them on the rear paper shelf beneath a stack of other scripts.

The Uncle disappeared into paper.

I still knew forty-three lines.

Both facts survived.

"Anything else?" I asked.

"Yes."

Of course.

She handed me the basket of belts.

"Costume rack."

I carried it to the costume rack.

Ordinary call remained honest.

When I came back, Rinna was at the front table with tickets arranged in four small stacks and one longer strip of paper beside them.

She looked up.

"Greg."

"Rinna."

"House."

I waited.

"Is that a role?"

"No."

"Good."

She pointed toward the front doors.

"First bill is selling heavier than yesterday. Kesh is late."

I had never met Kesh closely enough to know whether Kesh being late was a personality or an event.

"Who is Kesh?"

"The man who usually does what you're about to do."

Useful.

"What am I about to do?"

"Seat people."

I looked toward the house.

Benches.

Aisles.

Stage.

People not yet inside.

"That sounds easy."

Rinna looked at me.

I corrected myself.

"That sounds like something I have not done."

"Better."

She slid the long strip of paper toward me.

It was not a list of names.

Numbers.

Rows.

Small chalk marks beside some.

"Front two rows sold marked seats. Everything behind is open unless I tell you otherwise."

I looked at the benches.

"There are no numbers."

"Ends."

I went to the nearest bench.

A small chalk 3 sat low on the aisle end where I had never needed to look before.

"Ah."

"Tickets have row and place for the first two. After that, keep the center aisle clear and do not let six people turn one bench into eight."

"Strong civic standard."

"Also no standing in the side lane during performance."

"Why?"

"Actors use it."

I knew that side lane.

I had used it.

From backstage.

From the house it looked like empty wall.

Interesting.

Rinna continued.

"If someone asks for the end because they need the end, give them the end if you can."

I looked at her.

"Need how?"

"They'll tell you."

That was a better answer than the one I had been preparing.

"Tickets?"

"Bring them to me. You do not take money."

"Excellent."

"You also do not explain prices."

"Still excellent."

"You do not promise another show."

"Less excellent."

Rinna handed me three small chalk pieces.

"One for row marks if they rub. Two spare."

I stared at them.

"Authority."

"Chalk."

"Limited authority."

"Go check the house."

I went.

The first two rows had tiny place marks along the bench backs.

Not numbers exactly.

Short chalk divisions with small digits below them.

I had sat in this house dozens of times and never noticed because they had never mattered to me.

The third row had only the row number.

The fourth too.

The center aisle was wider than the side lanes.

I knew why from stage routes.

Now I knew why from audience movement.

Same floor.

Different job.

At the end of the second row, one chalk digit had blurred into something that might have been four or an accusation.

I rubbed it with my thumb.

Worse.

I looked at the chalk in my hand.

Then at Rinna.

She was already selling a ticket to a woman outside.

I considered improving the mark.

Dangerous word.

I waited until she finished.

"Second row, right side. Is that four?"

Rinna looked from the table.

"Five."

I looked again.

"That was not one of my options."

"Then your options were bad."

"Do I rewrite it?"

"Yes."

I wrote 5.

The number looked too large compared with the others.

I erased part with my thumb.

Now it looked wounded.

Davin passed behind me carrying a bench foot.

He looked.

"Art."

"Leave."

He did.

The doors opened before I felt ready.

This had also happened with acting.

Apparently buildings disliked confidence.

The first people came in slowly.

A couple with two tickets.

Row one.

Places two and three.

I checked the ticket.

Checked the bench.

Pointed.

"First row. Two and three."

The man looked at the stage.

"That close?"

I looked at the ticket.

"Yes."

"Can we sit back?"

I looked toward Rinna.

She was dealing with three people at the table.

The first two rows were marked because the seats had been sold specifically.

Everything behind was open.

Could they move back?

Probably.

Probably had become expensive in offices.

"Ask Rinna before I rearrange what you bought."

The woman laughed.

The man did not.

They went back to Rinna.

Excellent beginning.

The next group had no marked places.

Four people.

I sent them to the fourth row.

They chose the middle.

No problem.

Then a woman came in alone with a cane.

Not one of mine.

A real cane.

Dark wood, brass-colored cap, rubberless end.

Her ticket was open seating.

She stopped at the third row.

"End."

I looked at the empty bench.

Both ends open.

"Either."

"Right."

She took the right end.

That was easy enough.

Then she said, "Not that right."

I looked.

She pointed to the other side.

"Door side."

"Ah."

She crossed in front of me and sat on the left end, closer to the exit.

I had assumed right meant my right.

She had meant useful right.

Or maybe changed her mind.

The system survived.

A man behind her said, "Can I get past?"

She moved both knees aside.

He went.

I kept the center aisle clear.

For almost three minutes.

Then Marek entered carrying a wooden crate and stopped directly in it.

I stared at him.

He stared at me.

"What?"

"Center aisle."

"Backstage is blocked."

"House is opening."

"I am crossing."

"You are standing."

He looked down at the crate.

As though the crate might defend him.

Nessa shouted from the wing, "Marek."

He moved.

I had authority again.

Very specific authority.

Dangerous.

The first real seating mistake came from a family of five.

Mother.

Father.

Three children.

Open seating.

There was a bench in row five with enough visible room.

I pointed them there.

They went.

The youngest child climbed over the bench instead of around it.

The father caught him.

The mother sat.

Then an older man already at the far end said, "Those are taken."

I looked at the bench.

Nothing marked.

The mother looked at me.

I looked at the older man.

"Open seating."

He pointed at two folded scarves lying on the bench.

"Taken."

Scarves.

I had seen them.

I had interpreted them as cloth.

Technically correct.

Functionally wrong.

The mother looked at the remaining space.

Five people could fit if everyone became narrow and stopped owning elbows.

Not comfortably.

I said, "One row back."

She gathered the children.

No argument.

The father picked up one child.

They moved.

The older man said, "Sorry."

The mother said, "It's fine."

Nobody asked me to decide whether scarves could hold seats under theatre law.

Good.

I walked back to the door.

Rinna looked over.

"Problem?"

"Scarves have acquired property rights."

She glanced at row five.

"Ah. Those two are with the woman buying now."

"Open seating."

"Open when not held."

"That is a rule you omitted."

"I thought you knew cloth."

Hostile workplace.

The woman buying tickets came in.

She thanked the older man.

The scarves became people.

Evidence resolved.

The house filled unevenly.

Front rows first because people with marked tickets had already chosen certainty.

Then back rows because people without marked tickets often wanted distance.

Middle rows became complicated last.

I learned that couples preferred pretending one empty place beside them did not exist.

I learned that three children occupied either two seats or seven depending on emotional state.

I learned that people asking "Is this good?" did not want the same answer.

One man meant good view.

One woman meant good for leaving early.

A boy meant close enough to see Marek fall over if Marek fell over.

I could answer only the first by looking.

The second I moved toward an aisle.

The third I told the boy, "Statistically promising."

His father laughed.

Rinna heard.

"Do not promise falls."

"I promised probability."

"Greg."

"Fine."

Kesh arrived after the house was more than half full.

I knew him once I saw him.

I had seen him dozens of times.

That was embarrassing.

He was the broad man who sometimes stood near the front doors before performances and disappeared when the curtain opened.

Apparently I had categorized him as architecture.

He wore a dark vest and had a narrow strip of red cloth tied around one wrist.

He looked at the house.

Then at me.

"You're in my place."

"Late."

"Yes."

No defense.

He took the chalk from my hand.

Checked row two.

Stopped at my large wounded 5.

Looked at me.

"Five?"

"Eventually."

He rubbed it out and wrote a smaller 5 in one motion.

I disliked professionals.

Rinna called from the table, "Keep Greg through start."

Kesh nodded.

I had not been released.

He walked the front aisle once.

Moved one folded scarf.

Not the claimed scarves.

A different one that had fallen partly into the lane.

He checked the side lane.

Then opened the second front door wider.

I followed because apparently this was training now.

Not formal training.

Walking behind a man who knew where people were supposed to go.

"Center stays clear," he said.

"I know."

"Side stays clear after bell."

"I know."

He looked at my crutches.

Then at the side lane.

"You're fine there now. Not after."

"I know why."

"Good."

Everyone had that word.

A woman approached holding two tickets.

"Together?"

Kesh looked.

Different row marks.

"No."

She frowned.

"We bought together."

He looked at Rinna.

Rinna looked at the ticket backs.

Then at her stack.

"One copied wrong."

She crossed out the second row mark and rewrote it.

Kesh handed the tickets back.

"Now together."

No crisis.

Paper wrong.

Paper corrected by the person allowed to correct it.

I knew this religion.

The bell rang.

Not curtain.

First warning.

People in the doorway became urgent.

Kesh stopped letting them choose slowly.

"Open seats from row six back."

A group of three asked where.

He pointed once.

They went.

A man tried to stand at the rear wall.

Kesh said, "Bench."

"I prefer standing."

"Not there."

"Why?"

"Door."

The man sat.

No explanation of fire, flow, stage access, emergency, dignity, or civilization.

Door.

Useful language.

The second bell rang.

Rinna closed the ticket stack.

I thought I was done.

Kesh pointed to the left wall.

"Stay until opening."

"Why?"

"Late entry."

Of course.

The theatre had people who arrived after the theatre began.

I had known this from backstage only as door noise.

Now I became the door noise.

The lights lowered.

Not truly dark.

Dim enough.

The audience changed.

That was the strange part.

Five minutes earlier they had been coats, complaints, children, scarves, choices, knees, ticket marks, and people asking whether row four was good.

Then the stage lit.

They became a single quiet thing facing forward.

Not actually single.

Someone still coughed.

A child whispered.

A man in row seven adjusted his coat three times.

But the room had direction now.

I stood against the left wall with both crutches planted close and watched the stage from an angle I almost never used.

The first piece was The Doctor's Supper.

I knew it.

Hara as the physician.

Marek as the brother.

Pell was not in it today.

Another actor had the patient.

No announcement.

No emotional event.

The piece existed with the cast on the board.

Hara entered.

The audience settled another degree.

From backstage, laughter had always sounded like weather arriving through wood.

From here I could see where it started.

Not intellectually.

Physically.

Two people in the front row first.

Then three behind them.

Then the rest.

Marek said something stupid about medicine.

A woman near the center laughed before her husband did.

The laugh spread.

I caught myself watching the audience instead of the stage.

Kesh, beside the door, whispered, "Door."

I looked.

Two late people stood outside.

Right.

Employment.

I opened enough to let them in without throwing full street light into the house.

The man entered first.

The woman behind him stopped.

"Where?"

I looked at the dark benches.

This was harder now.

Empty places had become gaps between silhouettes.

Kesh pointed toward row eight.

Two seats at the end.

I led them only far enough to see.

The woman whispered, "Closer?"

I looked at Kesh.

He shook his head.

I whispered, "Not without crossing the front."

She accepted row eight.

The man did too.

They sat.

The door closed.

Onstage, Hara had reached the hunger line.

I knew because the audience broke.

I had missed the setup.

The laugh still worked without me.

Good.

For the rest of the first piece, late arrivals came in three small groups.

One wanted the front.

No.

One had a child already asleep against a shoulder.

Kesh found them an end.

One man insisted his brother was saving a place in row four.

He was.

A raised hand appeared from darkness.

We let him cross between scenes, not during.

That was apparently the rule.

I learned it by being stopped once when I started to wave him through.

Kesh touched my arm.

"Wait."

I waited.

Curtain.

Applause.

Then movement.

The brother crossed.

No speech.

The second piece required a fast reset.

This meant the audience became a problem again.

People stood.

Stretching.

Whispering.

One tried to leave by the side lane.

I caught him.

"Front."

He looked at the nearer side opening.

"That door."

"Stage route."

He looked through it.

Venn was already behind the flat holding a chair.

The man understood without needing philosophy.

"Front."

He went.

The little boy who had asked about Marek falling found me near the door.

"He didn't fall."

"First piece."

His father said, "Do not encourage him."

"I have been instructed similarly."

The boy looked hopeful about the second piece.

I refused to promise.

The reset finished.

Second bell.

People sat.

I stayed at the door.

The second piece was one I had never seen.

Good.

I watched more of it.

Not all.

Door.

Late people.

One woman leaving because she felt ill.

Not dramatic ill.

She said the room was too warm.

Kesh opened the outer door and let her stand in the entry until she felt better.

Her friend stayed with her.

I stood farther aside.

No medicine.

No Hessa.

No crisis.

Warm room.

When she returned, Kesh put her on the rear end instead of making her go back to the middle of row six.

No one objected.

I understood the rule after it happened.

Need the end if you need the end.

Rinna had said that.

The second piece was funnier than I expected.

Not because I understood the plot.

Because Hara entered late as somebody's aunt and changed the entire audience before she spoke.

I could see it now.

People recognized her.

Not celebrity.

Not fame.

Familiarity inside this room.

Bodies shifted.

Faces lifted.

A few people smiled before the line.

Then she gave them nothing for three beats.

The laughter came from anticipation collapsing under itself.

I had watched Hara do silence from backstage.

From the door, I watched the audience do the other half.

That was not my lesson.

It was simply visible from here.

I liked the door.

This concerned me.

After the second piece, Kesh said, "Done."

I looked at him.

"Already?"

"House work."

"You stay?"

"Evening."

"Now?"

"Reset later."

He handed me the chalk.

I looked at it.

"Why?"

"Rinna's."

Of course.

I returned the chalk to Rinna.

She was counting the unsold tickets.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Scarves can reserve seats."

"Yes."

"Kesh has handwriting that humiliates me."

"Yes."

"The left side door is not an audience door."

"Correct."

"Late people become invisible in the dark."

"Generally."

"People ask if seats are good without defining good."

Rinna looked up.

"Did the house fit?"

"Yes."

"Anyone left standing?"

"No."

"Anyone block the side?"

"Not after bell."

"Then fine."

That was the review.

No certificate.

No title.

No declaration that I had become front-of-house staff.

She handed me a folded stack of handbills.

"Rear table."

I carried them to the rear table.

Theatre preserved itself through insult.

The afternoon returned me to ordinary backstage work.

I moved two light props.

Held a flat while Jori changed a tie.

Took a costume bag upstairs one item at a time because the bag itself was too awkward with crutches.

Davin asked whether I had enjoyed being important.

"I controlled scarves."

"Power corrupts."

"You painted both sides of a sign the same."

"History."

Marek appeared wearing a hat.

Nessa called his name.

He took it off.

The world continued.

Pell did not appear.

Nobody discussed him.

That was probably the best available recovery.

Near late afternoon, Teren ran a short scene and asked me to stand in for one missing reader for six lines.

I read.

Not memorized.

No costume.

No role claim.

One line had a joke I could feel coming.

I almost leaned into it.

Then did not.

Teren stopped after the scene.

"Again tomorrow if I need it."

"Which part?"

"Reading."

"Strong career."

He was already walking away.

I smiled.

At the front, Kesh had gone.

The house was empty again.

I stood in the center aisle.

The benches looked ordinary.

Row five still held one forgotten scarf.

Not one of the reserved ones from morning.

Different scarf.

I picked it up.

Rinna had a small basket near the front table labeled FOUND.

Not with words.

With objects.

One glove.

A child's wooden ring.

A comb.

Two folded handkerchiefs.

I put the scarf in.

Then stopped.

"Does this reserve the basket?"

Rinna looked at me.

"No."

"Good."

She went back to writing.

I left before the scarf acquired a claimant.

Rinna paid me normal theatre money.

One ordinary day.

No emergency share.

No role share beyond the work she counted.

I did not ask whether house work paid differently because apparently it had not.

Money entered my purse.

The purse remained a purse.

Outside, Carrow was already cooling.

I bought bread.

Not because Lyssa had written a list.

Because we needed bread and I had eaten enough of the existing loaf to understand the future.

I bought onions too.

Two.

Then, thinking better of it, three.

The woman at the stall looked at me.

"Three."

"Yes."

"No paper today?"

"No."

"Growth."

Everyone in the city had learned the word.

I paid.

At home, Lyssa was not there yet.

The room looked ordinary.

Brown cloth.

Three pins.

Damaged shirt.

Three threads.

No Uncle pages in my coat.

That absence felt correct.

I put the bread on the table.

Onions beside it.

Then moved one onion because three in a row looked like I was conducting an experiment.

Lyssa came home after dark carrying a narrow wrapped bundle and a small paper twist.

She looked at the table.

"Three."

"Apparently tradition."

She put her things down.

"Work?"

"Theatre."

"Role?"

"No."

I considered the six read lines.

"Not really."

She waited.

I said, "I seated people."

That got more attention than I expected.

"You?"

"Hostile phrasing."

"Did they sit?"

"Mostly after instruction."

She smiled.

I told her about the scarves.

Only the scarves.

Not the entire architecture of late entry, side lanes, marked rows, open benches, audience movement, Hara's silence, or Kesh's superior 5.

Lyssa unwrapped the paper twist.

Dried herbs.

No connection to anything I understood.

"Reserved seats?" she asked.

"Apparently cloth can become law if everybody agrees."

She looked at the long brown cloth near the wall.

I followed her eyes.

Three pins.

Same fold.

I pointed.

"No."

She laughed.

Good.

We ate bread and onions with something she had brought in the wrapped bundle that turned out to be food.

This was an excellent development.

After supper, I hung my coat by the door.

Lighter without the Uncle pages.

Not physically enough to matter.

Still.

Tomorrow had no ordinary call written anywhere I owned.

Rinna had not told me to come.

Teren had said maybe reading if he needed it, which was not a call.

Kesh had not hired me into a new profession.

The house would open again whether I stood at the door or not.

I knew where row five was now.

I knew why the side lane stayed empty.

I knew a scarf could mean taken.

I knew late people entered through less light.

I knew none of this yesterday.

That was enough.

Theatre had not given me a role.

It had given me a door and then taken it back.

For one day, that was work.
