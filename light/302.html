TEXT READER · CHAPTER 302

THE LOW LAMPS

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lyssa asked me to go for a walk after I had already decided not to.

This was unfair.

I had come home from the theatre, reported the useful parts, failed to become the center of Tavi's horse story, eaten, and reached the portion of the evening where a chair begins making persuasive arguments.

Lyssa finished a line of stitching at the far end of the table.

The brown cuff remained beside her.

I did not ask what stage of life it had reached.

Progress sometimes looked like silence.

She bit the thread, set the garment down, and flexed both hands.

"Walk?"

I looked toward the door.

Then at my crutches.

Then at her.

"I did that today."

"You always do that."

"Not voluntarily."

She stood.

That ended negotiations.

Cala was washing a bowl near the basin. Tavi had fallen asleep on a folded blanket with one wooden spoon still in his hand, which meant civilization had won by attrition.

Cala looked at Lyssa.

"Low lamps."

"I know."

That meant something to both of them.

It meant something to me too, once I noticed the windows across the court.

The blue-white charm lamp in the upper room opposite had been covered with a brown cloth. Two doors down, someone was lowering the glow on a wall bead until it barely lit the threshold. An ordinary oil lamp burned under a gallery without anyone caring.

I looked toward the open strip of evening sky.

A pale thing crossed it.

Then three more.

Lampwings.

Not surprising.

I had seen similar flights in other cities, usually after warm dry days when the evening air stayed soft. Different places called them different names and swore their local variety behaved differently from every other one in creation.

The insects themselves were small, maybe the length of my thumb joint, with broad pale wings that caught magical light better than flame. When enough of them passed through a bright street, the dust along their wings held a faint sheen for a while.

One alone looked like an insect.

A few hundred looked like weather.

Carrow apparently expected a few hundred.

Lyssa went upstairs for a light wrap.

I stayed seated.

This was not laziness.

It was tactical conservation of standing.

Cala glanced at me.

"You going?"

"Apparently."

"West lanes quieter."

Lyssa came back down.

She had changed nothing except the wrap over one shoulder.

"Not market," she said.

"I was not planning market."

"Good."

"I can know things before being corrected."

Cala made a sound that was not agreement.

We left.

The front door opened cleanly.

Outside, the first lampwing hit the wall beside my head.

Not hard.

It touched plaster, fluttered twice, and drifted upward again as if embarrassed.

Lyssa laughed.

"Hostile city."

"You walked into its lane."

"This is my lane."

The lampwing declined jurisdiction.

The first two streets were still bright enough to walk comfortably. Most shops had not closed, but the ones using cool charm light had covered or hooded it. A cobbler had hung a square of red cloth over his sign bead. The sign still showed through, dim and pink.

Three lampwings circled it anyway.

He waved a broom at them without leaving his stool.

"Working?" I asked.

"No."

He kept waving.

A fourth joined the other three.

"Strong evidence."

He told me to keep walking.

Lyssa did.

I followed.

The evening changed as we moved west.

Not dark.

Lower.

Warm oil lamps stayed open. Window candles remained. The sharper magical whites disappeared behind cloth, shutters, cups, bits of painted tin, whatever people owned that could turn a bright point into a dull one.

The lampwings became easier to see because the city had stopped competing with them.

They floated through the lanes in loose waves.

Pale gray when they crossed shadow.

Pearl when they caught lamplight.

Every so often one held a faint green-white trace along the wing edge for a few seconds after passing a charm.

Children chased them.

Adults told the children not to.

The children treated this as atmospheric information.

At the first corner, two girls held an empty basket between them and tried to convince the wings to enter voluntarily.

Their mother stood in a doorway behind them.

"Not in the house."

"We know."

"You say that with a basket."

One girl lowered the basket.

The other did not.

Lyssa smiled.

"Did you do that?"

I asked.

"No."

"Too dignified?"

"No basket."

That was a better answer.

The lane narrowed ahead.

A delivery cart had stopped outside a spice shop, leaving just enough room for one person at a time between the wheel and the opposite wall.

Lyssa looked at it.

Then at me.

"Other way."

I nodded.

No discussion.

The crutches made narrow passing possible if everyone cooperated, but the lampwing traffic had collected three children and a woman with a bundle near the cart. There was no reason to turn an evening walk into geometry I could avoid.

We took the side lane.

Lyssa did not slow to accommodate me.

She slowed because the lane was rougher.

There was a difference.

I knew it because I had spent enough time walking beside her to know when she was matching me and when she was simply choosing her own pace.

Tonight we were both slow.

That felt good.

A lampwing settled on the wall at shoulder height.

Lyssa stopped.

I stopped because she did.

Its wings were not actually white. Up close they were mottled cream and gray, with darker veins and a powdery line along the outer edge.

No visible glow now.

The insect folded its wings.

Lyssa leaned closer.

"Fat one."

"Professional assessment?"

"Yes."

"Guild certified?"

She ignored me.

The lampwing opened again and drifted away.

We kept walking.

I had no urge to catch it.

This also felt suspiciously competent.

At the end of the side lane a vendor had set up a shallow iron pan over a brick stove. The oil inside smelled like onions, hot dough, and the sort of decision people regretted only after eating enough to make regret difficult.

The vendor used one shielded amber lamp.

No wings bothered it.

Lyssa looked at the pan.

I looked at Lyssa.

She kept walking.

I said, "Coward."

She stopped.

"What?"

"You looked."

"So?"

"Then walked."

"I can look."

"Not convincingly."

She turned back toward the stall.

I had won something.

I was not yet sure what.

The vendor lifted a twist of fried dough from the oil with a wire scoop. Small pieces, rough and brown, dusted with salt and something red.

"One bit," he said before I asked.

Lyssa looked at me.

I had two bits upstairs.

No.

I corrected myself.

I had two bits in my pocket because I had put them there when we left.

That had been a choice I had not examined too closely.

The three copper stayed home.

Two bits came with me.

I could pretend this was caution.

Maybe the door would charge admission.

Maybe the lampwings had organized.

The truth was simpler.

I had earned small money yesterday and wanted the option to spend small money today.

This was dangerous thinking.

"One," I said.

The vendor held out his hand.

I gave him a bit.

No conversion.

No copper broken.

No household money.

One bit for a paper twist of hot fried dough.

The transaction took four seconds and did not become a business plan.

I was proud of everyone involved.

The vendor shook more red powder over the top.

Lyssa said, "Too much."

He stopped.

"Good?"

She considered the twist.

"Now."

He handed it to me.

I looked at her.

"You negotiated my seasoning."

"You would complain."

"I was prepared to suffer quietly."

"No."

The first piece was too hot.

I bit it anyway.

Pain arrived immediately and correctly.

Lyssa watched my face.

"Hot."

"I know."

"You bit it."

"I know."

She took one from the top and held it for three breaths before eating.

This was infuriating.

We moved away from the stall before becoming part of its furniture.

The west lane opened into a small square with a dry fountain in the middle.

Not broken.

Seasonal, as far as I knew.

Nobody had gathered to explain it to me.

Benches ringed two sides. A broad tree leaned over the third.

The square was darker than the market roads because almost every shopfront had lowered its charm light.

Lampwings filled the space above the fountain.

Not a solid cloud.

Dozens.

Maybe more.

Enough that their pale wings kept appearing and disappearing against the dark upper walls.

A boy sat on the fountain rim with a tiny glass cup over one hand.

His father, or an adult with the exhausted authority of one, said, "Open it."

"But it glows."

"It glows outside too."

The boy opened the cup.

A lampwing escaped.

He watched it rise.

"I was keeping it safe."

"It was not asking."

Lyssa sat on the fountain rim two body lengths away from them.

I chose the bench.

She looked over.

"Old."

"Efficient."

The bench was lower than ideal but had arms.

I backed to it, set the crutches where they would not project into the walking path, put my right foot under me, and sat with the left knee clear.

No difficulty.

Lyssa moved from the fountain to the bench beside me after perhaps ten seconds.

"Better?"

"For you?"

She took another piece of fried dough from the twist.

"Yes."

"Then yes."

We ate.

For a while that was all.

The square did not require interpretation.

A woman closed shutters on a blue-lit apothecary sign.

Someone upstairs played three notes badly on a reed pipe and then repeated them as if repetition could become improvement through force.

The lampwings crossed above us.

A few caught light from hidden signs and carried it away in faint lines along their wings.

One landed on the back of the bench.

Lyssa ate another piece.

I said, "You wanted to see them."

She nodded.

"Why?"

She looked at me.

"Because I wanted to."

That was the complete answer.

I smiled.

She narrowed her eyes.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Greg."

"I was about to ask for a system."

"No."

"I stopped."

"Good."

She ate the last large piece before I could reach it.

Punishment was swift.

I took one of the smaller ones.

"How long?"

"Tonight. Maybe tomorrow."

"The flight?"

"Mm."

"Every year?"

She shrugged.

"Most."

Good.

Enough.

I did not ask where they came from.

I did not ask whether the held sheen was mana storage, dust response, wing structure, or something else.

I had known species in other places that carried light for mating.

Others used it to confuse night birds.

One desert variety absorbed enough spell glow near caravan roads that wealthy travelers paid children to sweep them off ward lanterns.

Those facts told me nothing certain about Carrow's lampwings.

So I left them where they belonged.

Flying.

Lyssa nudged my shoulder.

"Theatre?"

I looked at her.

"You did not ask earlier."

"Tavi was talking."

"His version was false."

"His version had horse."

"A powerful evidentiary standard."

"What happened?"

I considered giving her the long version.

The note.

The depth cloth.

The lamps.

The false corridor bending around my left crutch.

The cane test.

Jori's lamp adjustment.

Nessa's dead edge.

Teren telling me I had improved Greg instead of the door.

Pavin from row five.

The final scene.

There was a version of the story that could occupy the rest of the square.

Instead I said, "They made a hallway look longer than it was. My crutch broke the illusion. We tested until they fixed the stage instead of making me cross differently."

Lyssa nodded.

"Good."

"That is all?"

"Did it work?"

"Yes."

"You got paid?"

"Two bits."

"Then good."

I held up the paper twist.

"One bit now."

She looked at the remaining fried crumbs.

"That was fast."

"Capital moved."

"Into your mouth."

"Half into yours."

"Less."

I stared at her.

"You ate the largest piece."

"One."

"The largest."

She did not care.

I leaned back.

A lampwing crossed close enough to my face that I felt the air from its wings.

Then another landed in Lyssa's hair.

Not deep.

At the outer edge of the wrap near her temple.

I reached automatically.

She lifted one finger.

"Wait."

I stopped.

The lampwing crawled from a fold of dark hair onto the edge of her wrap.

For a second it sat there, pale against the cloth.

A faint line along one wing held the last memory of a charm lamp.

Green-white.

Very dim.

Lyssa turned her head carefully to look at it.

"You have one."

"I know."

"You want it removed?"

"No."

So we sat with it.

The light faded first.

Then the wing color returned to cream and gray.

A few seconds later the insect opened both wings and lifted away.

Lyssa watched it go.

I watched her.

She caught me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Again."

"I am improving."

"Slow."

Fair.

The boy with the glass cup had moved on to trying to catch the shadow of a lampwing with his foot.

This worked better for everyone.

We finished the fried dough.

The empty paper twist became my responsibility because I had paid for it.

There was a refuse crock beside the fountain.

I stood.

Right foot under.

Bench arm.

Crutches.

Easy.

I carried the paper to the crock and came back.

Lyssa was already standing.

"You done?"

"Yes."

We took a different route home.

Not because the first was blocked.

Because Lyssa wanted to.

That reason was becoming easier to accept without interrogation.

The lanes were darker now.

People had lowered more lamps as the flight thickened.

The dimness changed my walking.

Crutch tips mattered more when the stone edge disappeared into shadow.

I shortened my pace slightly on uneven stretches and let the right foot confirm the surface before committing full weight.

Nothing dramatic.

Just nighttime.

Lyssa noticed once when I chose the smoother center strip instead of the wall edge.

She moved to my left without comment so oncoming people had the wider side.

I noticed.

I did not thank her.

Not because I was ungrateful.

Because after enough time together, thanking someone for every ordinary adjustment starts making the ordinary adjustment less ordinary.

A man coming the other way carried a tray of six blue-white glow beads under a dark cloth.

One escaped at the edge.

Lampwings found it immediately.

Three struck the cloth.

One landed on his shoulder.

He swore and pulled the cover tighter.

Lyssa said, "Should use box."

He said, "Should mind yours."

She kept walking.

I waited until we were past.

"Helpful."

"He knows."

"Then why say it?"

"He was rude last week."

There it was.

A whole relationship I had not been present for.

"Who is he?"

"Doesn't matter."

I accepted that with difficulty.

The city continued without my documentation.

At the next corner, an older couple had placed two stools outside their doorway. They sat facing the lane with no food, no work, no obvious reason except the lampwings.

The woman lifted a hand to Lyssa.

Lyssa lifted hers back.

Neither stopped.

I did not ask who they were.

This was becoming exhausting.

A little farther on, the flight thinned.

The household court had fewer bright lamps than the west square even on ordinary nights, so less collected there.

Cala had left one oil lamp burning near the basin.

No charm light.

The window opposite remained covered.

Tavi was still asleep.

The spoon had been removed from his hand.

Probably for public safety.

Lyssa took off her wrap.

A little pale dust marked one edge where the lampwing had sat.

She rubbed it between finger and thumb.

Nothing glowed.

Then she shook the cloth once and hung it over the chair.

I took the remaining bit from my pocket.

Cala looked at it.

"One?"

"One spent."

"Food?"

"Fried dough."

She nodded.

No trial.

No demand for accounting.

I went upstairs and put the bit beside the three copper.

Three copper.

One bit.

The number had changed.

Nothing had broken.

That felt stranger than it should have.

For a long time, money had existed in categories.

Rent.

Debt.

Food.

Tool.

Emergency.

Work.

A coin needed a reason strong enough to survive losing it.

Tonight I had spent one bit because Lyssa looked at fried dough and kept walking.

I almost wrote that sentence down.

Then I imagined finding it later.

I left the charcoal wrapped.

Downstairs, Lyssa said something to Cala.

Cala laughed.

I could not hear the words.

I did not go down to ask.

A lampwing crossed the window outside my room, pale for half a second in the covered light from across the court.

Then it disappeared into the dark.

The remaining bit stayed beside the copper.

The empty space where the other one had been stayed empty.

That was all.
