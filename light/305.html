LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 305

THE CLEAR LANE

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The first mirrorhorn I ever saw nearly got me killed because I drew a sword.

The first mirrorhorn I saw in Carrow nearly got somebody else killed because he did the same thing.

Experience was efficient when it arrived forty years early.

I heard the shouting before I reached the lane.

Not panic yet.

The sharper thing that came just before panic, when several people had noticed a problem and none of them had agreed what kind.

A cart bell rang twice.

Someone yelled, "Back."

Someone else yelled, "Don't back."

A dog began barking with the full confidence of an animal contributing nothing.

I came around the corner and stopped.

The lane ahead had filled with animals that were not there.

Six mirrorhorns stood between the dye row and a broad stone warehouse.

I counted nine.

Then eleven.

Then six again.

The herd itself was easy enough to see if I watched the feet.

Four adults.

One younger animal nearly adult size.

One calf tucked too close to the largest female.

Everything above their knees was less reliable.

Mirrorhorns were built like lean mountain deer scaled up until carts became a relevant comparison. Long bodies. High shoulders. Narrow gray heads. Two backward-sweeping horns on the adults, dark at the base and almost clear at the tips.

The magic lived around those horns.

Light bent badly there.

Each animal carried two or three pale copies offset around its real body, transparent enough to see through and solid enough to fool the first glance. When the herd shifted, the copies lagged half a heartbeat and caught up.

Beautiful.

Terrible for traffic.

A fruit seller had abandoned half her stall.

Two carts sat nose to nose because neither driver could decide whether forward or backward was worse.

People had pressed into doorways and against walls.

Good.

Mostly.

A young man in a brown lane coat stood near the front cart with a short sword already out.

Bad.

Very bad.

The mirrorhorn female nearest him raised her head.

Her copies widened.

Three gray heads became five.

The young man stepped back.

Sword up.

I said, "Put that away."

He did not hear me.

Or chose not to.

The largest male turned slightly.

His horn tips caught sunlight.

For one instant the whole lane flashed white.

Several people shouted.

The dog barked harder.

The young man lifted the sword.

"Put it away," I said again, louder.

He looked at me this time.

Then at my crutches.

Then back at the herd.

Excellent.

Nothing improved a dangerous situation like deciding whether the cripple had earned the right to speak.

An older woman beside the first cart snapped, "Do it, Renn."

That helped.

Renn lowered the sword a little.

"These are in the city."

"Yes."

"They'll charge."

"Maybe, if you keep making the lane smaller."

That got his attention.

The woman beside the cart looked at me.

Gray braid.

Green work coat.

One hand still on the reins of a broad black mule that had decided complete stillness was a religious duty.

"Mirrorhorns?" she asked.

"Yes."

She looked at the herd again.

"Thought so."

Good.

Someone else knew the category.

I preferred worlds where I was not the only adult.

The woman said, "Never seen them this far in."

"Neither have I."

True enough.

In Carrow.

Renn pointed the sword toward the animals without extending his arm.

"What do we do?"

"First, put that away."

He looked at the older woman.

She said, "Renn."

He sheathed it.

The largest female lowered her head by an inch.

Not relaxed.

Less interested.

Better.

"Now kill the bright lamps," I said.

A shopkeeper behind me said, "What lamps?"

I pointed with my chin.

Three charm signs were still lit under the awnings despite the morning sun.

Blue-white.

Sharp.

One over a cloth shop.

One over a spice seller.

One little rotating bead above a moneychanger's shutter.

The mirrorhorn copies sharpened every time one of the animals turned toward them.

Not because mirrorhorns hated light.

Because bright fixed points gave the field more edges to throw around.

The herd saw its own false bodies too.

Too much reflected motion made nervous animals more nervous.

The old caravan woman who taught me that had used fewer words.

"Dark the damn lamps, boy."

I had been twenty-three the first time.

Older than this body.

Much younger than the memory inside it.

The cloth seller reached up and covered her charm with a wooden cup.

The spice seller twisted his sign bead dark.

The moneychanger was not present.

Renn crossed to the shutter.

"Can I touch it?"

"Turn the hood," somebody said.

Not me.

Local knowledge.

Renn found a black metal hood above the rotating bead and pulled it down.

The lane changed.

Not dramatically.

The mirrorhorns remained six animals surrounded by bad arithmetic.

But the copies softened.

The male's extra shoulders blurred.

One of the females stopped stepping sideways.

"Good," I said.

Renn looked at me.

"What now?"

"Open the middle."

He stared at the lane.

"It is open."

"No. It has two carts in it."

The two cart drivers looked offended.

I pointed.

"That one back half a length. Other one take the left mouth if you can do it without bells."

The second driver said, "Can't turn with them there."

"Then don't."

He looked ready to argue.

The gray-braided woman said, "He said don't."

I liked her.

The first cart was hers.

She backed the mule with two quiet clicks of her tongue.

No bell.

No shouting.

The mule had apparently been waiting for a plan and approved of this one.

The cart rolled backward.

Half a length.

Then another foot.

The clear strip between the herd and the lane mouth widened.

One mirrorhorn copy slid through the cart.

The real animal did not.

Useful distinction.

The dog continued barking.

I looked for it.

Small yellow thing tied outside a butcher.

Naturally.

The butcher had both hands on the leash and was trying to pull the dog farther inside.

The dog considered this cowardice.

"Take it through the back if you have one," I said.

The butcher said, "Door's blocked."

"Then cover its head."

He looked at me.

"With what?"

"You sell meat. Surprise me."

The gray-braided woman laughed once.

The butcher grabbed an apron from a peg and dropped it loosely over the dog's head.

The barking stopped.

Not because the dog became wise.

Because it became busy trying to understand cloth.

The nearest mirrorhorn's ears moved forward.

Then sideways.

The copies narrowed.

Renn saw that.

"So they're scared?"

"Uncertain."

"Difference?"

"Scared things run. Uncertain things decide."

"And if they decide wrong?"

"Then we have a worse morning."

He did not like that.

Neither did I.

Mirrorhorns were not harmless.

The adults weighed enough to break ribs without magic.

The horns were real under the light tricks.

A herd pressed in a narrow road could go from still to catastrophic before a person finished explaining that the animals were usually shy.

Neutral was not safe.

That lesson had cost people blood in my first life.

Sometimes mine.

I shifted farther into the stone doorway behind me.

Not retreating.

Positioning.

My right foot had a flat patch of stone.

Both crutch tips sat inside the threshold where they would not skid if I had to move fast.

Fast remained relative.

That mattered.

In my first life, if the herd broke, I could have cleared a shop awning before the first animal crossed the lane.

Now I needed the doorway to do some of that work for me.

Old experience.

Current geometry.

Same survival problem.

The calf moved.

It stepped from behind the large female and toward the abandoned fruit stall.

The copies followed badly.

One calf became three.

The fruit seller made a sound through her teeth.

"Leave it," I said.

She froze.

The calf sniffed a fallen yellow pear.

One of its false heads passed through the stall leg.

The real head lowered.

Renn said, "It's eating."

"Maybe."

The calf touched the pear with its nose.

Then jumped backward as if the pear had insulted its ancestors.

The crowd laughed.

Small.

Nervous.

Too loud.

The largest female snapped her head toward us.

Her copies vanished.

Every one of them.

My stomach dropped.

"There," I said.

Not loudly.

The gray-braided woman heard the change in my voice.

"What?"

"Nobody move toward her."

Renn said, "Copies are gone."

"Yes."

"Isn't that good?"

"No."

The female stood completely real.

Gray hide.

Dark legs.

Clear-tipped horns.

No doubled neck.

No false shoulders.

Nothing.

Mirrorhorns mirrored when they wanted the world to believe there were more of them.

They stopped when they had chosen to use the body they actually possessed.

The first time I had seen that happen, I had thought the magic was ending.

I had drawn my sword.

The caravan woman had hit my wrist with a stick.

Hard.

Then a male mirrorhorn had crossed thirty feet fast enough to bury a horn in the wagon where my chest had been.

I remembered the sound better than the pain.

Wood opening.

Canvas tearing.

My own breath after.

The woman saying, "Copies mean thinking. One means done thinking."

Forty years later, I finally appreciated the teaching method.

"Eyes down," I said.

Renn looked at me.

"Don't stare at her face. Give her the road."

The gray-braided woman lowered her gaze first.

Not submissive.

Practical.

Others followed because somebody had to be first.

I did too.

I watched the female's front feet instead.

Real hooves.

Real shadow.

Her right forehoof shifted.

Forward.

Stopped.

The calf moved back behind her.

The female's copies returned.

One.

Then two faint shoulders beside the real one.

I breathed again.

Renn whispered, "That was charge?"

"That was considering it."

"How do you know?"

"Because we're still talking."

He looked like he wanted a better answer.

He was not getting one while six mirrorhorns occupied the lane.

The male turned toward the open strip the carts had created.

Good.

The herd wanted somewhere to go.

That was the whole problem.

People saw dangerous animals in a city lane and thought the animals had chosen the lane as a destination.

Usually an animal wanted through.

Food.

Water.

Cover.

Herd.

Nest.

Mate.

Escape.

The road was often just in the way.

The male took one step.

Then another.

His copies flowed around him.

The first female followed.

The younger animal shifted.

The calf stayed tight to the dangerous female.

Renn moved his hand toward the cart as if to push it farther back.

"Don't," I said.

He stopped.

"Why?"

"Because they started."

That was enough.

Once a mirrorhorn herd accepted a corridor, changing the corridor was a fine way to make them reconsider.

Reconsideration was what we had spent the last several minutes avoiding.

The male walked.

Not ran.

Walked.

Through the middle of Carrow.

His shoulder came level with the first cart.

The mule closed its eyes.

I respected that strategy.

The male's nearest false body passed through the mule's nose.

The mule twitched but did not bolt.

The real mirrorhorn passed three feet away.

Its hide was not smooth.

Short gray hair lay over darker scales along the shoulder line.

I had forgotten the scales.

Or maybe this regional kind had more.

Not important now.

The horn tips held a thin inner glow, pale as morning moonlight.

No one spoke.

The second animal followed.

Then the younger one.

A child started crying somewhere behind a doorway.

Quietly.

Good child.

The fruit seller stayed absolutely still while the calf approached the fallen pear again.

This time it bit.

The pear vanished in two bites.

The seller watched her inventory leave the market.

I whispered, "Expense of doing business."

She made a strangled sound that might have been laughter.

The large female came after the calf.

Her copies brushed both sides of the lane.

The real animal stayed in the middle.

Exactly where we had left space.

She passed the fruit stall.

Passed Renn.

Passed me.

Closer than I wanted.

Her real head turned.

One dark eye found the doorway.

Found me.

For half a second every false head turned too.

Four eyes.

Six.

Ten.

I did not move.

The inner light in her horn tips brightened.

Not attack.

Attention.

I knew the difference.

Mostly.

She smelled like wet stone and crushed leaves.

Then she kept walking.

I waited until her hind feet cleared the first cart before breathing normally.

The last female followed.

No charge.

No broken stall.

No blood.

Six real animals.

At least eighteen opinions about where they had been.

They reached the far lane mouth.

The male stopped there.

Renn tensed.

I said, "Wait."

The herd bunched.

The copies thickened again.

Beyond them, a delivery wagon had turned into the cross street.

The wagon driver saw the mirrorhorns and did the smartest thing anyone had done all morning.

He stopped.

Backed.

Disappeared.

The male continued.

One by one the herd followed him out of the lane.

The calf was last.

It had somehow acquired a second pear.

The fruit seller whispered, "Thief."

I said, "You may file a complaint."

Then the last false gray tail slipped around the corner and vanished.

Nobody moved.

For three breaths.

Four.

The dog escaped the apron and barked once.

The entire lane shouted at it.

The dog sat down.

Civilization restored.

Renn looked at me.

Then toward the cross street.

"That's it?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"They are animals, not a signed contract."

The gray-braided woman relaxed her grip on the mule's reins.

The mule opened one eye.

She patted its neck.

"Good girl."

The mule had contributed more than most of us.

The two carts started negotiating who moved first.

The fruit seller picked up a basket.

The butcher removed the apron from his dog and called it several names I did not think belonged to the animal at birth.

Shop lamps came back on.

The lane reassembled itself.

That was how you knew a danger had been ordinary enough.

People complained before the adrenaline finished leaving.

Renn remained where he was.

He looked young now that the sword was away.

Maybe twenty.

A little older than this body.

Much younger than me in every way that mattered and several that did not.

"What are they doing here?" he asked.

"I don't know."

He blinked.

"You knew all that."

"I know mirrorhorns."

"Not why they're here."

"No."

The gray-braided woman said, "North road herd sometimes comes near the old lime fields."

Renn looked at her.

"This isn't the lime fields."

She looked around at the buildings.

"Strong observation."

Then she added, "Could be gate work. Could be noise. Ask drovers if you need the why."

There.

I knew the animals.

She knew the nearby route.

Neither of us owned the whole answer.

Renn rubbed his mouth.

Then looked at me again.

"What's your name?"

There it was.

Attention.

Small.

Ordinary.

Still attention.

"Greg."

"Greg what?"

"Greg remains sufficient."

The gray-braided woman laughed.

Renn did not.

"Lane watch keeps incident names."

"Then write yours."

He stared at me.

I stared back.

He sighed.

"Renn."

"Greg," I said again.

Renn studied me.

Then nodded.

"Where'd you learn mirrorhorns?"

That question was worse.

Not because I lacked an answer.

Because I had too many.

Northern salt roads.

Caravans.

A military supply route where frightened recruits killed a calf and turned a harmless crossing into injuries.

Decades.

None available to nineteen-year-old Greg.

"Travel stories," I said.

Not a complete lie.

Stories had traveled.

Some of them inside me.

The gray-braided woman looked at me.

She knew that answer was thin.

She was polite enough not to pull it.

Renn was not.

"That's more than stories."

I shrugged.

"Good stories."

He almost smiled.

Then someone called his name from the far end of the lane.

He turned.

A second lane watchman stood there gesturing toward the cross street.

Renn looked back at me.

"Stay?"

"No."

"We might need what direction they went."

"You can see the road."

"What if they stop again?"

"Then keep people out of the middle and find somebody who handles them for a living."

He frowned.

"You're not a handler?"

"No."

That mattered.

Old Greg had handled enough creatures to fake competence past the edge of wisdom.

Current Greg had two crutches, no rope, no animal gear, no authority, and no need to convert one useful memory into a profession.

Renn nodded reluctantly.

"Fine."

I left before fine became a job.

The gray-braided woman called after me.

"Greg."

I turned.

"Good call on the lamps."

"Good call on the cart."

She lifted one hand.

Fair trade.

I continued down the lane.

My hands were shaking slightly.

I noticed only after the first corner.

Not fear exactly.

After-fear.

The body's complaint filed once the meeting had ended.

I stopped beside a wall.

Right foot flat.

Crutches planted.

Breathed.

No pain.

No problem with the left leg.

No issue with the crutches.

Just blood still expecting six large animals to return through stone.

Old experience did not remove that.

Maybe it should not.

The first-life memory came back while I stood there.

Twenty-three.

Sword half drawn.

A mirrorhorn male becoming one animal instead of four.

The old caravan woman striking my wrist hard enough to make me swear.

"Copies mean thinking," she had said.

I had called that superstition.

Then the horn went through the wagon.

Afterward she made me help repair the canvas.

She never explained the magic.

She taught behavior.

That had been enough to keep me alive.

Years later I learned more.

Not everything.

Mirror fields changed with light, crowding, age, probably bloodline.

None of that mattered as much as the sentence I had almost ignored.

Copies mean thinking.

One means done thinking.

I had carried that for decades.

Today it had worked.

Not approximately.

Not maybe.

Not an uncertain future memory bent by forty years.

The animal had dropped its copies.

I had known what came next.

We had given it road.

It had chosen road.

I was fucking right.

The satisfaction arrived under the leftover shaking.

Warm.

Sharp.

Dangerous in its own way.

I did not need to sand it down into uncertainty.

I did not know why the herd was in Carrow.

I did not know whether the local route had changed.

I did not know whether the darker shoulder scales were regional or simply something I had forgotten.

Those uncertainties could live.

The behavior I knew.

That could live too.

I pushed away from the wall and kept walking.

At home, Cala was hanging a cloth under the gallery.

Lyssa sat at the table with a seam spread over one knee.

Tavi had found the bad-bean bowl again.

No one looked as if six mirrorhorns had recently occupied a commercial lane.

I opened the door.

Cala looked at me.

"You late."

"Animals."

She waited.

"Mirrorhorns."

Her eyebrows rose.

"Where?"

"Dye row."

Lyssa looked up.

"Inside?"

"Very."

Cala came away from the cloth.

"Six?"

I looked at her.

"How do you know six?"

"Someone came through."

Of course.

Carrow remained faster than walking.

Lyssa said, "She said eight."

"Six real."

Tavi said, "Eight."

"Do not join taxonomy."

Lyssa smiled.

Cala asked, "They hurt?"

"No."

"Cart?"

"No."

"Good."

That was the whole safety report she needed.

Lyssa looked at me more carefully.

"You did something."

I set the bag down.

"Possibly."

"You have that face."

"What face?"

"The one after Hessa says you were right about one small thing."

I was offended by the accuracy.

"Different authority."

Cala sat.

So I told them.

Not every detail.

The sword.

The bright lamps.

The open middle.

The female losing her copies.

The herd passing.

The calf stealing two pears.

Tavi immediately adopted the important part.

"Pear deer."

"Mirrorhorn."

"Pear deer."

"Incorrect."

"Two pear."

Lyssa laughed.

Cala did not know the mirrorhorn charge sign.

She knew people sometimes saw them north of the lime fields.

Same as the cart woman.

Lyssa had seen one once as a child from far enough away that somebody had lifted her onto a wall to watch.

"They were pretty," she said.

"They are."

"And bad?"

"Not bad."

"Dangerous."

"Yes."

She nodded.

No contradiction.

Cala asked, "Who was lane watch?"

"Renn."

She nodded.

Lyssa said, "He ask you?"

"Name. Where I learned it."

"What say?"

"Good stories."

She smiled.

"Bad answer."

"Excellent survival answer."

Tavi put the bowl on his head.

"Pear deer hat."

We had lost the room.

I went upstairs later and looked at the narrow scrap.

Seventy-three.

Seventy-four.

Seventy-five.

Near three in seven clean.

Still field improves observation.

Not proof it improves effect.

I did not add mirrorhorns.

I did not need to write down knowledge I had carried longer than this body had been alive.

That was new too.

Not all useful knowledge was a discovery.

Some of it was mine already.

I put the scrap back in the bag.

The three copper and one bit remained where I had left them.

No payment for the lane.

No reward.

No Guild note.

No theatre paper.

No new job.

Downstairs, Cala was telling someone at the door that yes, Greg had seen them and no, Greg was not a mirrorhorn handler.

I closed my eyes.

Attention traveled faster than caution.

Then Cala called up, "Greg."

I leaned over the rail.

"What?"

A woman I did not know stood in the doorway.

Not frightened.

Not official.

Holding a basket of onions.

She looked up at me.

"Mirrorhorns really stop copying before charge?"

There it was.

Not a job.

Worse.

A question.

I could have said maybe.

I could have protected myself with uncertainty until every useful edge went blunt.

Instead I said, "Yes. If the whole animal goes single and holds still, give it space. Do not test it."

The woman nodded.

"Good."

She left.

Cala looked up at me.

I looked down at her.

"That is how this starts."

"What?"

"People asking things."

Cala shrugged.

"They ask."

"Terrifying."

She went back to the kitchen.

By evening, nobody else came.

The lane stayed open.

No herd appeared at our door.

Renn did not send paper.

My debt remained exactly as unresolved as it had been that morning.

My magic remained thirty-six, thirty-two, seventy-five.

Nothing had changed on the page.

Something had changed anyway.

For weeks, most of my useful knowledge in Carrow had come from learning the city.

A price.

A route.

A local rule.

A repair.

A name.

Today the city had presented something I already knew better than the people nearest me.

Not because they were fools.

Because I had lived longer than I looked.

Because I had survived the mistake once.

Because some knowledge did not rot when the calendar moved backward.

At dusk, I stepped into the household court.

The charm lamp above the gallery came on.

Blue-white.

Sharp.

For half a second the light caught on the rim of a water cup and split into three pale reflections.

My body reacted before thought.

Then I laughed.

No horns.

No herd.

Just a cup.

I left the lamp on.

The road outside stayed clear.
