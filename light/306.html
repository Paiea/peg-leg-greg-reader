TEXT READER · CHAPTER 306

THE BAD TOOL

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Three copper had been enough money to make me curious.

That was the problem.

Curiosity had survived poverty by becoming theoretical.

Once I could afford things, it developed hands.

The morning after the mirrorhorns, nobody came to ask me about mirrorhorns.

This was excellent.

Cala made porridge.

Lyssa worked near the window with pale thread and dark cloth that I did not recognize and did not need to recognize.

Tavi had converted a spoon into a horse.

Ordinary civilization resumed.

I ate.

Then I went upstairs and looked at my money.

Three copper.

One bit.

The bit stayed where it was.

One copper stayed with it.

I took two.

Not because I had an errand.

Because I had decided to look at bad tools.

This was not a responsible sentence.

The lower market had several kinds of used goods, but the salvage edge was different from the ordinary clothing, pots, chairs, bags, and household things farther in.

Salvage meant somebody had already decided the object had failed one life.

Warehouse fittings.

Cart hardware.

Lengths of treated chain.

Bent brackets.

Old charm housings with the beads removed.

Beads with the housings removed.

Rope blocks that had spent years hanging over something heavier than me.

Cracked ward plates sold as metal instead of magic.

The broad categories were familiar.

The exact marks were not.

Good.

That was what markets were supposed to be.

I had spent enough of my first life around adventurers to know the ugly truth about equipment.

The useful thing was rarely the thing labeled useful.

A perfect military buckle could be useless for climbing.

A terrible wagon brace could save a bridge.

A cooking charm could become an excellent way to ruin a lock if a person had no respect for cookware or property.

Most of those discoveries had already happened by the time Old Greg encountered them.

Some had not.

That was the interesting part.

I moved slowly along the salvage line.

Not searching for treasure.

That was how people bought polished garbage.

I looked for constraints.

A copper-framed warming plate with one corner melted.

No.

A pair of old door wards that only matched each other and neither matching door.

No.

A brass pulley with a blue load bead split neatly through the center.

Beautiful corpse.

No.

A box of six light hooks whose glow surfaces had gone cloudy.

Possible repair problem.

Still no.

I had two copper in the inside pocket of my coat.

That was enough to buy something regrettable.

I intended to preserve the opportunity.

Then I saw the holdfast.

Palm wide.

Black iron.

Three folding teeth tucked under a round base.

A thick ring through the top.

One dull amber bead sat off-center under the ring, almost hidden by old grime.

No release tongue.

I stopped.

The woman behind the tarp looked at me.

She had short gray hair and forearms marked with old rope burns.

"Don't," she said.

I looked at the holdfast.

"Strong sales technique."

"You stopped at the bad one."

"That usually means interesting."

"Usually means bad."

Fair.

The holdfast lay among ordinary cargo fittings.

Two iron strap eyes.

A bent chain guide.

Three hook plates.

A narrow brass tension block with no bead.

The holdfast was the only thing with active magic still inside it.

Probably.

"Fixed hold?" I asked.

The woman looked at me more carefully.

"You know them?"

"Old ones."

"Old is right."

She picked it up by the ring.

The three folding teeth clicked against each other underneath.

No rust at the joints.

That mattered.

The iron body had been oiled recently.

"Warehouse pullout," she said. "North storage. They replaced the wall eyes. This was in a bin with the dead fittings."

"Dead?"

"Mostly."

She tapped the amber bead with one nail.

"This bastard still works."

Better sales technique.

I leaned closer.

A shallow maker's mark sat on the underside of the ring mount.

Three short lines.

One long line crossing them.

I knew that mark.

Not from a museum.

From being wet, tired, and very angry in a ravine while a rescue line depended on two pieces of hardware I had insulted for an hour.

Forty holds.

That was what people called them later.

Not the real product name.

The name that survived.

You set one against stone, timber, or another surface solid enough to take its bite.

Twist the ring.

The teeth seated.

The bead took hold.

For a short fixed interval, the thing became extremely difficult to move relative to whatever it had accepted as its surface.

Then it released.

Whether you wanted it to or not.

That last part had killed the cargo market.

A cargo anchor that could not be released early was bad.

A cargo anchor that definitely released itself later was worse.

Carters liked their loads attached until they chose otherwise.

Warehouse crews had similar opinions about walls.

Years after this time, people stopped pretending the old fixed holds were cargo equipment.

Rescue crews used them.

Climbers used them.

Dungeon teams used them.

Anyone who occasionally needed one point in the world to stay one point for long enough to do something stupid somewhere else.

I had used them.

A lot.

Not this one.

Not this exact unit.

But the mark and the missing release tongue put it in the old family I remembered.

My pulse picked up.

This was not Hessa's paper.

No uncertainty about whether I had once seen the category.

I had trusted my life to the damn things.

The woman said, "You going to stare or ask?"

"Both, if pricing allows."

"Two copper."

I stared at her instead.

"For the bad one."

"For the working one."

Also fair.

"No early release?"

"None."

"Warning before it lets go?"

"Three blinks if the bead isn't filthy."

"How long?"

She shrugged.

"Long enough to annoy you. Short enough to ruin cargo."

"Technical."

"Depends heat. Depends what it bites. Depends how long it sat cold. Old book said forty breaths."

There it was.

Forty holds.

Not because they always held forty breaths.

Because somebody had printed forty once and people enjoyed lying with numbers.

The woman pointed behind her stall.

A thick plank was bolted upright between two posts.

Its surface held scratches from tests.

"Want to see?"

"Yes."

She carried the holdfast over.

I followed.

Not under her hands.

Not near the teeth.

Old habits survived too.

She folded the three teeth outward.

Pressed the base flat against the plank.

Then twisted the top ring a quarter turn.

The amber bead lit.

Not bright.

A deep orange point under the iron.

The teeth moved.

Not much.

They bit into the wood with three dry clicks.

The woman took her hand away.

The holdfast stayed on the vertical plank.

She pulled the ring.

Hard.

Nothing.

She put both hands on it and leaned back.

The plank creaked.

The holdfast did not move.

"Works," she said.

"Load?"

"No idea."

"Ever fail under test?"

"Not with me. I don't test stupid."

A philosophy.

"How much is stupid?"

"More than my body."

Reasonable ceiling.

She stopped pulling.

The orange bead stayed steady.

I watched.

Counted breaths automatically.

Then stopped counting.

My breathing was not a clock.

Hessa had damaged me.

The woman saw me looking at the bead.

"Watch."

Eventually the steady orange dimmed.

Then flashed.

Once.

A pause.

Twice.

Shorter pause.

Three.

The woman stepped away from the plank.

So did I.

Two breaths later, the bead went dark.

The three teeth released together.

The holdfast dropped.

The woman caught the ring before the iron hit the ground.

"That's the bad part."

"That's the useful part."

She stared at me.

I felt the old memory open fully.

A ravine in spring flood.

A broken stair cut into black rock.

Water rising below.

Two fixed holds biting into a stone wall while six people crossed a rope line one at a time.

The warning blink on the first while the last man was halfway across.

Me shouting at him to stop climbing and swing.

Him objecting.

Me expressing a professional opinion about his parents.

The hold releasing exactly when we knew it would release.

The rope dropping free after everyone was safe.

No one had needed to go back for hardware under a waterfall.

The fixed release had not been a defect that day.

It had been the reason we could leave.

Another memory.

A mine brace.

A cracked beam.

One hold used long enough to slide a proper support under it.

Another.

A wagon wheel in mud, not as an anchor but as a temporary line point while three people pulled sideways.

Bad cargo tool.

Excellent temporary certainty.

The seller shook the holdfast once.

"Useful for what?"

I came back to Carrow.

"Anything that needs to stay where it is for a little while and absolutely does not need to stay there forever."

"That's not an answer."

"Rescue line. Temporary brace. Short pull. Controlled crossing."

She considered the holdfast.

Not stupid.

Not impressed.

"And when it lets go under load?"

"Everyone should already be done."

"Should."

"That word has killed more people than magic."

She laughed once.

Then handed me the iron.

I did not take it immediately.

"Cooldown?"

"Long."

"How long?"

"Never tried twice same hour."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because I wasn't going to believe a precise answer anyway."

She narrowed her eyes.

I liked her.

I took the holdfast.

Heavier than it looked.

Maybe three pounds.

Not enough to trouble the shoulder bag by itself.

Enough that I would not want three of them.

The folded teeth sat safely under the base.

The ring was thick enough for rope or a hook.

The amber bead remained dark and slightly warm from activation.

I turned it over.

Three short marks.

One long across them.

No doubt.

The mark mattered less than the design.

Later versions had release tongues.

Better for most people.

Worse for some uses.

The old blind holds had fewer moving parts and a habit of staying committed until the timer ended.

I had once trusted one because a newer release tongue had jammed full of sand.

Future knowledge did not always mean better technology.

Sometimes it meant knowing which obsolete failure you preferred.

I gave the holdfast back.

The seller raised an eyebrow.

"No?"

"Thinking."

"Do it away from the tarp."

I stepped aside.

Two copper.

Two-thirds of my private copper.

Three copper had taken several jobs to accumulate.

One had already become the shoulder bag.

That purchase solved a daily need.

This solved nothing today.

No client required it.

No household repair required it.

No theatre job required it.

Hessa had not asked for it.

The Guild had not approved it.

It did not make my own magic stronger.

It did not pay Vale.

It did not buy food.

It was not even guaranteed to be safe under the loads I remembered from later versions.

Different unit.

Old age.

Unknown service history.

Unknown threshold.

Two copper.

I could walk away.

I should probably walk away.

Then another memory arrived.

Old Greg at forty-eight, watching a young rescue crew open a crate of modern fixed holds.

One of them complaining that the new release tongues made the body too bulky for narrow stone seams.

Me telling him the old blind ones had been better for that.

Him laughing because by then old blind holds were collector junk and nobody issued them anymore.

I remembered the shape of this one.

The plain ring.

The three teeth.

The missing release tongue.

The ugly orange bead.

I did not remember a market price.

I did not remember this seller.

I did not know whether this exact piece would survive another fifty activations or five.

But I knew the job it could do.

That was enough.

I went back.

"Two copper."

The seller held out her hand.

I gave her two coins.

She bit neither.

Different merchant philosophy.

She turned them once in her palm, then put them into a small iron box.

"Yours."

"Any return?"

"If it kills you, no."

"If it doesn't?"

"Also no."

Clear terms.

She wrapped the holdfast in a square of old sailcloth and tied it with thin cord.

Not gift wrapping.

Finger protection.

"Keep teeth folded," she said.

"Yes."

"Don't turn ring while it's sitting flat against anything you care about."

"Yes."

"Don't put your fingers under it after set."

"Yes."

She looked at me.

"You really do know them."

"Enough to be afraid correctly."

"Good."

She handed me the bundle.

I put it in the shoulder bag.

The bag settled lower.

The stay bead on the first notch gave one faint green blink and held the strap position.

The extra weight was noticeable.

Not bad.

I walked ten steps.

Turned.

No swing into either crutch.

The seller watched from the stall.

"Bag holds?"

"So far."

"Good bag."

"One copper."

"You paid one for that?"

"Yes."

She looked offended on behalf of commerce.

"Good price."

I continued before she could sell me something else.

The remaining private copper and one bit were upstairs.

That felt different now.

Three copper had been a small pile.

One copper was a decision already made.

The walk home became more expensive with every step.

Not because the holdfast grew heavier.

Because I had crossed a line.

Until now, most of my private spending had been easy to defend.

The bag carried things while I used crutches.

Fried dough had been fried dough and required no theory.

This was speculation.

Not financial speculation exactly.

Capability speculation.

I had bought a tool before I had a job for it because I believed the constraint was more valuable than the market around me believed.

That could become very smart.

It could also become a shelf ornament that cost two copper.

Both outcomes deserved respect.

At home, Cala noticed the bag first.

"Heavy."

"Yes."

Lyssa looked up from the table.

Tavi looked up because everyone else had.

I set both crutches carefully against the wall, sat, and took the wrapped holdfast from the bag.

Cala looked at the bundle.

"What?"

"Bad tool."

Tavi said, "Bad."

"Thank you."

Lyssa put down her thread.

"How bad?"

"Two copper."

Silence.

Not angry silence.

Accounting silence.

Cala said, "Yours."

"Very much mine."

"Good."

Household money remained household money.

Private money remained private money.

That did not protect me from judgment.

Lyssa pointed at the bundle.

"Show."

I untied the sailcloth.

Did not unfold the teeth.

Did not place the base flat on the table.

The seller's warning had been excellent.

I held it by the ring so they could see.

Cala frowned.

"Anchor?"

"Temporary holdfast."

"For what?"

"Originally cargo, probably. Bad at cargo."

Lyssa looked at me.

"You bought bad cargo anchor."

"Yes."

"Two copper."

"That part remains unfortunate."

Tavi reached.

I lifted it higher.

"No."

"Bad."

"Correct."

Cala pointed at the amber bead.

"Works?"

"Seller tested it."

I explained the fixed interval.

Sets hard.

No early release.

Three warning blinks.

Then it lets go whether the user approves or not.

Cala's face flattened as the explanation continued.

"Bad anchor."

"For cargo."

Lyssa said, "For what good?"

That was the question.

I could not say ravines forty years from now.

I could not say military rescue teams that did not exist yet in the form I remembered.

I could say the logic.

"Temporary brace. Pull point. A place for a rope when you know you only need it for a short time. Something you want fixed now and gone later."

Lyssa considered that.

She understood tools through cloth, fit, tension, sequence.

"Like basting."

I looked at her.

"Exactly like basting if basting could break your arm when the stitch decided time was over."

She smiled.

"Bad basting."

"Powerful basting."

Cala said, "Bad."

Tavi said, "Bad."

Democracy had spoken.

I rewrapped the holdfast.

Lyssa asked, "Need it now?"

"No."

"Know you need later?"

"No."

"Then why buy now?"

I looked at the bundle.

Because I knew what it became.

Because I remembered trusting a version of it when newer tools failed.

Because useful knowledge had finally collided with enough money to act on it.

Because three copper felt like waiting and one copper felt like choosing.

I said, "Because it does one thing very well, and tools get expensive after people agree what the one thing is for."

Lyssa watched me.

Not convinced.

Not dismissive.

"Maybe."

That was fair.

Cala said, "Where keep?"

Good question.

Not near Tavi.

Not on the table.

Not flat against a wall with the ring exposed where someone could twist it by accident.

We put it upstairs on the high shelf above my folded clothes, still wrapped, ring turned sideways, with the base resting on the sailcloth rather than directly against wood.

I added a small scrap tag from an already-used edge of paper.

DO NOT TURN RING.

Tavi could not read it.

This was less reassuring than writing usually was.

I moved the bundle farther back.

Then farther again.

No activation.

No home test.

The seller had already demonstrated that this unit set and released once.

I had no reason to punch three teeth into Cala's furniture for science.

Hessa would probably have approved of that sentence.

Possibly after insulting the purchase.

I did not take the holdfast to the Guild.

It was not part of my magic protocol.

I did not take it to Rell.

I did not ask Jori what it was worth.

I did not walk back to the salvage market looking for three more.

One expensive bad idea was enough for a day.

In the afternoon I went out without money.

This felt wise after the fact.

The city behaved normally.

No bridges collapsed in need of temporary anchors.

No carts required rescue.

No warehouse beam cracked in front of me.

No client appeared with exactly the problem I had just spent money preparing to solve.

Good.

The world did not owe me immediate justification.

I passed a crew raising a shop awning.

They used rope, a wooden cleat, and two people who knew their work.

Better than my holdfast.

I passed a delivery man bracing a handcart on a slope with an ordinary stone under the wheel.

Cheaper than my holdfast.

I passed a woman tying a load to a roof rack with three loops of plain rope.

More reusable than my holdfast.

This was healthy.

A specialized magical tool did not make every mundane solution obsolete.

That was how people ended up carrying fifty pounds of enchanted nonsense to solve problems that required string.

Old Greg had done that too.

Several times.

Current Greg was carrying nothing but his normal shoulder bag.

The bad tool stayed home.

At supper, Cala did not mention the two copper.

Neither did Lyssa.

That made it worse in a mature way.

I mentioned it myself.

"I have one copper and one bit left."

Cala said, "I know."

Of course she knew.

Lyssa said, "Rich."

"Thank you."

"Very rich."

"Cruel household."

Tavi said, "Bad."

"You have one word now."

"Door red."

He had two.

After supper I went upstairs and looked at the shelf.

The wrapped holdfast sat exactly where I had left it.

A terrible cargo anchor.

An excellent temporary certainty.

Maybe.

The seller's unit had held her plank.

That was established.

It had warned before release.

Established.

It had released on its own.

Very established.

Its safe load was unknown.

Its cooldown was unknown.

Its remaining service life was unknown.

Whether it would behave on stone the way I remembered later units behaving was unknown.

Whether I would ever need it was deeply unknown.

But the category was not.

The use was not.

This was different from buying a mystery because I hoped to solve it.

I had bought a known bad interface because I knew a job where the bad interface became useful.

That did not make the purchase correct.

Not yet.

It made the bet legible.

I took out the narrow scrap.

Below the magic notes, I wrote:

FIXED HOLDFAST.

2 COPPER.

NO EARLY RELEASE.

THREE WARNING BLINKS.

SAFE LOAD UNKNOWN.

DO NOT TEST ON HOUSE.

I considered writing FUTURE USEFUL.

I did not.

That was not evidence available to anyone but me.

Instead I wrote:

USEFUL ONLY IF RELEASE TIME IS PART OF PLAN.

Better.

I put the charcoal away.

The three clean sheets remained untouched.

The horse page remained full.

My magic remained thirty-six, thirty-two, seventy-five.

The mirrorhorns remained somewhere else.

My debt remained unresolved.

No one had paid me today.

I had paid somebody else.

Two copper.

That fact sat heavier than the iron had.

I looked at the shelf again.

For the first time since I started earning private money in Carrow, I had spent most of a small pile on something that might matter later.

One copper left meant fewer mistakes available.

That was good too.

Possibility without consequence was just browsing.

The bad tool had made the consequence real.

I blew the charcoal dust from my fingers.

Downstairs, Lyssa laughed at something Cala said.

Tavi shouted, "BAD DOOR RED," combining his education.

I put the scrap back in the bag.

The holdfast stayed on the shelf.

Not treasure.

Not an upgrade.

Not profit.

A tool with the wrong job.

For now, that was enough.
