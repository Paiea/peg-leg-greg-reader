LIGHT EDITION · CHAPTER 307

THE RED POT

Text-first edition · no chapter illustrations

The holdfast remained on the high shelf all morning. For two copper, it was excellent at not helping.

I left it there.

By late afternoon, Cala had found a more immediate use for me. She stood beside the kitchen table with a clean gray scarf tied over her hair and pointed at onions, two carrots, a tied bundle of greens, a bowl of soaked beans, and the red cooking pot waiting with its lid upside down beside it.

"Dinner."

I looked at the ingredients, then at her. "Is dinner arriving?"

"You are making it."

Tavi looked up from the floor, where three wooden blocks and a spoon had become something involving a horse. "Greg cook."

"Do not encourage her."

Cala was already putting on a different blouse, which concerned me more than the vegetables. The gray scarf had narrow yellow stitching along one edge. Not house clothes. Not errand clothes. She was going somewhere.

"Where are you going?"

"Out."

"I had gathered that from the advanced scarf."

She lifted a covered bowl from the counter. "Cards."

I stared at her. Cala played cards. Apparently entire civilizations had continued in this house while I was busy surviving them.

"With who?"

"People."

"Specific."

"Yes."

Tavi nodded as if this settled everything. "Cards."

Cala pointed at him. "You eat what Greg makes."

His face changed. I had never seen a child understand betrayal so quickly.

"Now," I said, "we have a reasonable witness."

Cala ignored me. "Beans already soaked. Pot already filled. Greens last. Salt after boil. Red pot on left stone."

"I know how to make stew."

She looked at me.

I corrected myself. "I understand the theory of stew."

"Red pot on left stone. Ear to wall."

The pot had two short side handles. I looked at them. "Both ears exist."

"The cracked one."

One handle had a hairline split near the base, bound years ago with dark wire. "Why?"

Cala was already at the door. "Because."

Excellent instruction.

She opened it, stopped, and looked back toward the hearth. "Half heat. Not full."

"I know a heat stone."

"I know you know a heat stone."

That was somehow more insulting.

A knock came before I could answer. Cala opened the door wider. An older woman stood there with a flat wooden box under one arm and a green shawl looped over the other. She looked past Cala at me, then at the table.

"You left him cooking?"

"Yes."

The woman looked at Tavi.

"Greg cook," he said.

She winced.

I had not even touched an onion yet. "This household has very little faith."

The woman laughed. Cala did not. She handed Cala the shawl, took the covered bowl from her, and they left together without explanation, invitation, or concern that I might wonder who the woman was.

The door closed. Tavi and I looked at each other.

"All right. We have been abandoned."

"No."

"Good correction. We have been trusted."

He considered that. "No."

Fair.

The kitchen hearth was built low into the brick wall beside the counter. Three dark heat stones sat under iron plates, each fitted inside a clay ring with a small brass tongue at the front. Ordinary household work. I had used versions in inns, camps, guild kitchens, field tents, and one deeply regrettable mountain station where every stone had been set too hot by a cook who believed boiling was a moral quality.

Cala's left stone was old. Its black center had gone gray around the edges, and one amber line glowed brighter than the others when I pushed the brass tongue halfway in. That did not mean the stone was broken. It meant the stone was old. Household equipment was allowed to age without becoming a case.

I moved my chair closer to the table before I started. Cala had already done the important geometry for me. The pot held water but remained on the counter within easy reach of the hearth. The knife, board, vegetables, and salt were all at the table. She had not announced any accommodation. She had simply put the heavy things where I did not need to cross the room carrying them while using two crutches.

That was how the house worked now.

I sat with my right foot flat and my left knee clear under the table. Both crutches leaned against the wall within reach, angled so neither could slide. Tavi climbed onto the chair opposite me.

"You are not helping with the knife."

"I help."

"Not with the knife."

"Carrot."

"You may inspect the carrot."

He picked up the smaller carrot and held it to one eye. "Good."

"Professional opinion?"

"Good."

"Excellent. Put it down."

He did not.

I cut the onions first. This was not a mistake in principle. Humanity had survived onions. The mistake was believing I could cut them while Tavi narrated the carrot.

"Long."

"Yes."

"Orange."

"Strong work."

"Horse."

"No."

He put the carrot between two blocks. "Cart."

"That is better engineering."

The knife was dull enough to irritate me and sharp enough to punish carelessness. Normal knife. I liked normal knife. By the time I finished the vegetables, Tavi had built a stable around the remaining whole carrot and was charging the spoon admission.

I stood using the table edge and one crutch, then took the second. The pot was already full enough to be awkward, so I did not carry it across the kitchen. I shifted it six inches from the counter onto the left hearth plate with both hands while standing close, my right hip against the counter and both crutches braced behind me. Then I stepped back, resettled my grips, and checked the position.

The cracked ear faced the room.

Cala had said wall.

I turned the pot.

Tavi nodded. "Red pot."

"You knew that?"

"Red pot."

"That does not answer the question."

"Red pot."

I had once negotiated with a duke whose entire political method had been roughly similar.

I pushed the brass tongue halfway. The amber lines in the heat stone brightened, and dry stored warmth rose through the iron plate. No flame. No smoke. Nothing worth investigating. I knew the category. I did not know Cala's stove. That distinction had become useful often enough that I no longer resented it on principle.

I added the onions.

The first hiss came from the front side of the pot. I leaned close enough to feel the heat pattern against my knuckles. The plate ran harder along one quadrant, not dangerously, just unevenly.

So that was why the cracked ear went to the wall. With the pot turned that way, its deeper side sat over the hotter section.

Household science. Discovered by Cala through the advanced method of living here.

I stirred until the onions softened, then got distracted by the front door.

Lyssa came in with her hair partly loose and a small crease between her eyebrows. She carried no parcel, basket, or cloth bundle. Just herself and the posture of somebody who had spent a long day leaning over work.

She stopped when she smelled the kitchen. "Cala?"

"Cards."

Lyssa looked toward the hearth, then at me, then at Tavi.

Tavi pointed. "Greg cook."

Lyssa set one hand against the doorframe. "Oh."

I stared at her. "That sound was unnecessary."

"No."

"You haven't tasted anything."

"I can see."

"Hostile house."

She washed her hands at the basin and sat at the table without offering to take over. I appreciated that. I also resented it. Both could be true.

Then I remembered the onions.

They had gone past soft. Not black. Brown. Aggressively brown.

I stirred.

Lyssa leaned over my shoulder. "Burnt."

"Caramelized."

"Burnt."

"Those are adjacent categories."

She took one piece from the spoon, blew on it, ate it, and made a face. "Burnt."

Tavi joined from the floor. "Burnt."

"Wonderful. A panel."

Lyssa sat again while I added carrots, beans, and the onions to the pot.

"Greens later," she said.

"Cala said."

"Salt after boil."

"Cala said."

"Cracked ear to wall."

I turned slowly.

She smiled.

"You all know this?"

"Red pot."

From the floor, Tavi repeated, "Red pot."

There were conspiracies in Carrow. I had found one.

The stew began to warm. I returned to the table because standing beside a pot did not make it boil faster and did make my shoulders work for no reason. Lyssa took the carrot from Tavi's stable, broke the end off, ate it, and handed the rest back.

He stared at the missing piece.

"Tax," I told him.

Lyssa looked at me. "No."

"Strong government."

"No."

Tavi put the carrot behind his back.

Good citizen.

For a while nothing happened. That was the difficult part of cooking.

Old Greg had understood meals as objects that appeared after money, orders, or camp labor by people with more patience than me. Even when I cooked in the first life, it usually involved meat, fire, one pan, and the principle that hunger improved standards. Stew expected waiting.

I did not like systems where the correct action was nothing.

Hessa would have approved. I did not tell her.

The heat stone gave a faint click as one amber line shifted. Lyssa glanced over.

"Half."

"It is half."

"Then leave."

"I am leaving."

"You are staring at it."

"I am supervising."

She closed her eyes.

Tavi moved his blocks across the floor one at a time, extending the road under the table. I looked at the pot, then away. Lyssa's foot touched my right shoe under the table. Not a signal. Just there. Her eyes stayed closed.

I let my shoulder rest against hers.

The stew began to boil without my help.

Rude.

I stood again, lowered the brass tongue slightly, and watched the amber lines dim. Then I added the greens and a small pinch of salt. Cala had left the salt dish small on purpose. This was wise.

I stirred. The stew smelled better now. The burnt onion had become part of the argument rather than the winner.

Lyssa came over and tasted the broth from the spoon.

"More salt."

"How much?"

She pinched some between two fingers. "That much."

"This is not a unit."

She dropped it in. "Now it is."

I stirred and tasted.

Better.

I added nothing else.

Tavi wanted bread. There was bread. Of course there was. Cala had left half a round loaf under cloth near the window.

I had been assigned dinner in the same way a theatre worker might be assigned to move one chair after the entire stage had already been built.

Still counted.

We ate at the table. Tavi got the shallow bowl because he liked the shallow bowl. Lyssa took the chipped blue one. Mine was plain brown.

The first bite was hot enough to punish enthusiasm. Tavi blew on his. Lyssa did not. She had apparently accepted pain as efficient.

I watched her chew. "Well?"

She took another bite. "Food."

"That is a category, not a judgment."

"Food."

Tavi said, "Food."

I had created a coalition government.

The stew was decent. Not good enough to become family history. Not bad enough either. The onions were slightly bitter at the edges, the carrots softer than I preferred, the beans held, and the greens had not dissolved into green rope.

I took another bowl.

Lyssa did too.

Tavi did not. He ate bread instead and dipped it into the broth until the bread stopped being structurally honest.

Halfway through supper, somebody knocked. Tavi immediately said, "Cala."

It was not Cala.

A girl maybe twelve stood outside holding an empty covered bowl. I had seen her in the lane before but did not know her name.

"Cala?"

"Out."

She handed me the bowl. "For her."

I took it with one hand while bracing the door with my shoulder. "From?"

She looked confused by the question. "Us."

Useful.

"Excellent. I will preserve the chain of custody."

She had already turned away.

I shut the door. Lyssa looked at the bowl.

"Pickled beans."

"How do you know?"

"Smell."

I lifted the lid.

Pickled beans.

She did not explain who had sent them. Neither did Cala later. The bowl went on the counter.

After supper, Tavi became impossible. This was not caused by the stew. He had reached the hour when children transformed stored sunlight into bad decisions. He ran the spoon along the table edge until Lyssa took it, crawled under my chair, asked whether Cala was back six times, announced that the carrot was sleeping, and then lay on the floor beside it.

I washed bowls because this put me at a safe distance from parenting philosophy.

The basin was low enough that I could brace my right hip against the counter and work one-handed when needed. I stacked clean bowls on the cloth to my left. Lyssa dried because there were wet bowls and she knew where they went.

Tavi dragged his block road through the kitchen.

"Not under the crutches."

He moved the road three inches.

"More."

Three more.

"That works."

He accepted the engineering revision.

Lyssa handed me the plain brown bowl. "You can cook."

"Careful."

"Once."

"Thank you."

"Maybe twice."

"Now you're reckless."

She smiled and put the bowl away. The crease between her eyebrows was gone. I noticed and left it alone.

Cala returned after dark.

We heard her before the key. Laughter in the lane, not Cala alone. Two voices, then three, then somebody said something that made her laugh again. The voices continued past the house after she came in.

She closed the door with her hip and took off the gray scarf.

Tavi ran at her. "Cala!"

She caught him against one side. "Eat?"

"Greg cook."

Cala looked at me. "Alive?"

"All casualties are accounted for."

Lyssa said, "Burnt onion."

Betrayal.

Cala went to the pot. There was enough left for one bowl. She tasted it standing.

I waited.

This was worse than Hessa watching paper.

Cala took another bite. "Salt good."

Lyssa looked offended.

I sat straighter. "Thank you."

"Onion burnt."

I collapsed internally.

Tavi shouted, "BURNT."

Cala ate the whole bowl.

That was the only review that mattered.

She saw the covered bowl on the counter. "Oh."

That was all.

"Pickled beans," I said.

"I know."

"Of course."

She put them away.

I had questions.

I asked none.

Cala rinsed her bowl. I had already cleaned the rest. She noticed, said nothing, put hers on the drying cloth, and told Tavi it was bed.

He argued. She ignored the argument and carried him upstairs under one arm while he explained that the carrot also needed bed.

Lyssa leaned against the table.

"Good night?"

"For Cala?"

"Yes."

"Apparently."

"You sound surprised."

"I did not know she played cards."

"You don't know a lot."

"Specific."

"No."

She took my hand. "Come."

We went upstairs. The holdfast was still on the high shelf. Its warning tag hung from the sailcloth, and one copper and one bit remained where I had left them.

I did not touch any of it.

Lyssa sat on the edge of the bed and pulled out the pins holding the rest of her hair. I sat beside her.

"Burnt onion," she said.

"Go to hell."

She laughed, and I kissed her.

Downstairs, Tavi shouted, "CARROT BLANKET."

Cala said, "No."

A pause.

"Small blanket."

Lyssa put her face against my shoulder and laughed hard enough to shake the bed.

I had no answer for that.
