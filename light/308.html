TEXT READER · CHAPTER 308

THE DEBT HOUR

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The note arrived while I was eating the last of yesterday's bread.

Not the last bread in the house.

The last piece I had personally claimed before Tavi noticed it.

Important distinction.

He was standing beside my chair looking at the crust when somebody knocked.

Cala opened the door, took a folded square of paper from a boy, and handed it to me.

The seal was plain.

Vale office.

I knew that before I opened it because some kinds of dread developed handwriting.

Tavi pointed at the bread.

"Mine."

"No."

He pointed at the note.

"Mine."

"Also no."

He considered the two refusals and decided the bread remained the stronger legal case.

I unfolded the paper.

Greg,

Possible debt credit, if wanted.

Vale office. Ground floor.
One hour.
Seated.
Materials at table.
Cart covered.
Task: compare returned-goods tags against warehouse receipt list.
Mark MATCH, MISMATCH, or UNREADABLE only.
Do not assign cause.
Credit: one copper.

Reply yes or no.

Antonius.

I read it twice.

Not because the terms were complicated.

Because one copper existed in two places now.

One copper upstairs, privately mine.

One copper offered against money I already owed.

Same denomination.

Different direction.

The holdfast on the high shelf had made that distinction louder.

I had spent two copper because I believed an old bad tool would become useful later.

Vale did not care what I believed.

Vale cared what I owed.

Reasonable institution.

Annoying institution.

Cala watched me.

"Work?"

"Debt."

She nodded once and went back to cutting something green.

No lecture.

No concern about whether I should have bought the holdfast.

No accounting ceremony.

The two copper were already gone. The debt was already there.

The note was today's problem.

I looked at the terms again.

Ground floor.

Seated.

One hour.

Materials ready.

Transport covered.

Exact task narrow enough to fit inside a sentence.

Mark three categories only.

Do not assign cause.

Antonius had learned how to prevent me from becoming myself.

I found charcoal.

The boy waited at the door.

I wrote on the back:

YES.
ONE HOUR.
IF MATERIALS ARE READY AS STATED.

I almost added a question about whether the returned-goods tags were attached to the goods.

Stopped.

That was not required to accept the task.

I handed the note back.

The boy left.

Tavi took my bread.

I watched him walk away with it.

"That was theft."

"Mine."

Cala said, "You were reading."

Apparently lapse of attention transferred property.

Carrow law remained flexible.

*

The cart came before second bell.

Not a carriage.

A small two-seat hire cart with a broad step and a driver who had already turned the seat cushion sideways before I reached the door.

I knew the maneuver.

I had used Vale-covered transport before.

That did not make getting into a cart graceful.

Nothing made getting into a cart graceful.

I set one crutch inside, turned, used the frame and right leg, kept the left knee clear, and lowered myself onto the near side. The driver took the second crutch only after I handed it to him.

Good.

People who waited for the handoff were better than people who grabbed.

The shoulder bag stayed with me.

Inside it: narrow scrap, wrapped charcoal, nothing heavy.

The holdfast stayed on the shelf.

It had cost two copper and could contribute by remaining safely unemployed.

The ride to Vale's office was short enough that walking would have been possible.

That was not the point.

Transport had been included in the terms.

Taking it did not make the debt credit smaller.

Also, my shoulders were allowed to spend one hour not moving me through Carrow before spending another hour doing Vale's work.

Old Greg would have called this obvious.

Current Greg still occasionally treated saving effort like cheating.

Stupid habit.

The Vale building looked exactly as respectable as debt should.

Stone front.

Clean windows.

Brass plate beside the door.

No gargoyles.

A missed opportunity.

The driver brought the crutches around. I stood, settled both grips, and went inside.

Ground floor meant ground floor.

Antonius had not lied in the note.

Excellent beginning.

The side room already had a table, two chairs, a shallow wooden tray, a ledger, and nine flat goods tags laid in a row.

Nine.

The note had not promised a count.

Good.

A narrow green counting strip sat along the far edge of the table with six little brass sliders. Ordinary office equipment. I ignored it because it was not my task.

Antonius entered carrying a second ledger.

He wore dark gray today.

No coat drama.

No visible concern that I owned less money than three days ago.

"I expected a question," he said.

"I wrote one."

"You wrote a condition."

"Better."

He put the second ledger down and sat opposite me.

I looked at the nine tags.

Thin hardwood, each about two fingers wide, each with a string hole and a stamped number near one end. Some had ink words. Some had scratches. One had a corner missing.

No goods.

"Tags only?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because if you put damaged merchandise in front of me, I would start having opinions."

"I know."

There was no triumph in his voice.

Just accounting.

Antonius turned the first ledger toward me.

One page had nine receipt entries.

He tapped the top.

"Returned goods from East Store. The tags came back in one envelope. Warehouse copied the receipt list before the goods were dispersed for inspection."

"Dispersed where?"

"Not your task."

"Excellent."

He pointed to three small boxes ruled beside each receipt line.

"M. X. U."

"Match. Mismatch. Unreadable."

"Yes."

"What counts as match?"

"Receipt number and tag number agree. Written item family agrees where both are legible."

"Quantity?"

"Not on tags."

"Condition?"

"Not on tags."

"Maker?"

"Not part of this check."

"Cause of mismatch?"

"Not part of this check."

He had prepared for me.

I felt respected and attacked.

"What if number agrees and item word is partly legible?"

"Use judgment only on whether the word can be read. Do not infer missing letters from the receipt."

There.

Actual work.

Not solve the universe.

Read what existed.

I pulled the first tag closer.

Antonius slid a short pencil across the table.

I looked at it.

"Vale supplies tools now?"

"Return it."

Cruel.

The first tag read 442.

Receipt line: 442.

Tag word: BLUE GLASS.

Receipt: BLUE GLASS SLEEVES.

The word sleeves did not appear on the tag.

"Match?"

Antonius said nothing.

Right.

My task.

The item family agreed.

I marked M.

Second tag: 447.

Receipt: 447.

Tag: SMALL BRASS CAPS.

Receipt: BRASS CAPS, SMALL.

M.

Third tag had a dark smear over the last digit.

44-something.

Receipt said 451.

The item line on the tag was clear enough: WINDOW PINS.

Receipt: WINDOW PINS.

I stared at the number.

There was a vertical stroke.

Could be one.

Could be seven with the top lost under the smear.

Could be somebody's fingernail dragged through wet ink and created false confidence for nineteen-year-old idiots.

I marked U.

Antonius watched without commenting.

Fourth tag.

Receipt 453.

Tag 453.

Item: GREEN SEAL CLOTH.

Receipt: GREEN SEAL CLOTH.

M.

Fifth.

Receipt 455.

Tag 455.

Item line on the tag had been written twice.

The first words were crossed out.

RED BINDING.

Below:

BROWN BINDING.

Receipt said BROWN BINDING.

I nearly marked M.

Then stopped.

The task was whether the returned tag matched the receipt.

It did.

The crossed-out red line did not change the readable current label.

M.

Sixth tag.

Receipt 458.

Tag 458.

Receipt item: LAMP HOODS.

Tag item: LAMP HOOKS.

I read both again.

Hoods.

Hooks.

One letter.

Different goods.

X.

Antonius moved one brass slider on the green strip.

I looked up.

"What was that?"

"Nothing you need."

"Now I need it more."

"No."

I returned to the tags.

Seventh.

Receipt 461.

Tag 461.

Tag item: TWO-HOLE PLATES.

Receipt: TWO-HOLE PLATES.

M.

Eighth.

Receipt 463.

Tag 436.

Both said TWINE RINGS.

Easy X.

Too easy.

I marked it.

Ninth.

The broken-corner tag.

Receipt 468.

The tag number looked like 468.

Maybe.

The eight was stamped close to the broken edge.

Part of the lower loop remained.

Enough?

I turned the tag toward the window.

Antonius waited.

The item word was clear.

WICK CLIPS.

Receipt: WICK CLIPS.

I could reconstruct the eight because the receipt wanted an eight.

That was exactly what he had told me not to do.

I marked U.

Done.

Nine tags.

Five M.

Two X.

Two U.

I counted.

Then counted again because human beings had invented errors before they invented ledgers.

Five M.

Two X.

Two U.

Antonius looked at the page.

"Why sixth mismatch?"

"Hoods versus hooks."

He checked.

"Yes."

"Eighth is 463 versus 436."

"Yes."

He tapped the third.

"Unreadable."

"Last digit smeared."

"Ninth?"

"Enough of the eight is missing that I would be using the receipt to complete the tag."

Antonius nodded.

Then pointed at the fifth.

"Why match?"

"Current readable line says brown binding. Receipt says brown binding."

"The crossed line?"

"Red binding. Crossed out."

"Could indicate relabeling after return."

"It could indicate a clerk changed the label before lunch because red and brown were on adjacent shelves."

"Which happened?"

"I have no idea."

"Good."

He moved another brass slider.

I hated the strip now.

"What is that counting?"

"Finished checks."

"Mine?"

"Office."

"That is less interesting."

"Correct."

He gathered the first ledger but left the tags.

I looked toward the small sand glass on the shelf.

Less than half the sand had fallen.

"That's it?"

"That is the first pass."

Of course.

He opened the second ledger.

"Now copy your three categories into this return sheet."

"I thought the warehouse already copied the list."

"They copied receipt lines. You are adding comparison result."

"Why not mark the first ledger?"

"Because the first ledger stays intact."

Reasonable.

He turned the sheet toward me.

Nine lines.

I copied carefully.

Not fast.

My handwriting remained legible when I refused to race it.

Line six.

X.

Line eight.

X.

Line three and nine.

U.

The rest M.

I finished and checked each against my first marks.

Antonius had not spoken for several minutes.

This was one of the things I liked about him.

He could let silence be billable.

When I was done, he took the return sheet and compared it to the first ledger himself.

Not because he distrusted me personally.

Because paying for a check did not eliminate the need to check the check.

Also reasonable.

He stopped at line five.

"Brown binding."

"Yes."

He picked up the tag.

Turned it over.

On the back, faint pencil:

R TO B.

I had not looked at the back.

My stomach sank half an inch.

"Was I supposed to?"

Antonius said, "No."

I waited.

He looked at the note he had written for the task.

"Tag number and written item family. It does not say front only."

"That sounds like yes."

"It sounds like my instruction was incomplete."

That was not the answer I expected.

He turned the other tags over one by one.

Most were blank.

The eighth had a little warehouse mark.

The third had a thumbprint and nothing else.

The fifth alone had writing.

R TO B.

"What does it mean?" I asked.

"Unknown."

"Red to brown?"

"Possibly."

"Reassigned?"

"Possibly."

"Rewrapped?"

"Possibly."

"Reclassified?"

"Possibly."

I stared at him.

"You enjoy that word."

"It preserves money."

Fair.

"Does it change match?"

Antonius set the tag beside the line.

"The front tag currently matches the receipt. The back creates an unresolved note."

"So U?"

"No."

I frowned.

He tapped the three categories.

"Your task was not whether the return history is fully understood. It was whether receipt number and readable item family match."

"Then M."

"Yes."

"Back note separate."

"Yes."

He wrote a small line in the margin himself.

BACK NOTE: R TO B. MEANING NOT ESTABLISHED.

That was irritatingly familiar language.

I looked at him.

He did not look up.

"Hessa has infected commerce."

"Who?"

"Nobody."

He finished the note.

Then he pushed the tags back toward me.

"Turn all nine. Check only for additional writing. Do not reinterpret the comparison unless the additional writing changes the current readable item family or number."

I did.

Nine backs.

One warehouse stamp.

One number fragment that matched the front number but added nothing.

R TO B.

A charcoal line.

Nothing else.

No category changed.

That took six minutes.

The sand glass was still running.

Antonius gathered the tags and sheets.

"Done."

"Time remains."

"The task is done."

"I can sit competitively."

"I do not credit debt for decorative presence."

Harsh institution.

I leaned back.

My shoulders felt fine.

Hands fine.

No unusual residual-limb pressure.

The cart ride had reduced the day's crutch work enough that one seated hour barely registered physically.

Antonius opened a narrow receipt book.

"One copper credit."

He wrote the date.

My name.

TASK CREDIT: 1 COPPER.

No balance.

He signed it and tore off the small top slip.

I read it.

"Current balance?"

"In the account ledger."

"Not on receipt."

"No."

"Can I see it?"

"Yes."

I almost said yes.

Then stopped.

I knew I owed Vale.

I knew one copper had just moved against it.

The exact remaining total would matter if I was making a payment plan today.

I was not.

I folded the receipt and put it in my shoulder bag.

"Not today."

Antonius closed the book.

That was it.

No speech about responsibility.

No reminder about Bren.

No suspicious visitor.

No hidden offer.

Just one hour traded against old obligation.

I stood.

Right foot under me.

One hand on the table.

Crutches retrieved from the wall.

Antonius waited until I had both grips settled before speaking again.

"You asked for private work before."

I looked at him.

Had I?

Not today.

Apparently old Greg was not the only version of me leaving inconvenient records.

"Cash work," he clarified.

"Yes."

"Do you want it if I have something suitable?"

There it was.

My one copper upstairs became suddenly visible in my head.

So did the one bit beside it.

"Depends on the task."

"Obviously."

"And rate."

"Obviously."

"And whether it conflicts with debt labor."

"That is a real question."

Good.

I waited.

Antonius folded his hands on the table.

"If Vale offers you a cash task, it will be separate from debt credit. You may accept or refuse. I will not convert a debt-credit task into cash after the fact because you prefer the purse."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were thinking about asking."

"Hostile office."

"Do you want suitable cash work offered or not?"

"Yes."

"Fine."

"That is not a standing obligation."

"No."

"Not weekly."

"No."

"Not first refusal."

"No."

"Not exclusive."

Antonius looked tired now.

Good.

"Greg."

"Yes?"

"If I have a task, I will send terms. You can say no."

"Excellent."

"I regret asking."

"Also excellent."

He stood.

Meeting apparently over.

The cart was still covered because transport had been part of the one-hour task rather than charged by the minute.

I left with a debt-credit receipt and exactly the same private money I had arrived with.

This was less emotionally satisfying than being handed a coin.

That did not make it less real.

Annoying.

*

On the ride home, we passed the lower market.

Not the salvage edge.

A fruit line.

A woman had small yellow pears stacked in a shallow tray.

I considered buying one.

Then remembered I had no money on me.

Not poverty.

Planning failure.

Different problem.

The one copper and one bit were upstairs because I had not expected to need them at Vale.

I watched the pears pass.

No tragedy occurred.

At home, Cala was folding the gray scarf from yesterday.

Tavi had put the carrot back into ordinary food status. Progress.

Lyssa was not there yet.

Cala looked at me.

"Paid?"

"Debt."

"How much?"

"One copper credit."

She nodded.

"Money?"

"Still one copper and one bit upstairs."

Another nod.

No contradiction.

One number had moved in a ledger I did not carry.

Another had not moved at all.

I took the Vale receipt upstairs and put it under the existing narrow scrap rather than writing anything new.

The holdfast sat on the high shelf above it.

Two copper spent.

One copper credited against debt.

One copper and one bit still liquid.

These facts did not combine into a lesson.

They were just different directions money could go.

Lyssa came in later with a small paper twist of roasted seeds.

She ate three before I could ask where they came from.

Then she held the twist toward me.

I took some.

"Work?" she asked.

"Vale."

"Paid?"

"Debt."

She chewed.

"Still paid."

"Cala said the same thing without words."

"Cala is efficient."

I ate the seeds.

Salt.

Something bitter underneath.

Good.

"Antonius may send cash work if something suitable appears."

Lyssa looked at me.

"May."

"Correct."

She held the paper twist away when I reached again.

"Those cost money."

"I have money."

"Not with you."

Cruel accuracy.

I checked my pockets anyway.

Nothing.

She laughed and gave me three more seeds.

No invoice.

For now.
