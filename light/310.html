TEXT READER · CHAPTER 310

THE UNCUT LINE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Two mornings after the charge rain, Lyssa told me to move.

I looked at the table.

Then at my cup.

Then at her.

“I am using this.”

“I can see that.”

“That usually strengthens my claim.”

“It does not today.”

She already had the long side of the table cleared. My cup, charcoal, and folded narrow scrap had been pushed into a neat square at the far corner without disturbing any of it. A folded gray cloth covered the middle. Pins sat in a shallow dish beside her left hand. Chalk, thread, scissors, and a little wooden gauge were arranged along the right.

This was not a request developing.

It had developed before I woke up.

“Customer?” I asked.

Lyssa nodded.

“When?”

A knock sounded downstairs.

She looked toward the door.

“Apparently now.”

I moved.

There were faster ways to surrender territory, but most required two intact legs or less dignity. I got both crutches under me, planted my right foot, kept the left knee clear of the chair, and stood. Lyssa took the chair before I had finished turning.

“Efficient,” I said.

“You were slow.”

“I was majestic.”

“You were in the way.”

That was harder to argue with.

Cala was already out with Tavi. She had said something about onions, a broken basket handle, and a woman whose name I had missed because Tavi had been explaining why horses should be allowed on roofs. The apartment was otherwise quiet.

The knock came again.

Lyssa opened the door.

The woman outside was broad through the shoulders and dressed like someone who expected her clothes to lose arguments with walls. Dark trousers. Heavy shoes. Sleeves rolled above the wrist. Her hair was tied close at the back with a strip of red cloth. She carried a dark green work vest over one forearm.

Not a decorative vest.

Load vest.

I recognized the family before she stepped inside.

The old Greg had worn three or four kinds over the years. Caravan harnesses, casualty rigs, porter gear, climbing loads, two military versions that had been designed by people who apparently hated ribs. Different makers solved the same problem differently. Spread weight. Keep a load from turning the wearer. Give shoulders, back, and hips more places to share the work.

This one had two narrow glass-blue lines sewn from the upper back down toward the side panels, with flat brass beads set near the lower ends.

Common enough.

Not my work.

The woman held the vest up.

“It pulls here now.”

She touched the left side under the arm.

Lyssa did not take the vest immediately.

“Put it on.”

The woman looked at her.

“I brought it because it does not fit.”

“Yes.”

A pause.

Then the woman put it on.

I stayed by the wall.

Lyssa glanced at me once.

It was not a warning.

It was the thing before a warning, which was cheaper.

I took the hint and sat on the low bench near the window. My crutches went beside me, tips clear of the walking space.

The vest fit badly even before the load line woke.

The left side panel rode higher than the right. The arm opening bit into the woman's shirt. Across the back, the blue lines sat slack and dull.

Lyssa walked around her once.

“Who changed this?”

“Jari.”

“When?”

“Three weeks.”

“Before this shirt?”

The woman looked down at herself.

“Yes.”

Lyssa pinched the cloth under the left arm.

“Thicker.”

“It is one shirt.”

“It is cloth.”

“That is what shirts are.”

Lyssa looked at her.

The woman sighed.

“Fine.”

I liked her.

Lyssa tugged lightly at the side seam, then at the lower edge.

“Load it.”

The woman had brought that too.

A short leather strap was folded through one vest loop. She reached into the hall and dragged in a squat canvas sack with a wooden handle sewn across the top. It landed with a dense thump.

Sand, maybe.

Maybe metal.

Not my sand.

She clipped the strap into the vest.

The glass-blue lines brightened.

Not much. A thin color under the surface, like light trapped in deep water. The flat brass beads warmed from brown to gold. The vest tightened across the woman's back and settled downward.

The left side pulled hard.

There it was.

The upper edge pressed into her shirt under the arm while the right side stayed flat.

Lyssa crouched enough to sight along the lower seam.

“Off.”

The woman unhooked the sack.

The blue lines went dull again.

“I told you.”

“You told me it pulled.”

“It pulled.”

“Now I know where.”

That ended the exchange as far as Lyssa was concerned.

The woman was not finished.

“Can you move the blue?”

“No.”

The answer came before the last word had fully left the woman's mouth.

She frowned.

“You did not look.”

“I looked.”

“You looked at the cloth.”

“Yes.”

“The blue is why it pulls.”

“Maybe.”

I looked at Lyssa.

That was almost Hessa's word.

Almost.

Lyssa's version had less interest in proving anything and more interest in getting through the morning.

The woman tugged the vest off and laid it on the table.

“If the line sits lower, it stops biting.”

“Maybe.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because I am not cutting it.”

The woman put one finger on the glass-blue seam.

“I will pay another bit.”

My attention betrayed me.

Not visibly, I hoped.

Probably visibly.

One bit was sitting upstairs beside one copper.

One bit had just appeared on the table in sentence form and been refused before it became metal.

Lyssa did not even look at me.

“No.”

The woman stared at her.

“You do charm seam.”

“I do cloth around charm seam.”

“You fixed the red carry coat.”

“Cloth.”

“The line was in it.”

“And I left it there.”

The woman pushed the vest toward her.

“Then leave this one and make it stop pulling.”

“That I can try.”

“How much?”

Lyssa named a price.

One bit.

The same extra bit the woman had just offered for moving the blue.

My brain immediately did the arithmetic even though there was no arithmetic to do.

One bit for cloth.

Another bit available for blue.

Two bits sitting inside one problem.

Lyssa was choosing one.

The woman drummed two fingers on the table.

“Today?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Tonight.”

“Tomorrow.”

“I lift at first bell.”

“Then pick it up before first bell.”

“You will be awake?”

Lyssa looked at her.

The woman smiled.

“Tomorrow.”

Good.

Not everybody needed to win a negotiation. Sometimes the person with the scissors just waited.

Lyssa had the woman put the vest back on without load. She opened the left side with her fingers, found the current seam, then marked two short chalk lines below the arm and another near the lower edge.

“Here and here. I can give you more cloth without touching the blue.”

“How much?”

Lyssa held up two fingers.

“About this.”

“That enough?”

“I do not know until I baste it.”

The woman glanced at me then.

Not because I had moved.

Because I was looking at the vest.

“You know these?”

Lyssa said, “No.”

The woman looked at her.

I looked at her.

Lyssa kept marking chalk.

“I asked him.”

“I heard you.”

“I do,” I said.

Lyssa's chalk stopped.

That was the warning.

I should have listened.

I had, in fact, worn load lines. I had trusted them with wounded people, supplies, ugly climbs, worse descents, and one dead boar that had become everybody's problem because somebody had insisted we could carry it whole.

The first life had contained too many people to preserve exclusive names.

The woman turned a little toward me.

“This type?”

“Broadly.”

Lyssa resumed chalking.

The woman touched the lower brass bead.

“So if this moves down?”

“It might shift the pull.”

“Might?”

“Different lines set differently.”

Lyssa said, “Greg.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

The woman looked between us.

Interesting for her.

Less interesting for me.

I kept going because intelligence had never prevented me from making a bad conversational choice.

“If the cloth was taken in after the line was set, the anchor may be fine. The new shirt changes the route too. Moving the line could solve one pull and make another.”

The woman nodded slowly.

Lyssa put the chalk down.

“Finished?”

I looked at her.

“No.”

“Be finished.”

That did it.

I leaned back on the bench.

The woman grinned.

“You two always like this?”

“No,” Lyssa said.

“Yes,” I said.

Lyssa picked up the vest.

“Off.”

The woman took it off.

She was still smiling.

I was not.

Lyssa laid the vest flat and smoothed the green cloth with both hands. She did not touch the blue seam except to move the cloth beneath it. Her fingers stayed on what she actually intended to alter.

That irritated me more than it should have.

Because she was right?

Not entirely.

Because she had cut me off in front of a stranger?

Closer.

Because the extra bit still existed in my head?

Unfortunately.

The woman took a small coin from her pocket and set it on the table.

One bit.

Deposit or full price, I did not know.

Lyssa covered it with the pin dish while she worked.

That irritated me too.

Not because she was hiding it.

Because she was not thinking about it.

I was.

The woman stepped back into the hall.

“Tomorrow before first.”

“Yes.”

“If it still pulls?”

“Then you take it to someone who works blue.”

“And pay again.”

“Yes.”

“You are cruel.”

“Tomorrow.”

The woman left.

Lyssa shut the door.

For several seconds, the room was quiet.

I could have made it longer.

I did not.

“You could have charged two.”

Lyssa kept her back to me.

There are sentences a person hears after speaking them.

That was one.

She lifted the pin dish, took the bit from beneath it, and set the coin in a small wooden box on the shelf behind the worktable. The box already contained other things that were none of my business.

Then she turned.

“For what?”

“The line.”

“I am not doing the line.”

“You know enough to work around it.”

“Yes.”

“You knew what I was saying.”

“I knew what you were saying in front of my customer.”

There it was.

Not money.

Not first.

I shifted on the bench.

“She asked me.”

“And I said no.”

“She did not ask you.”

“She was in my room. With my work. At my table.”

“Our table.”

Lyssa stared at me.

I made a small correction.

“Currently your table.”

“Better.”

Not resolved.

The room stayed tight.

I looked at the vest.

The blue line had gone completely dull.

“You could have let me look.”

“You were looking.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“And I did not want you joining the fitting.”

I felt the answer in the part of me that still believed usefulness was a door that should open if I had the key.

“I know load gear.”

“I know.”

That stopped me for half a second.

“You know?”

“You have told enough stories.”

“I do not tell that many.”

Lyssa gave me a look.

Fine.

“I know load gear,” I repeated.

“You know wearing it.”

“I know more than wearing it.”

“Maybe.”

That word again.

Now it sounded hostile.

I stood.

Not because standing improved arguments.

It did not.

I needed to change position.

I got my right foot under me, set both crutches, and came up. The motion was clean. The room was familiar. Lyssa waited until I had settled before speaking again, which somehow made me more annoyed.

“You know enough to say maybe,” she said. “You do not know enough for my customer to come back when the blue breaks.”

“It was not going to break because I looked at it.”

“You were not only looking.”

“I gave her an answer.”

“You gave her three answers.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is when she starts asking what to pay you.”

“She did not.”

“She would.”

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

Lyssa picked up the vest and turned it inside out.

The construction became clearer.

The glass-blue line did not run through the side seam at all. It passed behind a reinforcing panel, then ended in a stitched pocket holding the lower brass bead. Jari's alteration had narrowed the ordinary cloth just beside that pocket.

I could see why Lyssa wanted to open the cloth instead of touching the line.

I could also see three possible ways to move the anchor.

Possible.

That was the problem with competence. It produced possibilities faster than permission.

“She offered another bit,” I said.

Lyssa looked up sharply.

There.

The real thing.

Not my proudest discovery.

“Ah,” she said.

I hated the sound.

“What?”

“You heard the bit.”

“I heard all of it.”

“You heard that part loud.”

“I have one copper and one bit.”

“I know.”

“I spent two copper on a tool that may or may not pay back. Vale gave me debt credit, not cash. Yes. I notice money.”

Lyssa's expression changed a little.

Not softer.

Less confused.

“Then notice mine is not yours.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

That landed harder than the earlier one.

I leaned more weight into the crutches than I needed to.

Not pain.

Just irritation looking for somewhere physical to live.

“I did not ask for her coin.”

“You asked why I did not take it.”

“That is different.”

“Yes.”

She sat.

That left me standing over the table like an idiot with equipment.

I moved to the other chair and sat too.

Lyssa picked up a length of ordinary dark thread.

“The blue fails under a load, who comes back?”

“You.”

“Yes.”

“If the cloth fails, who comes back?”

“Me.”

“So?”

“So I know cloth.”

“You know some of the blue.”

“I know enough not to cut it.”

The simplicity annoyed me because I had wanted the problem to be more interesting.

“What if one extra bit is enough for the risk?”

Lyssa threaded the needle.

“For you?”

“For the job.”

“No.”

“You did not think.”

“I did.”

“When?”

“Before I said no.”

I almost laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because she had managed to insult my preferred method of existence without raising her voice.

Lyssa pressed the needle through the first chalk-marked fold.

“I do not want every job,” she said.

That was the sentence I had not expected.

I looked at her.

She did not look back.

“I have work.”

“I know.”

“I have too much some days.”

“I know.”

“I have people who come back because I did what I said I would do.”

“I know.”

She pulled the thread through.

“If I start cutting lines because somebody waves one more bit, then I have line work.”

“You could charge more.”

“I do not want line work.”

I had no immediate answer.

Not a good one.

The old Greg had lived in a world where a useful capability tended to become an obligation through repetition. Could climb that wall? Congratulations, now every wall was yours. Could break that ward? Wonderful, there were six more. Could carry the injured mage through a collapsing tunnel? Apparently that qualified you for logistics.

I knew that.

I had spent years complaining about it.

Current Greg had one copper and one bit.

Money changed the shape of principles.

Lyssa made three loose basting stitches and tied them off.

“You want to test this?” she asked.

I blinked.

“Me?”

She stared.

“The vest.”

Right.

“Not the blue.”

“I understood.”

“You looked excited.”

“I am offended by that.”

“You looked excited.”

She turned the vest right-side out.

The left side now had a temporary fold let out near the arm. Ugly. Functional.

“What do you need?”

“Lift the sack onto the chair.”

That was not a smart two-crutch carry.

I looked at the squat canvas sack by the door.

Then at her.

“Slide?”

“Yes.”

Better.

I hooked one crutch around the wooden handle, dragged the sack across the floor about two feet, then switched position and nudged it against the chair leg. Lyssa took the handle and lifted it the rest of the way onto the seat.

No heroic porter work.

Civilization survived.

She clipped the vest strap to the sack and pulled the vest up against the chair back as if the chair were a person with terrible shoulders.

The blue lines brightened.

The brass beads warmed.

The left side drew in.

Less.

Not gone.

Lyssa watched the cloth.

I watched the line.

We were looking at the same garment and doing different work.

“The arm gets more room,” I said.

“Yes.”

“The lower pull is still there.”

“Yes.”

She unclipped the sack.

The light faded.

I waited.

Lyssa adjusted one pin.

Then another.

She did not ask what I thought.

I did not tell her.

This was apparently growth.

It felt mostly like being quiet.

After a moment she said, “The cloth can give more lower.”

“Will that twist the pocket?”

“Maybe.”

I looked at her.

She looked back.

We both started laughing.

Not much.

Enough to ruin the argument's clean shape.

I hated when life did that.

She went back to the seam.

I sat there another minute, then reached for my crutches.

“Where are you going?”

“Anywhere I am not being accused of entering a fitting.”

“You are still in the fitting.”

“The customer left.”

“The vest stayed.”

“Cruel.”

“That was already established.”

I stood and went upstairs.

I did not slam anything.

There was nothing useful to slam.

The fixed holdfast sat wrapped on the high shelf above my clothes.

For one deeply stupid second, I looked at it and thought about the two copper.

Then I looked away.

The thing had not changed.

Neither had the price.

I sat on the edge of the bed, took the Vale receipt from beneath the narrow scrap, and looked at the line that said one copper credit.

Still not cash.

Also still real.

I put it back.

Downstairs, Lyssa's scissors opened and closed.

Once.

Twice.

Then thread pulled through cloth.

No blue light.

No disaster.

No extra bit.

I stayed upstairs long enough for my irritation to become boring.

That helped.

When I came down again, the vest was on the table with the left side basted open farther than before. The blue strip remained exactly where it had been.

Lyssa was working on something else.

Different cloth.

Different thread.

The green vest had become one job among several again.

I stopped beside it.

“Looks straighter.”

“The cloth does.”

Of course.

I touched nothing.

Lyssa did not look up.

After a moment she said, “You still think I should cut it?”

I considered lying.

“No.”

She glanced at me.

“I think I know what I would try.”

“That was not the question.”

“I know.”

She returned to the other cloth.

I stood there for another second.

Then I took my cup from the far corner of the table.

My cup.

That claim, apparently, had survived.

I carried it to the window one careful step at a time, one hand on a crutch, the other around the cup, and sat where I was not in the way.

Lyssa kept working.

The blue line stayed uncut.
