TEXT READER · CHAPTER 316

THE EAST SEAT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Sevren arrived with a seat and terrible timing.

I had one bit.

The seat cost one bit.

This was how money developed personality.

He found me outside the building just after breakfast, leaning against the wall while Cala shook a rug over the gallery rail.

Sevren lifted two fingers.

"Seat."

I looked at him.

"That is not enough information."

"East loop."

Better.

"How far?"

"Bell Ford."

That was farther than Three Wells.

Not dramatically farther in the way maps liked to pretend distance was a personality trait. Farther in the practical sense.

Two days out if the road behaved.

One night there.

Two days back if the road continued behaving.

Roads rarely respected optimism.

"Passenger?"

"Passenger."

"Work?"

"No."

"Freight?"

"Not yours."

"Signing?"

"No."

"Carrying?"

"No."

"Solving mysterious road problem that nobody mentions until we are twenty miles away?"

Sevren stared at me.

"You make travel sound exhausting."

"Experience."

Cala stopped shaking the rug.

"How much?"

Sevren said, "One bit."

There it was again.

My entire liquid non-copper fortune.

One copper upstairs.

One bit upstairs.

Two copper currently transformed into a wrapped bad tool on a high shelf.

Vale debt still existed.

Debt credit was not cash.

No client waited.

No guaranteed work waited.

The sensible answer was no.

I knew this immediately.

Then Sevren said, "Leaves tomorrow before first bell."

Damn.

"Why is there a seat?"

"Rear bench. Small freight this run. Driver already takes two riders to Three Wells. One gets off there. Seat empty after."

"Driver?"

"Not Ressa."

Good.

Not because I disliked Ressa.

Because the world containing more than one carter was reassuring.

"Who?"

"Daro Venn."

Name meant nothing to me.

"Trust him?"

"Enough to sleep."

That was a meaningful courier endorsement.

Cala hung the rug over the rail.

"Go."

I looked at her.

"You are supposed to protect me from financial decisions."

"No."

"This household has failed."

Sevren smiled.

"One bit includes road meals at Three Wells and Bell Ford relay. Not Carrow food. Bring water."

"Sleeping?"

"Relay floor first night if beds full. Bell Ford bunk included if space. Floor if not."

"Luxury."

"You asked."

Lyssa came through the door carrying a folded dark cloth.

Not green vest.

The universe remained restrained.

She looked from Sevren to me.

"Road?"

"Apparently my face has become readable."

Sevren said, "Bell Ford."

Lyssa looked at me.

Then at my crutches.

Then back at me.

"You want?"

That was the problem.

I did.

Not because Bell Ford contained destiny.

I had been to more impressive places.

First life had ruined me for impressive places.

Cities built into cliffs.

Cities floating badly.

A monastery on a glacier that moved three feet every summer and forced the monks to rebuild the stairs.

Bell Ford was a road town with a bridge.

I wanted to go because it was not Carrow.

That felt almost embarrassingly simple.

"Yes," I said.

Lyssa nodded.

"Then go."

Everyone was being unhelpful in the same direction.

I went upstairs.

The money sat where it had sat.

One copper.

One bit.

The holdfast sat wrapped above my clothes.

I looked at it.

"You are not invited."

It did nothing.

Good tool.

I took the bit.

Left the copper.

For a moment the empty space beside the copper bothered me more than the amount itself.

One coin looked lonely.

That was stupid.

Coins did not become safer in groups.

I went downstairs and put the bit in Sevren's palm.

"If I die on the road, I want this refunded."

"To who?"

"Excellent point."

He bit the bit.

"Why does everyone bite money?"

"Because you keep giving us suspicious money."

"That came from theatre."

"More suspicious."

Cala said, "Tomorrow."

Sevren nodded.

"East freight yard. First bell minus a little."

"That is not a time."

"You know what it means."

I did.

He left.

The seat became real because I had paid for it.

This produced immediate administrative consequences.

Water bottle.

Folded seat cloth from the Three Wells run.

Spare shirt.

One extra sock for the right foot.

A narrow strip of clean rag for the left residual limb if sweat or friction became annoying.

Small soap scrap.

Bread for the first morning.

Brown shoulder bag.

Charcoal.

Narrow scrap.

I considered the clean household sheets.

No.

Three remained three.

I considered the holdfast again.

Still no.

I did not know its safe load.

I did not know its surface limits.

I did not know its remaining life.

Taking it on a road trip because roads sometimes had problems would be exactly the kind of thinking that turned a speculative purchase into a personality disorder.

It stayed home.

Lyssa watched me pack.

"Too much?" I asked.

"No."

"You answered too quickly."

"You have one shirt."

"Spare shirt."

"Two shirts. Very dangerous."

"I knew it."

She took the shoulder bag from me and checked the strap seam.

Not the stay bead.

The seam.

"Fine."

"Professional opinion?"

"Bag opinion."

"Important boundary."

She looked at me.

The Ch310 argument lived there for half a second.

Not pain.

Not resolution.

Just history.

Then she handed the bag back.

"Bring it back."

"The bag?"

"You."

"Less replaceable?"

"More annoying."

Fair.

That evening I ate more than usual.

Road travel punished people who treated food like decoration.

I checked both crutch tips.

Sound.

Checked hand grips.

Sound.

Right shoe.

Sound.

Residual limb skin.

Fine.

No heroic preparation.

No speech.

I slept.

Before first bell, Carrow was gray and cold enough to make every doorway look judgmental.

Cala handed me bread.

Lyssa was awake but not pretending to enjoy it.

Tavi was not awake.

Civilization had limits.

I left.

The east freight yard had already begun making noise.

Wheels.

Harness buckles.

People swearing softly because louder swearing wasted breath.

Sevren stood beside a longer cart than Ressa's Three Wells run.

Four wheels.

Canvas-covered freight bed.

Rear passenger bench bolted across the back under a shallow canvas lip.

Two draft animals.

Not horses.

Broad-backed dun road striders with thick split hooves and short dark ears.

Common freight animals.

First life had taught me they were patient until they were not.

One looked at me.

I looked at it.

We agreed not to develop a relationship.

Daro Venn was narrow, bald, and built like someone had stretched a normal man upward without adding more material.

He pointed at my crutches.

"Under rear bench. Leather loop. Tips inward."

Good.

"Seat cloth?"

I held it up.

"Use it. Rear plank is honest."

"Meaning?"

"Hard."

I liked him.

The other two riders were already aboard.

A woman with a basket covered in netting.

A young man holding a wrapped bundle across both knees.

No introductions.

Fine.

I boarded from the rear step.

Right foot up.

One hand on frame.

Crutches passed to Sevren only after I was stable.

He slid them beneath the bench and secured the leather loop.

I put the folded cloth beneath the back of my left thigh where the plank edge would otherwise become a problem after an hour.

Bag against my right side.

Water tied to the frame.

Daro checked everything.

Not me.

Everything.

Then he climbed forward.

The cart moved.

Carrow rolled backward around us.

I had done this before.

That changed it.

The gate did not feel like an event.

The witness bead flashed green beside Daro's board mark.

The road opened.

Fields.

Ditches.

Morning fog sitting low over irrigation channels.

The woman with the netted basket got off at Three Wells.

The young man stayed.

Sevren completed one relay handoff, received a narrow brass-edged token, and returned before I finished my second piece of bread.

"Still coming?" he asked.

"I paid."

"Could run away."

"On crutches?"

"Slow dramatic escape."

"Daro would catch me."

From the front Daro said, "Wouldn't."

"See?"

"He'd leave you."

"Different threat."

We rolled east.

Past Three Wells the road narrowed.

Not dangerously.

Just enough that passing carts negotiated with wheels instead of law.

The irrigation stones became less frequent.

Low field walls appeared.

Then orchards.

Small silver tags hung from some branches.

Wind tags.

Pest tags.

Ripening marks.

Could have been several things.

I recognized families, not exact use.

Did not ask.

The young man with the bundle finally said, "First Bell Ford?"

I looked at him.

"Yes."

"Bridge sings."

"Most bridges complain."

"This one sings."

Sevren said, "Don't tell him."

The young man frowned.

"Why?"

"He likes finding things."

"I do not."

Sevren looked at me.

"You absolutely do."

I considered defending myself.

Did not.

The road climbed.

By midday my right thigh was working harder than it had in Carrow.

Not pain.

Cart vibration.

Bracing.

I changed position when Daro stopped to water the striders.

Got down.

Walked twenty careful yards.

Stretched the right hip.

Checked the left side.

Warm.

Fine.

No skin issue.

Back aboard.

Road again.

At dusk we reached a relay house smaller than Three Wells.

No town around it.

Just stable, kitchen, long sleeping room, and a stone post carrying a pale amber road light.

The light brightened as the sky darkened.

Ordinary.

Useful.

Nobody worshiped it.

Daro said, "Floor tonight."

I looked at the long room.

Twelve bed platforms.

All occupied.

Floor indeed.

Sevren found two folded reed mats from a stack.

"Luxury."

"You promised."

"I promised floor."

"And you have delivered."

Dinner was bean stew, hard bread, and something pickled that fought back.

Included.

I confirmed.

Sevren looked offended.

"You asked at Three Wells too."

"That is why I still have one copper."

He stopped.

"One?"

"One."

"What did you buy?"

"Bad tool."

"Of course."

"It may become a good tool later."

"That sounds worse."

I told him enough about the holdfast to be annoying without pretending I knew more than I did.

Fixed interval.

No manual release.

Three warning blinks.

Two copper.

Sevren stared at me over his bowl.

"You spent two copper on a thing everyone stopped wanting because it lets go by itself."

"Yes."

"And then spent your last bit to sit on Daro's hard cart."

"When you say it together, it sounds irresponsible."

"It was irresponsible separately too."

I ate stew.

"Good road, though."

Sevren looked toward the dark window.

"Yeah."

That was enough.

The sleeping room filled with breathing, snoring, boots being removed, and one man insisting his blanket had been stolen before discovering it beneath him.

I lay on the reed mat with my bag under my head.

Crutches against the wall within reach.

Left knee clear.

Right leg stretched.

Body tired.

Not angry.

Outside, the road light held steady.

Tomorrow we would reach Bell Ford.

No prophecy waited there.

No client.

No guild assignment.

No guaranteed monster.

Just a bridge, a road town, Sevren's work, and a seat I had chosen to buy.

For the first time since waking in this life, I had spent every bit I owned on going somewhere I did not need to go.

That should have frightened me more than it did.

Instead I listened to the relay house settle around us and thought about the young man's warning.

The bridge sings.

Fine.

Tomorrow I would hear whether it had anything worth saying.
