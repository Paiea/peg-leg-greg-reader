TEXT READER · CHAPTER 323

THE HELD WEIGHT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The slab stopped falling three inches above a man's hand.

Nobody screamed until after.

That was probably why he kept the hand.

I had been halfway down Kiln Street, thinking about money in the vague and annoying way people think about a tooth they have decided not to touch, when six stoneworkers began moving a lintel across the road.

The lintel was gray, long, and large enough that ordinary men should have needed rollers, a cart, or a religious experience.

They had something better.

Maybe.

Two workers carried a black iron frame between them. It looked like a narrow doorway with no wall around it, shoulder high, crossbar thick, four short chains hanging from the lower corners.

The lintel hung beneath those chains.

Not lightly.

That distinction mattered.

The men still leaned into the load. Boots scraped. Shoulders worked. A third worker walked backward ahead of them with both hands raised, guiding the stone through the crowd.

But the lintel moved as if it weighed a quarter of what it should.

Maybe a third.

Enough less that six people could manage it.

Enough more that they were not pretending.

I stopped near a shuttered dye shop and gave them room.

Kiln Street narrowed at the bend. Everyone else gave them room too.

Carrow understood large stone.

The guide called, "Left corner. Left. Left. Hold."

The front pair adjusted.

One of the short chains clicked.

The sound was wrong.

I did not know the machine.

I knew that.

I also knew the difference between a working click and a load-bearing object asking to become a story people told later.

The guide heard it too.

His head snapped toward the left chain.

"Hold."

Everyone stopped.

The lintel dipped.

Not much.

An inch, maybe.

The black frame shuddered.

A brass disk at the top left corner went from dull yellow to white.

The worker under that side said, "That's not hold."

"Nobody move," the guide said.

Good advice.

Someone behind me tried to squeeze past anyway.

I put one crutch sideways across the gap.

"No."

He looked at me.

"I'm going around."

"Go around farther."

"I can fit."

The lintel dropped another inch.

He went around farther.

Also good advice.

The front-left worker had both hands on the frame. His right hand was near a small red lever fixed beneath the bright brass disk.

The guide saw it.

"Don't touch red."

The worker froze.

"Wasn't."

"You were thinking about it."

"I think about lots of things."

"Think quieter."

The rear pair laughed once.

Too sharp.

Fear laughter.

I knew that one.

The guide moved closer without stepping under the lintel.

He was a square man in a sleeveless leather work coat, dark hair cut nearly to the scalp, both forearms marked with old pale burns.

He stared at the white brass disk.

"How long white?"

The front-left worker said, "Just now."

"Before the click?"

"After."

"Sure?"

"No."

Excellent.

Nothing improved an equipment failure like uncertain chronology.

The guide looked toward a woman standing at the rear chain.

"Mera."

She shook her head before he finished.

"Don't ask me to bleed it."

"I wasn't."

"You looked at me in a bleeding-it way."

"Can you read the return?"

"From here? No."

That word mattered.

Return.

The same family of word I had been trying and failing to remember yesterday.

Not proof of anything.

A city contained more than one machine with a return.

Still.

I stopped thinking about fourteen copper.

The frame clicked again.

This time two corners answered.

Left front.

Right rear.

The lintel twisted.

The man at the front right moved automatically to catch the dropping stone.

I said, "Hands out."

So did the guide.

His version was louder.

"HANDS OUT."

The man jerked back.

The lintel fell.

Three inches.

Then stopped.

His hand had been under the edge a heartbeat earlier.

Nobody screamed until after.

The man stared at his fingers.

Then at the stone.

Then said, "Fuck."

That seemed correct.

The guide took one slow breath.

"Everyone still."

The brass disk stayed white.

The frame had not lowered with the lintel.

The chains had lengthened.

No.

Not lengthened.

They looked the same.

The stone hung lower because the left-front corner of the frame had rotated slightly inside its joint.

A load joint.

Maybe.

I knew better than to name things from across a street because they resembled things I had once broken.

The guide looked at the crowd.

"Need a wedge kit. Tan box. East yard."

One of the rear workers said, "I'll go."

"No. You're on the frame."

"We're all on the frame."

"Exactly."

He scanned again.

His eyes passed over me.

Came back.

Not because I was special.

Because I had already blocked the idiot from walking under the machine and I had two crutches, which made me visually memorable in a street full of people pretending not to stare.

"You mobile?"

I looked down at myself.

"Controversial question."

"Can you get to East yard?"

"Which East yard?"

"Kiln guild. Blue lintel over the gate. Two streets."

"Yes."

"Tell Jessa: Tan wedge box. South lift. White corner. Don't send a child."

"Why not a child?"

"Because they'll send the wrong tan box."

Fair.

I turned.

"Wait," Mera said.

I stopped.

She pointed at my bag.

"Charcoal?"

"Yes."

"Write white corner."

I pulled out the narrow scrap.

"On this?"

"Anywhere you won't improve the message."

I liked her immediately.

I wrote on the bottom edge beneath yesterday's notes:

TAN WEDGE BOX

SOUTH LIFT

WHITE CORNER

Then I left.

Two streets was not far.

It was farther when half of Kiln Street had decided to watch the almost-crushed-hand show instead of moving.

I took the outside edge, used a cart rut where the stones were flatter, and moved fast enough to make my shoulders complain without doing anything stupid.

The blue lintel was impossible to miss.

The East yard had a gate wide enough for stone carts and a painted board beside it:

KILN MASONS' COOPERATIVE

DELIVERIES EAST

PUBLIC COUNTER NORTH

I went east.

A woman behind a high table looked up.

"Public counter north."

"Tan wedge box. South lift. White corner."

She was already moving before I finished.

Good message.

"Who sent?"

"Square man. Burned forearms. Very committed to nobody touching red."

"Davin."

She shouted through a side door.

"Jessa! South lift went white. Tan wedges."

A voice answered, "Which tan?"

The woman looked at me.

I said, "He specifically said not to send a child because they'd send the wrong tan box."

She closed her eyes.

"Useful."

"I thought so."

Then she shouted, "FIELD TAN. SOUTH LIFT."

"That's different!" the voice yelled back.

"I KNOW."

A young woman came through the side door carrying a rectangular wooden case nearly as long as my thigh. Tan paint. Black corners. Two leather handles.

She took one look at me and handed me nothing.

Also good.

Instead she said, "Show me."

We went back together.

She moved quickly enough that I did not try to match her stride. I knew where she was going. She knew I had crutches. We could both survive twenty seconds without turning it into a moral lesson.

When we reached Kiln Street, nobody had moved.

The stone still hung three inches lower at the bad corner.

The white disk had dimmed slightly.

Davin saw Jessa and said, "About time."

Jessa said, "Fall on yourself."

Not warm.

Possibly affectionate.

Work relationships were difficult to classify from one sentence.

She set the tan case outside the drop line and opened it.

Inside were six black wedges, two hooked rods, a short mallet, and three flat pieces of pale stone with brass threads through them.

Not ordinary wedges.

Of course.

Jessa looked at the frame.

"Which corner first?"

"White."

"It isn't white now."

"It was."

"How long?"

"Long enough."

"Excellent measurement."

Davin pointed at Mera.

"She saw the return."

Mera said, "I said I couldn't read the return from there."

"Same difference."

"Those are opposite sentences."

Jessa ignored them both.

She crouched near the front-left leg of the frame without entering beneath the lintel.

One of the pale threaded stones came out of the box.

She pressed it against the black metal upright.

Nothing happened.

Then she moved it six inches higher.

The brass thread darkened.

She stopped.

Moved it lower.

Bright again.

Higher.

Dark.

"Load is sitting above the lower seat," she said.

Davin's face tightened.

"How much?"

"Enough that red would be stupid."

The front-left worker removed his hand from the red lever even though it had already been nowhere near it.

I respected that.

Jessa took a black wedge.

"Front right first. Then rear left. We take the live corner last."

Davin nodded.

The crew moved with irritating slowness.

Which was correct.

A wedge went between the lower frame foot and the street stone on the front-right corner. The hooked rod turned something I could not see. The frame clicked.

Nobody flinched this time.

Second wedge.

Rear left.

Another click.

The lintel did not move.

Jessa checked the pale stone again.

"Better."

Davin said, "Live corner."

"Not yet."

"Why?"

"Because I said not yet."

"Good technical explanation."

"You want the long one?"

Davin stopped talking.

I leaned on both crutches and watched.

This was not my machine.

That was becoming one of the more useful sentences in my second life.

I recognized pieces.

Not enough.

The pale stone was some kind of load reader, probably.

The wedges were doing more than holding the frame physically. The threaded brass in them responded when Jessa seated them. The black frame's weight changed subtly each time, not lighter exactly, but less eager to move.

I had used things like this.

Maybe.

Not this.

I remembered a siege engineer named Corl dropping a gate beam onto his own boot because he had insisted the stabilizers were "mostly mechanical."

They had not been.

His boot had become mostly flat.

That memory offered no useful specifications.

It did strongly support listening to Jessa.

She checked the live corner a third time.

"Now."

Davin shifted the crew.

"On my count. No lift. We are only taking slack."

The six workers adjusted hands and shoulders.

Jessa set the third wedge.

"One."

The hooked rod turned.

"Two."

The frame groaned.

"Three."

The brass disk flashed white.

Everyone froze.

The red lever jumped downward on its own.

Half an inch.

Jessa hit it sideways with the mallet before it could drop farther.

The lever snapped back up.

The lintel slammed down onto the three wedges.

Not far.

Enough.

The street shook under my right foot.

Someone screamed again.

This time because screaming was available.

Then silence.

The lintel stayed.

The black frame above it went slack.

All four brass disks darkened.

Jessa sat down directly on the street.

Davin said, "Good."

She looked up at him.

"I will kill you."

"After lunch?"

"Before."

"Scheduling problem."

The front-right worker started laughing.

Then Mera did.

Then everybody except Jessa.

Jessa eventually did too, which made it less concerning.

The crowd began breathing again.

Davin looked at the stone.

"Nobody moves the lintel until second frame gets here."

One worker said, "We can roller it."

"No."

"It's seated."

"No."

"We have six."

"Then six of you can stand here and admire it."

That ended that.

Jessa closed the tan case.

I had not touched anything.

This was also correct.

Davin looked at me.

"You ran clean."

"I moved aggressively. Running remains aspirational."

He glanced at my crutches.

"Fair."

Then he reached into a belt pouch.

I said, "No."

His hand stopped.

"No what?"

"If you're about to pay me for carrying six words two streets, no."

"It was seven."

"Then absolutely not."

He took out a small metal token instead of a coin.

Square. Black. Hole through one corner.

"Wasn't paying."

"Good."

"Yard chit."

I looked at it.

"Why?"

"Because you didn't improve the message."

Jessa, still sitting on the street, said, "Rare skill."

Davin held the chit out.

"Gets you through East gate if you need the public counter after deliveries start. Doesn't get you tools. Doesn't get you work. Doesn't get you into stores. Don't make it interesting."

I took it.

"You make a very compelling gift."

"Give it back."

I put it in my bag.

"Too late."

He looked mildly disappointed.

Good.

The worker who had nearly lost his hand was still staring at it.

Mera slapped his shoulder.

"Buy gloves."

"Gloves wouldn't help."

"Then buy better instincts."

"Where?"

"Public counter north."

I laughed.

Jessa stood and brushed stone grit from her trousers.

I nodded toward the black frame.

"What is it?"

Davin answered first.

"South lift."

"I gathered that."

Jessa said, "Old counterload frame. Cooperative owns two. This one is the worse one."

"Why use the worse one?"

"The good one is lifting a roof beam."

Reasonable.

I pointed with my chin toward the now-dark brass disk.

"White means?"

Jessa looked at me for a second.

"Depends how it gets white."

Good answer.

"Today?"

"Today it meant one seat stopped giving the frame somewhere safe to put what it was holding."

That was more specific than I expected.

"Holding weight?"

"Holding load."

"Difference?"

"Yes."

She picked up the tan case.

I waited.

She did not elaborate.

Also good.

Davin said, "Don't teach strangers the lift."

Jessa said, "I didn't."

"He's got the face."

I said, "What face?"

"The one where you think three questions becomes competence."

I considered objecting.

Then remembered the gate in my first life.

The wall beside it opening.

Teren calling us animals.

"Unfair," I said.

Davin nodded.

"Means I'm right."

He turned back to his crew.

The conversation was over.

That was fine.

I left before I became useful again.

The square black yard chit knocked lightly against the wrapped charcoal inside my bag.

Not money.

Not work.

Not an artifact.

Access.

A very small kind.

Yesterday fourteen copper had bought a dead mason's old field brace.

Today a cooperative-owned counterload frame had nearly eaten a man's hand while moving one lintel.

Both objects were expensive in ways I did not know how to price.

That was the part my old life had made easy to forget.

By fifty-nine, I had lived around institutions rich enough to own the strange things.

Guilds.

Companies.

Military stores.

City crews.

Adventuring houses with locked equipment rooms and quartermasters who could explain, at length, why whatever I wanted was not mine.

I had eventually owned expensive things personally.

Before that, I had used expensive things other people owned.

Those were different economic lives.

Current me had been thinking too much like every useful object had to become mine before it could matter.

That was stupid.

Not because ownership was bad.

Ownership was excellent.

I remained strongly in favor of it.

But a fourteen-copper brace and a cooperative lift frame were teaching adjacent lessons.

Reserve mattered.

Access mattered too.

So did knowing which person to listen to when a machine decided to become dangerous.

I reached the next corner and stopped under an awning to rest my hands.

The narrow scrap was still out.

At the bottom, beneath MARA QUILL and the brace notes, I added:

KILN EAST YARD

BLACK CHIT

COUNTERLOAD FRAME

WHITE != ONE THING

JESSA: LOAD NOT WEIGHT

Then I stared at that last line.

Load not weight.

I knew there was a technical distinction hiding in there.

I had probably known it once.

Maybe I still did somewhere inconvenient.

I did not dig for it.

There were enough people in Carrow who knew things now.

That was another resource I had spent too much of my first life pretending did not count.

I put the scrap away.

The yard chit went into the small inner fold of the shoulder bag where it would not fall out.

One copper still waited upstairs.

Fourteen copper still felt far.

The distance had changed shape again.

Not shorter.

More routes through it.

I liked that better.
