TEXT READER · CHAPTER 325

THE NIGHT LINE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The job paid two copper because nobody wanted to stand in the square after dark and tell strangers where not to stand.

I understood immediately.

That was exactly the kind of work strangers made difficult.

The notice was nailed to the side of a public board near the lower market, under three missing-cat notices and one offer to sharpen six knives for the price of five if all six belonged to the same person.

NIGHT LINE HANDS WANTED.

THIRD BELL TO LATE CHIME.

WEST GLASS SQUARE.

TWO COPPER.

KEEP PUBLIC OUTSIDE MARKED LINE.

NO LIFTING REQUIRED.

REPORT TO SEL VARRIN.

I read it twice.

Then a third time because I had learned recently that prices improved when I actually read the rest of the words attached to them.

Two copper.

No lifting.

Night.

Crowd work.

Marked line.

That sounded less like labor and more like being blamed by people who wanted to stand six inches closer to something dangerous.

I had done worse for free.

A second notice beneath it offered one bit for unloading sacks before sunset.

Closer.

Shorter.

Probably easier.

I stood between them long enough that a woman beside me said, "You taking the sacks?"

"No."

"Good."

She tore the sack notice from the board.

Apparently my economic hesitation had competition.

I took the night notice down.

Not because two copper solved anything.

It did not.

One copper upstairs plus two earned would make three.

Three was not fourteen.

Three was also not one.

More importantly, it was the first time since Mara's shop that I had looked at ordinary work and asked not only whether I could do it, but what it did to the space around my next decision.

That sounded pretentious.

So I stopped thinking it.

West Glass Square sat farther north than I usually wandered without a reason. I had passed the edge once with Lyssa months ago, but never crossed the center.

The name came from six tall windows set into the guildhall on the west side, all made from old green-tinted glass that turned the late light strange.

By third bell the square was already half closed.

Chalk marks made a wide rectangle around the center paving. Short poles stood at each corner. A thin cord ran between them at knee height.

Inside the rectangle, four workers were assembling something that looked like a chandelier designed by a person who hated ceilings.

A bronze central mast rose from a three-legged base. Six curved arms extended outward. Each arm ended in a shallow glass bowl the size of a wash basin.

The bowls were empty.

For now.

A man with a red scarf stood near a folding table checking names against a paper.

"Night line?" he asked when I approached.

I held up the notice.

"Unless I misunderstood the concept dramatically."

He looked at my crutches.

Then the notice.

Then me.

"No lifting."

"I noticed."

"Can you stand until late chime?"

"With breaks."

"Can you walk the line?"

"Yes."

"Can you shout?"

"Professionally."

He almost smiled.

"Sel Varrin."

"Greg."

He made a mark on the paper.

"North side. First hour. Rotate west after fourth bell. Sit if you need to, but keep eyes up. If anyone crosses the cord, put them back outside. Don't touch the mast. Don't touch the bowls. Don't touch anything the glassworkers carry."

"What if something catches fire?"

"Tell me."

"What if you're on fire?"

"Tell someone else."

Reasonable.

I looked toward the bronze structure.

"What is it?"

"Public light trial."

"Whose?"

"Not your problem."

Even better.

I liked jobs with boundaries.

A woman at the table handed me a flat strip of blue cloth.

"Tie that where people can see it."

I tied it around the strap of my shoulder bag.

She looked at it.

"Good enough."

The other line hands were already taking positions.

A thick man with a shaved head had the south side. Two younger women split east and west. I took north.

The cord was not magical.

I checked because it would have been embarrassing to spend the entire evening respecting a string for mystical reasons when ordinary rope was sufficient.

The first hour was easy.

Children tried to duck under it.

Adults pretended not to see it.

One old man stepped directly over the cord, looked at the bronze mast, and said, "I was here before the line."

"The line disagrees."

"I stood there yesterday."

"Congratulations."

"You can't tell me where to stand."

"I am being paid specifically to do that."

He stared at me.

Then at the blue cloth.

"By who?"

I pointed at Sel.

"Man with paper."

The old man looked at Sel.

Sel looked back.

The old man stepped outside the cord.

Two copper was already earning itself.

Near fourth bell, the glassworkers brought out the bowls.

Not the empty ones.

Six new bowls came from padded crates.

Each was clear except for a cloudy knot in the center, like someone had trapped a thumbprint inside the glass.

They seated one bowl onto each bronze arm.

No one applauded.

Good crowd.

A woman inside the line walked the full circle checking every bowl twice.

She wore thick leather sleeves and gray lenses over her eyes.

Sel moved toward her.

They spoke quietly.

Then he came to the line hands.

"When the trial starts, nobody crosses. Not even if they dropped something inside. Not even if they know someone. Not even if they say the city owes them money."

The shaved-head man asked, "Has that happened?"

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"All of them."

I respected cities.

Sel continued.

"If a bowl goes dark, shout dark and point. If one goes blue, shout blue and point. If one cracks, get people away from that side."

One of the women on east said, "How far?"

"To the paving seam."

There was a second seam about twelve feet beyond the cord.

Useful.

"If the mast drops?" I asked.

Sel looked at me.

"Run."

"Alternative?"

He glanced at my crutches.

"Move away urgently."

"Better."

The gray-lensed woman heard us and laughed.

Then the trial began.

No chant.

No dramatic countdown.

One worker at the base turned a brass key.

The cloudy knots inside all six bowls brightened.

White first.

Then pale gold.

The entire square changed.

Not brighter exactly.

Cleaner.

Shadows pulled inward. The green guildhall windows stopped throwing color across the paving. Faces looked sharper from farther away. Every line between stone blocks became suddenly obvious.

I had seen public light systems before.

Not this one.

Old Greg had slept under ward lamps, camp lamps, battle lamps, healer lamps, tunnel lamps, and one memorable lamp that attracted every biting insect within half a mile.

This was different mostly because I was standing still enough to notice it.

The crowd noticed too.

People pressed toward the cord.

There it was.

The actual job.

"Outside the line," I said.

A boy leaned over.

"But I can see better."

"You can see from there."

"Not the bottom."

"Then imagine the bottom."

His mother pulled him back by the collar.

The north crowd thickened faster than the other sides because the street behind me fed directly from market row.

I shifted along the cord.

Three people back.

One basket farther out.

A man with a cane tried to hook the cord down with it.

I put my crutch tip on the cord before he could.

"No."

"I wasn't crossing."

"You were improving the line."

"It blocks the view."

"That is one of its stronger qualities."

He gave me a look and moved left.

The light intensified.

The bowls did not glow much themselves. The air beneath each one did.

Six pale columns formed around the central mast, not beams exactly, more like clear places in the evening.

People became quieter.

That lasted almost twenty seconds.

Then someone on the west side shouted, "BLUE!"

Every head turned.

One bowl had changed.

Not fully blue.

The cloudy knot at its center had gone cobalt.

Sel pointed.

"West back to seam."

The west line hand started moving people.

The problem was everyone else wanted to move west too.

Of course they did.

The interesting thing had happened there.

My north crowd surged sideways along the cord.

A woman bumped my right crutch.

I caught myself.

"Back."

Nobody listened.

I had spent enough time backstage to know a crowd did not hear instructions when it thought the real event was somewhere else.

So I stopped telling them where not to go.

I pointed hard toward the east side of the mast.

"You can see the color change better from that angle. East side. Keep outside the seam."

Half the people nearest me turned the other direction.

Not because I knew that was true.

It was probably true enough.

More importantly, it split the flow.

The pressure on the north-west corner eased.

Sel saw it.

He did not praise me.

Excellent.

He just pointed two fingers east and repeated louder, "Better view east. Keep moving."

Now it became official.

People moved.

The west bowl stayed blue for maybe ten heartbeats.

Then returned to gold.

The gray-lensed woman checked something at the base.

Nobody died.

Strong trial so far.

At fourth bell we rotated.

I took west.

My hands were starting to ache.

I sat on a low stone post just outside the seam for a few minutes, keeping the cord in view.

A little girl stared at my left trouser leg.

Then at the crutches.

Then at the light.

"Would it grow back in that?"

Her father went rigid.

I looked at the glowing bowls.

"Probably not."

"Why?"

"Wrong kind of light."

"What kind grows legs?"

"Expensive."

Her father made a noise that might have been a cough.

She accepted this and returned to watching the mast.

I rested another minute.

Then stood.

The second hour was less crowded and more dangerous because people had decided they understood the rules.

That was always worse.

One man stepped over the cord while looking directly at me.

"I work glass."

"Not inside this line tonight."

"I know what they're doing."

"Then you know where the line is."

He stepped back.

Another woman tried to hand a wrapped parcel across to one of the workers.

"For Eren."

"After trial."

"It's his supper."

"Then he'll be hungry safely."

She looked offended.

I sympathized with Eren.

The bowls cycled three more times.

Gold.

Dim.

Gold again.

No blue.

Then the south bowl cracked.

The sound was tiny.

A wineglass sound.

Almost nothing.

The shaved-head line hand shouted before I located it.

"CRACK SOUTH!"

Good lungs.

Sel was already moving.

"South to seam. East corner clear. West hold."

The south crowd backed up badly.

People turned instead of stepping straight back.

Someone dropped a basket.

Apples rolled under the cord.

A child went after them.

Of course.

I was twenty feet away.

The nearest east line woman grabbed the child's coat before he crossed.

The bowl made another tiny sound.

A pale line appeared across the glass.

The light beneath it bent sideways.

Not a beam.

A distortion.

Everything behind it looked half an inch misplaced.

Sel shouted, "Paving seam. Now."

This time everyone listened.

The gray-lensed woman did not run toward the bowl.

She went to the base.

Good.

One worker turned the brass key.

Nothing happened.

The south bowl stayed lit.

Second turn.

Still lit.

The crowd made one collective noise.

Not panic yet.

Considering panic.

I knew that sound too.

The west side started drifting backward even though our bowl was fine.

That was acceptable.

Then two men behind me turned and pushed into people trying to leave north.

Bad geometry.

I pointed toward the west alley.

"West alley open. Use it. Don't cross north flow."

The first man ignored me.

The second followed my hand.

Then others did.

The flow split.

Again.

Stage blocking with worse actors.

The cracked bowl flickered.

The distortion jumped.

The gray-lensed woman pulled a red handle at the base.

All six bowls went dark at once.

The square vanished.

Not literally.

But after that much clean light, ordinary evening felt like someone had thrown cloth over my head.

People shouted.

I planted both crutches.

"Stay where you are. Eyes adjust."

That instruction worked because nobody could see enough to improvise.

For once darkness improved public behavior.

The guildhall windows slowly returned as green rectangles.

Street lamps became visible.

Faces reappeared.

The south bowl stayed intact.

Cracked, but intact.

The gray-lensed woman said something to Sel.

He nodded.

Then raised both hands.

"Trial over. Clear the square."

Someone booed.

Of course.

A magical light fixture had nearly failed in public and somebody wanted an encore.

I admired consistency.

Clearing the square took longer than the emergency.

People wanted explanations.

Sel gave none.

People wanted to know whether tomorrow's trial would still happen.

Sel said he did not know.

People wanted refunds.

There had been no admission fee.

A man demanded one anyway.

Eventually the square emptied.

The line hands gathered near the folding table.

My hands hurt enough that opening and closing my fingers felt useful.

The shaved-head man said, "You do this often?"

"Stand near things that go wrong?"

"Crowds."

"Theatre."

He nodded like that explained everything.

It did not.

Maybe enough.

Sel counted from a small lockbox.

Two copper each.

Actual copper.

He put mine on the table.

I looked at them.

Then picked them up.

No ceremony.

No moral lesson.

No sudden feeling that I had become financially responsible.

They were money.

That was useful enough.

Sel said, "You split the north surge well."

"I lied about the east view."

"Was it worse?"

"I don't know."

"Then you redirected."

"That is a generous verb."

"Want tomorrow if they run again?"

There it was.

Easy answer would have been yes.

Two copper again.

Three total would become five.

Reserve.

Choice.

Doors.

I looked at the cracked south bowl being lowered into a padded crate.

"Are they running again?"

"Don't know."

"Then ask me when you know."

Sel nodded.

No promise.

Good.

I put the two copper in the inner fold of my shoulder bag, separate from the black Kiln chit.

The bag suddenly felt more important than it had yesterday.

Not heavier.

Just more worth paying attention to.

On the walk home, I passed the public board again.

The sack notice was gone.

The knife-sharpening offer remained.

One new notice had appeared.

MORNING CART COUNT.

ONE BIT.

I read it.

Then kept walking.

Not because one bit was beneath me.

It was still money.

But tomorrow morning had other value too.

Sleep.

My hands.

The possibility that Sel might call if the trial ran again.

The possibility that something better might appear.

That was the part I had not understood when Mara said choice.

Reserve was not only the coins after a purchase.

It was the ability to say no before one.

At home, I went upstairs before anyone could ask why I smelled like square dust and public disappointment.

My original copper sat where I had left it.

I placed the two new ones beside it.

Three.

I looked at them for a while.

Not reverently.

Counting was allowed.

Three copper.

Zero bits.

Still nowhere near the brace.

Closer to a housing read.

Closer to a mark read without emptying myself.

Closer to buying something ordinary and still having money afterward.

Closer was dangerous because it encouraged arithmetic.

I took out the narrow scrap.

Under Mara's notes I wrote:

NIGHT LINE 2 C

WEST GLASS

CROWD MOVES TOWARD INTEREST

SPLIT FLOW > PUSH BACK

Then I stopped.

The last two lines looked like I was trying to turn one evening into doctrine.

I rubbed them partly out.

Better.

I wrote only:

3 C TOTAL

Then below it:

DON'T TAKE EVERY COIN JUST BECAUSE IT'S THERE

I stared at that.

Too much like advice.

I hated advice written by myself most of all.

I rubbed that out too.

The gray smear joined yesterday's gray smear.

My record was becoming more honest through abrasion.

I put the scrap away.

The two new copper stayed beside the old one.

Three was not enough.

Three was enough to make the next question different.
