TEXT READER · CHAPTER 331

THE MARKER FIELD

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Antonius paid three copper to spend half a day arguing with posts.

I accepted immediately.

Not because three copper was enormous.

Because three copper for half a day was better than one copper for two hours had been, and because the work involved leaving Carrow.

Both mattered.

He found me near the lower market just after first bell.

No note.

No messenger.

Antonius himself.

That was unusual enough that I stopped before he spoke.

He wore a brown coat with mud dried to one knee and carried a rolled survey sheet under one arm.

"Free today?"

"Depends."

"On?"

"Whether you're about to ask me to do something stupid."

"Moderately stupid."

"Pay?"

"Three copper."

"How stupid?"

"Outside city. Half day. Marking old boundary posts against a current line. Mostly walking and telling me when I am about to write down the wrong thing."

"Why me?"

"Because you notice when physical things disagree with paperwork."

That was flattering in a way I distrusted.

"Who else?"

"Me. Pell. Cart driver."

"Lifting?"

"No."

"Terrain?"

"Field road. Some ditch edge. One low slope."

"Return by?"

"Before dark unless the horse joins a religion."

Reasonable.

"Three copper cash?"

"Cash."

"No debt credit?"

"Cash."

"Done."

Antonius nodded.

No negotiation.

That bothered me for six seconds.

Then I remembered I had once missed his one-copper task because I was out of town.

Maybe the market had moved.

Maybe the task was worse.

Maybe he had simply decided my time was useful.

All acceptable possibilities.

I went home long enough to tell Cala where I was going, refill water, check both crutch tips, and move the oval case deeper into the shoulder bag so it would not knock around on the road.

The holdfast stayed home.

I looked at it once.

Field work.

Unknown old posts.

A magical fixed holdfast that had demonstrated adhesion to a wooden bench.

Tempting.

No.

Untested load.

Unknown surface behavior.

Unknown release reliability.

Carrying it because `field` sounded relevant was how collections became accidents.

Separate problems.

I left it.

Antonius waited beside a narrow two-wheel cart near South Gate.

Pell sat on the back rail with a slate on his knees.

I had met him once before.

Maybe twice.

Thin, pale beard, ears that seemed designed for hearing mistakes from far away.

He looked at my crutches.

Then at Antonius.

"He walking?"

I said, "Usually."

Pell ignored me.

Antonius said, "Yes."

"All of it?"

"No. Seat between stops if needed."

Pell grunted.

Good.

Not a personality problem yet.

The cart driver was a woman named Sera.

Not first-life Sera.

Carrow Sera.

Important distinction because memory enjoyed reusing names to confuse me.

She had a wide hat and no interest in conversation.

Strong start.

We left through South Gate.

The oval clicked once in the bag.

I did not inspect it.

The city wall fell behind us.

Fields began almost immediately.

Not endless farms.

Carrow's outside edge was messier than that.

Storage yards.

Kitchen plots.

Brick cuts.

Goat pens.

Then broader grain strips divided by drainage ditches and old stone lines.

The road stayed packed earth.

The cart wheels knew every rut personally.

I sat for the first stretch.

Not pride.

Math.

If the job paid for observation, wasting my shoulders before the observation began was stupid.

Pell unrolled the survey sheet.

"Current east boundary runs from canal tooth fourteen to hill stone three. Six registered intermediate markers."

I looked at the paper.

"Registered when?"

He glanced at me.

"Twelve years ago."

"Posts older?"

"Some."

"How much older?"

"Unknown."

Antonius said, "That is why we're going."

Good.

Pell tapped the sheet.

"We are not deciding ownership. We are matching physical markers to this line and noting condition."

"So if a post disagrees?"

"We note disagreement."

"Not fix it."

"Correct."

I liked jobs with nouns that stayed in their lane.

The first marker was boring.

Excellent.

A waist-high stone post beside a ditch, square top, one face carved with a shallow hook mark.

Pell checked the sheet.

Antonius measured from the canal tooth using a cord marked at intervals.

I stood near the post and looked at it.

"What am I checking?"

Antonius said, "Everything we might regret not noticing later."

Too broad.

Pell said, "Position relative to road and ditch. Visible mark. Damage. Whether base looks moved. Anything nearby that makes the current reading questionable."

Better.

I circled the post.

No obvious fresh disturbance.

Lichen on three sides.

One side cleaner.

"South face cleaner."

Pell looked.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning south face cleaner."

He looked at me.

I smiled.

He wrote it down.

Hessa had ruined me in useful ways.

Second marker leaned.

Not much.

Maybe five degrees.

The ground around it had sunk on the ditch side.

Antonius measured.

Pell read.

"Within line tolerance."

I crouched as far as practical.

The base stone had an old crack.

Mud filled most of it.

One edge did not.

"This moved recently?"

Pell asked.

"I don't know."

"Then why ask?"

"Because one crack edge is clean."

Antonius crouched beside it.

Touched nothing.

Looked.

"Could be washout."

"Could be."

Pell wrote:

BASE CRACK / EAST EDGE CLEAN / POSSIBLE RECENT WASH

No one solved it.

Good.

Third marker was missing.

That improved the day.

The sheet said there should be a narrow iron-capped post twenty-three marked cord lengths north of the ditch bend.

There was no post.

There was a goat.

The goat seemed confident.

Pell measured twice.

Antonius checked the road bend.

Sera stayed with the horse.

I looked at the goat.

"Registered?"

Pell said, "No."

"Shame."

The goat ate something.

Maybe evidence.

We searched without searching dramatically.

No digging.

No grid.

Just walking the expected area and looking for iron, disturbed stone, old socket, fallen post.

I moved slower than they did.

That helped.

Not mystical compensation.

Just pace.

My crutch tip caught against something hard under grass.

I stopped.

Not post.

A flat stone edge.

I moved the grass aside with the crutch shaft.

Square cut.

About hand-width across.

Pell came over.

"Socket?"

"Maybe."

Antonius cleared grass with his boot.

A shallow square depression sat in the stone slab, filled with soil.

No iron post.

No broken cap.

Pell checked distance.

"Twenty-two and three quarters."

Antonius said, "Close enough to note."

I looked toward the goat.

"Questionable suspect."

The goat ignored due process.

We noted socket, no post, no conclusion.

Then moved on.

The fourth marker was where the work changed.

The road climbed a low slope beside a stand of pale grass.

At the top sat two posts.

Not one.

One stone.

One wood.

Both marked.

Both old.

Both within ten feet of the expected line.

Pell swore quietly.

Antonius said, "There it is."

"You expected this?" I asked.

"I expected something."

"Useful."

"The parcel changed hands twice in six years. The filed line stayed the same. Two neighbors now disagree which post is controlling."

There was the real job.

Not ownership.

Not adjudication.

Physical record before somebody turned memory into fact.

Pell pointed at the stone post.

"Registered description says gray stone, waist high, split crown, two-cut face."

The stone post was gray.

Waist high.

Split crown.

Two-cut face.

Easy.

The wooden post was dark oak, chest high, iron collar near base, with the same two-cut mark burned into one side.

Less easy.

"Wood newer," I said.

Pell gave me a look.

"Probably."

"Not enough."

I moved closer.

The stone post's two-cut mark was weathered almost smooth.

The wooden one was darker in the cuts but not fresh.

Antonius measured from the third expected point.

"Stone is four feet west of filed line. Wood is one foot east."

Pell said, "Filed tolerance is two."

So stone visually matched description but sat too far west.

Wood sat within tolerance but did not match material description.

Excellent.

I hated it.

We spent the next hour proving how little we knew.

Stone base had lichen down to soil.

No obvious fresh move.

Wood base showed no recent tool marks.

Iron collar older than I expected.

Maybe not six years old.

Could have been ten.

Could have been twenty.

I was not dating iron from vibes.

Pell found a second burn mark on the back of the wood.

Not the same two cuts.

Three short lines under a half circle.

I knew that mark.

Not artifact mark.

Survey crew family.

Old Greg had hired line crews before.

Road work.

Camp boundaries.

Claims after monster clearing.

The half-circle over three lines meant temporary carry-forward marker.

I thought.

Maybe.

Memory was strong enough that I felt confident.

Which meant I slowed down.

"I recognize that mark family."

Pell looked immediately.

"From where?"

Bad question.

For me.

"Old work."

"What does it mean?"

"I think temporary continuation or carry-forward. Not permanent boundary."

Antonius said, "Think?"

"Yes."

"How sure?"

I looked again.

Three lines.

Half circle.

Not closed.

One line shorter.

First life memory: a surveyor cursing because a crew had left carry-forward stakes in place after the final stones were set.

A younger me asking why anyone cared if the final line was obvious.

The surveyor saying because twenty years later nothing was obvious.

Apparently he had been right.

Annoying dead people.

"Moderately sure on category. Not sure this exact mark means the same locally."

Pell stared at me.

"Locally?"

"Marks travel. Meanings don't always."

He looked at Antonius.

Antonius said, "Note recognition as unverified."

Pell wrote:

GREG REPORTS BACK MARK RESEMBLES OLD TEMPORARY/CARRY-FORWARD SURVEY FAMILY / LOCAL MEANING UNVERIFIED

Perfect.

No one promoted me to surveyor.

We kept looking.

The wooden post had an iron collar.

Not decorative.

Set low.

Two narrow holes opposite each other.

Pell said, "Tie points?"

Antonius shrugged.

I crouched.

The ground on the east side had a shallow groove leading away through grass.

Not recent.

Old depression.

Like something had been dragged repeatedly or a cord had cut soil under tension.

Maybe livestock.

Maybe survey line.

Maybe water.

I said, "There is a groove east."

Pell wrote it.

We followed the groove twenty feet.

It vanished near a low buried stone.

That stone had no visible mark.

Antonius measured.

It sat almost exactly on the filed line.

Now we had three physical things.

Excellent.

The day was becoming professional hatred.

Pell wiped his face.

"Could be anchor."

Antonius said, "Could be field stone."

I said, "Could be where somebody tied a goat."

The goat was no longer visible.

Suspicious.

Sera called from the cart, "You done making the land worse?"

"Not yet," Antonius called.

We ate standing near the road.

Bread.

Cheese.

Water.

Mine from home.

No one bought lunch.

The oval stayed quiet.

Good object.

Pell kept looking at the wooden post while eating.

"If that back mark is temporary, wood could've been a working post set before final stone."

"Could," I said.

"Then why is it closer to filed line?"

"Because temporary crew might have been closer than whoever set the stone."

"Or the stone moved."

"Yes."

"But it doesn't look moved."

"Now."

He frowned.

Antonius said, "This is why we record before arguing."

Good Antonius.

After lunch we did something better than speculation.

We searched for corroboration.

Not the whole field.

Specific things.

If the wooden post was a temporary carry-forward, there might have been another similar temporary marker farther along the same line family.

The fifth registered marker sat beyond the slope near a dry drainage cut.

We walked there.

Stone post.

Correct description.

Within tolerance.

No wooden duplicate.

But thirty feet behind it, near a hedge, stood a cut-off oak stub with an iron collar rusted almost through.

Pell stopped.

"Well."

I said nothing.

We cleared grass around the back face.

Three short lines under a half circle.

Same family.

Closer.

Maybe exact.

Pell looked at me.

"Temporary."

"Still not proven."

"Come on."

"Two examples make a pattern. They don't write the manual."

He looked deeply offended by caution.

Hessa would have enjoyed him.

Antonius measured positions.

The oak stub sat just east of filed line.

The final stone sat nearly on it.

That supported temporary working post later replaced by stone.

At marker four, the wooden post might have been the same thing, except final stone ended up west.

Why?

Moved stone.

Bad final placement.

Changed local reference.

Filed line error.

Later replacement pretending to be original.

Too many possibilities.

We recorded all of them as possibilities by recording none of them as conclusions.

Good work was often disappointingly grammatical.

Marker six was ordinary again.

I appreciated it more.

By then my shoulders were tired.

Not failing.

Tired.

Antonius noticed before I said anything.

"Cart to last check."

I nodded.

No pride problem.

I sat.

Pell climbed up beside me.

"You actually know those marks?"

"Some family of them."

"From survey work?"

"Adjacent."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I have made enough bad decisions around people with measuring cords to remember what they yelled about."

He laughed once.

Progress.

The final check was not a marker.

Antonius wanted a sightline from hill stone three back toward canal tooth fourteen to note whether the filed route crossed an old drainage berm.

It did.

Barely.

The berm curved.

The filed line cut the inside shoulder.

Pell said, "That could explain west stone."

Antonius said, "How?"

"Crew avoiding soft berm."

"Then why file straight?"

"Because filed line is straight."

"And physical marker isn't."

"Exactly."

I said, "That explains a reason. Not whether it happened."

Pell looked at me.

"You are irritating."

"Professionally."

Antonius smiled.

We finished before fourth bell.

No horse religion.

On the ride back, I looked at the fields differently.

Not romantically.

Lines everywhere.

Fences.

Ditches.

Paths.

Old stone.

New wood.

People loved turning invisible agreements into physical objects and then pretending the objects could not lie.

Old Greg had owned land.

Lost land.

Taken land.

Paid people to tell him where land ended.

At S-class, boundaries had often been political problems wearing geography as a costume.

Here, four feet of stone mattered because somebody might spend the next ten years paying for the mistake.

Scale changed.

Human behavior did not.

The oval clicked at South Gate.

I felt it in the bag.

Ignored it.

We crossed into Carrow.

Antonius paid me near the market.

Three copper.

Cash.

He counted them into my hand.

I counted once.

Not because I distrusted him.

Because money remained money.

Five copper total now.

That was meaningful.

Not dramatic.

Meaningful.

Pell rolled the survey sheet tighter.

"If they ask again, you available?"

Antonius answered before I could.

"Depends on the task."

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

"Good answer," I said.

"I learned."

No standing job.

Good.

Pell said, "You should learn the local survey marks."

That was more dangerous.

Not because it was a job offer.

Because he was right.

"Maybe."

"I have an old reference sheet."

"Reference, not apprenticeship?"

"Gods, no."

"Excellent."

"I'll copy the mark page if I remember."

"If you remember."

"Correct."

Boundaries.

Beautiful.

I walked home with five copper and sore shoulders.

The temporary black conduit still ran along the front hall wall.

The red keep-clear line was intact.

Nobody had died while I was gone.

Strong building.

Upstairs, I put the three new copper beside the two old ones.

Five.

The number did something to the room.

Not literally.

The room remained small.

The high shelf remained high.

The holdfast remained wrapped.

The basket of mostly garbage remained mostly garbage.

But five changed options.

Mara's mark read no longer consumed everything.

Housing read no longer consumed everything.

A one-copper purchase no longer cut me in half.

Fourteen was still far.

Good.

Far was allowed.

I took out the narrow scrap.

FIELD LINE HALF DAY 3 C

I paused.

Then wrote:

5 C TOTAL

Under that:

TEMP / CARRY-FORWARD SURVEY MARK FAMILY ?

2 EXAMPLES / LOCAL MEANING NOT VERIFIED

STONE MARKER 4 FT WEST OF FILED / WOOD 1 FT EAST

DO NOT SOLVE FROM ONE MATCHING DESCRIPTION

I stared at the last line.

Too broad.

I rubbed it out.

Hessa had infected me enough already.

Then I looked at Mara's old rates written above.

MARK READ 2 C.

HOUSING 3 C.

OPEN 4 C + MATERIALS.

Five copper.

For the first time, I could pay for information and still have money afterward.

That mattered.

I looked up at the holdfast.

Then at the oval hanging near the doorway.

Two unknown pieces.

One useful enough to carry.

One still wrapped because I had never tested beyond the seller's bench.

I did not immediately grab either.

That mattered too.

Five copper was not permission to spend.

It was permission to choose.

Downstairs, Tavi shouted something about a spoon.

Lyssa shouted back that spoons did not need names.

Tavi disagreed.

The oval turned once on its cord when the upstairs doorway opened.

Click.

Normal.

I put the scrap away.

Tomorrow's problem could wait until tomorrow.
