TEXT READER · CHAPTER 338

THE PUBLIC COUNTER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I arrived after first bell with the black chit in my bag and no idea what it was for.

This was becoming one of my more successful strategies.

East Kiln smelled like wet stone, hot metal, lime dust, and breakfast eaten too close to work.

Deliveries had already started.

That mattered because the note had said after first bell.

Before first bell, apparently, I was a hazard.

After first bell, I was paperwork.

Progress.

The East gate was not where I remembered it.

Not moved.

Just busier.

Two carts waited outside.

One empty.

One carrying squared stone under canvas.

A woman with a slate checked markings against the cart sides while a boy rolled a wheel under the rear axle and called numbers.

No one looked dramatic.

Good yard.

I stayed outside the gate line.

The black chit was in my hand.

Not raised.

I had enough dignity left not to wave it around like a child who had found permission.

A gate worker glanced at me.

Then at the chit.

"Public counter?"

"Apparently."

He pointed through the gate.

"Yellow line. Follow it. Don't cross white stone."

That was it.

No inspection.

No speech.

The chit did not glow.

Disappointing artifact design.

I followed the yellow line.

The yard was larger from inside.

That was stupid.

Obviously it was the same size.

But streets flattened places into walls and gates.

Inside, the cooperative had depth.

Covered sheds.

Stone racks.

Hoists.

Low rail carts.

Three load frames in different stages of use.

One of them had the same broad geometry as the frame that had failed weeks ago.

Not same machine.

Same family.

Counterload mast.

Low bed.

Guide arms.

Colored lever positions.

I did not stare long enough to get yelled at.

Yellow line.

Public counter.

The counter was literally a counter.

Wood.

Chest high.

Under a narrow awning along the inner wall.

Three windows.

RETURNS

CLAIMS

SURPLUS / RETIRED

Ah.

That was interesting.

Jessa stood beside the third window holding a mug.

She saw me.

"You came."

"You sent a courier."

"Some people still ignore those."

"I respect paid logistics."

"Courier wasn't paid for reply."

"He made that clear."

Jessa nodded.

"Good kid."

No further biography.

Excellent.

I held up the chit.

"Still good, apparently."

"For the public counter."

"I was worried it might secretly make me a mason."

"It doesn't."

"Good."

"You'd be terrible."

Less good.

She took a drink.

"Davin said you kept looking at the frame after the lift."

"I keep looking at most things."

"I noticed."

"Concerning."

She pointed at the third window.

"Surplus turns over today. Thought you might like seeing what happens to old yard gear when we stop trusting it."

That was better than a job.

Possibly worse for my finances.

"Do I need the chit to buy?"

"No."

Interesting.

"Then what does the chit do?"

"Gets you from gate to counter without someone walking you."

I looked at the square black piece in my hand.

That was it.

Access.

Literal access.

Not discount.

Not authority.

Not secret inventory.

A permitted route.

I liked it more immediately.

"Can anyone use the counter?"

"Public counter."

"Strong clue."

"Gate's working yard. Public doesn't wander through it. Chit means gate knows you're allowed to walk the marked line alone."

There.

Small privilege.

Real privilege.

Narrow enough not to become destiny.

Perfect.

The SURPLUS / RETIRED window had a slate board beside it.

Six lines.

Not six items.

Six categories.

STONE HARDWARE

FRAME FITTINGS

WORN HAND TOOLS

NONRATED LOAD PARTS

REGISTERED COMPONENTS AS MARKED

MIXED / BY WEIGHT

Prices were written beside categories, not individual magical objects.

Most were bits.

A few one-copper lots.

Two lines said ASK.

I hated ASK.

ASK was how money acquired personality.

Behind the counter sat shallow bins.

Iron collars.

Pins.

Bent hooks.

Wood blocks.

Leather straps.

Metal plates.

Enough ordinary industrial junk to make Tavi ascend spiritually.

A man behind the window looked at Jessa.

Then me.

"Friend?"

"Witness from south-lift mess."

"The runner?"

"Message carrier."

Good correction.

The man nodded once.

"Chit?"

I showed it.

He did not take it.

"Fine. Counter only."

"I have been warned about my masonry career."

He stared.

Jessa drank from her mug.

Traitor.

The man slid a flat board onto the counter.

Seven objects had been tied to it with cord.

Each had a small paper card.

"Today's marked components," he said.

I looked at Jessa.

"You arranged this?"

"No. Thursday arranged it."

Even better.

The world had systems.

The first was a brass guide roller.

No core.

No mark beyond yard stamp.

Retired for groove wear.

Two bits.

Second was a red-handled load pin.

Plain steel.

Retired because shaft measured narrow.

One bit.

Third was a thumb-sized black stone set inside a split iron collar.

Blue card.

There.

Registered component.

I leaned closer.

Did not touch.

The card read:

COUNTERLOAD REST STONE / SMALL

YARD REGISTER K-14-6

FUNCTION AT LAST CHECK: HOLDS FRAME BIAS WHILE MAIN LOAD IS STATIC

REMOVED FROM WEST BED TWO

REASON: RELEASE DELAY OUTSIDE YARD LIMIT

NO RATED USE

SALE AS RETIRED COMPONENT

PRICE 1 C

I read it twice.

One copper.

There were cheap magical things.

Of course there were.

Not powerful magical things.

Not trustworthy ones.

Not complete systems.

Components.

Used.

Retired.

Functionally narrow.

Potentially dangerous if misunderstood.

The market had a floor and a ceiling and a basement full of bad decisions.

I loved it.

Jessa watched my face.

"That's why I sent the note."

"You want me dead?"

"No. You asked Davin what happened to broken frame parts."

I had.

Apparently.

I vaguely remembered asking while everyone was still sweaty and annoyed.

The problem with saying many things was that other people occasionally remembered them.

"This came from that frame?"

"No."

Immediate.

Good.

"West bed two. Different assembly."

"Same family?"

"Maybe. Ask counter."

The man behind the window said, "Counterload part. Don't know if same pattern."

I nodded.

No free expertise from proximity.

I read the card again.

Holds frame bias while main load static.

Release delay outside yard limit.

That was not a minor defect.

Depending on system, delayed release could mean the frame kept expecting a load after the load changed.

Or held tension too long.

Or made the next movement wrong.

Or something else entirely.

I knew enough to be afraid in several directions.

Excellent.

"What does sale as retired component permit?"

The counter man answered.

"Ownership. Not use approval."

"Can it legally be mounted?"

"On what?"

"Anything."

"Then no answer."

Good.

"Can it be tested?"

"By someone who wants to test it."

"Here?"

"No."

"Do you have condition notes beyond card?"

"Public copy is card. Yard sheet isn't sale document."

There.

Information boundary.

I could buy the object for one copper and still not buy the cooperative's internal history.

"Can I inspect it without touching?"

"You're doing that."

I liked him more now.

The split iron collar was ordinary enough.

The stone inside was not.

Black at first.

Then dark red when light caught one edge.

Not glowing.

Translucent deeper in.

A shallow cut crossed the exposed face.

Not a maker mark.

Could be wear.

Could be installation witness line.

Could be someone with a knife and poor impulse control.

The collar had two bolt holes.

One slightly oval from wear.

The card said release delay.

I wanted it.

Immediately.

That was suspicious.

One copper was half my liquid money.

But not all.

That distinction mattered now.

I could buy it and still have one copper.

The old version of my finances would have turned this into disaster arithmetic.

The new version was simpler.

Did I have a use?

No.

Could I evaluate it well enough to know hidden value?

No.

Was one copper cheap relative to magical component exposure?

Probably.

Was cheap enough the same as worth buying?

No.

Annoying growth.

I looked at the other blue-card component.

Small rectangular copper plate with two white ceramic studs.

Card:

HEAT SPREAD PLATE

REGISTER K-22-3

FUNCTION: REDUCES LOCAL HOT SPOT UNDER CONTINUOUS CONTACT

REMOVED: EDGE CRACK

NOT FOR PRESSURE SERVICE

PRICE 2 BITS

Obvious.

Useful.

Damaged.

Cheap.

The crack ran from one corner almost to the nearest stud.

I did not want it.

Which was useful information about me.

Third marked piece was stranger.

A narrow iron fork with one tine shorter.

No blue card.

Yellow.

INCOMPLETE GUIDE FORK / MATCHED MATE MISSING / 1 BIT.

Mundane or maybe system-dependent magical geometry.

Not enough information.

The rest were ordinary retired parts.

Jessa finished her drink.

"Well?"

"Dangerous place."

"It's a counter."

"Exactly."

She nodded toward the rest stone.

"You're staring at the one-copper piece."

"It is staring back."

"No, it isn't."

"You don't know."

"I removed it."

That got me.

"You?"

"With two others."

Good correction before I made her magical stone surgeon.

"What was the release delay?"

She looked at the counter man.

He looked back.

"Public card," he said.

Right.

Jessa shrugged.

"Then that's what you get."

I appreciated that too.

Workplace knowledge was not automatically mine because someone liked me enough to send a note.

I asked, "Why sell it at all?"

Counter man said, "Because retired isn't cursed."

Beautiful.

"And because somebody buys metal and stone," Jessa added.

"For repair?"

"Sometimes."

"Study?"

"Sometimes."

"Parts?"

"Sometimes."

"Bad ideas?"

"Frequently."

There it was.

A whole secondary market under the polished artifact market.

Not hidden treasure.

Retired industrial magic.

Known faults.

Incomplete records.

Narrow legal transfer.

Buyers who might want material, comparison pieces, repair stock, or terrible experiments.

One copper suddenly felt less like a price and more like an admission fee to a category.

Which was exactly how I got in trouble.

I stepped back from the board.

"Not buying yet."

Jessa raised an eyebrow.

"Yet?"

"Dangerous word."

"You have until counter closes."

"Today only?"

"Marked board turns weekly. Unsold may roll, may go mixed, may go scrap."

That mattered.

Not fake urgency.

Actual inventory turnover.

I looked again.

One copper.

A retired rest stone with known release delay.

No use approval.

No test access here.

No detailed yard sheet.

If I bought it, I could bring it to Mara eventually.

Which would probably cost more than the component.

Artifacts were very good at generating service economies around themselves.

I hated them.

Jessa said, "Come on."

"Where?"

"Counter side."

"Chit says counter only."

"Yes."

"That sounded like leaving counter."

"It isn't."

She walked ten feet along the awning.

I followed the yellow strip painted in front of the counter windows.

Still public route.

At the far end was a waist-high iron stand bolted to the wall.

On it sat a large cracked block of pale stone with three metal loops embedded through it.

A sign above it read:

FAILED / RETIRED EXAMPLES

DO NOT REMOVE

Oh.

I loved civilization again.

"What's this?"

"People kept asking what failure notes mean."

"So you made a museum?"

"We put broken things by the counter."

"Museum."

"Don't call it that."

I would absolutely call it that.

There were five examples.

A bent load pin cut halfway through to show an internal crack.

A guide roller with a groove worn deep enough to shift alignment.

A ceramic heat plate with a repaired edge that had cracked again through the repair.

A braided red cord with one section glazed dark from heat.

And the pale stone block.

Its face had a clean-looking line across it.

Too clean.

The sign below said:

LOAD STONE / INTERNAL FRACTURE FOUND AFTER RINGING TEST

EXTERIOR LINE WAS POLISH MARK, NOT CRACK

FAILED BELOW SURFACE

I stared.

That was almost rude.

Visible mark not crack.

Hidden crack elsewhere.

The exact opposite of what a nervous buyer would assume.

Jessa tapped the sign, not the stone.

"We show this because people point at scratches and decide they understand condition."

"I know someone who would like you."

"Who?"

"Mara Quill."

"Don't know her."

Good.

Carrow was not secretly twelve people.

I looked at the cut pin.

"Can I ask something?"

"You have been."

"Why show public buyers failure examples if you can just sell scrap?"

Jessa shrugged.

"Fewer arguments."

That was possibly the most realistic institutional answer available.

The counter man called from the window, "And fewer returns."

"Returns on retired parts?"

"People try."

Of course they did.

A broad man in an apron approached the counter carrying a broken clamp.

We moved aside.

He placed it at RETURNS.

Different window.

Different problem.

The clerk there asked for yard mark.

The man gave a number.

They argued about whether one jaw had been filed after issue.

Not loudly.

Professionally.

Jessa waited until they were occupied.

"You helped on the lift because you carried the message exactly."

I looked at her.

"That sounds dangerously like praise."

"Don't get used to it."

"Good."

"You also didn't touch anything."

"Very skilled."

"You'd be surprised."

I would not.

She nodded toward the counter.

"So if you want to look, look. If you want to buy, buy. If you want yard work, this chit does not get you yard work."

"Understood."

"If someone offers you work, they tell you."

"Also understood."

"If you cross white stone, somebody yells."

"Strong system."

"Very old."

That was the whole invitation.

Not mentorship.

Not recruitment.

A worker deciding that a curious person who had behaved usefully once could be trusted to walk to the public-facing edge of the yard without supervision.

Small.

Huge.

I hated how often those were the same thing.

Jessa left me there.

Actually left.

"Shift," she said.

Then ducked under a chain and disappeared toward the load beds.

No tour.

No special demonstration.

I stood at the public counter alone.

That improved the experience.

I spent another half hour looking.

Not only at the rest stone.

That was important.

A woman bought four worn iron collars by weight.

A young man asked whether a yellow-tagged guide fork could be returned if he found its mate did not fit.

No.

A carpenter bought two retired leather straps after checking stitch holes.

A gray-haired man bought the cracked heat plate for two bits and told the clerk he wanted the ceramic studs.

There.

Not function.

Parts value.

The rest stone stayed.

A worker in a blue apron looked at it, read the card, asked if collar iron was included, then left.

Different valuation.

I learned more from that than from staring.

At one point the counter man changed the card on a bin of mixed fittings from one copper by basket to three bits because enough of the better iron had sold.

Dynamic pricing at the smallest scale.

Civilization was relentless.

I had two copper.

The rest stone cost one.

I could afford it.

That was not the same as I should buy it.

I thought about the harness.

Thirty-six copper.

Too far to act.

This was one copper.

Close enough to act.

The problem was almost harder.

When something was impossible, decision-making was cheap.

When something was possible, I had to be an adult.

Unfair.

What did I actually gain from ownership?

A physical registered retired component.

A known function at last check.

A known failure mode broad enough to matter.

A piece I could compare against future counterload parts.

Maybe a thing Mara could inspect later.

Maybe something I could learn from without activating.

What did I risk?

Half my cash reserve.

Storage.

Future appraisal cost.

Temptation to do something stupid because I owned it.

Potentially possessing a component I could not safely use.

One copper was not nothing.

It also was not existential.

That distinction had to mean something eventually or the economy model was fake.

I asked the counter man, "Does registration transfer with the component?"

"Sale slip lists old register number and retired status. Not active equipment registration."

"Fee?"

"Included at counter."

That mattered.

No extra copper surprise.

"Can I get a copy of the public card?"

"Card goes with it."

"Original?"

"Sale card. Not yard sheet."

Right.

I looked at the stone.

Then my purse.

Then stone.

I bought it.

One copper.

No thunder.

No destiny.

The counter man untied it from the display board.

He did not hand it directly to me.

He wrapped the iron collar and stone in coarse packing cloth, then placed it in a shallow wooden box barely larger than my palm.

"Keep card with it."

"I was planning to."

"People say that."

"You have a low opinion of buyers."

"I work returns."

Fair.

He wrote a sale slip.

One copper.

Retired counterload rest stone.

Register K-14-6.

No rated use.

Public card attached.

I paid.

One copper remained.

Not zero.

That felt important.

Also terrifying.

I put the shallow box in my shoulder bag.

It was heavier than the oval but not difficult.

The oval sat in its own wrapped section.

Two artifacts in one bag.

One working enough to be annoying.

One retired enough to be officially untrusted.

Excellent life choices.

At the gate, the worker glanced at the box.

"Slip?"

I showed it.

He looked at the register line.

Then waved me through.

The black chit stayed with me.

I stopped.

"Do you keep this?"

"Chit? No."

"Still good?"

"Until marked otherwise."

"How would I know?"

"Gate tells you."

Ancient technology.

I left East Kiln with one copper, one retired magical component, and a much more dangerous understanding of public infrastructure.

There were places where magical objects did not enter the world as legendary treasure.

They aged out of work.

They cracked.

They missed tolerances.

They got stripped for parts.

They moved through counters for bits and copper because their original use no longer justified trust.

That did not make them secretly valuable.

It made them available.

Different thing.

Possibly more useful.

At home, I put the box on the table.

Cala looked at it.

Then me.

"What did you buy?"

"A retired failure."

"Why?"

"It was one copper."

"That did not answer me."

Lyssa looked over from the window.

"He found cheap magic."

"Retired magic," I said.

"Worse," Cala said.

Tavi appeared from nowhere.

"Can I touch?"

"No."

"Can I see?"

"From there."

He leaned over the table from a medically unnecessary distance.

I unwrapped the box but did not remove the stone.

Read the card aloud.

COUNTERLOAD REST STONE.

Holds frame bias while main load static.

Removed for release delay outside yard limit.

No rated use.

Cala looked at me.

"What does that mean?"

"It means we do not use it."

"Good."

"Yet."

Her expression changed.

"Greg."

"For observation."

"You said yet."

"I regret language."

Lyssa smiled.

Tavi pointed at the stone.

"Does it make things lighter?"

"No idea."

"Does it hold things?"

"In a system, apparently."

"Can it hold spoon carrier?"

"Absolutely not."

His disappointment was profound.

I moved the box upstairs.

Not onto the holdfast shelf.

That shelf already contained enough poor judgment.

Different shelf.

Lower than holdfast but above Tavi reach.

The card stayed under the box lid.

Sale slip folded beneath it.

I wrote only three new lines on the narrow scrap.

EAST KILN PUBLIC COUNTER: RETIRED COMPONENTS / KNOWN FAULTS / NO USE APPROVAL

K-14-6 REST STONE / 1 C / RELEASE DELAY

CASH NOW 1 C

Then stopped.

I almost wrote:

CHEAP MAGIC EXISTS BELOW COMPLETE-ARTIFACT MARKET

Too broad.

Cheap retired components existed.

Different claim.

The distinction mattered.

I crossed nothing out because I had not written the stupid version.

More growth.

The holdfast waited above my clothes.

The oval hung near the doorway.

The rest stone sat in its shallow box.

Three objects.

Three completely different relationships to trust.

The oval was useful but unexplained.

The holdfast was demonstrated but unresolved.

The rest stone was documented and retired for a known problem.

Information did not move in one direction.

Sometimes the cheapest object came with the clearest warning.

That felt unfair.

Also healthy.

Downstairs, Cala called that food was ready.

I went.

The stone stayed on the shelf.

Which was, for the moment, the smartest thing I knew how to do with it.
