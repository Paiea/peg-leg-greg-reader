TEXT READER · CHAPTER 349

THE WALKAWAY

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Five copper was enough money to make me dangerous in places where everything cost one.

It was useless in places where anything interesting happened.

I discovered this on South Market's upper row.

I had not gone there for artifacts.

This was important.

I had gone because Cala needed lamp wick and the lower stall was out.

Ordinary errand.

One narrow paper strip.

LAMP WICK / MEDIUM / TWO LENGTHS

No hidden destiny.

No mysterious courier.

No one had whispered that a legendary object waited beneath a tarp.

I was simply walking uphill because household lighting had failed to respect narrative pacing.

South Market upper row was narrower than the lower one and more expensive in a way buildings managed before prices did.

Stone awnings.

Glass fronts on three shops.

Metal signs instead of painted boards.

One place selling writing instruments that looked too clean to touch.

One place selling shoes with actual paired displays instead of a basket of survivors.

Then a covered lane where repair sellers, retired guild stock, estate pieces, and things with paperwork problems lived together under the broad category of somebody else's decisions.

I slowed.

Not much.

Maturity.

A woman behind a table was arguing with a man about a cracked warming plate.

"It still warms."

"One side."

"That is more than no sides."

Good market.

I continued.

The lamp-wick seller was apparently three stalls farther.

I almost made it.

Then I saw the buckle.

Not a jewelry buckle.

Too large.

Palm-wide blackened brass body.

Two thick side loops.

One central jaw shaped like a flattened mouth.

Three pale ceramic teeth sat inside the jaw.

A red release tab protruded from the lower edge.

The whole thing rested on gray felt beside four ordinary rigging clamps and a coil of blue cord.

There was a price card.

18 C

I kept walking.

This was excellent discipline.

Three steps.

Four.

Five.

Then old memory arrived late enough to be irritating.

A roof in winter.

Wet slate.

Someone screaming at a foreman because the anchor had locked properly and the release had not.

A man hanging sideways against a chimney with both boots off the surface.

A different city.

Different life.

Same ugly mouth-shaped jaw.

I stopped.

Damn it.

I turned around slowly.

Not because I wanted it.

Because old memory deserved verification.

This was how lies began.

The seller was an older man with a flat gray cap and a left hand missing the last joint of one finger.

He noticed me return.

Of course he did.

"Rigging?" he asked.

"Sometimes."

Not a lie.

I had existed near ropes.

"Roof work?"

"No."

"Then probably not for you."

Excellent sales technique.

I looked at the four clamps first.

One normal iron jaw clamp.

One brass rope guide.

One little hinge thing with a cracked blue stone.

Then the blackened buckle.

"Load lock?"

His eyes sharpened slightly.

There.

Information leakage.

"Old one."

"Working?"

"Demonstrated this morning."

"On what?"

"Bench rail."

"Loaded?"

"Sand sack."

"How much?"

"Enough for demonstration."

Good seller.

No invented certification.

I looked at price.

"Eighteen?"

"Yes."

"For an old load lock with no rated load card?"

"For an old load lock that works."

Fair.

"Maker?"

He shrugged.

"Mark under body."

He did not pick it up.

Also good.

"May I look?"

"Without activating."

"Obviously."

He looked at me.

"Not obvious to everyone."

I believed him.

I leaned closer.

Under one side loop, partly hidden by wear, was a stamped mark.

Three stacked wedges.

Not famous.

Not later.

Not in any history book I remembered.

But familiar.

Kerrin dock pattern.

Or a copy.

Maybe.

I did not say that.

"How old?"

"Seller said pre-revision."

"Which revision?"

"Didn't say."

"Useful."

"I did not charge for the story."

I liked him.

The buckle body had two belt loops, but not for a normal waist belt.

Too wide.

Harness webbing.

The central jaw looked designed to bite onto a fixed rail, rope ring, or structural tongue.

Not something worn alone.

"What's the failure?" I asked.

He smiled.

There it was.

"You know the family."

Damn.

"Broadly."

"Then you know why eighteen isn't thirty."

Maybe.

I kept my face still.

"Tell me your version."

He laughed once.

"Good."

He pointed at red tab without touching it.

"Locks under load. Holds well. Release gets stubborn if the load remains on the jaw."

Yes.

There it was.

"Stubborn how?"

"Sometimes tab will not travel until load is mostly off."

"Mostly?"

"That is the word I was given."

"Not certified?"

"No."

"Repeatable?"

"I saw it twice this morning."

"Loaded both times?"

"Sand sack. Same rail."

"Released both times?"

"After sack was lifted."

The old memory sharpened.

Not an exact item.

Class.

Pre-revision load lock.

They were hated because the thing did its job too well in the worst possible moment.

A later revision added a secondary release path.

I remembered that much.

Maybe.

Or I remembered one maker adding one.

Memory was not a manual.

"So if someone is hanging from it," I said, "the release may be inaccessible until somebody unloads them."

"That would be a stupid use."

"People specialize."

"Correct."

He tapped the price card.

"For temporary work positioning. Not fall arrest. Not rescue gear. Not life support."

Good.

Very good.

"Paper?"

He reached under table and produced a folded strip.

Not original.

Resale note.

I did not take it.

He held it where I could read.

OLD LOAD LOCK / HARNESS MOUNT
WORKING DEMONSTRATION ONLY
NO LOAD RATING
NO LIFE-SAFETY CERTIFICATION
RELEASE REQUIRES LOAD REDUCTION
SOLD AS USED RIGGING EQUIPMENT

Clean.

"Eighteen," I said again.

"Eighteen."

"Why not twelve?"

"Because at twelve a roofer buys it for spare positioning gear before supper."

"Why not twenty-five?"

"Because at twenty-five a roofer buys a newer one with better release."

There.

Market intelligence from a man who did not need my help.

Good.

I should have left.

Instead I looked at the side loops again.

Harness mount.

Load jaw.

Release only after unloading.

Most users had two feet, two hands, and one problem.

I had one foot, two crutches, and several problems.

Which sometimes rearranged the arithmetic.

If the lock held body position against lateral load, then unloading did not necessarily require another person.

Not for me.

Maybe.

Two crutches could take weight while the right foot came clear.

Or one crutch and a seated transfer.

Or the lock could mount to a belt or work harness while my hands controlled release after body load shifted onto crutches.

That was not proof.

It was not even a plan.

It was a reason the known downside might be less severe for me than for a standard user.

Which was dangerous.

Because eighteen copper was suddenly not a price.

It was a problem.

I had five.

Twenty-six Vale.

No immediate payment due.

No scheduled theatre work.

No standing gate job.

Five liquid copper.

Thirteen short.

I hated arithmetic when it became desire.

The seller watched me thinking.

"You do roof work after all?"

"No."

"Then what are you doing?"

"Making a mistake internally."

"Cheap."

"Not if I continue."

He smiled.

I asked, "Would you hold it?"

"With deposit."

There it was.

I did not ask how much immediately.

This was progress.

I looked at the ordinary clamps again.

One copper.

Two.

Three bits on one.

Different world.

Then back to buckle.

"How much deposit?"

"Four copper. Three days. Nonrefundable if you walk. Applied to price if you buy."

Four.

I had five.

I could reserve it.

I could reduce my liquid cash to one.

Then find fourteen more copper in three days.

Which was stupid.

Not impossible in the abstract.

Impossible under current actual opportunities.

There was a difference.

"Can deposit be extended?"

"No."

"Can balance be partial?"

"No."

"Trade?"

"Depends what."

"Used artifacts?"

"Sometimes."

Dangerous.

Holdfast.

Rest stone.

Oval.

No.

The thought arrived and I rejected it before it became accounting.

I did not know enough about any of them to trade intelligently.

And the oval was useful.

The holdfast had unresolved value.

The rest stone had unresolved system context.

Trading mystery assets for one desired object was how people became stories told by market sellers.

"No trade," I said.

"You didn't offer one."

"Correct."

He looked amused.

"Four holds it."

I stared at price card.

Eighteen.

This was the exact kind of thing I had wanted money for.

Not food.

Not rent.

Not survival.

Option value.

An object appeared.

I recognized enough to want it.

I did not have enough to act.

Five copper was suddenly both evidence of progress and completely inadequate.

That felt correct.

"Can I see demonstration?"

"Tomorrow morning if it is still here."

"Why not now?"

"Sand sack is not here."

"Bench rail is."

"And I am one person."

Fair.

"What time?"

"First bell after open."

I nodded.

Then did the difficult thing.

I left.

No deposit.

Not yet.

The seller said nothing behind me.

That made it harder.

I walked to the lamp-wick stall.

Bought two lengths.

Ordinary household money from Cala's household purse, not mine.

I did not accidentally spend Greg copper because accounting was not a fog.

The wick seller wrapped both in paper.

I put them in my shoulder bag.

Then stood there like an idiot.

The upper row continued around me.

People buying things.

Not buying things.

Arguing.

Carrying.

One woman held a little blue-glass heater to her wrist while a seller demonstrated different settings.

A man tried on a rain hood that refused to stay over his ears.

Someone laughed from a tea stall.

The world contained magic people could afford and magic they could not.

I returned home with lamp wick and no load lock.

Cala took the package.

"Right size?"

"Yes."

"Both lengths?"

"Yes."

"Good."

End of errand.

Lyssa was at her new work table.

Flat line on.

Dark fabric spread across it.

Not yesterday's customer piece.

Something else.

Her life continued being offensively separate.

I put my shoulder bag down.

Then sat.

She looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have artifact face."

This was slander.

"I have a normal face."

"No."

Cala looked over from lamp.

"Did you buy something?"

"No."

"Good."

Tavi asked, "Did you steal something?"

"No."

"Bad."

At least one person supported me.

Lyssa put down chalk.

"What did you see?"

I considered not telling her.

Not secrecy.

Just dignity.

Then failed.

"Old harness load lock."

"What does it do?"

"Broadly, locks under load against a fixed point."

"Useful?"

"Potentially."

"Dangerous?"

"Known release problem if still loaded."

"Price?"

"Eighteen copper."

She stopped.

Good.

That was the reaction I wanted.

Cala said, "No."

"I did not ask."

"Good."

Lyssa asked, "How much do you have?"

"Five."

"Then also no."

"Excellent household support."

"You want it."

"Yes."

No point lying.

"Why?"

That was harder.

"Because the downside may fit me differently."

Lyssa looked at my crutches.

Then back at me.

"Explain without making me regret asking."

"If release needs body load reduced, most people need a partner, a support, or somewhere to sit. I already move body load onto crutches all day. Maybe that makes the release problem easier for me. Maybe."

She thought.

Not impressed.

Good.

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it is an eighteen-copper old load lock with bad release."

"You don't have eighteen."

"Correct."

"So why are we discussing it?"

"Because I am suffering."

She smiled.

Cruel.

"Deposit?"

I looked at her.

"How did you know there was a deposit?"

"Because your face is worse than eighteen copper."

Terrible intimacy.

"Four. Three days. Nonrefundable."

"Don't."

Immediate.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yes."

And I did.

Four copper to buy three days would be paying almost my entire reserve for an option I had no realistic path to exercise.

That was not bold.

That was buying anxiety.

I hated correct conclusions.

Cala said, "If you find the money tomorrow, will it still be there?"

"Maybe."

"Then find money first."

There.

Not don't buy.

Sequence.

Interesting.

Lyssa looked at her.

Cala shrugged.

"He wants it."

I stared.

"You are both being strangely reasonable."

"You did not bring it home," Cala said.

Fair.

That had purchased trust.

Tavi asked, "Can it hold me to the ceiling?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because if it could, eighteen copper would be cheap."

He considered this.

"Can I have eighteen copper?"

"No."

Household finance complete.

I went upstairs.

Five copper.

I counted it once.

Not because I had forgotten.

Because numbers had changed category.

Five was not reserve now.

Five was twenty-seven percent of something I wanted.

I hated percentages.

I wrote:

LOAD LOCK / USED / 18 C

HARNESS MOUNT

WORKING DEMO CLAIMED / SAND SACK

NO LOAD RATING

KNOWN: RELEASE MAY REQUIRE LOAD REDUCTION

PRE-REVISION CLAIM / MARK UNDER BODY

Then stopped.

I almost wrote:

MY CRUTCH LOAD TRANSFER MAY REDUCE DOWNSIDE

Too strong.

Changed it.

POSSIBLE: CRUTCH-SUPPORTED UNLOADING MAY CHANGE RELEASE PRACTICALITY

NOT TESTED

Better.

Then:

DEPOSIT 4 C / 3 DAYS / NONREFUNDABLE

NO.

I underlined NO once.

Then wrote:

NEED MONEY BEFORE OPTION, NOT OPTION BEFORE MONEY

That was ugly enough to be useful.

Five copper.

Twenty-six Vale.

Eighteen for the load lock.

Thirteen short.

No immediate path.

I should have felt frustrated.

I did.

But underneath that was something cleaner.

For the first time in a while, I knew exactly why I wanted more money.

Not because being poor was frightening.

Because something interesting might disappear before I could reach it.

That was a much better problem.

Still a problem.

But better.

Tomorrow morning there would be a demonstration if the load lock was still there.

I had not reserved it.

Someone else could buy it tonight.

That was the market.

I put the five copper away.

And did not go back.
