TEXT READER · CHAPTER 350

THE BUYER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I went back the next morning.

This was not the same as buying it.

Important distinction.

I told myself that four times before breakfast.

Then once more while putting on my shoulder bag.

Lyssa watched from the work table.

The pale flat line was already on.

She had a dark strip of cloth pinned along it.

"You are going."

"To observe."

"Of course."

"Observation is free."

"Walking uphill isn't."

"That cost is already sunk into having legs badly."

She looked at my crutches.

"Leg."

"Thank you."

Cala was cutting bread.

She did not ask where I was going.

That was worse.

I waited.

Nothing.

Finally I said, "I am not putting down the deposit."

Cala kept cutting.

"Good."

"I do understand money."

"Good."

"I am only watching the demonstration."

"Good."

This conversation had become hostile through repetition.

Tavi looked up from the floor.

"If it sticks, can they leave the sand hanging forever?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because then it becomes architecture."

He considered that.

"Can we buy architecture?"

"Not with five copper."

Lyssa said, "Apparently not with eighteen either."

Cruel household.

I left before support became fatal.

South Market upper row was already awake.

Different awake from lower market.

Less shouting.

More unlocking.

Shop shutters lifted one by one.

A glass-front place had a boy polishing the same section of window with the intensity of a man erasing evidence.

Repair lane smelled like hot metal, old rope, oil, and yesterday's rain trapped under canvas.

The old seller was there.

So was the load lock.

I noticed that first.

Blackened brass.

Three pale ceramic teeth.

Red release tab.

Eighteen-copper card.

Still mine to not own.

The seller saw me.

"You came."

"You sound disappointed."

"I did not say that."

"Is demonstration happening?"

He pointed behind the stall.

A squat iron bench stood with one end bolted to a post.

A rail ran along its side.

On the ground beside it sat a sand sack with two rope handles.

Maybe the size of a small child.

A dense child.

Good.

"After first bell," he said.

"That was the agreement."

"No agreement. You did not pay."

Fair.

I stood to one side.

Did not touch the lock.

Did not stare at it continuously.

This required effort.

A woman bought one of the ordinary brass rope guides.

Two copper.

No tragedy.

A man asked about the cracked blue-stone hinge.

Seller told him exactly what was cracked.

Man left.

Seller did not chase him.

Then first bell sounded somewhere down-market.

Not loud.

Enough.

The seller took the load lock off gray felt.

He held it by one side loop.

"Still want to see?"

"Yes."

"Then stand where you can see and not where the sack can hit you."

Excellent safety briefing.

I moved.

He threaded a wide piece of old harness webbing through the two side loops.

Not a full harness.

Demonstration strap.

The strap had a wood handle at one end.

He clipped the central jaw around the iron rail.

Not clipped.

Set.

The jaw opened when he pulled the red tab down and squeezed a small side lever I had not noticed yesterday.

Interesting.

He seated the rail between ceramic teeth.

Released tab.

Jaw closed.

No glow.

No chime.

No obvious magic.

Then he tugged strap sideways.

The lock slid six inches.

He tugged harder.

It slid another two.

Then stopped.

Not mechanical stop.

The ceramic teeth gave a faint gray-white line along their edges.

The body settled against rail.

Seller leaned his weight into strap.

Nothing moved.

There.

Working effect.

Not spectacular.

Useful.

He relaxed.

The pale edge-light faded.

He pulled red tab.

Jaw opened immediately.

Unloaded release.

"First part," he said.

"Fine."

He reset it.

Then hooked sand sack to the demonstration strap.

He did not lift the sack by hand.

A little screw hoist hung from bench post.

He turned handle until sack rose maybe a foot.

The strap angled from rail down to sack.

Load lock took weight sideways.

Gray-white line appeared along teeth.

The lock did not move.

Seller stopped hoist.

"Loaded."

"Yes."

He pulled red release tab.

It moved maybe a finger-width.

Stopped.

He did not yank harder.

Good.

"That's the defect?"

"That's the defect."

"Can you force it?"

"Probably."

"Do you?"

"No."

Also good.

"What happens if you force it?"

He looked at me.

"If I knew, the paper would say."

Fair.

He turned hoist backward.

Sand sack lowered slightly.

Not enough to touch ground.

Red tab still stuck.

Another turn.

Sack descended.

Strap angle softened.

The gray-white line along ceramic teeth dimmed.

Seller tried tab again.

Moved farther.

Still not full.

Another half turn.

The sack touched ground, but strap remained taut.

He tried.

The tab traveled almost all the way.

Jaw did not open.

Interesting.

He lowered one more fraction.

Strap sagged.

Light vanished.

Tab came down.

Jaw opened.

The lock released.

There.

Not "mostly" unloaded.

At least in this setup, nearly all.

I hated useful information.

The seller looked at me.

"That answer your question?"

"One of them."

"How many did you bring?"

"Questions?"

"Yes."

"Too many."

"Keep most."

Reasonable.

I looked at sand sack.

Then my crutches.

My theory had not died.

It had become narrower.

If the lock held me laterally while both crutches and right foot remained planted, maybe I could unload it.

If I needed the lock because one crutch had slipped, no.

If I was suspended, absolutely no.

If the anchor geometry forced me away from support, no.

If I could sit first, maybe.

If I used it as a third point while transitioning through a controlled work position, maybe.

That was still interesting.

Less magical.

More honest.

"Can you reset with load already pulling?" I asked.

"No demonstration of that."

"Seller note?"

"No."

"Any damage around the side lever?"

He looked at me.

Then at lock.

"Look."

He held it closer.

Did not hand it over.

I leaned.

The small side lever had wear polished around one edge.

Normal use.

Maybe.

The red tab had a shallow scrape on lower corner.

Could mean people kicked it.

Could mean nothing.

One ceramic tooth had a darker line near base.

Not crack that I could see.

Staining.

Old material.

I wanted a better light.

I did not ask.

The seller said, "Eighteen."

"I remember."

"No cheaper after demonstration."

"I did not ask."

"You were about to."

"I was thinking about asking."

"That counts."

Hostile market telepathy.

A voice behind me said, "Let me see it under load."

I turned.

A man stood at stall edge with a coil of yellow rope over one shoulder.

Thirty, maybe.

Heavy boots.

Roof-worker trousers with reinforced knees.

Leather waist harness rolled around one forearm.

Not rich.

Not pretending.

He looked at lock, not me.

Seller said, "Just did."

"I saw last release."

"Then you saw."

"Want to see hold."

The seller shrugged.

"One more."

He reset the demonstration.

The new man stepped closer.

"How old?"

"Pre-revision, seller said."

"Card?"

Seller showed resale note.

The man read faster than I had.

"No rating."

"No."

"Teeth replaced?"

"Not by me."

"Any slip?"

"Not on sand."

"How heavy is sand?"

"Not certified."

The man snorted.

"Nothing here is certified."

"Correct."

They liked each other less than seller liked me.

This reassured me.

The hoist raised sack again.

Lock settled.

Gray-white edge line.

The man put one hand on demonstration strap.

Not pulling.

Feeling vibration, maybe.

"Release."

Seller tried tab.

Stuck.

"Lower."

They lowered.

Same sequence.

The man watched tab instead of sack.

At near-unloaded tension he said, "Again."

Tab nearly traveled.

At slack, released.

He nodded.

Not disappointed.

That was interesting.

"You know these?" I asked.

He looked at me then.

"Old locks?"

"That release pattern."

"Enough."

Seller said, "He wants it for his crutches."

I stared at him.

"I did not say that."

"You stood here looking at your crutches."

The roof worker looked down.

Then at lock.

Then at me.

No ridicule.

Worse.

Thought.

"Positioning?"

"Maybe."

"Don't."

Direct.

I smiled slightly.

"Useful."

"I'm serious."

"I know."

He pointed at demonstration strap.

"That thing doesn't care why you're loaded. If you need it because you can't unload, you're stuck."

"Yes."

"If you can unload, why need it?"

Good question.

Not complete question.

But good.

"Controlled lateral support," I said.

He looked again.

"Maybe."

There.

Not approval.

Not dismissal.

"On flat work."

"Probably."

"With a proper harness."

"Obviously."

He looked at my missing lower left leg.

"Not hanging."

"Obviously."

"People say obviously after."

I liked him less now because he was correct.

I asked, "What would you use it for?"

He lifted rolled waist harness.

"Spare line positioner on scaffold braces. Not primary."

"Because release?"

"Because no rating."

"Release doesn't bother you?"

"It bothers me eighteen copper."

Good.

He looked at seller.

"Fifteen."

Seller said, "Eighteen."

"Sixteen."

"Eighteen."

"Old, unrated, bad release."

"Working, demonstrated, cheaper than new."

"Seventeen."

"Eighteen."

The roof worker stared at him.

Seller stared back.

No drama.

No secret signal.

Just two men who knew what a copper meant.

The roof worker looked at lock again.

Then at his harness.

Then at me, for some reason.

Maybe because I had occupied the stall like a decorative warning.

"Seventeen and the blue cord."

"Eighteen. Cord is two."

"That cord isn't worth two."

"Then don't buy cord."

I almost laughed.

The roof worker rubbed chin.

"Fine. Eighteen."

There it was.

Not theft.

Not rival.

Not villain.

A buyer.

A person with eighteen copper and a use.

The seller reached under table for paper.

I stood very still.

I had five copper.

The buyer had eighteen.

Markets were brutally literal.

He counted coins onto wood.

Not all copper.

Some silver-colored bits grouped and counted by seller.

Two larger copper pieces.

Small change.

I did not know his finances.

Maybe this was his entire reserve.

Maybe his foreman reimbursed him.

Maybe he had fifty more.

Not mine.

What mattered was that he could close.

I could not.

Seller wrote sale line.

USED LOAD LOCK / HARNESS MOUNT

NO LOAD RATING

NO LIFE-SAFETY CERTIFICATION

KNOWN LOADED-RELEASE LIMITATION

DEMONSTRATED PRIOR TO SALE

The buyer read.

Signed.

Seller signed.

Then wrapped lock in old cloth.

Not mine.

I felt stupidly angry.

Not at them.

Which made anger less useful.

The buyer tucked wrapped lock into his equipment bag.

I asked one last question.

"Why buy old one instead of newer?"

He tightened bag strap.

"Because newer is twenty-six where I buy."

Seller looked pleased at yesterday's arithmetic surviving contact with reality.

Buyer continued.

"And I need spare, not primary."

"Release problem acceptable?"

"No."

He looked at me like I had asked whether rain was acceptable.

"It's cheaper."

There.

Economics.

Not romance.

He nodded once to seller and left.

Yellow rope over shoulder.

Load lock gone.

The gray felt looked offensive without it.

Seller picked up price card.

Eighteen.

Removed it.

That was all.

No music.

No lesson.

I had watched someone else buy the thing I wanted.

This happened every day everywhere.

I hated realism.

Seller said, "You saved four copper."

I looked at him.

"By not depositing."

"Yes."

That was true.

If I had placed deposit yesterday, the lock would still be mine to fail buying in two days.

Unless I found thirteen copper.

Which I had not.

I would have purchased temporary possession of a problem.

Instead I had five.

Still.

And more information.

"Do these come through often?" I asked.

"Load locks?"

"Interesting used gear around that price."

He shrugged.

"Sometimes."

"Weekly?"

"No."

"Monthly?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"People selling things."

Hostile epistemology.

I waited.

He sighed.

"Good used working specialty gear under twenty goes fast if somebody has a use. Over twenty sits longer unless maker is known. Broken things sit forever unless repairer knows them."

That was useful enough to cost money.

He gave it free.

I stored it.

"Artifact pieces?"

He looked at me.

"That is not one category."

Good.

"Unusual working gear."

"Same answer, fewer pieces."

"Things with unknown function?"

"Worse buyers. Worse sellers. More arguing."

"Cheaper?"

"Sometimes."

"More dangerous?"

"Sometimes."

I smiled.

"Excellent market."

"You are nineteen?"

"Unfortunately."

"You ask like you're sixty."

I went still for half a second.

Too long.

He noticed.

Of course.

Then he laughed.

"That's not praise."

Good.

I exhaled.

"Thank you."

He pointed at ordinary clamps.

"Buy something you can afford."

"Never."

"Then stop blocking customers."

Also fair.

I left.

No lock.

No deposit.

No dramatic new opportunity waiting three stalls away.

This was important.

I walked downhill.

The market felt different now.

Not because it had changed.

Because I had.

Five copper in my room was not "some money."

It was five copper.

A number inside a market where useful things appeared at eighteen and twenty-six and disappeared when actual buyers arrived.

That did not make me poor.

I had food.

House.

Work routes.

No immediate cash emergency.

It made me undercapitalized.

Much more insulting.

At home, Lyssa looked up before I said anything.

"Sold?"

"Yes."

"To you?"

"No."

She nodded once.

Not sympathy.

Good.

Cala asked, "Did you lose money?"

"No."

"Then fine."

Also good.

Tavi looked devastated.

"Ceiling thing?"

"Gone."

"Forever?"

"Probably not forever as a category."

He frowned.

"That's not what I asked."

Smart child.

"That one is gone."

He accepted tragedy better than I did.

Lyssa returned to sewing.

I stood there.

"You're not going to say anything?"

"You want me to?"

"No."

"Then no."

I went upstairs.

Five copper on shelf.

Oval where it belonged.

Rest stone boxed.

Holdfast wrapped high.

None of them had transformed into eighteen copper while I was away.

Rude.

I opened notebook.

Yesterday:

LOAD LOCK / USED / 18 C

Today I added:

DEMO OBSERVED

HOLDS SAND LOAD AGAINST RAIL

GRAY-WHITE TOOTH EDGE RESPONSE UNDER LOAD

RED RELEASE TAB STUCK UNTIL STRAP NEARLY FULLY UNLOADED

AT SLACK: RELEASE CLEAN

SELLER DID NOT FORCE TAB

NO RESET-UNDER-LOAD TEST

NO LOAD RATING

Then:

CRUTCH HYPOTHESIS NARROWER

POSSIBLE ONLY WHERE I CAN UNLOAD DELIBERATELY

NOT RESCUE

NOT FALL SUPPORT

NOT "MOBILITY" GENERALLY

GOOD.

I stared at that.

Then wrote:

SOLD 18 C TO ROOF WORKER / SPARE POSITIONING USE

He had not given name.

Good.

Then:

USED WORKING SPECIALTY GEAR <20 C MAY MOVE FAST IF USE IS CLEAR

Not law.

Seller opinion plus one example.

I added:

ONE EXAMPLE.

Old me had survived long enough to become suspicious of conclusions.

Current me was finally learning to be suspicious of wanting.

I turned page.

Money.

5 C CASH

26 C VALE

I almost wrote:

NEED 18 C

Wrong.

That was yesterday.

The object was gone.

I crossed the blank thought before it became ink.

What I needed was not eighteen copper.

I needed enough capital that the next useful thing did not become a spectator sport.

Different.

Harder.

Better.

I wrote:

CAPITAL TARGET: ENOUGH TO ACT BEFORE OPTION CLOSES

Then stopped.

Too vague.

I hated vague.

I tried again.

OPERATING RESERVE: 5 C

NEXT PURCHASE RANGE OBSERVED: ~18 C

That was factual.

Then:

DO NOT DESTROY RESERVE FOR BAD HOLD TERMS

Also factual.

Then I sat back.

The load lock had taught me something by leaving.

Annoying.

I would have preferred the object.

But that was probably why the lesson worked.

I looked at five copper.

Still mine.

Not enough.

Not nothing.

Outside, somebody shouted about onions.

Downstairs, Lyssa's flat line clicked off.

Tavi ran into something and denied it immediately.

The world continued.

I closed the notebook.

Next time, I wanted to be the buyer.

That was different from wanting to be lucky.
