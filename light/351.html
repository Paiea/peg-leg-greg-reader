TEXT READER · CHAPTER 351

THE DARK LINE

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The flat line died halfway through a sleeve.

Lyssa pressed the brass tab again.

Nothing.

She pressed it harder.

Still nothing.

I looked up from the dining table.

"I don't think force improves electricity."

"It's not electricity."

"Then force may work."

She looked at me.

"Helpful."

I had been doing arithmetic.

Not useful arithmetic.

Five copper.

Eighteen copper.

Thirteen missing.

The load lock was gone.

The numbers remained because my brain had apparently decided grief required bookkeeping.

I closed the little scrap page.

"Wall feed?"

Lyssa checked the white braided cord.

Still plugged into the plate near the front window.

She pulled it.

Looked at the two flat contacts.

Clean enough.

Plugged it back in.

Pressed brass tab.

Nothing.

Cala came in from the kitchen side carrying a pot.

"Lamp."

We all looked at the lamp.

Dark.

It was daytime.

This made the observation less useful.

Cala put the pot down and pressed the little wall switch beside the plate.

The lamp did not answer.

"Feed," she said.

There.

Solved at household level.

Not why.

Not where.

Enough.

Lyssa looked at the dark flat-line rail.

Then at the gray-green fabric spread across her new work table.

"Of course."

"Deadline?" I asked.

"Tomorrow morning."

Not the copper-knot case.

Different work.

A short coat sleeve and two matching cuffs for somebody who apparently believed wrists required urgency.

Lyssa had been working since breakfast.

The flat-line table had been open by the window.

Daylight was good.

The pale guide line had been better.

Now she had daylight and resentment.

Cala looked toward front door.

Then upstairs.

Then at wall plate.

"Check hall plate."

I did not move.

"Me?"

"You are closest."

I was not.

Tavi was under the work table.

Again.

But he did not count as infrastructure staff.

I stood.

Crutches under arms.

Right foot.

Turn.

The hall plate sat low on the wall near front door.

Not an outlet.

A little household indicator with three tiny glazed windows.

Usually one pale green.

One clear.

One dark.

Now all three were dark.

"Dead."

Cala nodded.

"Street."

Lyssa asked, "How do you know?"

Cala pointed with chin.

"House fault leaves red. Street fault leaves nothing."

I looked at indicator.

Useful.

Household magical literacy again.

Not mine.

"Always?"

Cala looked at me.

"Usually."

Healthy answer.

I approved.

She did not need approval.

Outside, someone shouted from two houses down.

"Feed's out!"

Excellent community diagnostics.

A second voice answered.

"We know!"

Confirmed.

Tavi crawled out from under table.

"Can I touch the plate?"

"No," three adults said.

He seemed offended by coordination.

Lyssa stared at her work.

"I can still sew."

"Yes."

"I can still cut."

"Yes."

"I can still measure."

"Yes."

She looked at me.

"Stop agreeing."

"I am supporting independence."

"Go support it elsewhere."

Reasonable.

The problem was not that the work became impossible.

The problem was that one very good tool had become a normal tool in about three days.

Then disappeared.

That was how convenience betrayed you.

You survived twenty years without something.

Then used it twice.

Then its absence became injustice.

I understood Lyssa perfectly.

I had once lived without magic.

Then with S-class magic.

Then without enough magic again.

Different scale.

Same rude principle.

Cala opened the little cabinet beneath the lamp.

Inside sat spare wick.

Old wick.

One small oil bottle.

And the two fresh lengths I had bought on the upper row.

Right size.

Both lengths.

Successful errand.

"Good," she said.

This was the full reward for my heroism.

She took one fresh length out.

The wall lamp was not only feed-powered.

I had noticed the oil cup before.

Never thought about it.

Now Cala opened a side latch, turned a little brass wheel, and pulled down a shallow glass chimney.

Behind the feed-light stone sat an ordinary wick channel.

Backup.

Of course.

Carrow had experienced infrastructure before.

"Dual source?" I asked.

"Lamp," Cala said.

"That is not an answer."

"It lights."

Hostile household epistemology was spreading.

She threaded new wick through the little channel.

Tavi watched closely.

"Can I do it?"

"Next time."

"Why not this time?"

"Because I want light today."

Cruel.

Cala added oil.

Struck spark.

The lamp took flame.

Small.

Yellow.

Very ordinary.

The feed stone beside it stayed dark.

"There," she said.

Lyssa looked at daylight coming through window.

"I don't need lamp yet."

"You will."

"Probably."

"Then it is ready."

Good household planning.

I returned to dining table.

Tried to reopen my arithmetic.

Could not.

Lyssa worked without the line.

She laid a wooden straightedge over fabric.

Marked chalk.

Measured twice.

Cut.

Slower.

Not dramatically.

A little slower.

The table still helped because it was broad, stable, hers.

The magical rail was one feature.

Not the table's entire value.

Good.

I hated when objects resisted becoming metaphors correctly.

There was another shout outside.

"Marker's black at south post!"

Cala went to door.

I followed because apparently I had become interested in outages.

Not enough to investigate.

Enough to stand at threshold.

Three neighbors were outside.

One old woman in slippers.

A man with flour on both forearms.

A girl carrying a bucket she had forgotten to put down.

At the end of our short row, near the corner, a narrow ceramic marker was fixed to a street post.

Usually it held a pale blue stripe.

Now the stripe was black.

Not dark.

Black like burned glass.

"That bad?" I asked.

Cala shrugged.

"Means closed."

"Closed by what?"

"Feed people."

"Not damage?"

"Could be damage. Could be them closing it."

Good.

Black did not mean explosion.

It meant unavailable.

The flour-armed man said, "West lane still has theirs."

Old woman said, "Then it is this branch."

Nobody panicked.

Nobody formed an adventuring party.

A boy farther down street was sent toward the junction office with two other errands.

This was apparently enough.

"How long?" Lyssa called from inside.

The old woman shouted back, "Until it comes back!"

Carrow public service announcement.

Lyssa swore softly.

I liked her more for it.

Cala went back inside.

I stayed at door another moment.

The black marker bothered me because I did not know it.

Not magically.

Practically.

In first life I had lived in places with feed grids, branch closures, overload marks, city repair systems.

Different cities.

Different eras.

Different conventions.

I remembered red flags.

Blue wax seals.

Little brass forks that rang when a branch destabilized.

A black ceramic stripe meant nothing to me beyond what Cala had just said.

That was correct.

I went inside.

No need to turn ignorance into a quest.

At midday the feed was still out.

Lyssa finished one cuff.

Then the second.

The sleeve remained.

Tavi had discovered that if he lay on floor and looked up through the work table braces, he could see the underside of the flat-line rail.

"There are marks."

Lyssa did not look.

"Do not touch them."

"I'm not."

"Then good."

I looked despite myself.

Small maker characters.

Same ones I had deliberately ignored when table arrived.

Still not my table.

Still not my problem.

Growth was repetitive.

I looked away.

Cala made lunch.

No magical cooking dependency apparent.

Pot.

Heat.

Food.

Civilization survived.

We ate at dining table while Lyssa's work table stayed open.

That was already normal now.

I noticed because I had spent an entire chapter rearranging furniture and the room had promptly stopped feeling rearranged.

That was how homes worked.

Changes became walls.

After lunch somebody knocked.

I expected feed worker.

It was not.

A neighbor girl, maybe fifteen, stood with a covered clay jar.

"My aunt says if Cala has spare lamp oil she can trade tomorrow."

Cala asked, "How much she need?"

"Just tonight."

Cala looked at our bottle.

Then at lamp.

Then poured some into a small cup.

No discussion of price.

The girl took it carefully.

"She'll bring back."

"Fine."

Door closed.

I looked at Cala.

"No copper?"

"Neighbor."

There.

Economy without market.

Annoying.

Useful.

I had spent two days thinking about liquid capital.

Five copper.

Eighteen copper.

Opportunity windows.

And Cala had just moved lamp oil across a doorway because sometimes reserve was social instead of metallic.

I did not write this down.

I wanted credit for restraint.

No one gave it.

By afternoon the light outside changed.

Clouds.

Of course.

Lyssa's work slowed more.

Not because she could not see.

Because dark cloth under gray daylight was becoming unpleasant.

Cala lit the lamp early.

Yellow light filled front half of room.

Not as bright as feed lamp usually was.

Enough.

Lyssa moved her table slightly closer.

I helped only by moving one chair out of route.

Then stopped.

No management.

No efficiency lecture.

No invention.

She knew her work.

She measured sleeve mouth.

Chalked.

Cut.

Pinned.

Unpinned one section.

Did it again.

I asked, "Would the line actually save much time here?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

She looked at me.

"Enough."

"That is a unit now?"

"Yes."

Fair.

I sat with my notes.

Not the money page.

A different page.

Old Hessa counts.

53.

53.

115.

I did not practice.

The outage did not create permission.

No independent magic.

That rule had become boring.

Good rules often did.

I put page away.

The feed came back near evening.

Not dramatically.

The hall indicator clicked once.

One glazed window turned pale green.

The street marker outside changed from black to dull blue over maybe ten seconds.

The wall lamp's feed stone gave a faint white glow even though oil flame was still burning.

Cala immediately turned wall switch off.

Then extinguished oil flame.

No ceremony.

Lyssa pressed brass tab.

The flat line returned.

Thin.

White-blue.

Perfectly straight.

She stared at it.

"I missed you."

I said, "That is how dependence starts."

"Quiet."

She aligned the sleeve.

Marked two points.

Then stopped.

"Actually."

She unplugged the table.

The line vanished.

I looked at her.

"What are you doing?"

"Checking."

She used wooden straightedge.

Measured same points.

Compared.

One mark matched.

The other differed by maybe half a finger width.

She frowned.

Plugged table in.

Line returned.

She realigned fabric.

The cloth edge itself was not straight.

There.

The line had not been wrong.

Her assumption about the edge had.

She laughed once.

Not happy.

"Good."

"What?"

"Good that feed failed?"

"No. Good that I checked."

"Ah."

She moved chalk mark.

"The line makes it easier to be confidently stupid."

I stared at her.

"That is an excellent sentence."

"Do not write it down."

I was already reaching for paper.

She pointed scissors at me.

I stopped.

Respect.

The sleeve took another hour.

Finished before full dark.

Lyssa folded it with cuffs.

Set all three pieces in a cloth wrap.

Deadline survived.

Not because magic saved her.

Not because I solved infrastructure.

Because she had a straightedge, lamp, table, experience, and enough annoyance to keep going.

The feed line was useful.

Not foundational.

That mattered.

Dinner happened with table folded.

Again.

Tavi told us he had discovered five marks under it.

Lyssa told him he had discovered the underside of furniture.

Cala asked if anyone had heard what broke the feed.

No one had.

The neighbor boy came back later and said only that junction office had closed our branch for repair and reopened it.

"Repair what?" I asked.

He shrugged.

"Didn't say."

Perfect.

No mystery earned.

No mechanism claimed.

After dinner I went upstairs.

Five copper remained where I had left it.

I counted none of it.

That was progress.

Maybe.

I opened my notes anyway.

The load lock page stared back.

SOLD.

I added nothing.

Then below the old capital line I wrote:

RESERVE IS FOR BEING ABLE TO CHOOSE

I stared at it.

Too broad.

Too wise.

Suspicious.

I crossed out BEING ABLE TO.

RESERVE IS FOR CHOICE

Still terrible.

Crossed that out too.

Finally:

KEEP 5 C FOR NOW

There.

Operational.

Not philosophy.

Downstairs, Lyssa laughed at something Tavi said.

Cala told someone to stop climbing a chair.

The wall feed hummed faintly through the plate.

Not enough to hear unless I listened.

The house had gone dark for most of a day.

Nothing important had become mine.

Nothing important had been lost.

No copper moved.

No magic count changed.

Lyssa still met her deadline.

The load lock was still gone.

Tomorrow could contain money again.

Today had contained a lamp.

Apparently that was allowed.
