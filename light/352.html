TEXT READER · CHAPTER 352

THE COUNT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Money came the next morning.

Not thirteen copper.

Obviously.

The universe had standards.

It came as a boy carrying a blue wooden token and asking whether Greg lived here.

I was eating bread.

This felt increasingly like how employment worked.

Cala opened the door.

The boy looked maybe twelve.

Hair tied badly.

One sleeve rolled higher than the other.

Blue token in one hand.

"Greg?"

I raised bread.

"Unfortunately."

He looked at me.

Then at Cala.

Then back at me.

"Merren's counting shed needs a third hand."

I swallowed.

"Who is Merren?"

"Merren."

Excellent start.

"What counting shed?"

"South quay returns."

That was more useful.

"Why me?"

The boy held out blue token.

Stamped into it were three narrow vertical lines and a little hook at the bottom.

Not city mark I knew.

Probably house token.

"Saret said you can read tags and don't make things up."

I stopped.

There.

Ch347 had developed consequences.

Not friendship.

Not standing employment.

One sentence passed sideways through city labor.

Good.

"Saret recommended me?"

"Merren asked east gate if they knew temporary counters."

Better.

Bounded.

"What am I counting?"

"Returns."

"That word has many crimes inside it."

The boy shrugged.

"Crates. Pieces. Seals."

"Magic?"

"Some."

Dangerous answer.

Lyssa looked up from her work table.

The flat line was on again.

Civilization restored.

"Pay?" she asked.

The boy looked at her.

"Three copper. Meal. Full day."

I looked at him.

"One day?"

"One day unless second wagon comes before dark. Then maybe tomorrow too."

"Maybe is not employment."

"Merren said one day."

Good.

Three copper.

I had five.

Eight after.

Still ten short of yesterday's dead opportunity.

Which did not matter because yesterday's opportunity was dead.

This was exactly the kind of arithmetic I wanted to stop doing.

So naturally I did it immediately.

"What does third hand do?"

"Read return slips. Match crate marks. Count loose pieces. Call mismatch."

"Move crates?"

"No."

"Open sealed crates?"

"No."

"Lift anything heavier than paper?"

"Maybe small pieces."

"How small?"

He looked at my crutches.

"Merren said if you can't carry it, don't."

I liked Merren already.

Cala asked, "Where?"

"South quay. Blue shed behind return wall."

"Before what bell?"

"Second."

I looked at bread.

Then at boy.

"I need ten minutes."

"I can wait outside."

"You do not need to."

"I have two more tokens."

Ah.

Not special.

Wonderful.

He left.

Lyssa said, "Three copper."

"I heard."

"You are going."

"Probably."

"Why probably?"

"Because I am trying not to become a man who accepts every coin-shaped sound."

Cala said, "It is counting."

"Yes."

"You can count."

"Usually."

"Then go count."

Household strategic council complete.

I finished bread.

Did not take the rest stone.

Did not take holdfast.

Did not take anything magical except the oval already living in the shoulder bag.

I considered removing it.

Then left it.

Different job.

No route restriction.

No reason to dramatize bag contents.

Five copper stayed upstairs.

The South quay smelled like wet rope, river mud, old wood, and commerce trying to hide all three.

I had been near it before.

Not this return yard.

The main quay ran long and loud beside dark water.

Loaders moved sacks.

Porters shouted numbers.

A narrow-bellied freight boat sat low under bundled reed mats.

Two cranes turned slowly over the water.

Not magical cranes.

Wood, rope, men.

One had a little pale-stone brake plate near its drum.

Magic used where useful.

Not everywhere.

Good.

The return wall was exactly that.

A long brick wall separating the main quay from a yard filled with goods that had apparently disappointed somebody.

Blue shed behind it.

Three vertical white lines painted on door.

Same as token.

I handed token to a woman at a waist-high desk.

Not Merren.

She had narrow eyes, dark braid, and a pencil tucked behind one ear.

"Greg?"

"Yes."

She looked at crutches.

Then at a stool beside desk.

"Sit when you need. Do not block lane."

"Excellent."

"Merren is inside."

The blue shed was larger than expected.

Not one room.

A roof over three open bays.

Bay one had sealed crates.

Bay two had open crates and sorting tables.

Bay three held loose equipment arranged in ugly rows.

Hooks.

Lamp housings.

Ceramic feed plates.

Lengths of braided conduit.

Two boxes of brass contact tongues.

Ordinary magical infrastructure pieces.

Some cracked.

Some clean.

Some still carrying chalk tags.

I felt my brain lean forward.

No.

Work first.

A thick man in a blue vest waved me over.

Gray beard cut short.

One knee wrapped under trousers.

Merren.

Probably.

"You read?"

"Yes."

"Fast?"

"Usually."

"Add?"

"Dangerous question."

He did not smile.

"Saret says you stop when tag is wrong instead of deciding what it meant."

That was oddly flattering.

"That is true."

"Good."

He handed me a board.

Three sheets clipped to it.

Top headings:

RETURN LOT
SOURCE
SEAL
EXPECTED
FOUND
HOLD

"Today is municipal returns and contractor rejects."

"Difference?"

"Source line."

"Not procedure?"

"Not for you."

Excellent.

"My job?"

"Bay two. Ressa opens. Durn counts physical. You read expected and mark found after Durn calls. If count differs, do not fix arithmetic. Mark HOLD and call me."

"Why three people?"

Merren looked at me.

"Because one person lies to himself. Two people agree with each other. Three people annoy everyone."

I smiled.

"Institutional wisdom."

"Also insurer requires it on sealed returns over ten copper book value."

There.

Actual reason.

"Do I know book value?"

"No."

"Do I need to?"

"No."

"Beautiful."

He pointed at a chalk line painted across floor.

"Stay this side unless Ressa says move. Fork carts cross behind you."

"Fork carts?"

A low four-wheeled platform rolled past.

No animal.

A man walked behind it holding a long brass handle.

The platform moved with a quiet hum.

Two pale stones sat under its rear axle.

Not autonomous.

Assisted.

"Those."

"Understood."

"If bell rings twice, stop counting."

"Why?"

"Cart crossing."

"If bell rings three?"

"Feed fault."

"Four?"

"Fire."

"Five?"

Merren stared.

"If someone rings five, leave."

Fair.

Ressa turned out to be the narrow-eyed woman from front desk.

Durn was younger than me by maybe two years and built like every barrel had personally insulted him.

He wore fingerless gloves.

"Crutch man?"

"Counting man today."

"Good. I hate writing."

"I hate lifting."

Partnership achieved.

First crate was easy.

Return lot 41.

Source: North Lamp Cooperative.

Seal mark matched.

Expected: twelve feed-switch housings.

Ressa cut seal.

Opened lid.

Durn counted.

"Twelve."

I marked FOUND 12.

No hold.

Second crate.

Eight ceramic wall plates.

Eight found.

Third.

Twenty brass contact tongues.

Durn counted nineteen.

I looked at expected.

Twenty.

"Again," I said.

He counted.

"Nineteen."

Ressa looked at me.

I marked FOUND 19.

HOLD.

Called Merren.

He came.

Did not accuse anyone.

Did not look under table dramatically.

He checked seal strip.

Checked source line.

Asked Ressa whether seal was intact before cut.

"Yes."

Asked Durn count.

"Nineteen twice."

Asked me expected.

"Twenty."

Merren wrote one note.

Moved crate to side.

"Next."

That was it.

No missing-tongue conspiracy.

No secret smuggling ring.

Just nineteen where paper said twenty.

I respected this job.

By midmorning we had twelve lots done.

Three holds.

One because count mismatch.

One because the seal mark had an extra notch not on slip.

One because a returned heating plate was the right model but wrong size.

That last one caused Durn to argue.

"It says six-inch."

"It is six-inch."

Ressa put a little brass gauge over it.

The plate did not fit.

"Seven."

Durn looked offended.

"Looks six."

I said, "This is why civilization invented gauges."

He looked at me.

"You always talk like that?"

"Unfortunately."

Ressa marked hold.

At first rest, I sat outside bay on stool.

South quay moved around us.

Fork cart bell twice.

People stopped lane.

Cart crossed.

Then work resumed.

No one explained the entire port to me.

Good.

Merren brought meal packets.

Bread.

Soft cheese.

Half an apple.

Apparently temporary labor across Carrow had standardized around food wrapped in paper.

I supported this policy.

He sat on a crate across from me.

"You worked east gate one day?"

"One."

"Why not stay?"

"It was one-day work."

"Good answer."

"Was there another?"

"People hear 'city gate' and invent careers."

I understood.

He ate.

I asked, "How often do you need temporary counters?"

Merren looked at me.

I immediately regretted sounding eager.

Capital face.

Terrible.

"Depends on returns."

"Weekly?"

"No."

"Monthly?"

"Sometimes twice. Sometimes none."

"Standing list?"

"Yes."

There.

Not a job.

Access.

Potentially more useful.

"How does someone get on it?"

"Finish day without making me regret token."

Reasonable.

"Pay always three?"

"For counters. More for seal clerks."

"What does seal clerk require?"

"Training. Bond. Two references."

Not today.

Good.

"Fork handler?"

"Training."

"Bay lead?"

"Years."

"Merren?"

"Bad decisions."

I smiled.

He continued eating.

This was useful hierarchy.

Not because I wanted to climb it.

Because money had structure.

Three copper for one day of accurate counting.

More pay attached to more trust, more training, more liability.

Exactly as it should.

I did not ask how much seal clerks made.

Restraint.

Afternoon wagon arrived late.

Not second wagon.

The first one had apparently been delayed.

This meant our one-day work still had work inside it.

We moved to bay three.

Loose equipment returns.

These were worse.

Not because magical.

Because loose.

Expected sheets described families, counts, condition grades.

Twelve conduit clamps, grade B.

Six lamp stones, inactive.

Three regulator cups, cracked.

Eight wall indicators, housing intact.

Nothing rare.

Nothing mysterious.

Mostly city hardware that had spent enough time in walls to become ugly.

One regulator cup caught my attention.

Dark bronze.

Three vent channels.

A little spiral scoring around base.

Old first-life recognition flickered.

Not artifact.

Not treasure.

A common branch regulator style from somewhere I had known later.

Maybe earlier model.

Maybe not.

I looked at slip.

EXPECTED: 3 REGULATOR CUPS / CRACKED / SCRAP HOLD

Durn counted three.

I marked three.

That was all.

This job did not pay me to become interested.

Next lot.

Then something actually happened.

Not magical.

Worse.

Durn said, "Four."

Expected said five.

I looked at table.

Four small white ceramic fuse sleeves.

Ressa said, "Again."

Durn counted.

"Four."

I marked found four.

Hold.

Called Merren.

Before he arrived, a man from another bay leaned over chalk line.

Blue apron.

"That lot came open. One was broken in wagon."

Useful information.

Dangerous timing.

I looked at slip.

Seal column blank.

Open return.

He continued.

"Just mark four. Break was known."

"I did mark four."

"Then no hold."

"Expected says five."

"Because slip was written before wagon."

Could be true.

Probably was.

Not my authority.

"Merren can clear it."

The man sighed.

"You're making work."

Durn looked at me.

Ressa did not.

She was very carefully not helping.

Good.

"Yes," I said. "Three people annoy everyone."

Merren arrived.

Blue-apron man explained.

Merren asked, "Break tag?"

The man paused.

"Driver had it."

"Where?"

"Don't know."

Merren looked at me.

"Expected?"

"Five."

"Found?"

"Four."

"Hold stays."

He walked away.

Blue-apron man swore quietly.

Not at me exactly.

At process.

I understood.

Five minutes later a driver brought a little red break tag.

Lot number matched.

Merren clipped it to sheet.

Cleared hold.

No one died.

No theft uncovered.

Paper caught up with reality.

That was apparently worth three copper.

Near final bell my shoulders were tired.

Not injured.

Just tired from standing, sitting, writing, moving between bays, holding myself upright longer than a household day.

I took rest when available.

Did not perform endurance for money.

Durn noticed once.

"You good?"

"Yes."

"Need stool?"

"I have stool."

He looked at stool beside me.

"Right."

Excellent medical care.

We finished thirty-one return lots.

Seven had holds at some point.

Five cleared with additional paper or recount.

Two remained held when day ended.

I did not know what happened to them.

Not my shift anymore.

Merren took my board.

Read sheets.

Checked my totals.

Not all of them.

Enough.

Then handed me three copper.

Actual coins.

One.

Two.

Three.

"You available if token comes again?"

There.

Not promise.

Not schedule.

"Usually."

"I will put name on temp list."

"Good."

"Doesn't mean work."

"I know."

"Doesn't mean same pay if job differs."

"Also good."

He looked at me.

"You like rules."

"I like knowing which ones exist."

"Same problem."

Maybe.

I put three copper in inner pouch.

Eight total now.

Not eighteen.

Not enough to recover yesterday.

There was nothing to recover.

Eight was simply more than five.

That needed to be enough.

On way home I passed a little street cart selling fried dough.

One bit.

I stopped.

Not because hungry.

Because I had money.

Dangerous.

One bit was not one copper.

Still money.

I looked at cart.

Then kept walking.

Not deprivation.

I had eaten.

I did not want fried dough enough.

This distinction mattered to me now.

At home Lyssa was folding finished coat pieces.

Cala had lamp open for some reason unrelated to yesterday's outage.

Tavi was trying to stack three cups.

Normal life.

Lyssa looked at me.

"How much?"

"Three."

"Good."

"I am now forty-four percent of a thing I cannot buy."

She stared.

"You need a different hobby."

"It is gone. I know."

"Then stop dividing yourself by it."

Fair.

I sat.

Shoulders complained quietly.

Cala asked, "Work again tomorrow?"

"Maybe if second wagon came. It didn't. Merren put me on temp list."

"Good."

"Not standing work."

"Still good."

There.

That was probably the right frame.

Not income solved.

Access improved.

One person now knew I could do accurate temporary counting.

That could matter later.

Or never.

Three copper mattered now.

I went upstairs after dinner.

Five old copper.

Three new.

Eight.

I counted once.

Allowed.

Then opened notes.

I wrote:

SOUTH QUAY RETURNS

TEMP COUNTER / 3 C + MEAL

NO STANDING WORK

TEMP LIST

Then:

3-PERSON COUNTING ON SEALED RETURNS

EXPECTED != FOUND -> HOLD

DO NOT FIX PAPER YOURSELF

That last one amused me.

Then money.

8 C

VALE 26 C

I stared at eight.

The urge arrived immediately.

How far to twenty?

How far to thirty?

What kind of opportunity did eight unlock?

What did fifteen unlock?

I almost started a table.

Stopped.

The load lock had taught one lesson correctly.

Capital mattered.

It did not mean every copper needed a destiny before I earned it.

So beneath eight I wrote:

KEEP IT LIQUID

That sounded annoyingly sophisticated.

I crossed out LIQUID.

KEEP IT

Better.

Then I added one more line.

MONEY CAN COME FROM BEING BORING CORRECTLY

I stared at it.

Too good.

Too broad.

Possibly true.

I left it.

Downstairs someone dropped a cup.

Tavi shouted that it was not broken.

Cala shouted back that this was not the same as success.

Lyssa laughed.

I put eight copper away.

Tomorrow did not owe me another three.

That was fine.

Today had paid.
