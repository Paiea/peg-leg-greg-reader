TEXT READER · CHAPTER 354

THE HIGH SHELF

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The problem with putting dangerous things out of a child's reach was that children considered reach a temporary condition.

I learned this the next morning.

Tavi had moved a chair.

Not toward the shade unit.

That would have been simpler.

He moved it toward a spool of red string on the shelf below it.

I came upstairs and found him standing on the chair with one foot on the seat, one knee against the back, both hands reaching upward, and his entire body arranged according to the engineering principle of eventually falling.

"No."

He froze.

Not guilty.

Offended by interruption.

"I need the red one."

"Get down."

"But I can almost get it."

"That is not a supporting argument."

"If I just move the chair little more, I can."

"That is the opposite of a supporting argument."

He looked over his shoulder.

The chair shifted under him.

I moved faster than dignity preferred.

One crutch planted.

Other crutch sideways against the chair leg.

Right hand on his shirt at the back.

"Down."

"I'm not falling."

"You have begun negotiating with gravity. Down."

He climbed down.

I moved the chair away.

Then looked up.

The Three Reeds shade unit sat on the high shelf.

Inactive.

Family card beneath it.

The holdfast sat farther over, wrapped, ring sideways.

Rest stone boxed on the lower high shelf.

All of them supposedly out of Tavi reach.

Except the red string had been out of Tavi reach too.

This was a useful demonstration.

I hated useful demonstrations.

"Why did you need red string?"

He held up the spoon carrier.

The carrier had developed wheels.

Not good wheels.

Two wooden buttons threaded on a little stick beneath one side.

One button was larger than the other.

The spoon still sat in the center like a passenger who had made several bad life choices.

"Blue string bends too much. Red string is stronger."

"Is it?"

"It looks stronger."

Excellent materials science.

"Ask next time."

"You were downstairs."

"Then ask downstairs."

"But I was upstairs."

There it was.

Tavi did not have Greg-style dry answers.

He had logistics.

Messy, sincere logistics.

"You moved a chair to climb near things you are not allowed to touch."

"I wasn't touching those."

He pointed upward.

"I know."

"I was touching string."

"I know."

"So why are you mad about the other things?"

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

Because his question was not actually stupid.

"I'm not mad about you touching them. You didn't. I'm saying the shelf is not safe enough if you can get that close by moving a chair."

He looked at chair.

Then shelf.

"I can move bigger chair too."

"Thank you, Tavi."

"The kitchen one is taller."

"Please stop helping."

"And the little table if I put it on the chair."

"Absolutely not."

He frowned.

"I didn't do it."

"Good. Preserve that record."

Cala appeared at the top of the stairs.

She looked at chair.

Looked at Tavi.

Looked at me.

Then looked at the shelf.

She did not ask what happened.

This was because she had eyes.

"Downstairs," she told Tavi.

"But red string."

"I will get the string. Downstairs."

He went.

Not happily.

Not dramatically.

He took the spoon carrier with him and narrated its wheel problem all the way down.

"The left one is not really left because when it turns around then it is the right one but it still sticks and if I use red string I think the spoon can pull it instead of the chair pulling it because the chair is too heavy and also I need another button but not the big button because that makes it crooked."

His voice faded.

Cala watched him go.

Then pointed at the shelf.

"No."

I leaned on both crutches.

"I agree."

That surprised her enough to pause.

"Good."

"I would like credit for agreeing quickly."

"No."

Reasonable.

She walked to the shelf but did not touch anything.

"What happens if one falls?"

"Depends which."

"That answer means no."

"The shade unit is inactive. The holdfast is inactive. The rest stone is boxed."

"What happens if one falls?"

I looked at them.

The honest answer was irritating.

"I don't know."

"Then they do not live above where people walk."

Simple.

Correct.

I had been thinking about reach.

Not drop path.

Different problem.

"Agreed."

"And not above your clothes."

"Why clothes?"

"Because if something leaks, heats, stains, sparks, or does whatever old magical rubbish does, I do not want your shirts becoming part of the test."

Cala had more imagination than I preferred.

"Also agreed."

She looked around my room.

This was not a large room.

Bed.

Chair.

Small table.

Shelf.

Clothes.

Shoulder bag.

Crutches when I was not using them.

Several decades of first-life experience had not prepared me for the storage consequences of being nineteen and economically irresponsible in a small room.

"Where?" I asked.

"Not my problem."

Then she stopped at the door.

"Actually it is partly my problem because this is my house."

"I was going to say that."

"No, you were not."

"I was going to think it."

"Find somewhere safe before dinner."

She left.

I looked at the shelf.

Three objects.

Four if I counted the oval, which lived near my bag because I carried it often.

I counted the oval.

Of course I counted the oval.

The problem had stopped being ownership.

It was inventory.

Not market inventory.

Physical inventory.

Where things existed when I was not thinking about them.

This had not mattered when I owned one weird object.

Then two.

Then three.

Apparently four was where a home became a warehouse with opinions.

I moved the red string first.

Downstairs.

Tavi accepted it without comment because he had already moved on to attaching the wrong button.

Then I spent an hour discovering that our household contained many storage spaces and almost none of them belonged to me.

Kitchen cabinet.

No.

Cala did not even have to say it.

Under bed.

Too easy for Tavi.

Top of wardrobe.

Same chair problem, plus drop path.

Behind clothes chest.

Damp wall.

No.

Inside clothes chest.

Clothes.

No.

Shoulder bag.

Could not fit holdfast and I needed the bag for ordinary life.

I briefly considered wrapping the objects in bedding and storing them in a corner.

Then remembered the sentence DO NOT ENCLOSE ACTIVE CORE and became angry at an inactive object for limiting imaginary storage methods.

It was inactive.

Enclosure was probably fine while inactive.

Probably.

I did not enjoy probably enough to build a household system around it.

Lyssa came in during this process carrying a folded length of dark cloth.

She stopped.

My bed had become an artifact staging area.

Holdfast wrapped.

Rest-stone box.

Shade unit inactive.

Family card.

Oval wrapped separately.

Three ordinary cloth pieces labeled in charcoal because I had apparently become a clerk in my own room.

"What are you doing?"

"Failing storage."

She put her cloth on chair.

"Why are they all out?"

"Tavi moved a chair."

She looked at shelf.

Then at me.

"Did he touch anything?"

"No. Red string."

"Ah."

That was all.

She understood immediately.

"Cala said they can't stay high."

"Also drop path."

"Yes."

"You both have become safety professionals overnight."

"You bought five copper of old magic yesterday."

"One old magic."

"You already had the other old magics."

"Different old magics."

She sat on the chair.

"That does not improve the sentence."

I looked around again.

"Do you have any idea?"

Lyssa considered.

Not instantly.

That mattered.

She did not exist to solve Greg problems.

"There is the cedar chest in the back room," she said eventually.

"Cala's winter things."

"Not that one. The smaller one under the folded frames."

I did not know there was a smaller cedar chest under the folded frames.

This was because other people had lives.

"What's in it?"

"Nothing useful. Old account books, broken curtain hooks, some lamp paper, probably dust."

I looked at her.

"Lamp paper?"

She looked at me.

"No."

"I only reacted to noun."

"You reacted with your entire body."

"What lamp paper?"

"I don't know. Old household papers. I saw a lamp drawing once when I was looking for a buckle pattern. It could be a receipt. It could be instructions for a lamp we do not own. It could be somebody drawing a lamp because they were bored."

"Interesting."

"Storage first."

There.

Lyssa was getting better at managing my brain despite not being my manager.

Dangerous.

"Does chest lock?"

"There is a key hole."

"Key?"

"Ask Cala."

I went downstairs.

Cala was cutting onions.

My onions.

At least the purchase had generated household value.

"There is a smaller cedar chest in the back room?"

She did not look up.

"Yes."

"Does it lock?"

"Maybe."

"Do we have the key?"

"Maybe."

"You sound like me."

"That should frighten you."

She finished one onion.

Wiped knife.

Went to a drawer.

Not a key drawer.

A drawer containing string, chalk, three small nails, folded paper, a spoon with no matching set, and at least seven things that proved every household eventually became Tool Lane.

She moved two items.

Found a small iron key.

"Try this."

"Why do you know where that is?"

"Because I live here."

A devastating explanation.

The back room was mostly household overflow.

Folded bed frame against one wall.

Two baskets.

A rolled mat.

Broken stool that had somehow earned storage instead of execution.

And beneath two folded wood frames, a small cedar chest bound at corners with dark iron.

Not magical.

I checked.

Not because I had reason.

Because my life had become like this.

Lyssa helped me move the folded frames.

Not the chest.

I could not safely move the frames alone while on crutches.

She took one end.

I used one crutch and one hand on the other.

Awkward.

Slow.

No speech about independence.

Just furniture.

Chest lid opened.

Dust.

Account books.

Curtain hooks.

Two old lamp chimneys wrapped in paper.

A packet of household instruction cards tied with faded blue string.

I stared.

Lyssa said, "Storage."

"You are cruel."

"Storage."

We emptied the chest onto a low table.

Not discarded.

Sorted.

Account books stayed together.

Hooks into a small box.

Lamp chimneys to upper household shelf.

Instruction cards remained tied.

I did not open them.

This was a physical act of maturity so painful I expected applause.

None came.

The key fit.

Turned with resistance.

Lock caught.

Good.

Cala inspected chest.

"Where will it sit?"

"Back wall."

"Not against wall."

"Damp?"

"Yes."

We set two ordinary wood blocks beneath and behind it so air could move.

Then came the harder question.

What went inside?

Not the oval.

I carried it enough that locking it away defeated purpose.

Also its known behavior was not active danger while wrapped.

Probably.

Again.

I hated that word.

The rest stone stayed boxed.

Into chest.

The holdfast stayed wrapped, ring sideways.

Into chest, with cloth around base so it could not roll or strike the stone box.

The shade unit went in last.

Inactive.

I hesitated.

"Card says don't enclose active core," I said.

Cala looked at me.

"Is it active?"

"No."

"Are you going to activate it in the chest?"

"No."

"Then put it in the chest."

I did.

But not wrapped tight.

Set upright.

Handle folded.

Vents unobstructed anyway because habits were cheap.

Family card went into a separate paper envelope on top, not under the object where oil or heat residue might damage it later.

Lyssa noticed.

"You are already planning for it to leak."

"I am planning for paper to remain paper."

"You bought a bad lantern and became a librarian."

"Artifact ownership has layers."

"You have problems."

"Also layers."

Cala said, "Key."

I handed it to her.

She looked at my hand.

"Why are you giving it to me?"

"Because if I keep it in my room, Tavi can find it."

"He can find things in my room too."

Fair.

"Then where?"

She thought.

This became a household security conference about a key the size of my smallest finger.

Tavi joined halfway through because he heard his name.

"I don't find your things."

Cala said, "You found the red string."

"That is everybody string."

"You moved a chair."

"Because it was high."

"That is why we are talking."

He looked at chest.

"What's in there?"

"Things you don't touch," I said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He looked offended.

"I didn't touch them."

Again.

True.

Important.

I was turning a near-miss into an accusation he had not earned.

"You're right," I said. "You didn't."

His face changed immediately.

Not smug.

Just less defensive.

"But you could have fallen near them, and I don't know what happens if they fall. That's my problem, not yours."

He considered.

"Can I move chairs?"

"For sitting."

"What if I need something high?"

"Ask."

"What if nobody is there?"

"Wait."

He hated this answer.

I could see him trying to build alternatives.

"What if it is important?"

"Then call someone."

"What if it is really important and nobody hears?"

"Then don't build a tower."

He frowned.

"I wasn't building a tower."

"Future policy."

Cala said, "No furniture towers."

Tavi sighed with the exhaustion of a child living under tyranny.

"Fine."

The key ended up on Cala's key ring.

Not because she became artifact custodian.

Because she already had a key ring and I did not.

I could ask for it.

That created friction.

Good friction.

An object that required the chest required another person if I wanted access while home.

I did not love this.

Which meant it was probably safer.

"What if I need something when you're out?" I asked.

Cala looked at me.

"Plan."

Disgusting.

I had expected artifact constraints like sunlight, blood, mana cost, temperature, activation words.

Instead I got Cala.

Possibly stronger.

By afternoon my room looked normal again.

That was the strangest part.

Shelf had clothes and ordinary things.

No bad lantern.

No wrapped holdfast.

No industrial stone box.

The artifacts still existed.

Just not in my visual field.

This changed my brain more than expected.

When they sat on shelves, each one produced a little unfinished thought whenever I looked up.

Holdfast.

Fixture.

Rest stone.

Safe system.

Shade unit.

Rated lamp.

Now the room contained a wall.

A shirt.

My bag.

Nothing asking to be solved.

I sat on bed.

This was suspiciously peaceful.

Lyssa came in later for her folded dark cloth.

She looked at empty shelf.

"Better."

"I hate it."

"Why?"

"I can't see them."

"That's how storage works."

"What if I forget what I own?"

She stared at me.

"Greg."

"Yes."

"You have four weird things."

"Three in chest. Oval in bag."

"You are not going to forget."

"Collections expand."

There.

She set the cloth under her arm.

"Are you planning to buy more?"

I paused too long.

"That was not a difficult question."

"I am planning to encounter more."

"That is not what I asked."

"I would like the freedom to make good decisions."

Lyssa took a breath.

Not angry.

Longer than her usual quick jabs.

"Okay. Then this is the part I mean. I don't care that you like these things. I care when the plan becomes 'I found something interesting, therefore the house has to absorb it.' Yesterday it was five copper. Today it was shelves and Tavi and a chest we were using for papers. The next one could be bigger, or hot, or loud, or something you can't carry with your crutches. You can't keep deciding only whether the object itself is worth the price. You have to decide whether you have somewhere for the whole problem it brings with it."

I listened.

Because she was right.

Annoyingly complete.

Also because she had not said I should stop.

That mattered.

"Whole problem," I repeated.

"Yes."

"Purchase price plus storage, transport, repair, compatible equipment, people in the house."

"And whether Cala wants old magic under her winter blankets."

"There are no winter blankets in that chest now."

"You know what I mean."

"Yes."

She looked at me.

"I'm not telling you not to buy things."

"I know."

"Good. Because I don't want this to become you telling people I told you not to buy things."

"I would never weaponize household nuance."

"You absolutely would."

Fair.

She left.

I sat with the phrase whole problem.

Did not write it immediately.

That would make it sound like Lyssa had delivered wisdom for my notebook instead of having an opinion about living in the same house as my increasingly stupid collection.

Later, though, when I updated the narrow scrap, I wrote:

ARTIFACT STORAGE: CEDAR CHEST / LOCKED / BACK ROOM / BLOCKED OFF DAMP WALL

CALA HAS KEY

OVAL STAYS WITH BAG

NO ACTIVE OBJECTS IN CHEST

PAPERS ABOVE OBJECTS / SEPARATE

Then:

SHADE UNIT STILL NEEDS RATED LIGHT CONTEXT

And below that:

3 C CASH / 26 C VALE

I stopped.

Then added one more line.

NEXT OBJECT COSTS MORE THAN PRICE.

That was close enough to Lyssa's point without stealing her sentence.

I looked at it.

Not a rule against buying.

A rule against pretending ownership ended at payment.

Good.

Downstairs, Tavi had finally made the spoon carrier roll three handspans before one button came off.

He shouted for everyone to look.

We looked.

The spoon tipped sideways.

The red string held.

Tavi threw both hands up.

"It worked!"

"Partly," I said.

He turned on me.

"No. It worked. The wheel fell after. That's different."

I paused.

Then nodded.

"Fair."

He grinned.

Useful distinction.

I did not write that one down.

Not everything in the house belonged to my system.
