TEXT READER · CHAPTER 378

THE PASSENGER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Being on a boat was easier when nobody expected me to get on or off it.

This was my first major finding of field work.

Kel handled stern.

Vessa watched river ahead.

Maro watched river everywhere else.

Dask watched gauges.

Sori watched Dask.

I sat with record case wedged against right thigh and watched reeds.

Excellent job.

Reeds were tall enough to hide houses.

Sometimes they did.

A roof would appear behind green wall, then chimney, then whole little riverside house on posts, then disappear again.

Some had narrow docks.

Some had cages hanging beneath docks just above water.

Fish traps, probably.

Could ask.

Did not.

I was growing.

Tavi would hate it.

River after Glasswake still carried wrong light.

Not dramatic like Merecross.

Thin pale seams moved under brown-green water, usually near shade.

Every so often one climbed reed stalk for few inches and vanished.

Maro noticed me watching.

"Residue."

"I wasn't asking."

"Didn't say you were."

Maro pointed toward next bend.

"Out here it catches different. Less cut stone. More root, mud, old channel glass."

"Channel glass?"

"There. Asking face."

Fuck.

He smiled slightly.

"Old flood deposits. Small reflective mineral lenses mixed into clay. Some natural. Some from older works upstream. Depends where."

Dask looked up.

"Calling all of it channel glass is imprecise."

"Calling all of it anything else takes afternoon."

"Classification matters."

Sori said, "Not to passenger."

Passenger.

There.

I liked it.

Nobody needed me for next twenty minutes.

Maybe thirty.

I leaned against side and watched world move.

That used to bother me.

First life, late enough, idle travel became wasted time.

Train mana.

Review route.

Check equipment.

Sleep if efficient.

Eat if needed.

Talk only if useful.

There had always been something I could turn into preparation.

Because there was always another fight.

Another rank.

Another contract.

Another thing that would kill someone if I had been lazy at wrong hour.

Now I had a fat account in a bank nobody else could see.

Not power exactly.

Proof.

I had been good once.

Very good.

Strong enough that entire categories of fear stopped feeling permanent.

That did not mean this body could cash every check old me had written.

It couldn't.

Bronze.

One leg.

External effects unreliable enough I still remembered every clean one.

Shoulders that got sore.

Hands that blistered.

Mana that ended.

But account existed.

Method.

Judgment.

Memory of what improvement felt like from inside.

I knew, somewhere under all current inconvenience, that if life cornered me badly enough I could start withdrawing.

Train harder.

Study narrower.

Turn days into repetitions.

Turn repetitions into reliable effects.

Turn reliable effects into combinations.

Fix body problems one by one where they could be fixed.

Work around ones that couldn't.

Climb.

Maybe not into same man.

Probably shouldn't.

But into some version of him.

Enough.

That knowledge made laziness feel safer.

Not stupidity.

Not negligence.

Room.

I did not need to squeeze every day until it screamed.

If I needed more from myself later, I knew where more lived.

That was worth something.

Retirement fund.

Do not touch unless world becomes expensive.

I smiled.

Sori looked over.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You smiled at reeds."

"Good reeds."

She returned to papers.

"Passenger."

Perfect.

Then river narrowed.

Kel stopped humming.

Hadn't noticed he was humming until he stopped.

Vessa shifted forward.

Maro put hand on gunwale.

Not alarm.

Attention.

I sat up.

Ahead, two skiffs waited along west bank.

Not tied.

Held by poles.

One carried baskets stacked under wax cloth.

Other carried three people and a goat.

Goat looked furious.

River between them and opposite bank seemed normal.

Mostly.

Except a line of reeds lay flat across water.

Not broken.

Bent.

All pointing upstream.

Kel said, "Root drag."

Maro said, "Maybe."

Dask sighed.

"Thank you."

Maro ignored him.

Vessa called to nearest boatman.

"How long?"

Older woman standing with pole answered.

"Started after first bell. Opened twice. Closed again when grain boat tried middle."

"Bottom snag?"

"Nothing on pole."

"Current pull?"

"Not enough to explain reeds."

Kel slowed us before waiting boats.

"We hold."

Fine.

Field adventure.

Waiting in boat.

Goat screamed.

I respected it.

Maro took narrow brass disk from black case.

Not artifact exactly.

Tool.

Maybe magical tool.

Everything here had categories I did not know yet.

He hung disk from cord over water without touching surface.

Disk turned quarter circle.

Stopped.

Turned back.

Dask wrote time.

Maro moved three feet downstream and repeated.

Different response.

Vessa asked, "Passable?"

"Not enough yet."

Boatman with goat said, "I crossed this every day for nine years."

Kel said, "Then waiting one morning won't ruin streak."

"Goat disagrees."

Goat screamed again.

Kel nodded.

"Strong position."

I looked at flattened reeds.

Old me supplied possibilities automatically.

Subsurface flow reversal.

Root mat catching return.

Something pulling mana gradient.

Predator.

Buried structure.

Artifact.

None earned.

So I shut up.

That was competence too.

Maro took another reading.

Then frowned.

"It isn't stationary."

Vessa: "Moving which way?"

"Downstream. Slow. Maybe."

Dask said, "Your first point changed twelve degrees."

"Yes."

"Second changed five."

"Yes."

"That is not same movement."

"Correct."

Dask looked almost pleased.

Vessa turned to Kel.

"Bank route?"

"No landing for cart connection until Red Hook. Skiff can pole shallows west if we unload two cases and walk bank thirty yards. Mud unknown."

My favorite phrase.

Mud unknown.

Vessa looked at me automatically.

Then didn't ask.

Good.

I was passenger until needed.

Maro said, "Give it ten minutes. If drift continues, center may clear."

We waited.

I ate dried apple.

Goat watched me.

I offered none.

Boundaries.

Eight minutes later, reeds began lifting.

Not together.

West side first.

Then center.

Upstream-pointing bend relaxed until stalks stood again.

Water never visibly changed.

Maro's disk stopped turning backward.

Kel looked at older boatwoman.

She nodded.

"I'll take first."

Vessa said, "We'll take second."

No Greg solution.

No need.

Older woman poled through center slowly.

Nothing happened.

Basket boat followed.

Then goat boat.

Goat screamed halfway across but seemed unrelated.

Our turn.

Kel moved us through.

At middle, my left crutch shaft gave tiny buzz where it lay secured beside me.

I noticed.

Said, "Crutch shaft vibration. Brief. Middle channel. No contact with water."

Dask looked immediately.

"Which crutch?"

"Left."

"Duration?"

"Less than one breath."

Maro said, "Any right?"

"Not noticed."

"Don't reproduce."

"Wasn't planning to."

Dask wrote.

Then we were through.

That was it.

I liked that too.

World could be strange without owing me explanation.

Alder Basin appeared after another hour.

First thing I saw was roof.

One enormous low roof, green with moss, stretched along riverbank like somebody built warehouse for weather.

Then basin opened.

Not town exactly.

Not city.

A bowl of water and work.

River widened into broad shallow fan around stone islands.

Dozens of narrow channels split and rejoined between them.

Walkways crossed on timber trestles.

Buildings sat higher than Merecross, most on stone feet or thick wooden piles.

Ropes ran everywhere.

Not decorative.

Working lines between docks, roofs, cranes, drying frames.

Long racks held pale translucent sheets that flashed when sun hit them.

Fish?

Glass?

Leather?

Asking face.

I restrained myself.

Good Greg.

Boats moved constantly.

Flat-bottom cargo punts.

Little one-person reed skiffs.

Two broad ferry platforms pulled along fixed ropes rather than rowed.

One platform carried cow.

Cow calmer than goat.

Alder Basin smelled like mud, smoke, boiled grain, fish oil, and something sharp-sweet I couldn't place.

Maro breathed in.

"Home."

I looked at him.

"You're from here?"

"Near. West shelf."

Dask said, "He means six miles west."

"Near."

Sori gathered papers.

"Nobody ask Maro where anything is unless you want answer involving what used to stand there twenty years ago."

Maro said, "That's how location works."

Kel aimed skiff toward survey landing.

Survey landing here was three poles, floating plank, and sign tilted enough to insult literacy.

CARROW had theatre.

Merecross had paperwork.

Alder Basin apparently had ropes.

I liked it already.

Transfer off skiff went worse than transfer on.

Not dangerously.

Embarrassingly.

Floating plank sat lower than Merecross dock and moved sideways when I put right foot down.

I froze like taught.

Kel held forearm.

Waited.

Plank settled.

I moved.

Then shoulder bag caught one dock post.

I swore.

Sori said, "Graceful."

"Passenger."

"Former passenger."

Cruel.

I made land.

No applause.

Good.

Our road cart had not arrived.

Of course.

Vessa checked board at landing.

"Road delay at Reed Three. Axle inspection. Expected before fifth bell."

Kel frowned.

"Whose axle?"

"Doesn't say."

"Useful."

Sori: "It says inspection, not break."

"Still useful."

We had essential cases from skiff.

Camp bulk and more food on cart.

Vessa looked at sky.

"We have three hours before we need decide lodging fallback. Survey house first. Maro, office. Sori, custody. Kel, cart notice. Dask, gauges. Greg, with me."

Work again.

Alder Basin Survey House sat across two rope ferries and one raised plank lane.

I learned this because Vessa walked there.

Fast.

Normal Greg returned.

Meaning I said, "You know I have one leg, right?"

Without slowing she said, "Application mentioned it."

"Good. Worried paperwork missed detail."

"Keep up."

"Cruel employer."

"Temporary employee."

There.

Comfortable.

I kept up enough.

First rope ferry was simple platform with waist rail and overhead line. Operator pulled hand-over-hand while passengers stood.

I hated it immediately.

Not fear.

Geometry.

Moving platform plus crutches plus people who thought standing near exit made them arrive faster.

I planted wide.

Vessa watched once.

Didn't hover.

Good.

Across channel, market started.

Not central square.

Long strips along raised walkways.

Goods hung from rails because ground was mostly water or mud.

Bundles of reed fiber.

Fish smoked black.

Ceramic jars sealed with green wax.

Tools with broad wooden handles.

Small cages of pale insects that glowed faintly under shade cloth.

There.

Artifact pressure maybe.

Not artifact.

Interesting.

One stall had three metal objects under glass domes.

I looked.

Kept walking.

Retirement fund did not mean spending account.

Vessa noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You looked."

"I am capable of looking without buying."

"Merecross suggests otherwise."

"I bought nothing in Merecross."

"You tried to understand twenty-eight-copper object while owning sixteen."

"Free."

"Until it isn't."

Fair.

Survey House was old mill office converted badly.

One wall still had enormous wooden wheel axle entering through stone though wheel outside was gone.

Clerk behind desk looked at Vessa's papers.

Then me.

Then crutches.

Then papers again.

Not hostile.

Accounting.

"Record hand?"

"Yes," Vessa said.

"North Cut?"

"Tomorrow if route holds."

Clerk sucked teeth.

"East steps wet."

Vessa: "We have morning read."

"Morning read was before Basin spill."

There.

Vessa went still.

"What spill?"

Clerk pointed toward back board.

Fresh notice pinned crooked.

WEST SHELF SETTLING POOL 4
RETURN-LIME SLURRY RELEASE
SECONDARY CHANNEL CLOSED BELOW GREEN POSTS
CAUSE: VALVE SEAT FAILURE, UNDER REVIEW
NO DRINKING DRAW / NO WADING / NO NETTING

Maro had not seen because notice newer than his departure.

Vessa read twice.

"When?"

"Half bell ago."

"Volume?"

"Initial notice says six holding drums equivalent, maybe eight."

"Into which secondary?"

Clerk traced map.

Channel fed toward north access side.

Not directly North Glass Cut.

Near enough to matter.

Vessa asked, "Any Cut closure?"

"Not yet. Field reader sent."

Adventure.

Actually field now.

She looked at me.

I waited.

No switch.

Not yet.

Vessa said, "We're not solving spill. We need know whether route remains valid."

"Good," I said.

Clerk looked at me.

"You new?"

"Very."

"Keep that attitude. People come Basin and decide every wet problem belongs to them."

"I have enough problems."

He nodded approval.

Vessa started questions.

Closure boundary.

Expected reader return.

Alternate north route.

Camp options if delayed.

I took notes.

Clean.

No performance.

Then clerk mentioned something that made my pencil stop.

"Old settling line has three return gates. Two are ordinary. Third is retired glass-lock. If slurry reached that, they'll hold north channel until they know whether lock took charge."

Glass-lock.

I looked up.

"Took charge?"

Asking face fully deployed.

Clerk said, "Stored return. Old industrial artifact. Maybe artifact. Depends who writes inventory."

Maro entered behind us just in time.

"Which lock?"

"Four-C."

Maro's expression changed.

Not panic.

Interest with teeth.

"Four-C was decommissioned."

"Retired, not removed."

Dask, somehow also arriving, said, "Those are different words."

Sori behind him said, "Nobody encourage him."

I looked at Vessa.

She looked at notice.

Then crew.

"We wait for field reader. Nobody goes near closed channel."

Correct.

Maro said, "If Four-C took charge, discharge timing matters for Cut access."

"Then reader tells us."

"Yes."

He sounded unhappy.

Good specialists hated waiting too.

I wrote:

ALDER BASIN
RETURN-LIME SPILL
SECONDARY CLOSED
FOUR-C RETIRED GLASS-LOCK MAY OR MAY NOT MATTER
NO CLAIM YET

Then under it:

DO NOT VOLUNTEER FOR INDUSTRIAL ACCIDENT

Sori leaned over shoulder.

"Good note."

"Learned recently."

Cart arrived before field reader.

Axle inspection had been ours.

Nothing broken.

One wheel collar heating more than Kel liked.

He greased and reset it himself while swearing at road contractor's maintenance standards.

Camp bulk arrived intact.

Vessa secured two rooms at Survey House bunk annex under route-delay coverage.

Not inn.

Six bunks.

One room smelled like wet rope.

Other smelled like older wet rope.

I chose first.

Luxury.

By evening, field reader still hadn't returned.

Route decision moved to dawn.

That could have annoyed me.

Instead I had Alder Basin.

We were allowed two hours off duty after meal, with instruction not to cross green-post closures, not to get drunk enough to miss dawn, and not to buy anything that needed crew transport without asking.

Vessa looked directly at me for last one.

"This feels targeted."

"It is."

So I went market.

Alone.

Not far.

Raised walkways glowed with oil lamps and little insect cages.

Pale insects brightened when people passed close, then dimmed.

Children ran fingers along cage bars to make waves of light.

A woman sold waterproof paper by sheet.

A man sharpened knives using wheel turned by tiny water channel under stall.

Someone cooked flatbread on black stone.

Orin's list said nothing about it.

Promising.

I bought food with meal allowance? No.

Survey meal already eaten.

Did not need second dinner.

Cala would disagree.

I kept copper.

Then found metal objects under glass domes again.

Different stall from one I passed.

Three pieces.

One looked like bent nail.

One like flat brass tongue.

One like little iron cage with no door.

Seller sat reading.

I stopped.

She did not look up.

"If you ask what they do, answer costs nothing and is mostly disappointing."

"Excellent sales method."

"Keeps idiots short."

"What do they do?"

She looked up.

Maybe fifty.

One eye clouded white.

"Nail hums near active return-lime. Brass tongue warms near open pressure seam. Cage catches one kind of marsh light for about minute if it feels cooperative."

"Prices?"

"Two. Three. Seven."

Copper, presumably.

Cheap enough to matter.

Not cheap enough to impulse all.

"Documented?"

She smiled.

There.

Good question.

"Nail, yes. Basin Works surplus mark, retired because sensitivity inconsistent beyond ten paces. Tongue, workshop claim only. Cage, absolutely not."

"Why seven?"

"Because tourists like it."

Rational market.

Beautiful.

I pointed nail.

"Two copper for inconsistent industrial detector seems high."

"Then don't buy."

"Could be useful tomorrow."

"Then two seems low."

Also rational.

I liked her.

Did not buy.

Yet.

Because Survey crew probably had better instruments.

Because I had twenty-three copper and twenty-three copper Vale debt and did not need turn symmetry into destiny.

Because retired tool near current spill was exactly kind of thing my brain wanted to overvalue from timing.

I asked seller name.

"Hara Venn."

"Greg."

"Theatre?"

I stared.

"Why does everyone know?"

"You stand like performer when pretending not interested."

Insulting.

I left.

Passenger day.

No giant accomplishment.

No maximum output.

No magic.

New river.

New basin.

New problem that did not belong to me.

Potentially useful object I did not buy.

And somewhere north, a retired glass-lock might have swallowed enough magical industrial runoff to change our route.

I had reserves.

Money now.

Skill before.

Power eventually.

Most important, I did not have to spend all of any of them just because they existed.

Passenger.

For today, that was enough.
