TEXT READER · CHAPTER 298

THE CUSTOMER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

I woke up because my shoulder had opinions.

Not loud ones.

Persistent.

Roll wrong.

Pain.

Reach wrong.

Pain.

Exist ambitiously.

Pain.

Good morning.

Lark Spur bunks were narrow enough that turning over required planning anyway.

Across room, Dask slept on his back with mouth open.

Teeth quiet.

Either cured or plotting.

Sori had blanket over face.

Kel already gone.

Maro sat by window eating something from paper.

"What?" I asked.

"Breakfast."

"Looks like insulation."

"Oat cake."

"Same thing."

He held out half.

I took it with left hand.

Current shoulder wanted less work.

Patient.

Still.

Annoying.

Outside, Lark Spur had morning color.

Dust.

White bark.

Canvas awnings snapping.

Stable smell.

Signal mast with three arms all behaving themselves for once.

Market tables reopened before road crews finished breakfast.

Of course they did.

People liked money more than sleep.

Relatable.

I checked shoulder before doing anything stupid.

Forward lift better than yesterday.

Side lift still unpleasant.

Grip sore but functional.

No numbness.

No new swelling I could tell.

Right crutch still had tooth marks.

I ran thumb over deepest groove.

Ugly.

No crack.

Fine.

Vessa found me outside.

"How bad?"

"Less bad."

"Useful number."

"Seven bad yesterday. Five bad now."

"That is not useful."

"You started it."

She looked at range once.

"Low load again today."

"Yes."

"No practice."

"Yes."

"No fighting."

"Planning not to."

"Good plan."

There.

Authority preserved.

Then she looked past me toward market.

"You going back to brass thing?"

"Maybe."

"Why?"

Good question.

Not accusation.

So I answered.

"Because eight copper is enough that I should decide instead of pretending I'm not interested."

She nodded.

"Fair. We leave second bell."

One hour.

Customer.

Apparently that was today.

I went back to hardware table.

Young seller was there.

Same chin scar.

Same expression suggesting civilization had disappointed them personally.

The line quietener still sat under little square of glass.

Palm-sized brass.

Three notches.

Black-slash retirement mark.

Eight copper.

I did not touch.

Seller looked at me.

"You came back."

"Against everyone's advice."

"Good customers do."

"That sounds predatory."

"Market."

Fair.

I pointed at quietener.

"Show me maintenance card again."

Seller slid it over.

Stamped old district works card.

Short copper run.

Tone suppression tested under echo-return conditions.

Retired after weak true tone suppression contributed to missed warning.

No magic poetry.

Good.

"You said district stopped using them twelve years ago."

"This style. Some newer ones have split threshold plates."

"And cost?"

"More than you want."

"Try me."

"Forty-two copper new, if you can get order accepted."

I stared.

"I didn't want."

Seller shrugged.

"Told you."

Good market.

Eight for obsolete dangerous version.

Forty-two for modern safer one.

Rational.

I asked, "What exactly counts as short run?"

Seller leaned back.

"Card says under three relay lengths."

"Local length?"

"Yes."

"Which means nothing to me."

"Then don't install it."

"Not planning to."

"Then what are you buying?"

There.

Good seller.

I thought.

Not a solution.

Not crew equipment.

Not something I could legally slap onto station line.

Personal artifact-ish hardware with known rule: suppresses some false/echo tones on short copper, but risks suppressing weak true tone.

Potentially useful for experiments later.

Potentially useful around weird tone systems.

Potentially dangerous enough to be interesting.

Eight copper.

I had twenty-three.

Vale twenty-three.

Buying would leave fifteen.

Not poverty.

Not trivial.

Night Rest had been twenty-eight.

Step Keeper six.

Three Reeds five.

This fit current artifact tier.

Question wasn't can.

Worth.

I asked, "Can it be used disconnected?"

Seller's expression changed half degree.

Better question.

"Test bench, yes. Needs copper line in and out. Doesn't need active station."

"Minimum?"

"Handspan each side works for bench. But that's not same as field behavior."

"Good. Any residual response without tone source?"

"Not that card says."

"Any known interaction with reflective field cases?"

"No claim."

"Return-light?"

"No claim."

"Mana load?"

"Passive plate."

"Can it be opened?"

"Physically."

"Safely?"

"Depends who."

"Helpful."

"Accurate."

I liked seller more.

Bad sign.

"Name?"

"Tirren."

"Greg."

"I know."

I stared.

"How?"

"You fought hounds at Three Posts."

There.

Fuck.

"That was yesterday."

"Road's faster than you."

Of course.

Reputation.

Uneven.

Regional.

Not fame.

Still annoying.

"What did you hear?"

Tirren counted on fingers.

"One-legged survey hand. Crutches. Hit dog. Three people alive. Somebody said magic. Somebody else said mud."

"Last person smarter."

"Probably."

"And you know my name?"

"Medical cart stopped here. Pellin talks."

Human network.

Not prophecy.

Good.

I looked at quietener again.

"Seven."

Tirren laughed.

Actually laughed.

"No."

"It's retired."

"Yes."

"Known dangerous failure mode."

"Yes."

"Nobody sane installs it."

"Correct."

"Seven."

"Eight."

"You like it too much."

"You came back."

Damn.

I tried different angle.

"Maintenance card included?"

"At eight."

"Copper bench leads?"

"No."

"Two handspans scrap lead?"

Tirren thought.

"Nine with leads."

"Eight with leads."

"Eight and one bit."

Bits.

Unknown cursed economy lane.

No.

"Eight flat. You keep the bit."

Tirren narrowed eyes.

"That's not how bargaining works."

"It is now."

Long pause.

"Fine."

There.

I bought it.

Eight copper.

Greg twenty-three to fifteen.

Vale twenty-three.

Symmetry broken.

Good.

Sori would enjoy that less than me.

Tirren wrapped quietener in wax cloth, maintenance card separate, two short copper leads coiled around outside.

"Do not use it on live signal line unless someone who owns line says yes."

"Good rule."

"Do not decide false tone is safer than weak true tone."

"Better rule."

"Do not open plate with knife."

"Why?"

"Because people do."

"That wasn't answer."

"Spring leaf. Cuts fingers."

"There."

I paid.

Customer.

Actual artifact-ish acquisition.

Not hidden miracle.

Not bargain of century.

Dangerous obsolete tool with known use and known reason world stopped trusting it.

Perfect.

I put it in shoulder bag carefully.

Then immediately moved shoulder bag to left-side carry because right shoulder complained.

Perfect less.

At station, Sori saw purchase wrap.

"You bought it."

"Yes."

"Eight?"

"Eight."

"So fifteen."

"Do not say numbers like judgment."

"I am literally custody."

"Of Survey things."

"Emotionally broader."

Dask appeared.

"Can I see?"

"Later."

"Why?"

"Because we leave."

He looked injured.

"You bought experimental hardware and won't let me inspect?"

"Correct."

"Cruel."

"Growth."

Vessa checked wrap.

"Personal. Not field equipment."

"Yes."

"No live-line use under my assignment without local owner authorization."

"Yes."

"No testing while moving."

"Yes."

"Good."

Maro said, "I hate it already."

"You haven't seen it."

"You like it."

Fair.

Second bell.

We left Lark Spur.

Road toward Greyhook Rise changed fast.

Dry ridge climbed.

Marsh fell away behind.

White-barked trees thinned into low dark scrub.

Stone underfoot became red-brown and sharp-edged.

Wind stronger.

No reeds.

No water smell.

After days of wet country, dryness felt wrong.

My crutch tips clicked instead of sank.

Good.

Shoulder less good.

Kel carried one shared case again.

I carried only bag and self.

Greyhook road had old iron hooks hammered into stone every fifty paces.

Huge.

Rust-black.

Some empty.

Some held chains disappearing downslope.

I waited almost ten minutes before asking.

Personal best.

"What are hooks?"

Maro said, "Old freight anchors."

Dask added, "And storm line points."

"Same hooks?"

"Often."

"For wagons?"

Kel answered.

"Heavy freight on crosswind days. Chain wagon to next hook, move chain, repeat."

I looked down slope.

Steep enough.

"That sounds miserable."

"It is."

Good.

World had infrastructure for weather.

We climbed.

At first rise, wind hit hard enough to shove coat against body.

My balance hated lateral force.

Vessa saw.

"Need route adjustment?"

"Not yet. Shorter plants."

Right shoulder protested if I planted too far.

So shorter.

Current body again.

No pride.

At one iron hook, strips of cloth tied around base.

Not shrine.

Maybe warning.

Maro stopped.

"Wind cut ahead."

Kel looked sky.

"How bad?"

"Normal bad."

Dask said, "That is not category."

Everyone ignored him.

Wind cut was narrow saddle between two stone shoulders.

Road crossed exposed for maybe sixty yards.

Black chain ran waist-high along uphill side between hooks.

Local sign:

HIGH WIND
ONE HAND / LINE OR RAIL
NO LOOSE LOAD
WAIT ON RED FLAG

Flag green.

Fine.

One hand line.

Problem.

I had two crutches.

Maro said, "There is lower route. Adds half mile."

I looked at saddle.

Could do it.

Probably.

Right shoulder still compromised.

One-hand line meant one crutch and one foot, ugly.

Harness maybe.

No.

Vessa asked, "What do you want?"

There.

Not can.

Want.

I wanted upper route.

I also wanted not to be idiot two days after shoulder strain.

"Lower."

Dask looked at me.

No comment.

Good man.

We took lower.

Longer path cut behind stone shoulder through little ravine.

Protected wind.

More distance.

Less stupid.

Patient becoming annoying identity.

Halfway through ravine, we found wagon.

Not ours.

Small two-wheel freight cart tipped against wall.

One wheel off.

Driver sitting on rock with bandaged hand.

Young woman standing over broken axle pin swearing continuously.

Not short Greg swearing.

Long narrative swearing.

"I told him, I told him if that pin went twice in wet season it was going to go again because wet season isn't fucking magic that resets iron, and he says no no, Mora, you grease it and it forgives you, like iron has religion, and now look, now fucking look at it."

There.

Dialogue variance alive.

Kel stopped.

"Need help?"

Mora said, "Need new pin. Unless one of you is secretly blacksmith."

Dask almost raised hand.

Sori physically put hand on his sleeve.

"No."

Driver said, "We have spare blank. Need fit."

Kel checked.

"I can shave wood, not iron."

Mora pointed north.

"Greyhook yard has fitter. Two miles. Cart blocks half path but people can pass. We'll sit."

No emergency.

No Greg.

Except something under cart caught my eye.

Small metal disk in dirt.

Not theirs maybe.

I pointed.

"Yours?"

Mora looked.

"No."

Driver leaned.

"Old hook token."

Maro said, "Don't pick yet."

There.

Interesting.

Disk iron-gray, maybe two inches across, square hole center, one edge polished bright.

Half buried near old anchor socket in ravine wall.

Mora said, "Those wash out sometimes. Freight tally thing."

Driver corrected, "Storm tally."

"Whatever."

Dask crouched.

"Not same."

Sori said, "Of course not."

Maro looked at socket.

"Could belong old anchor system."

"Useful?" I asked.

"Maybe historically."

"Magical?"

"Maybe not."

Dask: "Could be reactive iron."

Mora: "Could be trash."

Best answer.

I did not pick it.

Not because scared.

Because local road had owner system.

Vessa asked Mora, "Road authority claim salvage?"

"Marked hardware goes Greyhook yard. Loose unmarked usually whoever, but old anchor pieces maybe Roads wants."

There.

No free loot.

Vessa marked location for road notice.

We continued.

Greg wanted disk.

Greg had just bought quietener.

Greg could survive wanting two things.

Growth.

Greyhook Rise appeared near fourth bell.

Not town.

Fortified work ridge.

Stone terraces cut into hill.

Wind towers with big slatted arms.

Freight yard.

Repair sheds.

Rows of anchor chains hanging from frames.

One long public stair climbed center ridge like challenge written personally at me.

No.

Side ramp existed.

Good civilization.

At entrance, road board listed upper return beds and something new:

**NORTH REACH SURVEY HOLD - WEATHER WINDOW PENDING**

Vessa read.

"That's us."

Good.

Greyhook station lead came out before we entered.

Tall woman in red coat with hair braided tight against wind.

"River Survey?"

Vessa handed papers.

Woman scanned.

"You're late enough to be interesting but not late enough to be problem."

"Three Posts delay."

"Heard. Hounds."

Her eyes found me.

Of course.

"Crutch man?"

"Unfortunately."

"Don't fight anything here. Wind does enough."

"Strong policy."

She introduced herself **Aven Ro**.

Greyhook road/survey liaison.

Different speech.

Fast, declarative, no patience.

"North Reach weather window opens maybe tomorrow second bell, maybe not. Upper return beds walkable. Final ridge isn't. Crosswind. We hold crews here until tower strips agree for full half bell."

Vessa: "Any alternate?"

"Lower freight tunnel, closed collapse inspection. West shelf mule line, cargo only and miserable. So wait."

There.

Adventure.

Weather.

Not enemy.

Aven continued.

"You have two nights authorized if weather holds bad. After that route order recalculates. Bunks six. Food ugly. Baths nonexistent. Market tiny. Repair yard excellent."

Repair yard.

I looked at crutch.

Aven noticed.

"That thing bitten?"

"Yes."

"Bring to Rusk. He repairs staff shafts, poles, crutch wood if hardware standard."

There.

Useful.

Not artifact.

Actual road life.

We entered.

Greyhook wind made everything louder.

Chains knocked.

Tower slats clapped.

People shouted from twenty feet because otherwise conversation belonged sky.

I liked it.

Shoulder hurt.

Quietener in bag.

Fifteen copper left.

Road north temporarily held by weather.

And somewhere behind us, an old iron token waited in dirt for Road authority to decide whether it was trash.

Customer.

I had bought one thing.

Not every thing.

That felt more dangerous than buying nothing.

Because now I had started.
