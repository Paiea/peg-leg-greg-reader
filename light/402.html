TEXT READER · CHAPTER 365

THE SHOPPER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Westbank had a market.

This was dangerous.

Not because of thieves.

Probably.

Because Mella Torr mentioned it while I was eating my second potato.

"Boat yard row sets tables after noon," she said. "Mostly hooks, cord, smoked things, old river junk. Since the second sun started, everyone's selling anything shiny for twice what it's worth."

I stopped chewing.

Sori saw.

"Finish lunch before you shop."

"I haven't said anything."

"You stopped chewing."

"Temporary condition."

Mella pointed spoon at me.

"You the artifact boy?"

"No."

"Dog boy?"

"Also no."

"Crutch boy?"

I looked down.

"Unfortunately evidence supports that one."

Mella nodded.

"There's a woman two doors past cooper who sells retired ferry hardware. Don't buy anything she calls lucky."

"Why?"

"Because she calls everything lucky."

Sori closed eyes.

"Mella."

"What?"

"You just gave him directions."

"He looked sad."

I had not.

Probably.

Vessa finished bread.

"We have the afternoon. Second observation before sunrise. Greg can look at junk if he remains available."

There.

Authorization.

Sori pointed at Vessa.

"You're worse."

"I'm not his mother."

"Neither am I."

"Then stop parenting his purse."

I liked Vessa more every day.

Maro drank tea.

"Fourteen copper."

I looked at him.

"Why do you know?"

"You talk."

"Apparently too much."

Dask had already unfolded yesterday's observation sheet beside his plate.

Vessa tapped it once.

"Eat first."

He folded it again.

Progress remained terrifying.

We spent the first part of afternoon doing actual work.

Unfortunate.

Fenner sent copies of local logs upstream with a boy who ran like roads were optional.

Sori compared five mornings of timing.

Dask drew the bridge and false-band boundaries until his paper looked diseased.

Maro walked Westbank shore with Toma Grell to ask about current, wind, insects, fish, ferry behavior, and every other thing fishermen could lie about with confidence.

Kel checked our cases and arranged pre-dawn mule hold.

Vessa spoke with Westbank road clerk about what happened after second outside-witness morning.

I got the clean-copy witness ledger.

My job was to turn six people's observations into something a stranger could read without accidentally inheriting our guesses.

Harder than fighting dogs.

Less biting.

Mostly.

I wrote:

**OBSERVED: FALSE WEST-FACING SUNRISE REFLECTION PRESENT WHILE PHYSICAL SUN VISIBLE EAST.**

Then:

**OBSERVED: PHENOMENON APPEARS TO REQUIRE CONTINUOUS RIVER-SURFACE CONTEXT UNDER CURRENT SIMPLE TESTS. COLLECTED BUCKET WATER DID NOT RETAIN FALSE REFLECTION.**

I stared at second sentence.

Too strong.

Crossed out **require**.

Changed to:

**OBSERVED: PHENOMENON WAS NOT RETAINED IN COLLECTED BUCKET WATER UNDER CURRENT SIMPLE TEST.**

Better.

Dask leaned over shoulder.

"Continuous surface context is still likely."

"Likely isn't observed."

"We could write probable."

"Could."

"Will we?"

"No."

He considered.

"Okay."

There.

I had broken him.

Or improved him.

Same silhouette from distance.

By midday my shoulder had gone from ache to low complaint.

Good.

Hand still tender where hound bite-force had traveled through wood into palm.

Also good.

Pain shrinking into memory.

Not gone.

I tested crutch wrap once outside.

Solid.

Then Mella yelled from inn door.

"Tables are up!"

Sori looked at ceiling.

"Why are you helping him?"

"Commerce."

Excellent woman.

Westbank market was not a market by Carrow standards.

It was twelve tables, three blankets, two carts, and one man selling eels from a barrel while loudly denying they were yesterday's eels.

"Fresh!"

Toma Grell, passing behind him, said, "They know your name."

The eel man ignored her.

Boat-yard row smelled like tar, wet rope, smoked fish, sawdust, and river mud.

Useful things everywhere.

Hooks.

Nails.

Copper rivets.

Waxed cord.

Net needles.

Pulley cheeks.

Float corks.

Old ferry tokens.

Bent brass plates.

A box labeled **SUN GLASS** containing ordinary green bottle shards.

I picked one up.

Seller said, "Caught wrong dawn in it yesterday."

"Did it stay?"

"No."

"Then what am I buying?"

"Memory."

"I have one."

He lowered price from one copper to three bits without me asking.

I put shard down.

No universal bit conversion.

Also bottle.

Two victories.

Kel found me near cooper.

"Vessa says stay in village."

"I am."

"She means buildings, not the road."

I looked at him.

"Oppressive leadership."

"You want coffee?"

"Yes."

He handed me cup.

Free.

Best price.

The retired ferry-hardware woman sat exactly where Mella said.

Two doors past cooper.

Table under blue cloth.

Woman maybe sixty, maybe forty after river weather. Hair white at temples, arms brown and ropey, left little finger missing first joint.

Sign:

**RIVA SORN**

**FERRY HARDWARE / OLD SIGNAL / RIVER SALVAGE**

Under it, smaller:

**NOT RESPONSIBLE FOR PREVIOUS OWNERS**

Promising.

Riva watched me approach.

"Dog boy."

"No."

"Twelve dogs."

"Zero."

"Less impressive."

"Safer for dogs."

She looked at crutches.

"Those kill twelve?"

"They participated in zero deaths."

"Shame."

Kel drank coffee beside me and refused to help.

Riva's table was better than shiny-bottle man.

Not because things looked magical.

Because they looked used.

A brass ferry bell clapper with one side flattened.

Three ceramic current tags.

A spool of black waxed cord.

Two palm-sized slate plates etched with parallel grooves.

An old route token with center hole worn oval.

A little silvered mirror in leather frame, cracked corner.

A copper cup with no handle.

A fist-sized cage made from six thin brass ribs around empty center.

That one caught eye.

Of course.

Riva saw.

"Lucky."

Mella had warned me.

"What does it do?"

"Keeps ferry lantern from going out in bad crosswind."

"How?"

"By being lucky."

"Actual answer."

Riva smiled.

"Old wick cage. Air enters low, leaves high. Brass warms, keeps draft from biting flame directly. Mundane. Two copper."

There.

Good seller.

"Then why lucky?"

"Because people pay more when scared."

Kel coughed into coffee.

I picked up one grooved slate plate.

"This?"

"Current reader."

Interesting.

"Meaning?"

"Old ferry type. Set flat at waterline. Wet it. Grooves show cross-current by how film pulls."

"Magic?"

"Barely, depending who you ask. Slate's treated. River men call everything magic when they don't want to explain surface tension."

"Does it work?"

"Yes."

"Why retired?"

"Because painted floats are easier to see from boat."

I turned plate.

Back had stamped marks.

**WS-F/9**

White Span Ferry maybe.

Riva said, "One copper."

One copper.

I had fourteen.

I did not immediately buy it.

Growth.

"Can you demonstrate?"

She looked offended.

"Yes."

Better.

She took shallow tray from under table.

Poured water from bucket.

Set plate at one edge.

Then dragged spoon through water sideways.

Film across grooves pulled visibly toward spoon wake, leaving darker wet lines on downstream groove edges.

Simple.

Useful.

Not magical enough to ruin day.

"Treatment wears?"

"Eventually."

"How know?"

"Water stops beading clean."

"Can renew?"

"River shops. Wax-mineral rub. Cheap."

"Any false readings?"

Riva looked at me properly now.

"Rain. Oil. Soap. Mud. Bad angle. Person who already decided what current should do."

Excellent.

"Last one expensive."

"Always."

I wanted it.

Problem.

Not because one copper hurt.

Because wanting cheap useful thing was how bags became heavy.

First-life me had owned rooms full of equipment.

Some useful.

Some theoretically useful.

Some purchased because I had once thought, **I could imagine a situation.**

A situation was not a reason.

I looked toward river.

Tomorrow we were literally observing surface behavior.

That was a reason.

Maybe.

But local crew already had current ribbons and floats.

That weakened it.

"White Span has these?"

Riva snorted.

"Had. That's from White Span."

"Why'd they retire them?"

"New float boards eight years ago. Easier from bridge. Plates still good close-bank."

"Why are you selling station property?"

Kel finally became interested.

Riva pointed at stamped slash beside code.

Tiny diagonal punch.

"Retirement mark. Bought lot legal. Ask Fenner if you enjoy paperwork."

I did not.

Sori would.

"One copper?"

"One."

"With renewal rub?"

"No."

"How much rub?"

"Two bits."

There was bits again.

I still didn't know conversion universally because world hated accounting.

"One copper with enough rub for one renewal."

Riva stared.

"You bargain badly."

"Why?"

"Because I would have given two renewals."

I stared back.

Kel laughed so hard he spilled coffee.

"Fuck you."

Riva smiled.

"One copper. Two renewals."

"Fine."

I paid.

Fourteen to thirteen.

There.

New object.

Small.

Useful.

Not destiny.

Riva wrapped plate in old cloth and added thumb-sized tin of gray wax-mineral paste.

"Don't use paste until beading fails. Too much makes grooves sluggish."

"How much?"

"Rice grain each edge. Rub thin. Warm with palm."

"Good."

She tapped cracked mirror.

"Lucky river mirror. Three copper. Shows drowned men before they drown."

"No."

"Two."

"No."

"One."

"You said three because scared people pay more."

"You're learning."

I left.

Kel said, "You bought one thing."

"Yes."

"I'm proud."

"Don't make it weird."

"Could've bought dead-man mirror."

"It was cracked."

"That the problem?"

"Among several."

We brought plate back to inn.

Sori inspected retirement punch before I even asked.

"Legal enough."

"Thank you, mother."

She looked at me.

"I will throw it in river."

"Then we can test current."

"I hate that answer."

Vessa examined plate.

"Personal hardware."

"Yes."

"You don't add it to official test tomorrow unless Fenner agrees."

"Yes."

"You don't use it as excuse to stand somewhere stupid."

"Define stupid."

"No."

Fair.

Dask appeared.

"Is that treated slate?"

I put it back in cloth.

"No."

"I can see it."

"Then why ask?"

"May I inspect it?"

There.

Progress again.

I handed it over.

He checked grooves without touching treatment surface first.

Even more progress.

"Old F-series ferry reader."

"Riva said."

"These are good."

"Retired."

"Good and retired can coexist."

There.

Artifact philosophy.

Or old people.

Same thing eventually.

Maro returned from river with Toma around third bell after noon.

He looked at plate.

"Had one as kid."

"Of course."

"Grandfather used it checking side channels."

"Did it work?"

"If he agreed with it."

Perfect.

Toma pointed at me.

"You bought Sorn's junk?"

"One junk."

"Good plate."

"You just called it junk."

"Both."

River culture understood artifacts better than scholars.

We spent rest of afternoon not doing anything important enough to become crisis.

That mattered.

I walked Westbank slowly.

Boat repair sheds had hulls upside down on blocks.

Children scraped old pitch from planks for coins.

A woman smoked tiny silver fish over alder wood.

Two men argued whether a boat named **MOTHER'S PATIENCE** had ever possessed either.

A shrine near upstream dock held river stones stacked in pairs.

No one explained it.

I didn't ask.

The Two Suns had already added a second painted yellow disk beside old heron sign.

Badly.

Mella was delighted.

"Tourists like bad paint. Feels authentic."

"Authentic to what?"

"Paying me."

Respect.

Late afternoon, messenger arrived from White Span.

Not emergency.

Lessa.

She walked in carrying a board and immediately stole bread from our table.

"Fenner says tomorrow's surface won't be as calm. North wind expected before dawn."

Dask sat up.

"How strong?"

"Enough to make little chop. Not enough to close ferry."

There.

New variable.

Vessa said, "Does local request still want observation?"

"More. Fenner says if wrong light needs calm water, maybe tomorrow tells us something."

Dask looked almost happy.

Lessa pointed bread at him.

"Don't."

He controlled face.

"I am not doing anything."

"You're thinking loudly."

Sori pulled clean sheet.

"Expected wind direction?"

"North to northeast. Starts weak before dawn, strengthens around sunrise if weather office isn't lying."

Maro said, "River runs east-southeast there."

Lessa nodded.

"So cross-ripple."

Greg brain moved.

Not locked.

Just interested.

If phenomenon required mirror-like surface, chop should break it.

If it appeared anyway and followed each small face of water, different evidence.

If it appeared only in sheltered patches behind piers, useful.

If nothing appeared, still useful but not proof.

One morning absence meant one morning absence.

Dask said, "We should establish chop before first light."

"Yes," Vessa said.

"And compare sheltered water under west bank."

"Ask Fenner."

"And perhaps place a controlled flat pan adjacent to river surface to test sky geometry without river continuity."

That was actually good.

I looked at him.

"That's better than bucket after."

He looked pleased.

"Yes."

Sori said, "Don't encourage him."

Lessa swallowed bread.

"Fenner has wash basin."

"Clean?" Dask asked.

"No."

"Can it be cleaned?"

"Probably."

"Material?"

"Metal."

"Reflective?"

"It's a basin, Dask."

"That does not answer."

"Then come look at stupid basin."

He did.

Of course.

Vessa sent Maro too.

I stayed.

That was growth.

Or shoulder.

Both.

Evening came slow over Lume.

From Westbank, physical sunset happened west like respectable celestial behavior.

No second sun.

No wrong reflection.

Just orange river and smoke.

I sat on inn step with new slate plate in lap.

Thirteen copper now.

Vale twenty-three.

Quietener eight.

Plate one.

Bath one.

Money moving.

Not disappearing.

Becoming things.

Some temporary.

Hot water.

Some durable.

Tools.

Some future obligation.

Vale.

That distinction mattered more than purse number.

First life, eventually, I had enough money that small purchases stopped being decisions.

I had thought that was freedom.

It was.

Also made it easier to accumulate stupid shit.

Second life had taught me something poverty had not.

Not **never spend**.

That was fear.

Spend where object changed options.

The quietener did.

Maybe.

The slate plate did in smaller way.

Hot bath changed one evening and next morning's shoulder.

Also worth money.

Different durations.

Different value.

Sori came outside.

"You're counting."

"Thinking."

"Same face."

"Thirteen."

"I know."

"Stop knowing my money."

"You announce purchases like military victories."

"One copper."

"Historic."

I held plate up.

"Useful."

"Probably."

"Rude."

She sat beside me.

For a while we watched river.

Then she said, "You know tomorrow might show nothing."

"Yes."

"Dask will be unbearable."

"Yes."

"Fenner will look personally betrayed by water."

"Probably."

"You?"

I thought.

"Write nothing."

She nodded.

"Good."

That was witness job.

But shopper job had been better.

More immediate.

Pay copper.

Get thing.

Cause and effect.

Beautiful.

River did not respect commerce.

Before bed, Vessa gathered us for ten minutes.

Tomorrow plan changed slightly.

Leave Westbank later than Kettle Vale departure because White Span only half mile away.

Arrive while still dark.

Measure wind/chop before first light.

Use same witness positions as morning where possible.

Fenner would place one cleaned metal basin on west-bank rise, filled from river before sunrise, left undisturbed beside sight frame.

Not to prove mechanism.

Just compare isolated surface with river surface during same sky.

No one would shade it, touch it, move it, or add objects during event.

Good.

Current slate plate could be offered as **supplemental local-approved observation only**, not core test.

I asked, "Worth it?"

Maro said, "If Fenner wants."

Vessa said, "Ask him."

Dask said, "Yes."

Nobody had asked Dask.

Sori wrote that down emotionally.

Not literally.

Probably.

Then Vessa looked at me.

"Shoulder?"

"Better."

"Hand?"

"Better."

"Sleep."

"Yes."

No argument.

There.

Definitely growth.

Upstairs, my room faced river.

Maro already asleep in other bed by time I finished checking bag.

Quietener wrapped.

Tavi's rock.

New slate current reader.

Renewal tin.

Knife.

Clothes.

Small pieces of a life temporarily carried instead of stored.

I put bag under bed.

Crutches against wall.

The reinforced bite marks caught moonlight from window.

Ugly.

Good.

Evidence.

Outside, Lume moved in dark.

Tomorrow it might reflect wrong sun again.

Might not.

Wind might break it.

Might reveal it differently.

Metal basin might show nothing.

Might show something.

Plate might be useful.

Might just become another object in bag.

I liked that.

Not uncertainty exactly.

Options.

I had thirteen copper.

A debt.

A repaired crutch.

A strange brass quietener I did not fully understand.

A one-copper river plate old ferries no longer wanted.

A home far south.

A river outside.

And tomorrow morning still unspent.

Not bad.
