TEXT READER · CHAPTER 368

THE EARNER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Bellweather paid me in the morning.

That was satisfying.

Not poetic.

Better.

Pera Lonn had three papers, two stamps, one clerk beside her, and the expression of a woman who believed money should only move after paperwork had suffered enough.

"Vessa Harn signed completion. Local extension verified. Four payable field days. Two copper each. Eight copper."

She pushed a small cloth pouch across desk.

I picked it up.

Weight.

Real.

Not theoretical money.

Sori said, "Count it."

"I trust bureaucracy."

Pera looked offended.

"Don't."

Fair.

I counted.

Eight.

Thirteen became twenty-one.

Vale remained twenty-three.

Close enough to annoy me.

Not close enough to matter.

Sori watched me put the pouch away.

"Don't."

"Again, haven't said anything."

"Trade basin. Twenty-one copper. Finish the road first."

"Purchases."

"Same posture."

Pera handed Vessa route packet.

"Sable Quay annex closes fourth bell. If you don't make it today, Rill House can stamp lodging. Road is clear through mile eight. Mile nine has freight-control rotation because east embankment work."

Kel asked, "Delay?"

"Depends carts. Foot crews pass faster."

"Mules?"

"Controlled batches."

Dask asked, "Mirror guild hours?"

Pera looked at him.

"I regret mentioning mirror guild."

Olden Marr, passing behind us with a ledger, said, "I mentioned it."

Pera pointed at him.

"Then I regret you."

He kept walking.

Bellweather remained excellent.

We left before second bell.

Twenty-one copper felt different in purse.

Not because weight.

Because choices.

At thirteen, some prices ended conversation.

At twenty-one, they became questions.

Still not yes.

Question.

Could afford did not mean should buy.

This was adulthood.

Terrible system.

East road out of Bellweather widened quickly.

Commercial road.

Two carts abreast in places.

Packed gravel instead of hard dirt.

Stone drainage.

Mile markers maintained enough to deserve numbers.

The Lume widened too.

At Bellweather it had been broad river.

East of Bellweather it started behaving like infrastructure.

Docks every mile or two.

Short loading ramps.

Tether posts.

Warehouses standing alone beside road for no obvious town.

Flatboats moved downstream under dark square sails, heavy enough that water climbed their sides.

One carried stacked blue ceramic jars.

Another carried timber.

Another something under canvas that smelled like pepper even from road.

I liked trade roads.

Things moved because someone wanted them somewhere else badly enough to pay.

Simple motivation.

Complicated world.

Around mile three we passed a roadside exchange board.

Not market.

Prices.

Freight listings nailed under awning.

**BELLWEATHER -> SABLE QUAY**

**BARREL SPACE**

**HALF CRATE**

**LIVE BIRDS EXTRA**

**NO OPEN OIL**

Kel stopped long enough to read transport rates.

Vessa said, "Considering cart?"

He nodded.

"Shoulder?" she asked me.

"Fine."

Not perfectly.

Fine.

Low ache only after first hour.

Hand mostly normal.

Current body was coming back online.

Kel said, "Walking keeps us flexible. Cart batch could save two hours if timing good."

Sori said, "Costs?"

"Assignment transport only if route need documented."

Vessa looked at road.

"No need yet."

Good.

We walked.

At mile five, trade road split around a low quarry cut.

Main carts took lower stone lane.

Foot traffic climbed upper shelf.

Sable Quay first appeared from there.

Not town.

Shape.

Far east, river opened wide enough to become silver-gray basin.

Buildings clustered on both banks and on low artificial-looking islands or piers reaching into water.

Masts.

Cranes.

Smoke.

Three bridges visible from miles away.

One high and narrow.

One broad.

One maybe rail or freight-only.

Dask stopped walking.

Sori said, "Walk."

"I am."

"Not currently."

Maro shaded eyes.

"Bigger than last time."

"When?" I asked.

"Nine years."

"How much bigger?"

"That east crane field wasn't there."

There.

World changing without us.

Good.

Sable Quay became destination instead of concept.

And then road made us pay for looking.

Mile nine freight-control rotation.

Pera had not lied.

East embankment work narrowed road to one reinforced lane between river drop and active stonework.

A red-cloth control post held maybe thirty carts.

Foot traffic could pass in side corridor.

Mules could not.

Our shared cases rode two pack mules.

There.

Delay.

Not crisis.

A control woman named **Tess Arlo** explained.

"Mule batch in thirty-five minutes if no westbound priority. Forty-five if stone train comes. Foot crew can go now, animals stay here with registered handler."

Vessa looked at Kel.

Kel looked at cases.

"I stay."

Sori said, "Custody?"

"I'll sign hold."

Vessa considered.

"We split?"

Normally I disliked split parties.

Stories punished them.

Life sometimes rewarded logistics.

Sori said, "Annex paperwork can start without physical cases if seal numbers logged."

Dask said, "Mirror guild is in Quay."

Nobody acknowledged him.

Vessa decided.

"Kel stays with mules. Sori stays for custody?"

Sori shook head.

"Kel can hold under transfer if control post countersigns. I should take records forward."

Tess said, "We countersign."

There.

Vessa assigned.

Kel with mules/cases.

Rest of us foot corridor into Quay.

We'd regroup at annex or Rill House depending timing.

Kel said, "Bring food."

"Specific?" Maro asked.

"Anything not pear."

Reasonable.

Foot corridor ran narrow against river side.

Guard rope on one side.

Stonework on other.

Workers set dressed blocks into embankment using tripod hoists and shouted cadence.

Not magical spectacle.

Labor.

One foreman used a little brass level that flashed green when stone sat true.

I noticed.

Kept walking.

Twenty-one copper did not require purchasing every useful thing visible.

Important reminder.

A worker shouted, "One-leg, watch rope!"

I did.

Loose hauling line crossed path knee-high for two breaths.

I stopped.

They lifted.

I passed.

No pride.

No performance.

Normal accommodation between strangers doing work.

Good.

Sable Quay west gate wasn't gate.

It was tax arch.

Two stone pylons framing road with hanging boards listing fees by wagon, beast, barrel, dock class, and probably phases of moon.

Foot entry free.

Excellent.

Inside, sound changed.

Carrow had crowded streets.

Sable Quay had systems colliding.

Dock bells.

Auction calls.

Cart wheels.

Boat horns.

Saw mills.

Metal striking metal.

People yelling berth numbers.

People yelling prices.

People yelling because everyone else was yelling.

Buildings leaned commercial.

Tall warehouses, narrow offices, inns stacked over shops, guild signs, weighing houses, rope halls, chandlers, repair yards.

The river basin occupied center of everything.

Water wide enough that opposite bank looked like another district instead of across street.

Floating docks formed lanes.

Cargo cranes swung over barges.

One barge carried an entire small house.

Dask said, "Mirror guild."

I said, "Survey annex."

He looked at me.

"Yes."

Pain.

We found annex first.

Good people.

Three-story stone building beside west basin office.

Sign:

**LOWER LUME SURVEY ANNEX**

Below:

**TRAFFIC / SIGHT / FLOW / INCIDENT RECORDS**

Inside smelled like ink and wet coats.

A clerk named **Nema Vos** took Vessa's packet and immediately became alarmingly competent.

She was maybe thirty, dark curls tied high, sleeves rolled, six pencils behind one ear.

Six.

Dask looked at pencils.

She noticed.

"Don't."

He looked impressed.

Vessa summarized White Span and Bellweather in five minutes.

Nema asked good questions.

"White Span six consecutive mornings?"

"Yes."

"Two outside-witness?"

"Yes."

"Broad band calm morning, sheltered patch choppy morning?"

"Yes."

"Bellweather archive comparable record?"

"None in forty-two surviving indexed years."

"Cause?"

"Unknown."

"Connection to signal anomalies upstream?"

"Not established."

Nema nodded.

"Good. Keep it that way until reason."

Dask and I both liked her.

Dangerous.

She read Ira's extension authorization.

"Your crew can close regional extension here if you want. Or annex can issue lower-Lume continuation if we find reason."

There.

Choice existed.

Not automatic.

Vessa asked, "Any reason yet?"

Nema looked toward ceiling like records lived above it.

"Maybe. Not the exciting kind."

Best kind.

She pulled one thin folder.

"Three ferry captains reported unusual dawn glare this week near **Glassmouth Reach**, two miles east of Quay basin. Not wrong-direction sunrise. Not multi-minute band. Just glare strong enough to obscure channel marks for seconds at a time. Could be ordinary angle because water level dropped four inches."

Dask inhaled.

Nema held up hand.

"Could be ordinary angle."

He closed mouth.

"We're checking tomorrow anyway. If you're still here, outside eyes useful. If not, your White Span packet stands alone."

Good.

No railroad.

Nema stamped receipt.

"Your cases?"

"Delayed at mile nine," Vessa said.

"Fine. Bring by third bell."

Sori handed seal numbers.

Nema checked.

"Lodging?"

"Not arranged."

"Survey rate at Dockhouse Seven if rooms open. If full, Rill House west road or private inns. Food not covered unless assigned overnight work."

Economy.

Excellent.

Then she said dangerous thing.

"Mirror guild is two streets north."

Dask smiled.

Sori looked at her.

"Why would you tell him?"

Nema looked at Dask.

"Because I want to see what happens."

Chaos clerk.

We had maybe one bell before Kel arrived.

Vessa said, "One bell. No purchases for assignment. Personal time. Stay in west basin district."

There.

Freedom.

I did not immediately go to market.

Important.

I went with Dask to mirror guild.

Different.

Guild occupied narrow brick building with black glass windows.

Sign showed silver oval crossed by blue line.

Inside, front room displayed mirrors.

Not household mirrors.

Signal plates.

Sight plates.

Inspection mirrors.

Heat-reading dark glass.

Tiny convex mirrors for machine housings.

Long narrow pieces mounted in brass.

Prices.

Oh.

There were prices.

**FOG SIGHT PLATE, NEW - 18C**

**LAMPSIDE GHOST CHECKER - 11C**

**SILVER-BACK FIELD MIRROR - 9C**

**SPLIT-ANGLE CALIBRATION PAIR - 27C**

**RIVER DAWN PLATE, BLUE-BACK - 34C**

There.

Twenty-one copper suddenly became medium money.

Useful.

Not enough.

Exactly right.

A guild woman approached.

**Elya Senn**, badge said.

Forties.

Shaved head.

Silver dust embedded in cuffs.

"Survey?"

Dask said, "Yes."

I said, "Personal browsing."

She looked at my crutches, then bag, then Dask's face.

"Which one is dangerous?"

"Him," I said.

Dask said, "Probably him."

Fair.

Elya asked what we wanted.

Dask launched into Bellweather plate backing.

I wandered.

Not far.

Still listened.

Elya explained blue-black dawn backing reduced off-axis glare and improved contrast, yes, but no, plates did not create light.

Normal.

Then I found used shelf.

Danger.

Not junk.

Guild-inspected used.

Tags had condition grades and reason retired.

Better danger.

A palm-sized folding inspection mirror with edge chipped.

4c.

A two-piece sight bracket missing one clamp.

3c.

A narrow smoked plate retired for backing peel.

6c.

A little brass housing containing three rotating glass slits.

Tag:

**PARALLAX FINDER, PF-3**

**USED / GRADE C**

**10C**

**FAULT: CENTER DETENT SLIPS UNDER VIBRATION**

I picked it up only after checking sign said handling allowed.

Heavy for size.

Three slits aligned different angles when ring turned.

Old field tool for checking whether apparent offset came from observer position or target displacement.

Interesting.

Very interesting.

Ten copper.

I had twenty-one.

Vale twenty-three.

Half my liquid almost.

No.

Not immediate.

Good.

Elya appeared beside me.

"That one bites people who love clever tools."

"Because detent slips?"

"Because ten copper feels cheap compared to thirty-six new."

"How much repair?"

"If only spring, two to four. If ring channel worn, six to nine. If axle bent, throw it at enemy."

"Tested?"

"Bench stable. Vibration fault confirmed. Axle appears straight. No guarantee under field abuse."

There.

Rational price.

"Use?"

"Line-of-sight comparison. Surveyors. Machine aligners. Mirror fitters. People who think their eyes are trustworthy until tool insults them."

I wanted it.

Problem.

Could it change options?

Yes.

Would I use it beyond current river mystery?

Probably.

Could I afford ten?

Yes.

Should I spend nearly half current liquid on another bounded problem while owing Vale twenty-three?

Different question.

I put it back.

Elya nodded.

"Good."

"What?"

"Most people who touch that one buy it because they already imagined saving thirty-six."

"I imagined twelve different uses."

"Worse."

"Agreed."

Dask arrived.

Saw PF-3.

Made noise.

I said, "No."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face."

There.

Justice.

We left without buying.

Twenty-one survived mirror guild.

This deserved medal.

Outside, Sable Quay market district spilled north.

I could see rows of awnings.

Hear auction bell.

Smell spice, hot metal, fried dough.

I did not go.

Not yet.

One bell meant one bell.

Crew before market.

Painful maturity.

Kel arrived at annex near third bell with mules and cases.

Delay had been forty-two minutes.

He looked annoyed.

"Stone train."

"Food?" he asked.

Maro handed him fried bread he'd bought in street.

Kel immediately forgave civilization.

Cases checked in.

Seal intact.

Nema countersigned delivery.

Vessa closed route segment.

Then asked crew what we wanted.

Not work assignment yet.

Today could end here.

Tomorrow optional Glassmouth observation if we remained.

Or extension could close and we could start south/west return network.

Or wait for new orders.

Sori said, "We should stay one night at least. Cases just arrived."

Kel said, "Agreed."

Maro said, "Glassmouth is two miles. Might as well look if annex wants outside eyes."

Dask said, "Mirror guild opens first bell."

Everyone ignored him.

Vessa looked at me.

Sable Quay outside.

Twenty-one copper.

A ten-copper parallax finder sitting on used shelf.

Market I hadn't entered.

Home much farther west than I'd planned weeks ago.

Shoulder almost normal.

Purse healthier because I had worked.

This mattered.

Not luck.

Not found money.

Not inheritance.

Labor.

Field days.

I had been useful long enough that road paid me for remaining useful.

I liked that more than expected.

"Stay," I said.

Vessa nodded.

"One night. Glassmouth dawn if annex confirms. Then reassess."

Good.

We got Dockhouse Seven rooms.

Survey rate.

Two rooms, three beds each.

Food personal.

I bought a hot meat bun from street for one bit.

Bits remained unspecified.

Copper stayed twenty-one.

Then, finally, I walked market.

Not to buy.

To look.

Mostly.

Sori came with me because she did not trust "mostly."

Sable Quay market was not twelve tables.

It was streets.

Tool row.

River row.

Salvage row.

Textile court.

Spice lane.

Auction yard.

Used magical hardware behind wire screens with licensed sellers.

Prices that made twenty-one copper look like childhood allowance.

Forty.

Sixty.

One hundred twenty.

Silver marks.

Gold once, on a locked case I did not even approach.

There.

Economic ceiling moved upward.

Not because world got more expensive.

Because I had reached market where expensive things lived.

Important distinction.

Sori watched me realize.

"Happy?"

"Extremely."

"You're not buying anything."

"Also true."

"Growth."

"Temporary."

She laughed.

We stopped at one licensed salvage window.

Inside sat a squat iron ring the size of dinner plate with six engraved notches and a tag:

**LOAD-SHARE COLLAR / RETIRED DOCK CRANE / 48C**

Known use notes listed.

Known failure notes listed.

Price forty-eight.

I read everything.

Could not afford it.

Not remotely with Vale.

And that was fine.

Better than fine.

There were things worth wanting beyond current reach.

Good.

Money had direction now.

Not hoard.

Access.

Option.

Future yes.

We walked back at dusk.

Sable basin lights came on one dock at a time.

Mirrored in water normally.

Hundreds of them.

No wrong sun.

Just commerce.

I had twenty-one copper.

Twenty-three owed.

A bag of small tools.

A body mostly recovered.

A crew that had somehow become people I expected beside me at dinner.

And a market big enough to remind me I was still poor in interesting ways.

Excellent.

Tomorrow, maybe Glassmouth.

After that, who knew.

For once, earning money had not made me want to go home and protect it.

It made road look longer.
