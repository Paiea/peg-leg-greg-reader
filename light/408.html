TEXT READER · CHAPTER 395

THE MEDIATOR

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Dask nearly missed the boat because of mirrors.

Of course he did.

Fourth bell had not rung yet.

It had only threatened to.

But packet crew had already started pulling the last freight net tight when Dask came running down Sable Quay with one hand on his coat, one on a narrow paper tube, and absolutely no dignity.

Sori stood beside me under the covered middle deck.

"Leave him."

"Cruel."

"Educational."

Maro leaned against rail.

"He bought something?"

"If he did, I'm throwing it overboard."

Dask reached gangplank.

Deckhand blocked him with arm.

"Ticket."

Dask bent over, breathing hard.

"Pocket. Left. No, other left."

Deckhand looked at us.

I pointed.

"His left."

Ticket appeared.

Blue.

Stamped.

Valid.

Dask boarded three breaths before gangplank lifted.

Sori said, "You look terrible."

"Guild opened late."

"Did you buy the finder?"

"No."

There.

Growth everywhere.

"What is tube?" I asked.

"Comparative backing samples. Paper only."

Sori narrowed eyes.

"Free?"

"Yes."

"Suspicious."

Packet horn sounded.

Sable Quay began moving backward.

No.

We did.

That remained one of river travel's little lies.

The packet was called **Bright Gull**, painted on stern in blue letters over older red letters someone had failed to sand completely away.

Long low hull.

Two decks.

Cargo middle and forward.

Passenger deck aft and center.

Covered roof over maybe half.

Open benches along rails.

Small galley window.

Lower washroom exactly two stairs down and four more inside, which clerk had technically described without mentioning how narrow everything was.

I managed.

Crutches fit if turned sideways.

Bag stayed under bench between feet.

Quietener wrapped.

WS-F/9 wrapped separately.

Tavi rock in side pocket.

Twenty-two copper.

Vale twenty-three.

No job.

No Vessa.

That last one felt stranger than expected.

Not bad.

Just space where instruction usually lived.

Captain was **Iven Sarr**, woman maybe forty-five, dark hair cut at jaw, scar under lower lip, voice made for being heard through rain.

She gave passenger rules from center deck.

"Three scheduled stops before Duskport. We do not leave channel without cause. We do not wait for private arguments. If you fall overboard, yell before you become difficult to find. Meals cost bits unless ticket says otherwise. Night deck lights out one bell after dark except marked rail lamps. Anyone touching cargo seals will spend rest of trip discussing regret with my mate."

Good captain.

Mate was **Rell Koss**, thin man with enormous mustache and no visible sense of humor.

Probably hidden under mustache.

Passenger mix maybe thirty.

Traders.

Families.

Two soldiers off duty.

A woman with cages of white river birds.

Three apprentices sharing one blanket despite not being cold.

Old man asleep before Quay disappeared.

One child carrying wooden wheel everywhere.

And a woman named **Tessa Morn**.

I noticed her because she wanted something too loudly.

Not at first.

At first she just watched west bank.

Late thirties maybe.

Brown coat too warm for day.

Hair braided tight.

One small leather case under both hands like it might run away.

She asked Rell twice how long to **Mile House Landing**.

He answered both times.

"About three bells."

Second time she asked, "If river's good?"

"If river's river."

Not helpful.

Accurate.

Bright Gull cleared Sable basin by fifth bell.

Quay noise fell behind.

Lower Lume widened.

Banks flatter.

More reed islands.

Channel markers taller and painted in pairs.

Freight barges moved in slow lines downstream.

One carried timber stacked high enough to offend balance.

Dask read paper samples.

Sori pretended not to nap.

Maro watched shore like someone seeing childhood story he had missed.

I enjoyed doing nothing.

For maybe an hour.

Then Tessa Morn began asking everybody.

Not us first.

Crew.

A merchant.

Bird woman.

One soldier.

Rell again.

Finally Sori heard enough to become curious.

Dangerous.

"What's wrong?" she asked.

Tessa looked at her.

Then at our group.

"My son is at Reedbank."

There.

Not Mile House.

Different.

Sori said, "Reedbank isn't scheduled?"

Tessa shook head.

"Private landing. Half mile beyond Mile House on north bank."

Rell, walking past, said without slowing, "We are not stopping there."

Tessa turned.

"You don't know what I'm asking."

"You've asked four crew. I know."

"Then stop."

Rell stopped.

Mustache remained in motion from momentum.

"Captain decides channel deviations. Private landing is outside route."

"It's not outside channel."

"Landing is shallow side cut."

"At this water it isn't."

"You a pilot?"

"I grew up there."

That changed texture.

Rell's face did not.

"Captain decides."

Tessa looked at us like crew had become wall.

Sori said, "What happened to your son?"

Tessa gripped leather case.

"He cut his leg five days ago. Mill blade. Not deep enough to take it. Deep enough to get dirty. Reedbank healer wrote me yesterday. Fever started. I have feverglass and redroot tincture from Sable Quay. If I wait Duskport, then packet back, that's two days. Maybe more."

Human problem.

There it was.

"Why not get off Mile House and go overland?" Maro asked.

"Road washed two weeks ago. Foot path exists. Three miles through reed flats."

She looked at my crutches then away quickly.

"For me, maybe two bells if dry. With case. At night if we arrive late."

"Could hire cart?" Sori asked.

"Mile House has one cart when axle isn't broken. I don't know."

Dask said, "How old is your son?"

"Fourteen."

Tessa's voice changed on number.

Not dramatic.

Worse.

Held.

"His name is Oren."

Nobody asked.

She needed us to know.

Captain Iven eventually came because Rell told her passenger was recruiting opinions.

I liked Rell slightly more for phrasing.

Iven listened.

All of it.

Then said, "No Reedbank stop."

Tessa went white with anger.

"You didn't even look."

"I know Reedbank cut."

"At summer low?"

"At this draft."

"You carry less than full cargo."

"Still too much to promise turn room if north wind pushes stern."

"Then put me in skiff."

That was new.

Iven's jaw tightened.

"Skiff is emergency equipment."

"This is an emergency."

"Not aboard."

There.

Procedure.

Clean line.

Ugly result.

Tessa looked around like she wanted somebody to say what all of us were thinking.

Fourteen-year-old with fever.

Medicine in mother's hands.

Skiff hanging twenty feet away.

"What would make it emergency enough?" she asked.

Iven said nothing for one breath.

Then:

"Aboard emergency. Collision. Person over water. Fire. Hull failure. Medical crisis on packet. Skiff launch into side cut for private delivery risks crew, schedule, passengers, and equipment."

"Schedule."

Tessa spat word.

Iven's eyes hardened.

"Yes. Schedule. There are people aboard with reasons too."

And there it was.

Other half.

Bird woman raised hand slightly.

"My birds have to reach Duskport quarantine before morning inspection. Miss it, I hold them another day and lose contract."

One merchant said, "I have auction papers that close tomorrow second bell."

Soldier near rail said, "My sister's wedding."

Other soldier looked at him.

"You said funeral."

"Wedding feels longer."

Not helping.

A thin man in gray coat stood from bench near stern.

"My daughter is being sentenced tomorrow."

Silence.

There.

He had not spoken all morning.

"Appeal hearing," he corrected. "If I am late, she goes without family witness."

Tessa stared at him.

He stared back.

Neither apologized.

Good.

Because neither should.

Iven said, "Mile House is scheduled. We reach before dusk. Anyone leaving there accepts own onward travel. Reedbank is not scheduled."

Tessa said, "So correct procedure is my son waits because your skiff belongs to emergencies that happen on your deck."

Iven's face changed slightly.

Not because accusation false.

Because it was true enough to hurt.

"Correct procedure keeps thirty passengers and crew from making every private emergency the packet's emergency," she said.

Also true.

Fuck.

Everyone looked uncomfortable.

I was not involved.

Beautiful.

Then Tessa looked at me.

Of course.

"You were Survey?"

Road gossip.

Maybe crutches.

Maybe Sori records case.

"For a while."

"You know routes. Risk."

"Some."

"Tell her."

No.

Absolutely not.

"Tell her what?"

"That half mile isn't going to kill thirty people."

There.

She wanted a claim.

Not truth.

A weapon shaped like truth.

My normal skill finally became problem.

I could not bound that into clean answer because I did not know Reedbank cut, boat draft, wind, current, skiff handling, captain's liability, Oren's fever severity, Mile House cart status, path condition, or how much delay mattered to everyone else.

Correct thing was say I didn't know.

I did.

"I don't know enough to tell her that."

Tessa flinched like I had struck her.

"Of course."

There.

Procedural honesty.

Human cost.

I hated feeling of it.

She stood.

"Everybody on this boat knows how to explain why they can't help."

Then walked forward.

Sori watched her go.

"That was fair."

"I know."

"Also not fair."

"I know."

Maro said, "Both can happen."

Dask looked miserable.

Good.

He had no instrument for this.

Neither did I.

For next half bell, nothing happened.

Which meant everybody thought about it.

Worse.

I went to captain.

Not to tell her what to do.

Iven stood near wheelhouse with route slate.

"You're here to argue?"

"Maybe ask."

"Ask."

"What's actual Reedbank risk?"

She studied me.

"You believe her?"

"I believe she has medicine and wants to get it to son. I don't know fever."

"Good answer."

"Annoying."

"Useful."

Vessa would like her.

"Actual risk?"

Iven pointed river chart.

"Main channel here. Mile House dock south bend. Reedbank cut enters north side after bend. Bright Gull can enter mouth if water holds. Turning inside is problem. We would need back out or continue shallow loop that rejoins main river two miles later."

"Can skiff do it?"

"Yes."

"Then why hard no?"

She looked at me long enough.

"Because launching skiff needs two crew. Skiff returns maybe half bell, maybe bell if landing bad. Packet drifts or anchors. We miss Gate Nine traffic slot."

"How much delay?"

"Could be one bell. Could be three. Gate Nine after dark can hold us until morning depending freight priority."

There.

Not fifteen minutes.

"So thin man's appeal?"

"Potentially missed. Bird quarantine definitely. Auction likely."

"Could Tessa ride skiff and skiff continue to Reedbank then not return?"

"Emergency skiff isn't passenger taxi and Reedbank has no packet-certified return crew."

"Could one crew stay?"

"Then I'm short one trained hand overnight through Gate Nine."

All bad.

"Could another boat take her from Mile House?"

"Maybe."

"Maybe meaning?"

"Local fishers sometimes run Reedbank. No guarantee."

"Can we know before Mile House?"

Iven paused.

There.

Question.

Not answer.

"Signal tower at Mile House can see private-dock flag board if clear."

"Does Reedbank have flag?"

"Yes."

"Can Mile House signal ahead to see if boat is available?"

"They can ask."

"Can we ask now?"

Iven looked upriver toward next relay post.

"At Marker Seventy-two. Fifteen minutes."

There.

Not solution.

Information.

My favorite drug.

But user wanted human problem, not procedure magically fixing it.

So I asked next.

"If no local boat?"

"Then she's at Mile House with foot path. Or I break route."

"Would you?"

Iven looked at passengers behind us.

"I don't know."

There.

Captain finally had no clean answer either.

Good.

Bad.

We reached Marker Seventy-two.

Iven signaled tower.

Flags went up.

Questions relayed.

Mile House answered ten minutes later.

No cart.

Axle broken.

Of course.

Private boat?

One Reedbank fisher at Mile House currently.

Good.

Boat leaving north before dusk.

Better.

Capacity?

Two passengers plus light bag.

There.

Answer.

Almost too clean.

Then final flag.

Fisher would not take north cut after sunset because channel stake missing near Willow Tooth.

Bright Gull expected Mile House about half bell before sunset if no delays.

Window tight.

If packet lost twenty minutes, Tessa misses fisher.

And river already had freight queue ahead.

Human problem remained.

Iven told Tessa.

Hope made her worse.

"Then don't lose twenty minutes."

Iven said, "I'll do my job."

"Your job keeps getting between me and mine."

"My job is carrying you there alive and on published route."

"My son is already there alive. Maybe."

There.

Knife.

Iven turned away because captain could not afford reply.

Freight queue appeared around next bend.

Three barges.

One timber raft.

Gate-control tug moving upstream.

Bright Gull slowed.

Tessa stood at rail counting minutes without clock.

Everyone felt it.

Bird woman too.

Gray-coat father too.

Nobody became villain.

That was worse.

Dask asked me quietly, "Can packet overtake?"

"Ask captain."

He did not.

Good.

We lost twelve minutes.

Maybe fourteen.

Tessa started crying without sound.

Just tears.

I looked away.

Then freight tug signaled our lane open earlier than expected.

Bright Gull moved.

Not fast.

Packets didn't become racehorses because mother cried.

But Iven took every legal opening.

I could tell because Rell changed deck rhythm.

Lines ready before turns.

Cargo checks early.

Meal service delayed.

No one announced favor.

They just worked.

That mattered.

Mile House dock came into view with sun low.

Small stone landing.

Three houses.

Signal tower.

One broken cart visibly missing wheel.

And a narrow fishing skiff tied outside pier.

Tessa made sound.

Not word.

Relief breaking.

Bright Gull docked.

Fisher was **Noll Renn**.

Of course.

Dask started.

Noll held up hand.

"No."

Dask looked wounded.

Tessa barely heard any of it.

She was down gangplank before Rell finished saying wait.

Iven snapped, "Passenger Morn!"

Tessa froze.

Captain walked to rail.

"Your ticket continues Duskport. If you leave here, packet owes no onward transport."

"I know."

"Reedbank fisher says north cut dark unsafe after sunset. You have maybe half bell."

"I know."

"If he refuses midway, you do not order him through."

Tessa looked at captain.

Anger still there.

Gratitude too.

Neither canceled other.

"I know."

Iven nodded.

Tessa looked at me.

I expected thanks.

She said, "You could've helped sooner."

There.

Good.

Because she was right from her side.

"Maybe."

She shook head.

"You people love maybe."

Then left.

Noll shoved off with Tessa and medicine case.

Skiff angled north.

We watched until reeds swallowed them.

No confirmation.

No success yet.

No clean closure.

Bright Gull departed on schedule plus some minutes.

Bird woman still had chance at quarantine.

Gray-coat father still had chance at hearing.

Tessa had chance at son.

Captain had not broken route.

Procedurally, excellent.

Emotionally, nobody felt excellent.

Sori sat beside me after dark.

Deck lamps low.

River black.

Dask and Maro somewhere forward buying soup.

"You found workaround," she said.

"Captain did."

"You asked question."

"That's not same as helping."

"Tessa thinks so."

"She thinks I should've made captain stop."

"Would you?"

I looked at river.

There.

Question.

All options hurt somebody.

If I had authority and no fisher existed.

Stop and maybe cost father hearing.

Bird woman's contract.

Crew safety.

Thirty people schedule.

Or don't stop and maybe cost boy leg.

Maybe life.

No clean answer.

"I don't know," I said.

Sori nodded.

Not disappointed.

That helped less than expected.

"You usually know what not to claim," she said.

"Yes."

"Different problem."

"Yes."

"Eventually you have to choose anyway."

There.

Fuck.

She went for soup.

I stayed.

Bright Gull moved downriver.

Somewhere behind us, Tessa was trying to reach Oren before dark.

Somewhere ahead, gray-coat man was counting distance to daughter's hearing.

Bird woman checked cages for fourth time.

Captain watched channel.

Everybody wanted something badly.

River did not care.

Procedure cared only enough to keep system from becoming thirty private wars.

Necessary.

Insufficient.

That was new.

I had spent months learning not to pretend uncertainty was certainty.

Useful skill.

Not enough skill.

Sometimes facts narrowed choices until all remaining choices were cruel in different directions.

Then somebody still had to choose.

No Vessa tomorrow to say which.

No captain for every problem.

No protocol that could carry blame away.

I looked at dark water and felt Duskport pulling east.

Chosen road.

Apparently choice included this too.

Shit.
