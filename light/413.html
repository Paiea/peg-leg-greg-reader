TEXT READER · CHAPTER 330

THE DRINKER

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Duskport was famous for beer.

Apparently.

Nobody had mentioned this until I was already drinking it.

That felt dishonest.

"Why does it taste like rope?"

Sori lowered her cup.

"Rope tastes better."

Maro swallowed with face of man accepting medical procedure.

"Smoke."

"There's smoke," I agreed.

"And salt."

"Beer should not have salt."

Dask sniffed his.

"I think that's mineral."

"You think everything is mineral when you don't know."

"That's not true."

"Name three things."

He looked at beer.

"This may be mineral."

Good.

We had been told to come here by six different people.

Not one recommendation.

Six.

Pell Varo at Anchor's End.

Nali.

A fish seller.

A porter who had never spoken to us before.

Dask's mirror-guild woman.

And an old sailor who overheard Sori ask where people went after work and shouted, "Black Keel!" from across street like city had appointed him.

The Black Keel sat three streets inland from East Hook.

Low ceiling.

Black beams.

Long tables scarred by knives, mugs, hooks, and one circular burn mark large enough to have story.

A ship's bell hung over bar.

Every wall carried brewery plaques.

**DUSKPORT BLACK**

**OLD BREAKWATER STOUT**

**SALT HOUSE NINE**

**THREE ANCHORS STRONG**

**GULL'S REGRET**

That last one should have warned me.

Bartender asked if first time.

Sori said yes.

He smiled.

Not kind smile.

Then brought four squat mugs of **Old Breakwater Strong**.

"Local," he said.

"Good?" I asked.

"Local."

Second warning.

I drank anyway.

Now here we were.

"Why is Duskport famous for this?" I asked.

Woman beside us turned around.

"Because it survives ships."

She had one eye, both arms, and enough shoulders for three people.

"What does that mean?"

"Strong. Keeps. Doesn't turn on long runs. Water goes bad, weak beer goes sad, Breakwater stays angry."

"It tastes angry."

"That's how you know it's working."

She raised mug.

I looked at mine.

"How strong?"

Bartender, somehow listening from fifteen feet away, called:

"Strong enough."

Everyone nearby laughed.

Fucking city.

This was after second Merry Wake day.

Which had started before sunrise with rain.

Not storm.

Harbor rain.

Cold enough to make wood slick and everyone mean.

Pella was out.

Jerr had one fewer diver than planned.

Tavren looked at sea, looked at crew, and cut target list in half.

Good employer.

Annoying for money.

I reported with right hand still stiff from yesterday's writing.

Nali handed me waxed slate instead of paper.

"Rain."

"I noticed."

"You'd be surprised."

She had different foot on today.

Same socket and side stay.

Narrower sole with deeper cross-grooves.

I noticed.

She noticed me noticing.

"Wet deck foot."

"You change feet?"

"Sometimes."

"How long?"

"Two minutes if clean. Ten if sand gets in lock. Forty if angry."

"The foot?"

"Me."

Good.

Then work.

Merry Wake gave us steering housing first.

Then a copper-bound locker with no personal mark.

Then half a deck winch that weighed enough to make crane complain.

Greg did not become exciting.

I tallied.

Nali conditioned.

Jerr swore at rain.

Tavren swore at insurance paperwork.

Sori had come to watch first lift and somehow ended up helping Hessa Brin copy custody numbers because Duskport apparently converted visitors into labor if they stood still too long.

Maro left after breakfast to see coastal herb market.

Dask returned from mirror guild with a pamphlet nobody asked for.

Normal.

Then near third bell, diver slate came up with:

**LOWER CABIN / BRASS CASE / BLUE MARK / FIXED UNDER BENCH / OWNER UNKNOWN**

Tavren read it.

"Blue mark shape?"

Runner shrugged.

"Slate says blue."

"Useful."

Second slate ten minutes later.

**OVAL BLUE / THREE WHITE CUTS / CASE BOLTED / CAN REMOVE BENCH OR CASE**

Nali stopped.

"Three cuts?"

Hessa looked up from custody table.

"Courier medical."

Tavren said, "Maybe."

Hessa nodded.

"Maybe."

There.

People learning.

Old courier emergency medical cases used blue oval with three white slash marks in some routes.

Not all.

Merry Wake had carried passengers and dispatch cargo.

Eight years underwater.

Case could contain nothing.

Could contain spoiled supplies.

Could contain charged medical hardware.

Could belong to ship.

Could have been personal physician kit.

Salvage contract did not specifically list medical equipment.

Sel Marr appeared within twelve minutes because money sensed hesitation.

"If it's bolted to ship, fixture."

Hessa said, "Not necessarily."

"Bolted."

"So are private lockboxes."

"Under bench."

"People bolt things under benches."

Sel looked at Tavren.

"Lift it. Determine after."

Tavren looked at Hessa.

Hessa looked at contract.

Then me.

Why me?

I was tally.

"Any old manifest category?" I asked.

She flipped pages.

"Merry Wake manifest lists one ship medical cabinet under operating equipment. No serial. One passenger physician, Dr. Alen Marr, declared professional kit at embarkation. No description."

Sel's face changed.

Small.

Marr.

There.

"Relation?" Sori asked from custody table.

Sel looked at her.

"Grandfather."

Oh.

Good.

Human problem had arrived for work.

"He died on Wake?" I asked.

"Yes."

Sel folded arms.

"And before anyone gets sentimental, salvage company bought rights. My family claim lapsed decades ago."

"Eight years," Hessa said.

"Feels decades."

"Claim window closed six years ago."

"Exactly."

Sori watched him.

"You don't want it?"

Sel's jaw moved.

"I want contract executed."

Not answer.

Nobody pushed.

Tavren asked, "Can divers identify mount without disturbing case?"

Jerr said yes.

Next slate:

**CASE HAS OWN BASE PLATE. BASE PLATE BOLTED TO BENCH. QUICK RELEASE CORRODED CLOSED.**

That changed things.

Ship fixture could be base plate.

Case could be removable equipment.

Or ship equipment designed removable.

Still not answer.

Sel said, "Cut release. Lift case."

Hessa said, "Custody class first."

"How? Underwater?"

"We can hold."

"Holding costs me."

There.

Again.

Tavren looked at rain.

Divers had limited bottom time.

Weather window narrowing.

If we spent twenty minutes debating one case, second planned winch lift might die for day.

That cost yard.

Cost Sel.

Cost crew opportunity.

If we lifted uncertain case into wrong custody lane, could compromise claim.

Correct procedure did not hand us clean answer.

Sori asked, "Is there a neutral action?"

Hessa said, "Photographic slate if diver has plate."

Jerr shook head.

"Plate topside."

"Send down?"

"Ten minutes rig, maybe."

Sel laughed once.

No humor.

"Ten minutes for picture of box."

I looked at him.

"You care."

He looked at me.

"Don't."

"Okay."

I stopped.

Not my story to pry open because surname appeared.

Tavren made call.

"Send plate. We lose second winch if we lose it."

Sel said, "That's my salvage day."

"Yes."

"I'm paying for it."

"Yes."

"And you're choosing picture over recoverable hardware."

"Yes."

Tavren did not explain himself into innocence.

That mattered.

Plate went down.

We waited.

Rain tapped crane hood.

Nali adjusted left socket once.

I held slate under coat.

Picture plate returned with case image.

Blue oval.

Three white cuts.

And along lower corner, almost gone:

**A. MARR / PHYS.**

Sel stared.

Nobody spoke.

Hessa said, "Personal professional property. Family claim expired. Under salvage contract, unclaimed personal effects transfer to insurer estate, not salvage buyer."

Sel closed eyes.

"Of course they do."

Sori said softly, "You can file descendant petition."

"I know."

"Different from salvage claim."

"I know."

There was anger in second one.

Not at her.

Maybe everyone.

Tavren said, "Lift?"

Hessa considered.

"Authorized to family-custody rack under insurer estate. Sealed. No opening."

Sel laughed again.

"So my company pays divers to recover my grandfather's case so insurer can hold it while I petition them to give it to me."

Nobody had useful response.

Because yes.

That was exactly what happened.

"Fuck me," he said.

Nali said, "Probably separate form."

He looked at her.

Then laughed for real.

Small.

Needed.

Case came up.

I recorded wet weight.

Ownership mark.

Custody destination.

No condition opening.

Sel stood six feet away and did not touch it.

When Hessa sealed family rack, he said:

"My mother said he carried silver instruments."

Not to anyone.

Then walked off.

We lost second winch.

Tavren paid crew anyway.

Not full extra work because not performed.

Fair.

Late afternoon we did custody inventory because rain shut further lift.

Nali assigned me.

There.

Potential fifth copper.

Inventory was less exciting than wreck.

Which meant dangerous.

Excitement kept brain awake.

Inventory made numbers become furniture.

I checked tags.

Rack positions.

Seal counts.

Weight sheet references.

One mismatch.

Not dramatic.

Temple chest listed blue lane rack 4 on intake sheet, physically rack 5 because rack 4 had drainage leak.

Move had been authorized.

Record not updated.

I found move chit clipped under Hessa's board.

Corrected cross-reference.

Second mismatch was mine.

Yesterday's loose fitting basket had two sub-bags, not one.

I had recorded combined disposition without sub-bag count.

Nali made me fix it.

Good.

Third was not mismatch.

I thought it was.

Safe A seal number looked 7716.

Custody sheet 771G.

I stared too long.

Hessa said, "Old stamp. G has flat tail."

"That's terrible."

"Yes."

"Why use it?"

"Because city bought nine thousand seals before anyone complained."

Economy.

Beautiful enemy.

At end, Nali signed both day records and custody inventory.

Tavren counted five copper into hand.

"Useful."

Twenty-seven to thirty-two.

There.

Five copper.

Largest single day's ordinary earned pay in current life, I thought.

Maybe.

Would need records.

Did not claim.

Thirty-two copper.

Still not enough.

Much closer to eighty-four.

Still silver problem beyond that.

Good.

Then Nali said, "Beer."

"What?"

"You worked two days at East Hook. Duskport requires beer."

"Law?"

"Worse. Culture."

Tavren, passing behind her, said, "Black Keel."

"Why?"

"Beer."

"Good?"

He kept walking.

"Strong."

Third warning.

At Anchor's End, Pell Varo asked how yard went.

"Useful."

"Then Black Keel."

"Why does everyone say that?"

"Beer."

"Good?"

"Duskport."

Fourth warning.

Maro returned smelling like herbs.

Dask returned carrying pamphlet titled **REFLECTIVE FABRICATION STANDARDS OF LOWER COASTAL GUILDS, ABRIDGED**.

Sori had ink on fingers from custody forms.

I said, "Apparently we have to drink beer."

Maro said, "Good."

Dask said, "Duskport beer is famous."

"How do you know?"

"Mirror guild told me."

Of course.

Sori said, "One."

"One what?"

"Beer. I am not carrying any of you up twelve stairs."

"I can climb."

"Sober."

"Also drunk."

"Not experiment."

So Black Keel.

Old Breakwater Strong.

Rope beer.

The one-eyed woman introduced herself as **Hena Quill**, deck boss from north-coast grain ship.

She explained Duskport beer while insulting us for not knowing.

Port had been brewing strong dark beer for generations because grain came downriver, salt ships needed something that kept, and harbor water historically tasted like metal in summer.

Brewers roasted grain hard because damp storage spoiled lighter malt more easily.

Then sailors developed taste for it.

Or claimed they did.

Then breweries competed over strength.

Then Duskport started exporting barrels.

Then reputation became fact because enough people repeated it.

"So it's famous because it doesn't spoil?" Sori asked.

Hena shrugged.

"Started there. Now famous because Duskport says so louder than everyone else."

That I believed.

"And people like taste?"

Hena took long drink.

"Some."

"Do you?"

"No."

I stared.

She grinned.

"But I'm from Duskport."

There.

City explained.

First mug tasted terrible.

Halfway through, tasted terrible warmer.

This was not improvement.

It was strong though.

Very.

I felt it behind eyes before mug empty.

Current body had different tolerance than first life.

Important data.

Sori's cheeks went red.

Maro became more talkative.

Not loud.

Just sentences longer than normal.

Dask became exactly same except faster.

Terrifying.

"The bitterness isn't actually primary problem," he said. "It's finish. There is a mineral persistence after swallow which interacts with smoke character and perhaps salt air perception. If consumed inland it might be different. We would need comparison."

Sori pointed at him.

"No experiments."

"That's not experiment. That's tasting."

"You are not taking beer inland."

"I didn't say I was."

"You just proposed inland comparison."

I laughed.

Too much.

Beer.

Hena slapped table.

"Second round."

Sori said, "No."

Bartender already pouring.

"No," Sori repeated.

Hena said, "Visitors get two."

"Why?"

"First teaches. Second forgives."

"Does it taste better?"

"No."

Honest city.

Second mugs arrived.

I should not.

Work before first bell tomorrow.

Current body.

Crutches.

Stairs.

Money target.

Responsible.

I took sip.

Still fucking horrible.

"This is worse," I said.

Maro said, "You know what's coming now."

"That's worse."

Sori drank hers and shuddered.

"I hate Duskport."

"You bought second."

"I did not buy this."

Hena raised hand.

"I bought."

Sori pointed at her.

"I hate you."

"Good. Drink."

People around us sang eventually.

Not coordinated.

Several songs at once.

One table sang ship-launch song.

Another answered with rude version.

Someone rang bell.

Bartender yelled because bell apparently had rules.

Nobody listened.

Maro told story about being twelve and falling through greenhouse roof because he tried to retrieve kite for girl who did not like him.

That was new.

"Did she like you after?" I asked.

"No."

"Did kite survive?"

"Also no."

"Successful operation."

"I broke two ribs."

Sori laughed hard enough beer came out nose.

Worth Duskport.

Dask admitted he once spent three days proving his uncle's kitchen table was tilted.

"Was it?" I asked.

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Three millimeters over length."

Sori put head on table.

"Why three days?"

"He kept moving it."

"Because you were measuring his table."

"That affected method."

I loved him a little.

Dangerous beer.

Then Sori looked at me.

"Your turn."

"No."

"Story."

"I have no stories."

Three people stared.

"Fine."

I considered first life.

Too many.

Some not funny anymore.

Some never were.

I chose one where I tried to impress a mercenary captain by jumping from stable roof at twenty-three and landed in manure because I misjudged darkness.

I left out S-class.

Did not matter.

Maro laughed before ending because he knew where story went.

Sori asked, "Did captain hire you?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Said anyone willing to commit that hard to bad idea could be pointed at enemy."

Dask nodded seriously.

"Reasonable."

"No," Sori said.

"Historically successful."

"Still no."

Second mug became half.

Then less.

I stopped there.

Not discipline exactly.

Stomach had vote.

Beer sat like smoked brick.

Strong beer plus horrible taste had natural braking system.

Maybe design.

Hena approved.

"You'll live."

"Was that uncertain?"

"First-timers sometimes try third because pride."

"No."

"Smart."

"I've been called that."

"By who?"

"Me mostly."

She laughed.

At some point work left shoulders.

That was point.

Not beer.

Not Duskport reputation.

We had been moving for days.

Survey.

Road.

White Span.

Bellweather.

Sable Quay.

Packet.

People wanting things.

Duskport.

Job.

Peg.

Money.

Wreck.

For one evening, none needed solving.

Sori stopped tracking purse.

Maro stopped watching room exits.

Dask stopped taking notes.

Actually.

He put pamphlet away.

I stopped calculating silver I did not have.

Thirty-two copper stayed thirty-two whether I stared at it or not.

We were merry.

There.

Stupid word.

Accurate.

When we finally left, Duskport air felt excellent because it did not taste like beer.

Sori walked straight.

Mostly.

Maro sang one line of launch song and forgot next.

Dask explained that forgetting lyric did not mean song lacked lyric.

I used crutches carefully.

Very carefully.

Wet cobble plus alcohol plus one leg was not place for pride.

I was not drunk enough to be stupid.

I was drunk enough to know I could become stupid.

Useful boundary.

At Anchor's End, twelve stairs waited.

Sori pointed.

"Fuck."

"Correct."

Pell Varo looked up from desk.

"Black Keel?"

"Yes."

"Breakwater?"

"Yes."

"Good?"

Four of us said, "No."

He smiled.

"Duskport."

I climbed stairs one at time.

Crutch.

Right foot.

Lift.

Crutch.

Right foot.

Lift.

Slow.

Not elegant.

Safe.

Halfway, I thought about work leg.

Then told brain to shut up.

Tomorrow.

Tonight I had earned five copper, lost argument with beer, learned Maro had once fallen through greenhouse for girl, and discovered Dask could make drunkenness sound peer reviewed.

Useful day.

Maybe good one too.

Beer remained unforgivable.
