TEXT READER · CHAPTER 349

THE LOOKOUT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

Lerrin woke in colors.

White from south head.

Amber farther north.

Green once from bay mouth.

Then nothing for maybe half minute.

Then white again.

I watched through attic window while Dask put on boots.

"What does white mean?"

"Depends station."

"You don't know."

"Not yet."

"Growth."

He looked offended.

Good.

South Lamp House breakfast cost one bit for oat porridge and something claimed to be apple.

Might once have known apple.

Public head walk opened second bell.

We went.

Trial leg stayed wrapped.

Not because skin bad.

Because South Head path looked like stone, stairs, railings, exposed wind, and public people who would step around me while staring at lights.

Crutches better.

Tool for terrain.

Not moral issue.

Lerrin town stretched along bay instead of stacking up cliff.

Low black roofs.

Oil stores behind stone walls.

Signal masts everywhere.

Not decorative.

Shop roofs had little numbered plates facing head.

Warehouses had painted circles visible from towers.

Even fish market bell had colored shutters.

Dask pointed.

"Red shutter."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Fish seller said, without looking up, "Means market wants harbor runner and not fire brigade."

Dask slowed.

"There are color conventions below beacon level."

Seller looked at him now.

"There are colors everywhere, love."

Dask nearly wrote that down.

I stopped him with face.

He wrote anyway.

South Head public path began behind packet office.

A sign:

**PUBLIC WALK / SECOND TO FOURTH BELL**

**STAY BEHIND WHITE LINE**

**DO NOT TOUCH MIRRORS**

**DO NOT SHOUT SIGNAL COLORS AS JOKE**

Specific rules told history.

Attendant at gate wore blue coat with one silver stripe.

"Two bits each."

We paid.

Copper unchanged.

"Repair yard closed. Upper relay closed. Public platform only. If bell rings twice, path clears immediately."

Dask said, "What does three mean?"

"Not your problem."

Excellent.

South Head was less tower than machine village.

Short stone pylons stood in rows along outer ridge.

Some held mirrors.

Some glass lamps.

Some metal shutters.

Two tall masts carried suspended glass drums like giant lantern lenses without flame.

Between them ran narrow channels in paving.

Protected wire maybe.

Or light conduit.

Or both.

Beacon staff moved constantly.

Not priests.

Workers.

One cleaned salt from mirror with rag.

One swapped colored plates.

One checked tiny brass windows in pedestal.

One yelled at gull.

Gull ignored institutional authority.

Public path had plaques.

Dask read every one.

I read maybe half.

Old South Relay housing sat under shelter.

Big iron frame with rotating shutter wheel.

No longer connected.

Plaque said first network used line-of-sight mirror flashes by day and oil-color drums by night.

Later charged glass improved distance.

Current system mixed all three because replacement schedules were apparently written by someone who hated uniformity.

Dask loved it.

"Hybrid generations."

"Yes."

"Different failure modes."

"Yes."

"Redundancy."

"Sometimes."

He looked at me.

"Sometimes?"

"Different systems can also disagree."

He smiled.

"Good."

We had become terrible people.

Third pylon flashed white.

Far north answered amber.

Then closer inland station answered amber.

South Head worker stopped wiping mirror.

Another looked up.

No alarm.

Just attention.

Dask stopped talking.

Good instinct.

Far north flashed amber again.

South pylon answered white-white.

I didn't know language.

Could feel grammar.

Question maybe.

Confirmation maybe.

A worker crossed public line to pedestal and opened brass panel.

Gate attendant said, "Keep moving."

Public continued.

Then two bells rang.

There.

Everyone stopped.

Attendant voice changed.

"Public off head. Now please."

No panic.

We turned.

Dask looked back once.

"Don't," I said.

"I wasn't."

"You were spiritually trespassing."

He sighed.

Halfway down path, packet-office clerk came running uphill.

Actually running.

Not good for bureaucracy.

"South Head! Vey packet is at tide gate!"

Beacon worker coming down shouted, "Then hold her!"

"Captain says amber is stale!"

"Captain can say it from dock!"

There.

Human problem.

By time we reached packet yard, argument already had five people.

Captain of Vey packet.

Packet clerk.

Beacon duty officer.

Young relay runner.

Old woman holding basket who seemed uninvolved but emotionally committed.

Vey packet visible at lower quay.

Narrow twin-masted vessel.

Cargo already aboard.

Maybe twenty passengers.

Tide going out.

Captain had red scarf and expression of person losing money by minute.

"North Nine has been amber since dawn," she said. "Sea is fine. Bellweather runner came through an hour ago."

Duty officer said, "North Nine amber is not sea-state only."

"I know what amber means."

"Then you know it can mean visibility relay uncertain."

"I can see north cape."

"You cannot see Vey approach from here."

"My lookout can from outer water."

"After you leave protected bay."

Captain swore.

Packet clerk said, "If she misses this tide, next departure tomorrow. Twenty-three passengers, perishables, mail."

Duty officer said, "If North Nine cannot confirm green corridor, departure stays local-risk only."

Captain said, "I'm accepting local risk."

"Passengers didn't buy local-risk passage."

That changed room.

Good problem.

Not simple.

Captain could go.

Maybe.

But passengers' contract assumed network-confirmed route.

Correct procedure mattered.

So did tide.

Nobody asked me anything.

Excellent.

Dask whispered, "What happened?"

"Don't know."

We stood edge.

Packet office board updated:

**VEY / HOLD / NORTH RELAY UNCERTAIN**

Passengers started coming inside.

Young man angry.

Woman with baby tired.

Two traders talking spoilage.

One old sailor said he'd sailed without colored lamps for thirty years.

Someone told him congratulations.

Then beacon runner arrived from head.

"North Nine receiving. Return line intermittent."

Duty officer: "Cause?"

"Unknown. Bellweather Seven repeating amber because Nine hasn't returned sequence complete."

Captain: "So it's relay fault."

Duty officer: "Maybe."

"Sea is fine."

"Maybe."

"You people love maybe."

"Sea loves killing people who hate it."

Not sarcasm.

Old professional fight.

Dask leaned closer to me.

"Return line intermittent. Could be timing."

"Could be anything."

"Yes."

Growth continued.

Duty officer ordered manual optical confirmation from South Head to Bellweather Seven, then Bellweather to North Nine if visibility permitted.

Packet clerk groaned.

"How long?"

"If clean, twenty minutes."

Captain looked at tide marker.

"I have thirty-five before channel costs me draft."

There.

Clock.

Dask's face did thing.

"No," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face wants Lot Sixty-Seven."

"It measures delay."

"It has sticky bead and no seconds scale."

"Correct."

"We are not calibrating national beacon network with five-copper auction trash."

"Regional."

"Dask."

"I agree."

Good.

We waited.

This was more educational than public walk.

Not because we were involved.

Because city operating under uncertainty was better than plaque.

South Head flashed sequence.

White-white-amber-white.

Bellweather answered maybe fifteen seconds later.

Hard to know.

My count unreliable.

Interesting.

Did not unpack tool.

Again.

Bellweather then flashed north.

We could barely see next station.

Tiny white point.

Wait.

Nothing.

Wait.

Amber.

Duty officer said, "Again."

Captain said nothing.

Second sequence.

South.

Bellweather.

North.

This time north answered white.

Then green.

Packet room changed.

Not celebration.

Motion.

Captain: "That's corridor green."

Duty officer: "One clean return. Need two."

"You'll cost tide."

"Need two."

Captain slammed palm table.

Not tantrum.

Fear with money attached.

"If second takes twenty, I lose departure."

Duty officer said, quieter, "If I lower standard because your tide is expensive, then standard isn't standard."

Captain stared.

There was no villain.

Again.

I hated book sometimes.

Third sequence began.

This one fast at South.

Bellweather answered.

North nothing.

Five seconds.

Ten.

Dask muttered numbers.

I let him.

Fifteen.

North flashed white.

Then green.

Duty officer looked wall clock.

"Two clean returns. Corridor green. Packet may depart under normal terms."

Captain was already moving.

Packet clerk shouted down quay.

Passengers surged.

Problem solved?

Not entirely.

Captain had maybe twelve minutes before safe draft threshold.

Loading already done.

People had to board fast.

Mother with baby couldn't move fast.

Old sailor could but complained anyway.

Packet crew started sorting.

No pushing.

Priority families down first.

Freight doors shut.

Captain herself came back for old woman with cane.

Interesting.

She wasn't monster.

Just wanted ship out.

Vey packet cast off with maybe four minutes to spare.

Duty officer watched from yard.

No satisfaction.

Just next board entry.

Dask exhaled.

"That was excellent."

Packet clerk looked at him.

"It was terrible."

"Technically excellent."

"You're not helping."

"Correct."

We left.

South Head public walk remained closed.

Fine.

Lerrin had shown itself anyway.

We ate lunch on sea wall.

Dask drew signal sequence from memory.

"You sure?" I asked.

"Mostly."

"Then label mostly."

He did.

Good.

I looked at Road Response Kit in pack.

Could characterize safely now.

Not network.

Bench.

Low stakes.

We found empty stone table behind Lamp House sheltered from wind.

No trial leg today.

Road Response Kit out.

Dry cloth.

No oil.

Track inspected.

Sticky near four.

I mounted clamp to table.

Nonstandard jaw held.

Test one: Dask displace tongue, I press manual trigger when I see movement.

We repeated ten times.

Marks mostly between two and three.

One near four.

Sticky zone?

Maybe.

Swapped roles.

Dask mostly one to two.

Again.

Not time.

Comparison.

Then controlled test.

Instead of reaction, Dask displaced tongue and pressed manual trigger himself immediately with other hand.

Ten repetitions.

Mostly below one mark.

Two hung near four.

There.

Instrument error visible.

Not human reaction.

Sticky track created false long delays occasionally.

Good.

We wrote:

**Lot 67 characterization, Lerrin sheltered table. Manual same-person immediate trigger: 8/10 below one mark, 2/10 false long near fourth-mark hesitation. Comparative human-response readings unreliable until sticky-track failure isolated. Do not use for body/magic conclusions yet.**

Dask said, "Can disassemble?"

"Not until know track material."

"Could ask beacon repair people."

"Public repair yard closed."

"Could ask someone outside."

"Ask, yes. Enter, no."

He nodded.

We packed.

Tool had failed usefully.

Not broken.

Characterized enough to know current limitation mattered.

That felt like progress.

Afternoon I walked lower bay alone while Dask went looking for public beacon manuals.

Packet board now:

**VEY DEPARTED**

**OLD MERROW / TOMORROW THIRD BELL / 4C**

**HARROW CAPE / WEATHER HOLD / CHECK DAWN**

**BELLWEATHER ROAD COACH / TOMORROW SECOND BELL / 2C**

There.

Next roads with prices.

Vey departure gone today.

Could take another on fifth day later.

Old Merrow tomorrow four copper.

Bellweather road two.

Harrow Cape uncertain.

I asked clerk.

"What's Old Merrow?"

"Island town."

"How far?"

"Packet half day if sea kind."

"Why four copper?"

"Water."

"Compelling."

"You asked."

"Harrow Cape?"

"Farther north-west. Rough crossing. Weather permitting means weather actually permitting, not passengers having feelings."

"Bellweather?"

"Road town. Beacon relay. Two copper coach, maybe five bells."

"Lodging?"

"Cheap. Boring."

"Useful."

"If you like boring."

I did sometimes.

Could stay Lerrin.

One copper bunk.

Could go Bellweather for two.

Could go Old Merrow for four.

Could wait Harrow Cape.

Dask would probably want Bellweather because relay station.

Maybe I didn't.

Interesting.

At dusk Dask returned with pamphlet and expression.

"Bellweather Seven has public lower relay room."

Of course.

"Tomorrow?"

"Second bell coach."

"Two copper."

"Yes."

"You going?"

"Probably."

"Good."

He looked.

"You?"

"Don't know."

He accepted.

Actual growth.

We paid another one copper each for South Lamp House because tonight already needed.

Mine forty to thirty-nine.

Food bits.

No other copper.

Before bed, I used trial leg.

Not outside.

Attic corridor.

Flat wood.

Twenty minutes.

Task was pack rearrangement.

Both hands.

Road Response Kit now needed better place.

I stood at bunk, trial leg bearing enough to free hands while I moved harness straps, wrapped brass frame, shifted clothes, retied crutch wrap.

Minimal walking.

Maybe eight flat steps total.

More standing than walking.

At fifteen minutes hip tired.

At eighteen gait on turn sloppy.

Stopped twenty.

Skin:

upper pink mild.

Lower-front faint.

Cleared at twenty.

No pain.

Note.

**Lerrin South Lamp House. 20 min. Indoor flat wood. Standing packing task, both hands. Minimal steps. Hip fatigue ~15 min. Turn quality degraded ~18. Mild upper/lower-front pink cleared.**

Tool and leg interacted indirectly.

Artifact created packing work.

Leg freed hands to do packing.

Tiny system.

Real.

Dask read pamphlet while I checked skin.

"Bellweather signal room has old line-delay drum."

"Good for it."

"Public display."

"Congratulations."

"You're tired."

"Yes."

"You don't want Bellweather?"

I thought.

"Maybe I do. Maybe I want boat."

"Old Merrow?"

"Maybe."

He looked at pamphlet.

Then me.

"We can split."

Simple.

No hurt.

"Yes."

That was becoming normal.

Outside beacon lights moved through sequence.

White.

Amber.

Green.

Someone far away watched.

Someone answered.

Nobody owned whole line.

Every station only saw part and trusted next station to carry rest.

Maybe companion worked like that too.

Maybe not.

Too neat.

I rejected metaphor.

Good.

Tomorrow I would choose road or water.

Tonight I watched lights.
