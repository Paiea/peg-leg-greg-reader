TEXT READER · CHAPTER 372

THE COORDINATOR

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The first thing I learned about managing people was that nobody wanted to be managed by a stranger with two crutches.

Reasonable.

The second thing I learned was that they were perfectly willing to yell at me when nobody else had decided what happened next.

Also reasonable.

Cinder Cross Tool House opened before breakfast had finished becoming breakfast.

The front doors were still barred when I arrived, but the yard gate stood open because freight apparently had different religious beliefs about morning.

I had twelve copper and eight silver.

Vale still open.

Two silver possibly traveling toward me from Lakeward if six bundles of reed glass survived a road, an inspection, and at least one person with authority finding a reason to be difficult.

I did not count it.

Pending money was not money.

It was hope wearing paperwork.

The buyer with the magnifying lens stood in the yard beside yesterday's broken water-drive lot.

Two iron housings.

One crate of snapped drive teeth.

One black vane bracket that still looked irritatingly similar to East Fen's lower draft assembly.

I looked at it once.

Then looked away.

Growth.

The buyer noticed.

"Not buying it?"

"I don't know what it is."

"Yesterday that didn't stop you looking."

"Looking is free."

"Not if you do it long enough."

I respected that.

A wagon rattled into the square outside.

Then another.

Then a third cart came around the north corner too fast, braked hard, and made everybody nearby discover religion.

The Tool House buyer swore.

"That's early."

"What is?"

"Everything."

Excellent.

The first wagon carried a long cast frame wrapped in rope and sacking. The second held two squat iron drums and a crate stack. The third cart carried six men, three timber rollers, and enough chain to imprison a minor god.

None of them belonged to the same crew.

I knew because all three groups started arguing before the wheels stopped.

A narrow man in a blue cap jumped down from the first wagon.

"We were told west bay."

A broad woman from the chain cart said, "West bay's blocked."

A Tool House worker shouted from the loading door, "It isn't blocked."

She pointed.

"It has a fucking axle stand in it."

"That's not blocked."

"It is if you're moving an eight-span frame."

"Move the stand."

"With what hands?"

"Yours?"

Promising workplace.

The buyer closed his eyes.

I said, "You seem busy."

He opened them.

"You seem unemployed."

"Temporarily between revenue recognition events."

"I remember regretting you."

"Strong continuity."

He pointed the magnifying lens at me like a weapon.

"Can you organize an unload?"

I almost said no.

Not because I could not.

Because the question was dangerously broad.

"Organize how?"

"Three deliveries. Two receiving crews. One yard lead who was supposed to be here."

"Where is he?"

"At home vomiting."

"Good reason."

"Terrible timing."

"What's moving?"

He pointed in order.

Long wrapped frame.

Two drums.

Crates.

Then yesterday's broken water-drive lot near the inner wall.

"The frame goes to west bay for inspection. Drums stay under north awning. Crates go inside after seal count. Yesterday's lot needs to shift three paces east so the frame can turn."

"Weight?"

"Too much for me to guess safely."

"Who knows?"

Blue-cap driver raised a hand without being asked.

"Frame's marked eleven hundred."

"What unit?"

He told me.

I knew the broad scale.

Heavy enough that my opinion became mostly geometric.

"Drums?"

Second driver answered.

"Four hundred each."

"Who owns the rollers and chain?"

Broad woman.

"Us."

"Who is 'us'?"

"South Chain."

"Who has stop authority during lift?"

She stared.

"Me, if they're using my chain."

Good.

"Who has yard authority?"

Buyer said, "Me."

"Who has receiving record?"

A Tool House clerk at side table lifted slate.

"Me."

"Who decides final placement?"

Buyer.

"Who is paying me?"

Silence.

Important question.

Buyer said, "Three silver."

I laughed.

He did not.

"You're asking me to coordinate three independent crews moving enough iron to pulp somebody."

"You're not lifting."

"That makes the job cheaper?"

"It makes your shoulder less relevant."

"Five silver."

"Three."

"If the long frame clears west bay before second bell and nothing gets put in the wrong place, five."

He squinted.

"Why would I pay more for the same morning?"

"Because you care about the second bell."

He hated that I had noticed.

Blue cap said, "We do too. Road levy starts if our wagon is still in square after second bell."

There.

Delay cost.

Buyer looked at him.

Then me.

"Four if everything clears."

"Five if frame clears west bay and receiving record closes before second bell. Three if we finish later but clean."

The broad South Chain woman barked a laugh.

"He's charging you for your own urgency."

"Quiet."

She did not.

"Five," buyer said. "No injuries."

"That's not a fee condition. That's a baseline."

"Fine."

I held out hand.

He looked at crutch.

Then shook hand anyway.

Good.

I had just sold part of a morning again.

Not ideal.

But this time six other people's labor multiplied what my hour could affect.

Different enough to test.

First problem.

The west bay was not blocked by an axle stand.

It was blocked by three separate truths.

The stand occupied the middle.

A rack of finished brackets occupied the inside wall.

The long frame could enter only if the wagon backed nearly square to the door, but the north awning posts prevented that angle while the drum wagon remained where it had stopped.

Everybody had been arguing about the stand because the stand was easiest to point at.

I walked the route once.

Crutches on hard cinderstone.

Dry.

Slight slope toward street.

No reason to wear trial limb in a freight yard full of chain, rollers, and people who had not met my balance.

The frame wagon had to move first.

The drum wagon had to move before the frame wagon could angle.

The chain cart had to unload rollers before either.

Which meant the smallest cart moved first.

Nobody liked that because the long frame had arrived first.

Arrival order was emotionally powerful and mechanically irrelevant.

I said, "Chain cart unloads rollers at west edge. Then clears north. Drum wagon pulls forward and waits by black block. Frame wagon backs to bay. Tool House moves axle stand during that. Crates stay sealed until frame is inside."

Blue cap said, "My team can move the stand now."

Tool House worker said, "Your team isn't touching our stand."

Blue cap opened mouth.

I pointed at buyer.

"Yard decision."

Buyer said, "My people move stand."

Good.

I pointed at South Chain woman.

"Your chain, your lift calls."

She nodded once.

"Drivers keep their own teams."

Both drivers nodded.

"Anybody can call stop for danger. Only chain lead calls movement once load is under chain."

That got a reaction.

A younger Tool House worker said, "Anybody?"

"Yes."

"What if they're wrong?"

"Then we lose ten seconds."

South Chain woman said, "He's right."

Better authority.

She had earned it in this yard before I arrived.

Use it.

I asked her, "What do you need kept clear?"

She walked the long-frame route with me.

"Nobody inside this side when we turn. Nobody behind wagon wheel on slope. And if I say slack, I mean slack. Last crew heard 'back' and nearly took a finger."

"Good."

She looked at me.

"Good?"

"Specific failure mode."

"You write those down for fun?"

"Unfortunately."

She shook head.

"Strange little man."

I looked down at six-foot-three worth of me.

"Little?"

"Spiritually."

Fair.

We started.

The chain cart unloaded rollers.

Not elegantly.

One man carried a timber roller on shoulder.

Another tried to carry two.

The broad woman slapped one out of his arms.

"One."

"I can carry two."

"You can drop two too."

He carried one.

Different people.

Different competence.

Useful.

The drum wagon moved forward.

One horse disliked the black cinderstone corner block and sidestepped.

Driver corrected without drama.

I stayed out of it.

The axle stand took four Tool House workers and a pry bar.

One of them complained continuously.

Not because he was weak.

Because complaining seemed to be part of how he generated torque.

"Of course we put the bastard here. Best possible place. Middle of the only fucking bay. Beautiful. Art."

Nobody answered.

He kept going.

"Could've put it anywhere. Roof. Street. Buy it a room."

The stand moved.

West bay opened.

Second problem.

The frame wagon could make the angle.

Barely.

Blue cap knew his wagon.

The South Chain woman knew the chain.

The Tool House buyer knew where he wanted the frame.

I knew enough to stop trying to be all three.

That was the harder part.

Old S-class rooms had trained me out of a particular kind of stupidity.

If someone was better than you at the exact thing happening, shut up early.

I stood where I could see wagon rear, bay edge, and the slope.

"Blue cap," I said.

He looked offended.

"Name's Joren."

Damn.

A named person.

"Joren, you own wagon movement."

"Obviously."

"Good. Chain lead owns load movement."

"Also obviously."

"Then we are having a beautiful morning."

He almost smiled.

The South Chain woman said, "Mave."

There.

Fine.

"Greg."

"I know. Buyer called you strange before you got here."

"Excellent network."

Mave looked at the ground.

"Two rollers on approach. Third inside threshold."

One of her men said, "Third's split."

She turned.

"What?"

He showed her.

Long crack along one timber roller.

Mave swore.

No spare.

Joren said, "Use mine."

"You have one?"

"Under tool box."

Different crew.

Useful resource.

Mave inspected it before accepting.

Not trust.

Verification.

Good.

They set.

Joren backed wagon.

Slow.

Horse steps.

Wheel.

Stop.

Correct.

Again.

The frame's wrapped end cleared north awning by less than handspan.

Tool House worker at door said, "More south."

Joren said, "Can't."

Buyer said, "You can."

I said nothing.

Mave stepped back, sighted the angle, and said, "He can't. Rear wheel drops gutter if he goes south."

Buyer frowned.

Then walked out and saw it.

"Fine."

Good.

Nobody needed me.

Progress.

They rigged chain.

I watched hands.

Not because I knew rigging better.

Because hands disappeared first when too many competent people focused on the big thing.

Mave did too.

She slapped a wrist away from a pinch point without looking.

"Keep fingers."

"Had it."

"You still do."

That was probably old conversation.

The frame lifted enough to free wagon bed.

Rollers under.

Chain took weight.

Joren's crew eased wagon forward.

The frame settled onto rollers.

Eleven hundred units of somebody else's bad day.

Mave said, "Take."

Her crew pulled.

Tool House workers guided with bars.

The frame moved half a pace.

Stopped.

Again.

Half a pace.

It was boring.

Good heavy work often was.

The dangerous part usually arrived when boredom convinced somebody nothing bad was happening.

We were three pulls from threshold when the rear roller walked.

Not failed.

Walked.

Its left end shifted downhill maybe three fingers.

Tiny.

The frame followed.

Tiny.

Mave saw it same time I did.

"STOP."

Everybody froze except one Tool House worker who took one more pull because his back was turned.

The frame shifted again.

"STOP MEANS STOP," Mave roared.

He dropped line.

Silence.

The frame sat angled.

Not falling.

Not safe enough to pretend.

One roller now visibly off square.

One guide bar trapped under lower edge.

The complaining Tool House worker was on wrong side of it.

Not crushed.

Too close.

I pointed.

"You. Out."

He looked at frame.

"I can get bar."

"No."

"It's ours."

"Congratulations. Leave it."

Mave said, "Out."

He came out.

Good.

No debate after expert confirmed.

Mave crouched outside load path.

"Ground's pushing left."

The yard slope.

Not much.

Enough.

Joren said, "Chock right."

Mave said, "Not while it's loaded like that."

Buyer started, "What if we just pull the nose-"

"No," three of us said.

He looked offended.

I enjoyed that more than I should have.

Mave rubbed jaw.

"We need weight back a finger, reset roller square, then chock downhill."

"Can chain take reverse?"

"Yes, but not from current hook angle."

Joren pointed to wagon.

"Use rear tree as redirect."

Mave looked.

"No. Too high."

Joren frowned.

Then nodded.

Actual disagreement.

No sarcasm academy.

No one-line genius.

Good.

One of Mave's men said, "What about door post?"

Buyer said, "Absolutely not."

"Post's stone."

"Arch isn't."

Useful information.

I looked at available geometry.

Three independent teams.

Three separate tools.

Nobody had complete answer yet.

Old me stirred.

Not power.

Arrangement.

"What needs to be true before you can reset the roller?" I asked Mave.

"Load needs to come right and back without lifting."

"What can create controlled rightward pull?"

"Chain from north anchor."

Buyer said, "North anchor's behind drum wagon."

There.

I pointed toward street.

"Move drum wagon."

Driver said, "We're already staged."

"Yes."

"We move, we lose slot."

"What slot?"

"North awning."

"You're not unloading until frame clears anyway."

He stared.

Then looked at buyer.

Buyer said, "Move."

Driver swore under breath.

Not at me specifically.

Maybe at logistics as concept.

Drum wagon moved.

North wall anchor exposed.

Old iron ring.

Mave inspected it.

"Rated?"

Buyer said, "For mill housings."

"Recent?"

"Used last month."

She still checked masonry.

Good.

Chain rerigged.

Joren supplied two wheel chocks.

Tool House supplied another pry bar.

I supplied almost nothing physical.

That was the point.

Mave directed.

Small tension.

Stop.

Chock.

Reset roller.

The trapped guide bar came free without anyone reaching under.

Good.

Then frame moved again.

This time with downhill chock repositioned after each pull.

Slower.

Nobody complained.

Except the complaining man, who had moved from existential to technical.

"Chock's chewing cinder."

Mave said, "Then move it flat."

"Flat slips."

"Other face."

"Other face is chipped."

Joren handed him a different chock.

He took it.

No thank you.

Workplace intimacy.

The frame crossed threshold.

Second bell had not rung.

Not yet.

Inside bay, final placement belonged to Tool House.

I stepped away.

Buyer said, "Where are you going?"

"Crates."

He looked toward street.

Right.

We were not done.

The drums moved under north awning.

No chain issue.

No drama.

The sealed crates were easier.

Which did not mean casual.

Receiving clerk counted seals.

Two matched.

Third had wax chipped but cord intact.

Carrier record already noted chipped wax at departure.

Good.

No mystery.

No Greg investigation.

Record matched.

Crates moved inside.

Second bell rang while last slate entry was being signed.

Buyer looked at bell tower through open gate.

Then at clerk.

"Closed?"

Clerk finished line.

Stamped.

"Closed."

Joren's wagon was already rolling out toward road levy post.

Mave's crew gathered chain.

Drum driver had feed bag over horse.

Nobody applauded.

Excellent.

Buyer counted five silver into my hand.

I still had twelve copper. Silver moved from eight to thirteen.

I counted because transaction required counting, not because thirteen silver had become a spiritual condition.

He said, "You didn't do much."

"Correct."

"You mostly told people the order."

"Correct."

"Mave solved the frame."

"Yes."

"Joren solved the turn."

"Yes."

"My people moved the stand."

"Eventually."

He glared.

"Then why did I pay you five silver?"

I looked across yard.

Three deliveries.

Three crews.

One closed receiving slate.

No one injured.

No levy delay.

No crate put in wrong bay.

"Because none of them owned the whole morning."

He considered that.

Mave walked past carrying chain hook.

"He kept your mouth out of lift calls."

Buyer said, "That alone might be worth two."

"Put it on my reference."

He looked at me.

"You want a reference?"

"Specific one."

"For what?"

"That I coordinated a multi-crew freight unload under your yard authority and completed receiving before second bell. Not that I know rigging. Not that I know wagons. Not that I know water-drive parts."

He laughed once.

"You're collecting paper again."

"Paper gets me into rooms."

"Money gets you into better rooms."

"Working on both."

He went inside.

I assumed that meant maybe.

Five minutes later the receiving clerk brought out a narrow Tool House slip.

Not glowing praise.

Better.

**CINDER CROSS TOOL HOUSE**

**GREG**

**COORDINATED THREE INBOUND FREIGHT TEAMS FOR WEST/NORTH RECEIVING. NO LIFT AUTHORITY. NO APPRAISAL AUTHORITY. RECEIVING CLOSED BEFORE SECOND BELL.**

Signed by buyer's mark and clerk witness.

I read it twice.

Mave leaned over.

"That say you know chain?"

"No."

"Good."

"You worried I'd steal your career?"

"You'd last half a morning."

"Generous."

She looked at my crutches.

Then at frame inside bay.

"Maybe whole morning if you stayed out from under things."

That was almost affection.

Or professional tolerance.

Different species.

I folded the slip into notebook.

Eighteen copper.

Twenty-three Vale.

Five commission still pending.

Ten-copper floor.

No sudden wealth.

No obligation-clearing montage.

Not yet.

But the day had answered another question.

Could I manage people?

A little.

Under a narrow scope.

When the people already knew their jobs.

When I did not mistake coordination for expertise.

When I could make the interfaces cleaner.

That sounded familiar enough to hurt.

Old support work had rarely meant telling the strongest person how to swing a weapon.

It meant making sure the healer knew when the striker would commit.

Making sure the retreat route was clear before anyone needed it.

Making sure somebody had authority to stop a bad move.

Making sure three specialists were not each solving a different version of the same problem.

I had not become that person again.

One freight morning did not earn mythology.

It earned a slip.

Good.

Slips were useful.

At the post counter, there was still no Lakeward commission notice.

Reasonable.

The glass wagon had left yesterday.

Distance remained stubbornly physical.

There was, however, a message from Lakeward district routing.

Not money.

Not Hessa.

Not Hark & Venn.

A short copied notice attached to my earlier Bracken report number.

**RIDGE INSPECTION RECEIVED. WEST-ACCESS CLOSURE MAINTAINED PENDING REPAIR. PRIOR REPORT TIMESTAMP CONFIRMED. FINAL HAZARD REFERENCE AVAILABLE ON REQUEST.**

There.

Another old piece becoming usable.

Not cash.

Capacity.

I paid postage in bits for the reference request.

No copper.

Then I looked at the private freight board.

One notice had been added since morning.

**SOUTH ROAD SEASONAL CREWS / INTAKE + ROUTE STAGING / ORGANIZER NEEDED / THREE CREWS / REFERENCES / FEE BY SCOPE**

I knew that shape.

Not this exact job.

The shape.

Yesterday I might have copied it.

Two days ago I might have told myself I needed more experience.

Today I had a Tool House slip in my notebook and enough sense to know one good unload did not make me a field boss.

Both could be true.

I copied the contact.

Not applied.

Not yet.

First ask scope.

How many people?

Whose hiring authority?

Who handles wages?

Who owns safety calls?

Who provisions?

Who can fire somebody?

Who absorbs delay?

Questions before confidence.

Good.

I bought lunch with bits.

Sat on black cinderstone edge outside the square.

My shoulder was tired.

Not injured.

Crutch palms sore.

Right leg had done a full morning of standing, shifting, backing out of other people's way, and refusing to enter crush zones because pride was expensive.

No trial limb.

No magic.

Fifty-nine.

Fifty-three.

One hundred thirty-nine.

Still.

The world had moved anyway.

That mattered too.

I opened notebook.

Wrote:

**14c -> 18c.**

Then:

**5c Lakeward reed-glass fee still pending. Do not count.**

Then:

**Tool House coordination reference secured. Scope narrow.**

Then:

**Bracken final hazard reference now available. Request sent.**

I stared at the money line.

Eighteen.

A few months ago I would have felt rich.

A few chapters ago I would have felt safe.

Now I felt capable of making another move.

Different.

Better.

Not because thirteen silver was a bigger emotion.

Because the purse had stopped being the only number that mattered.

I had cash.

Pending commission.

Vale balance.

Work-limb money already sunk into something useful.

References.

Two markets.

One seller relationship.

One buyer relationship.

One yard that had now seen me coordinate people without pretending I could rig eleven hundred units of iron.

Optionality.

That was the word.

Not wealth.

Not yet.

I closed notebook.

Across the square, Mave's crew was arguing about whose turn it was to buy beer.

Joren had already left.

The Tool House buyer was yelling at somebody about a crate label.

Nobody needed me.

Good.

I had spent the morning making myself useful in a system that continued after I walked away.

That might be the closest thing to support I'd done in this life.

I got up.

South-road notice in one pocket.

Tool House slip in another.

Eighteen copper in purse.

Five more maybe coming later.

And, for once, no desire to turn any of that into a speech.

There was work to price.
