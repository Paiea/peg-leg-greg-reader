TEXT READER · CHAPTER 382

THE CLIENT

Text-only reading · no chapter illustrations

The letter from Duskport arrived three days after I told Lakeward exactly how stupid I had been on purpose.

That was good timing.

Not emotionally.

Administratively.

Lakeward could keep deciding whether I had become expensive paperwork. I had already answered the question in front of me.

Hark & Venn had apparently found a different one.

The packet runner caught me on the stairs.

"Greg?"

"Yes."

"Duskport."

He handed me a folded sheet with a workshop stamp I recognized before I read the name.

HARK & VENN.

My entire left side became interested.

Not magic.

Body.

Different system.

I signed receipt.

The runner left.

I stood on the landing with two crutches, one sealed letter, and the sudden urge to open it with my teeth.

I did not.

Hands existed.

Use them.

Upstairs, Lyssa had half our table occupied with a gray coat turned inside out. One sleeve was pinned flat. The other looked like it had lost an argument with chalk.

She glanced at the letter.

"Duskport?"

"Yes."

"Hark and Venn?"

I looked at her.

"How?"

"You made that face."

"I have multiple faces."

"You have purchase face, road face, Hessa face, and leg shop face."

"This is profiling."

"Open it."

Hostile household government remained stable.

I sat.

The seal came apart cleanly.

Inside was one page.

Masta's handwriting had not improved.

Good.

Some institutions survived distance.

**GREG.**

Excellent opening.

**NEXT SOCKET SHELL READY FOR SECOND FIT. TEMPORARY ASH SHAFT AND MEDIUM TEST FOOT RETAINED HERE. BRING LINER. DO NOT PAY ANYTHING YET. QUOTE UNCHANGED.**

I read that line twice.

No payment due.

Good.

Not because I could not pay.

Because paying without reason was still stupid.

Progress.

The next lines:

**I HAVE FITTING SPACE SIX DAYS FROM RECEIPT, THIRD BELL THROUGH FIFTH. IF YOU CANNOT MAKE THAT WINDOW, SEND WORD. I WILL GIVE ANOTHER. NOTHING EXPIRES BECAUSE YOU HAVE A LIFE.**

I looked at Lyssa.

"She knows me too well."

Lyssa held out a hand.

I gave her the letter.

She read.

Then the last part.

Her mouth changed.

Small.

Trying not to smile.

"What?"

She turned the page toward me.

I had not reached bottom.

**BRING ONE REAL TASK YOU WANT TWO HANDS FOR. NOT STANDING THERE FEELING IMPORTANT.**

I stared.

"Rude."

"Very specific."

"I stood for one second."

"You told everyone."

"I had hands."

"You currently have hands."

"While standing."

She gave letter back.

I read the line again.

Real task.

Not demonstration.

Not walking for walking.

Not standing there feeling important.

Masta remained Masta.

Good.

I put the letter beside Lakeward copy.

Two papers.

One said I had knowingly joined a controlled-export problem.

One said a new socket shell existed six days away.

Life had range.

Lyssa returned to coat.

No immediate speech.

Good.

I tried same.

Lasted maybe thirty seconds.

"Six days."

"Yes."

"Duskport."

"Also yes."

"I could make that."

She pulled one pin out and moved it half inch.

"Probably."

"I need route."

"Then get route."

There was something insulting about living with someone who refused to turn every decision into event.

Useful.

I went to the travel board after breakfast.

Not work board.

That distinction took effort.

Carrow had three ways east that mattered.

Passenger coach.

Freight passage.

Packet carriage with spare seat if clerk liked your face or your paperwork.

Old Greg would have optimized.

Current Greg also wanted to optimize.

Disease survived reincarnation.

Passenger board showed a Duskport connection leaving tomorrow morning, one overnight stop, then direct east after second bell.

Fare: one silver, three copper.

Food not included.

Return not included.

I stared at price.

Not because impossible.

Because possible.

That was new enough to need a second look.

One silver, three copper did not threaten rent.

Did not threaten food.

Did not threaten Hark & Venn final balance.

Did not threaten Vale because Vale was dead.

Beautiful sentence.

It did reduce reserve.

Real money.

Not ceremonial.

Freight board had cheaper options.

Of course.

**EAST HARDWARE / RIDER ACCEPTED / LOAD + UNLOAD / DUSKPORT TERMINAL / TWO COPPER + WORK**

There.

Old friend.

Cheap travel disguised as labor.

My shoulder was fine.

Hands fine.

Could load.

Probably.

Another listing:

**MAIL AUXILIARY / EAST / NIGHT DEPARTURE / ONE BIT / NO GUARANTEED SEAT**

Tempting.

Awful.

Then:

**TIMBER WAGON / DUSKPORT OUTER YARD / PASSAGE FREE WITH TWO DAYS STACKING**

No.

Not because stacking beneath me.

Because Hark & Venn wanted body information, and arriving after two days of timber work would change the body I brought to the fitting.

That mattered.

I looked back at passenger fare.

One silver, three copper.

Paying money to arrive in a useful condition.

Annoying concept.

I had spent months treating travel like something work should subsidize.

Sometimes rational.

Sometimes cheap because I was broke.

Sometimes cheap because I enjoyed making logistics prove affection.

The clerk behind window looked at me.

"Buying?"

"Thinking."

"Do it farther left."

A woman behind me wanted board.

Fair.

I moved.

Sat on stone bench.

Did arithmetic without conversion.

Twelve silver-ish reserve.

Ordinary copper and bits.

One silver, three copper outbound.

Food.

Lodging included at connection? Board said no.

Fine.

Return later.

Final limb balance still future.

No Vale.

No current fine from Lakeward.

No new contract.

The old version of me would have turned uncertainty into reason to preserve every coin.

The even older version would have said money existed to remove friction and bought whatever was fastest.

Both men were annoying.

I read freight listing again.

Two copper plus work.

Could save nearly a silver-scale chunk.

Could also arrive with shoulder fatigue, hand soreness, and whatever stacking did to residual-limb volume after long standing.

Then Masta would spend fitting time separating travel labor from socket problems.

Bad experiment.

There.

Hessa disease.

Again.

I hated cross-domain lessons.

I bought passenger ticket.

One silver, three copper.

The clerk took money without music.

No achievement.

No one nearby understood that I had just paid not to work.

Excellent.

Ticket folded into notebook.

Then I bought return option?

No.

I almost did.

Stopped.

I did not know how long fitting would take.

Masta might want another next day.

Might say go home.

Might need adjustment.

Buying return schedule before shop told me current state would be me inventing future to feel organized.

I kept money.

Restraint.

Disgusting.

Back home, Lyssa had changed coats.

Not physically.

Different garment now on table.

Brown cuff.

I recognized it because it had survived more chapters than some governments.

I put ticket down.

"Passenger coach."

She looked.

"Work ride full?"

"No."

"Bad route?"

"No."

"Then why?"

"I paid."

She stopped.

"You paid passenger fare?"

"Yes."

"Without trying to earn it back on the way?"

"Yes."

She looked suspicious.

"This legal letter changed you."

"Temporary condition."

"How much?"

"One silver, three copper."

She winced slightly.

Good.

Not nothing.

"Why not freight?"

I told her.

Two days stacking.

Body state.

Fitting data.

She nodded.

"That makes sense."

I hated how much I wanted praise for basic resource allocation.

Did not ask.

Training.

"Leaving tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

She resumed stitching.

"Good."

"Good?"

"You usually tell me a return date you invented."

I opened mouth.

Closed.

Damn.

"Need anything from Duskport?" I asked.

"No."

"Thread?"

"No."

"Needles?"

"No."

"Glass blue line thing?"

She looked up.

"No."

"Great. Efficient."

"Come back with your own leg problem."

"Technically socket."

"Greg."

"Yes."

That was enough.

Packing took less time than expected.

Also progress.

Crutches.

Bag.

Notebook.

Money separated.

Lakeward copy stayed home.

No reason to carry it east like a worry charm.

Liner.

Important.

I almost forgot liner.

That would have been embarrassing enough to justify religion.

Lyssa saw me stop halfway down stairs.

"What?"

"Liner."

She pointed upward.

"You put it beside bed."

"Yes."

I went back.

Tavi was in lower room building something from four sticks and a bent spoon.

He looked at liner in my hand.

"Leg?"

"Part of leg."

"New?"

"Old part. New shell."

"Can you run?"

"No."

"Can you kick?"

"Probably badly."

"Can you jump?"

"Absolutely not."

He considered.

"What can it do?"

That was fair.

I looked at the liner.

Then letter.

"Maybe let me stand and use both hands longer."

He looked unimpressed.

Children had terrible product instincts.

"What will you do with hands?"

There.

Masta's question through smaller mouth.

Real task.

I had been thinking about it badly.

Carry something.

Use tool.

Open door.

Write while standing?

Not useful enough.

Old Greg could fight with both hands.

Current Greg did not need a sword demonstration in prosthetic shop.

I thought about South Road.

Clipboard.

Not both hands.

West Market.

Records.

Again.

Theatre.

Props.

Could carry.

Lyssa work.

Not mine.

Kitchen.

Pot.

Interesting.

At home, using crutches meant any object larger than bag became route problem.

Cup from table to stove.

Bowl.

Laundry.

Box.

A chair.

Not heroic.

Daily.

"What do I want to do?" I asked.

Tavi nodded.

"Carry soup."

I laughed.

"Why soup?"

"If spill, bad."

Correct.

High-fidelity task design by child.

Cala looked over from basin.

"Don't bring soup to leg shop."

"Thank you."

Tavi objected.

"They asked."

"They did not ask for soup."

"Real task."

I stared at him.

He had heard enough.

Dangerous household.

Lyssa came downstairs behind me.

"Carry a tray."

I turned.

"That's less funny."

"That's why."

A tray.

Two cups.

Maybe a bowl.

Weight low.

Hands occupied.

Walking surface visible.

Balance errors visible.

Actual household function.

Masta would approve.

Probably.

I wrote on bottom of her letter:

**REAL TASK: carry loaded tray across room without using hands for support.**

Then added:

**Only if Masta says safe.**

Hessa disease advanced.

Lyssa read over shoulder.

"Good."

Tavi said, "Soup."

"No soup."

"Water."

"Maybe."

"Two waters."

"Why two?"

"Harder."

Kid understood progression systems.

Terrible.

I packed.

Then did something strange.

Nothing.

No last-minute work board.

No trip financing scheme.

No attempt to turn Duskport into three commercial purposes because one city could theoretically hold several.

I had one purpose.

Fit.

Maybe two.

Come home.

That counted.

Afternoon passed.

Lyssa left for a customer fitting.

Cala went downstairs.

Tavi disappeared into courtyard with spoon machine.

I sat alone.

The Lakeward statement remained in records.

Did not move.

Duskport ticket stayed in notebook.

Did not become investment thesis.

I checked money once.

Not six times.

Still enough.

At evening Lyssa came back with fewer pins and more thread.

We ate.

I told her coach time.

She told me morning market would be loud because two grain carts were due.

Separate information.

Normal.

Before bed, I set liner on top of bag.

Then ticket under it.

Then moved ticket back to notebook because putting paper beneath padded leather was stupid.

Lyssa watched.

"Ready?"

"Mostly."

"Scared?"

I considered lying.

Not useful.

"A little."

"Walking?"

"Fitting."

She nodded.

"Because?"

"Last time my body kept making bad fit look better."

"That sounds like you."

"Everybody has one joke."

She smiled.

Then serious enough.

"You don't have to be good at it."

Masta had said walk honestly.

Lyssa said same thing differently.

Not mentor.

Not therapist.

Just person who had watched me turn being useful into religion more than once.

"I know."

This time I meant it.

Morning came.

I left on two crutches.

Not carrying a new leg.

Not carrying a debt.

Not carrying Lakeward.

One bag.

One liner.

One passenger ticket I had paid for because arriving useful was worth money.

At the coach yard, the freight wagon from yesterday was also loading.

Two men rolled hardware crates up plank.

For one second I regretted the silver.

Then one crate slipped sideways.

Not dangerous.

Caught immediately.

Still.

My shoulder remembered enough imaginary labor to shut up.

I boarded passenger coach.

Seat near aisle.

Bag under knees.

Crutches strapped to side rack where I could reach them.

The driver checked ticket.

"Destination?"

"Duskport."

"Work?"

"Client."

Word felt strange.

Not worker.

Not debtor.

Not patient exactly.

Client.

I had paid a shop to build something for my body.

I had paid a coach to bring that body there in useful condition.

That was enough job for one road.

The coach rolled east.

For the first hour I kept expecting somebody to hand me a crate.

Nobody did.

Passenger travel was deeply inefficient.

I sat.

A woman across from me knitted something red while the road moved under us. Beside her, a man with a merchant badge slept with both hands folded over a narrow brass case chained to his belt.

Interesting case.

Not mine.

I looked out the window.

Carrow flattened behind us.

Road widened past the east farms, then narrowed again where old stone drainage cuts climbed through low hills.

I knew some of this route.

Not all.

Forty years of old life did not make every ditch nostalgic.

At first stop, driver called fifteen minutes.

My right leg appreciated standing.

Shoulders fine.

Residual limb ordinary.

No fitting shell yet, just crutch body doing what it knew.

A freight handler nearby recognized the passenger coach and asked if anyone wanted two copper for moving sacks from one cart to another before departure.

My head turned automatically.

Two copper.

Easy.

Probably.

I looked at sacks.

Then at shoulders.

Then at ticket.

"No."

The handler shrugged and asked next person.

Nothing happened.

Excellent.

The world survived my refusal.

I bought water and a meat pastry instead.

Capital deployment.

Very advanced.

Back on coach, the knitting woman said, "You could've done that."

"Probably."

"Then why not?"

"Paying a woman in Duskport to tell me whether my body is lying."

She stopped knitting.

"Ah."

That was apparently sufficient explanation.

Good.

The brass-case man woke an hour later, checked chain, and went back to sleep.

I never learned what was inside.

Also good.

First overnight stop came at a road inn large enough to have three stables and no architectural interest in beauty.

The driver announced departure after first bell.

Floor space was cheap.

Beds were more.

I stood in doorway and had same argument with myself again.

A floor would not kill me.

I had slept on worse.

A floor also meant low transfers, bad shoulder angles, and waking stiff before another day in coach.

I paid for a bed.

Not expensive enough to deserve emotional accounting.

Expensive enough that early Greg would have called it weakness.

Current Greg called it sleeping.

The mattress was lumpy.

Financial independence had limits.

I slept anyway.

Morning body report:

Shoulders normal.

Right hip stiff for maybe ten steps.

Residual limb skin clean.

No swelling worth naming.

Good.

I wrote none of that down.

Masta had not asked for a travel diary.

The second day changed landscape.

More wet ground.

More old industrial cuts.

By afternoon, Duskport began announcing itself through smell before buildings.

Wet metal.

River mud.

Smoke.

Work.

I had missed none of those things.

Still recognized all of them.

The city came into view under low cloud.

Warehouses first.

Then cranes.

Then roofs.

No revelation.

Just place.

That mattered more now.

I had lived here long enough for some corners to exist in body memory.

Coach entered west terminal after fourth bell.

Driver unstrapped my crutches.

"Client," he said.

I looked at him.

"What?"

"Your destination answer."

"Oh."

"Hope it fits."

There.

Random stranger had now joined project.

"Me too."

I stepped down.

Duskport under crutches felt familiar immediately.

Different stone spacing than Carrow.

Wider freight lanes.

More people who expected you to move with traffic instead of around it.

I moved.

Anchor's End still existed.

Good.

I paid posted room rate without interrogating desk clerk about cheapest possible alternative.

This was getting dangerous.

Bag upstairs.

Liner on top.

Then I went outside.

Hark & Venn was three streets away.

I knew that.

My appointment was not today.

Also knew that.

I walked one street toward it anyway.

Stopped.

This was stupid.

Not illegal.

Different category.

Masta had fitting space three days from now.

Showing up early would not make shell more ready.

Could ask whether she had cancellation.

Could also become man who turned other people's schedule into his enthusiasm.

No.

I turned toward market instead.

Not artifact market.

Food.

Mostly.

Duskport had enough old hardware in every second stall that "not artifact market" was philosophical.

I bought dinner.

Saw one retired signal clamp with a bead still in it.

Did not ask price.

Saw a broken tide lens with maker mark I almost recognized.

Almost.

Kept walking.

Three days.

I could work if useful.

Could visit people if natural.

Could do nothing if nothing needed me.

That last option remained suspicious.

At Anchor's End that evening, I wrote Masta one reply even though I was already in city.

**ARRIVED. FITTING WINDOW CONFIRMED. LINER WITH ME. REAL TASK: LOADED TRAY, IF YOU APPROVE. I WILL NOT ARRIVE EARLY AND HAUNT YOUR SHOP.**

I paid a boy one bit to deliver it tomorrow morning.

Then added after thought:

**NO SOUP.**

Tavi deserved representation.

I folded note.

For once I had arrived somewhere before the problem.

No job attached.

No debt attached.

No emergency.

Three days until fitting.

Enough money to sleep in a bed.

Enough sense not to spend the night proving I deserved it.

That was probably what being a client felt like.

Annoyingly passive.

Potentially useful.

I could survive three days.

Probably.
