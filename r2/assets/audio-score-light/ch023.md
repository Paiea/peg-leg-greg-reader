# Chapter 23: The Adventurer

---

Noll stopped sleeping on my floor after one night.

The smoke stayed longer.

By the third morning Lower Rook Street smelled less like a burned building and more like a building that had recently lost an argument with water.

Progress.

The cookroom stove remained cold.

Every landing still had a full bucket beside the stairs.

Nobody had moved them.

Noll had acquired a mattress from somewhere that had opinions about fleas but remained structurally committed to being a mattress.

I had given him my soap twice since the fire.

That part of history was recovering beautifully.

I left before breakfast because the corner stall had raised the price of eggs again and I preferred being insulted by the Guild for free.

Sella looked up when I came in.

Then she looked at my coat.

"You still smell burned."

"Building fire."

"I heard."

Of course she had.

Carrow's Guild knew about structural emergencies, monster sightings, unpaid hazard slips, marriages that affected contract names, and at least three kinds of public embarrassment before breakfast.

Sella slid a stack of work chits aside.

"You here for another ward shift?"

"No."

The answer came quickly enough to surprise me.

Meren's work had paid.

It had also been useful.

I would do it again.

That was different from wanting to spend the next several weeks waiting in Carrow for someone named Kesra Dain to arrive and tell nineteen-year-old me how badly I performed the first five inches of a discipline fifty-nine-year-old me had once mastered.

The assessment was still weeks away.

Weeks were a lot of life if you stopped treating them like a hallway.

I looked at the board behind Sella.

City work occupied most of the left side.

Warehouse checks.

Escort inside the walls.

Two commercial ward assistants.

A tannery response that I avoided on principle.

The field postings were farther right.

Fewer.

Colder.

Better handwriting, somehow.

One had three copper marks beside the Guild stamp and a road-office seal below it.

**WEST CUT ROUTE VERIFICATION. FOUR DAYS MINIMUM. LIGHT ESCORT. SURVEY PRIORITY.**

I read it twice.

"That road still exists?"

Sella leaned back.

"Apparently that's what they're paying someone to find out."

The West Cut had been old when I was young the first time.

Quarry road originally. Limestone and timber came through it before the western freight road was widened. Later, when I knew Carrow as a place I returned to instead of a place I lived, the Cut was mostly foot traffic, goats, and people trying to avoid tolls.

There had been a stone crossing halfway out.

A spring under the north bank.

A bad bend near the quarry face where wagons always took too much room.

I remembered all three.

I did not remember which of them existed yet.

Useful distinction.

"Why now?"

Sella pointed north with one finger.

"Gate traffic."

Of course.

Loaded wagons were still restricted through the North Gate. Small freight had been bleeding toward river routes and the other city roads for weeks. Apparently someone in the road office had finally become curious whether an old quarry cut could carry couriers and pack strings without requiring three signatures and a prayer.

"Who's surveying?"

"Harl Fen. Road office."

No memory.

Good.

"Escort?"

Sella pulled the chit down.

"Edda's leading. Needs one more Bronze."

That got my attention.

Edda had been the spear fighter in the tannery yard.

Short weapon. Good feet. Better judgment than the young idiot with the long sword.

She had known when to stop chasing a hooktail after the problem stopped being ours.

Useful person to follow onto an old road.

"How long?"

"Four days if Harl likes the road. Five if he hates it. Six if weather makes everyone stupid."

"And if the road is gone?"

"Then you walk back and write down that it is gone."

Adventure had changed since I was nineteen.

Or maybe I had.

At nineteen the word had meant monsters, ruins, money, and a reasonable chance of returning with something sharp embedded somewhere inconvenient.

At fifty-nine it had included a lot more mud.

I looked at the contract again.

Four days away from Carrow.

Maybe six.

The filtration venture would continue without me.

Mara's next packet would not arrive because I stared at the packet office.

Noll had his terrible mattress.

Kesra remained a future appointment instead of a current event.

Nothing required me to stay.

That was a strange kind of permission.

"I'll take it."

Sella did not make a speech about broadening my professional horizons.

She stamped the chit.

One reason I liked her.

"West Gate at first bell tomorrow. Bring four days of food. Harl has the mule. Edda has the route packet."

"Weather gear?"

"If you have to ask, yes."

I hated competent administration.

Preparation cost three copper and most of the afternoon.

Not because I owned nothing.

I had traveled with Jorren.

I had a bedroll, a serviceable cloak, a small cook tin, cord, whetstone, waterskin, and enough experience to know that carrying half a house was another way of arriving tired.

The problem was that road gear deteriorated even when nobody framed it as progression.

My boot seams needed wax.

One sock had a hole at the heel.

The bedroll strap had begun splitting near the buckle.

My dried food supply consisted of one apple, a heel of bread, and confidence.

Confidence was light but nutritionally disappointing.

I bought cheese, dried meat, two onions, oat cakes, wax, and a replacement strap.

Then I stood in front of a wool merchant for longer than I wanted to admit because a second pair of socks cost enough to become a philosophical question.

I bought the socks.

Old Greg approved.

Nineteen-year-old Greg would have called him soft.

Nineteen-year-old Greg had apparently never spent three days walking on a wet blister.

Educational gap.

When I returned to Lower Rook Street, Noll was sitting on the front step with his repaired gray shirt across one knee.

The sword cut had removed most of one sleeve.

He was sewing the rest badly.

"Leaving?" he asked.

I stopped.

"Four days. Maybe six."

Noll nodded toward the pack.

"Work?"

"Road survey."

"You surveying roads now?"

"No. I'm standing near the man who surveys roads while carrying a sword."

"Ah. Skilled labor."

"Exactly."

He bit the thread and inspected the shirt.

"West?"

"West Cut."

That made him look up.

"Old quarry road?"

"You know it?"

"Used to take carts out there."

Another current fact.

I waited.

"When?"

"Before the brick yard."

I did not know about the brick yard.

Noll knew this from my face.

"You really don't remember much."

Not accusation.

Worse, somehow.

Just accurate.

"Pieces."

He looked back at the shirt.

"Stone crossing was cracked last time I went. North side."

My memory supplied a later version with one whole parapet and one repaired side.

There.

A lead.

"How long ago?"

"Two years. Maybe three."

"Useful."

Noll shrugged.

"Try not to cut anybody's laundry out there."

"No promises."

I went upstairs.

The next morning the city ended at the West Gate with less ceremony than I expected.

Carrow did not know I was beginning anything.

No bells changed tone.

No Guild clerk ran after me with a forgotten prophecy.

The guard checked our tokens, complained about Harl's mule, and waved us through.

Edda was already outside the wall.

Same short spear.

Same boiled leather.

Her hair was shorter than I remembered from the tannery yard, or I had simply failed to notice it before.

She looked at my sword.

Then at me.

"Still bought the expensive one."

"It remains purchased."

"Good."

That was our reunion.

Harl Fen was perhaps forty, narrow as a measuring rod, with a brown coat that had been patched in three different shades and a leather case full of stakes strapped to the mule.

He introduced the mule first.

"Pate. Don't stand behind him."

Pate looked offended by the implication.

"Does he kick?" I asked.

"Only people."

Useful category.

Harl carried a survey chain over one shoulder and a flat board under his arm covered in old route marks.

He looked at me the way tradesmen looked at equipment they had not selected personally.

"You know the Cut?"

"Later."

Edda looked at me.

I corrected.

"Some of it. Been years."

That answer remained technically horrible and socially functional.

Harl tapped the board.

"Knowing where a road used to be does not make it passable."

"Agreed."

He paused.

Apparently he had expected an argument.

We started west.

The first two hours were almost disappointing.

Fields.

Low stone walls.

Cold air.

A road wide enough for two carts until the farms thinned and the wheel ruts narrowed.

Edda kept us moving at a pace Harl could maintain while checking drainage, shoulder width, and old marker stones.

He stopped often.

This annoyed Pate.

Pate expressed annoyance by trying to eat survey stakes.

Nobody had mentioned that part of route certification.

The West Cut began where the maintained road turned south.

No sign.

No gate.

Just an older line continuing between two slopes while the good road bent away from it.

Grass had grown through the center.

One wheel rut had become a shallow stream.

Harl stopped.

He looked at the road.

Then at his board.

Then at the road again.

"Passable is not useful," he said.

Edda sighed.

"You practiced that sentence, didn't you?"

"Because guards keep saying things are passable."

"We're not guards."

He looked at both our swords.

Edda looked at my sword too.

"Don't help him."

I did not.

We continued.

By midday I understood what Harl meant.

A person could walk almost anywhere if sufficiently motivated.

A courier route needed something more annoying.

Reliable footing.

Enough width for a loaded mule.

Drainage that did not become a ditch after rain.

A turning radius where two animals could pass without negotiating ownership of the same cliff.

Every few hundred paces Harl measured something I would have called obviously fine.

Several were not fine after measurement.

Current expertise continued its campaign against my dignity.

Clouds arrived from the west after lunch.

Not dramatic clouds.

Low gray ones.

The kind that made Edda look at the sky once and tighten the cover on her pack.

"Rain?" I asked.

"Cold rain."

"Better."

"You say that now."

We reached the stone crossing an hour later.

Noll had been right.

The north side was cracked.

More than cracked.

Half the parapet had fallen into the water and one line of surface stones had sunk toward the gap.

The stream beneath it was wider than I remembered.

Later there had been a clean stone face on the north side.

Repaired.

Future knowledge worked perfectly.

It told me someone eventually fixed this.

Uselessly.

Harl crouched near the damaged edge.

Edda caught the back of his coat before he leaned farther.

"No."

"I need to see the bed stone."

"See it from attached ground."

He looked annoyed.

He obeyed.

Domain authority had limits apparently.

Harl spent ten minutes measuring the surviving width.

Then he shook his head.

"No loaded animals."

I looked at Pate.

Pate was loaded.

"We turn back?"

"Not yet."

Harl pointed upstream.

"Old ford on the route sheet. Quarter mile. If it's usable, the crossing fails but the route might not."

I remembered a spring north of the road.

A shallow bank.

A willow stand that had been old even later.

I looked upstream.

The willow stand was there.

Smaller.

Still there.

"Ford is below those trees."

Harl looked at me.

"You said it had been years."

"It has."

"How many?"

Too many.

"Enough to be wrong."

That answer satisfied him more than a number would have.

We walked upstream.

Rain began halfway there.

Edda had been right about the temperature.

I resented this because weather already had enough advantages.

The ford existed.

More importantly, the ford still behaved like a ford.

Broad stones under shallow moving water. Low approach on both banks. No obvious drop immediately downstream.

Harl did not trust my memory.

Good.

He took a pole from Pate's load and checked the bottom himself.

Then he crossed without the mule.

Then back.

Then once more diagonally.

"Usable," he said.

Edda looked at me.

"You get to look smug once."

I considered it.

"Too wet."

"Good choice."

Pate disagreed with the crossing.

Strongly.

He planted all four feet at the edge and became a monument to private property.

Harl pulled.

Pate became more committed.

Edda pushed from the side.

Pate discovered lateral resistance.

I stood in the rain watching three forms of expertise fail against a mule.

Then I took an oat cake from my pack.

Pate noticed.

Harl looked at me.

"Don't reward him."

"I'm not."

I broke the cake in half and walked into the stream.

Pate followed me immediately.

"That's rewarding him."

"After he crosses, it will be."

The water came just below my knees.

Cold enough that my legs hurt within three steps.

Pate placed one hoof.

Then another.

The current pressed against him and his head came up.

I kept moving.

No barrier.

No trick.

Just food and the expectation that I would not suddenly vanish into the river.

Halfway across, one rear hoof slipped.

Pate lurched.

The lead rope snapped tight in my hand.

I could not hold a mule.

Important fact.

I did not try.

I moved downstream with him instead, kept the rope from wrapping my wrist, and pulled his head toward the shallow line.

Edda was in the water before I asked.

She took the pack frame with one hand and shoved sideways.

Harl shouted something about the survey case.

Nobody cared.

Pate found stone again.

One hoof.

Then two.

Then all four.

He climbed the far bank with the wounded dignity of a man who intended to rewrite the story later.

I followed.

My boots were full of water.

So were my new socks.

Old Greg had been right to buy two pairs.

Infuriating man.

Harl checked the pack before he checked us.

The leather case was dry.

"Good," he said.

Edda stared at him.

"We're fine too."

"I can see that."

"I dislike surveyors."

"Common among people who damage roads."

I gave Pate the oat cake.

Harl objected.

Pate ignored him professionally.

Rain followed us for the next hour.

The West Cut climbed away from the stream and narrowed between limestone banks.

The city disappeared behind the slope.

Not gradually.

One turn, a line of bare trees, and Carrow was simply no longer visible.

I had left the city before.

With Jorren.

With freight.

With problems that belonged to someone else first.

This felt different.

I had chosen the contract because I wanted the days.

The money mattered.

The road mattered.

The chance to use a sword if something tried to eat Harl probably mattered more than was professionally healthy.

But underneath all of that was a simpler fact.

I had been waiting without admitting I was waiting.

Waiting for money.

Waiting for instruction.

Waiting for letters.

Waiting for the future to arrive close enough that I could compare it to memory.

The road did not care.

It was wet now.

That was enough.

Edda stopped near dusk at a limestone overhang where the old quarry face cut into the hill.

Dry ground beneath it.

Room for Pate.

A trickle of water nearby that Harl inspected before letting anyone drink because apparently roads were not the only things he distrusted.

We made camp.

No ceremony.

Bedrolls.

Cold food because everything dry enough to burn had become a theoretical resource.

Wet socks hung from a cord.

Pate tried to eat the cord.

I moved it higher.

Edda handed me the second watch.

"Two hours. Wake me if you hear people, animals, falling rock, running water where there wasn't running water, or Harl explaining certification in his sleep."

Harl looked offended.

"I don't talk in my sleep."

"Then we're safe from one thing."

Night came early under the clouds.

When my watch started, the rain had thinned to drops sliding from the quarry lip.

Edda slept with her spear beside one hand.

Harl slept near the survey case.

Pate slept standing because apparently mules had solved bedding.

I sat with my cloak around my shoulders and the good sword across my knees.

No city noise.

No packet bells.

No kiln.

No neighbor through a wall.

Just water, wet leaves, the occasional shift of an animal in the dark, and three people depending on me to stay awake for two hours.

I had done this hundreds of times.

Different body.

Different road.

Different people.

Same job.

Watch.

Listen.

Stay awake.

Wake the others if the dark changed shape.

The word arrived without invitation.

Adventurer.

I had spent so long thinking about the parts of Old Greg that did not fit anymore that I had forgotten some identities were not stored in mana capacity, rank, or memory.

Some were just things you did again.

I shifted the sword off my knees before I could turn that into a philosophy.

The blade had begun collecting rain from the edge of my cloak.

Maintenance first.

I wiped it dry.

Then I watched the road...
