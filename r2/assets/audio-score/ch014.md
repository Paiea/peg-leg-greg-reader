Sella had work for me.

Of course she did.

Two days watching a bonded warehouse near the river while the regular night crew testified in a theft case.

Easy work.

Probably.

That word again.

Pay good enough to irritate me.

Not good enough to justify dying if probably turned out to be ambitious.

I read the slip twice.

Two days.

Mara left in two days.

There.

Simple.

My brain immediately began making it complicated.

Night shift.
Sleep afternoon.
See Mara morning.
Pack food.
Skip yard.
Maybe second shift ends early.
Maybe leave a message.
Maybe do both if every unrelated person in Carrow agrees to behave on schedule for forty-eight consecutive hours.

Sure.

I put the slip back.

Sella looked at it.

Then me.

"No?"

No.

She waited.

That was the whole interrogation.

Somehow worse than questions.

"You need money."

I know.

"All right."

Contract moved to another stack.

No lesson.

No approving face because I had discovered human beings sometimes outrank silver.

Ten minutes later another Bronze took it while I was still pretending to read the board.

He looked pleased.

Good for him.

Bad for my purse.

Both.

I left before another contract became reasonable in my head.

The brass key felt small in my pocket.

Mara's mother's house was three streets south of river market, behind a bakery I remembered only after I smelled it.

Not bakery.

Corner.

Warm yeast.
Burnt flour.
Rain on stone.

Something moved behind my eyes.

Didn't become a memory.

Fine.

Keep walking.

Narrow house.
Two floors.
Green shutters faded gray.
Brass bell pull.

I reached for bell.

Then key.

Mara said keep it because her mother would hit me with a spoon if I made her explain me to myself.

Permission?

Threat?

Both maybe.

I tried the key.

Lock turned.

That hurt.

Why did a lock turning hurt?

Locks are supposed to change when you stop belonging somewhere.

Apparently nobody told this one.

I stepped in.

"Shoes."

Freeze.

Woman's voice from kitchen.

Not surprised.

Didn't ask who came in.

Shoes.

I looked down.

Mud on one edge.

Some rules survive anything.

Shoes off.

Mara's mother came into hall carrying a wooden spoon.

Of course.

Shorter than Mara. Broader shoulders. Gray starting at temples. Flour on apron.

Her eyes reached my face.

Stopped.

One second.

Then spoon.

Forearm.

Not hard.

Instructional.

"Ow."

"Six weeks."

Right.

I rubbed the spot.

Mara told you.

"Mara told me enough."

She looked again.

Anger made room.

Free hand touched my cheek.

Once.

I did not move.

"You look wrong."

There it was.

People who knew me kept saying some version of that.

No answer.

She turned.

"She's upstairs. Boxes are in the back room. If you came to make this harder, leave before I waste good flour feeding you."

Reasonable terms.

Back room at end of hall.

Door open.

And...

Small bed.
Narrow desk under window.
One shelf bowed in middle.
Nail by door with nothing on it.

Storage now.

Crates.
Blankets.
Broken chair upside down on desk.

But underneath that...

Scale.

My body knew scale.

Left edge of bed would creak.

Window stuck in wet weather.

Water stain above shelf shaped like a boot.

I looked up.

Paint.

Stain gone.

Hand to window latch.

Stuck.

Lift first.

Then pull.

It opened.

Cold river air.

I stood there holding the frame.

Eight months.

I slept here eight months after my parents died.

The room remembered better than I did.

"You found it."

Mara behind me, folded shirts in arms.

Apparently.

She put shirts in crate.

No hug.
No surprise.

Packing.

Good.

I picked up second crate.

Where?

"My room."

Upstairs.

Mara owned more books than seemed financially defensible.

Ledgers too.
Practice books with columns in three hands.
Shipping schedules.
River tables.
Map of western routes crooked above desk.

Halden circled.

Not new ink.

Faded edge.

I stared.

She saw.

"I wanted the job before they offered it."

How long?

Since winter.

Of course.

Another whole want I had missed.

She handed me books.

"Heavy ones on bottom."

I almost said I know.

Didn't.

We packed.

Easier than talking.

Harder than silence.

Books.
Clothes.
Shoes wrapped in paper.

Chipped blue cup she refused to leave.

Her mother yelled from downstairs that Halden had cups.

"Not this one."

She packed it herself.

Didn't ask.

Ten minutes, and my feet remembered a dip in the floor by wardrobe.

Twenty, western window rattles in wind.

Thirty, Mara handed me a small wooden box and my body knew not to open it.

I stared at my hands.

Both under box.
Thumbs nowhere near lid.

She noticed.

"You remember that?"

No.

"Then why are you holding it like it bites?"

Apparently I had boundaries.

"Occasionally."

She took it.
Packed it herself.

Good.

Her mother brought bread and cheese upstairs without asking.

I ate two pieces before remembering manners.

Mara watched.

"That part's the same."

Good.

The word came out too fast.

She raised eyebrow.

I chewed.

Fine.

No.

Swallow.

"What else?"

What else what?

"Is the same."

She sat on floor beside crate.

I sat across.

She thought long enough that asking felt like a bad decision.

"You still count exits."

My eyes went to door.

Then window.

Ah.

"You still read price boards even when you're not buying anything."

Yes.

"You still hate asking for help until the problem becomes embarrassing."

Less good.

"You still decide somebody is competent and then immediately become competitive with them."

Sella.
Meral.
Arlo.

Oh.

"Was I always this irritating?"

"Worse. You had less evidence."

That got me.

We both laughed.

For a moment the room was easy.

Then she looked at the crate.

"You were coming with me."

Quiet sentence.

No accusation exactly.

Eventually, I said.

"That's what you told yourself."

Contracts west.
Halden first.
Maybe farther.

Young Greg again.

Restless.
Planning.
Carrow small.

"Did you want me to?"

Then? Yes.

Then.

Clean little wall of a word.

"Now?"

She took time.

I let her.

"Now I don't know you well enough to ask you to move cities for me."

That hurt.

Sane.

"I don't know me well enough either."

"That's not reassuring."

I know.

Loose thread on blanket.

She picked at it.

"Why did you come today?"

Because I promised?

No.

Too transactional.

Because six weeks disappeared into things that all felt urgent while I was doing them.

Because warehouse exists tomorrow and Mara doesn't.

No.

Mara exists.

Here doesn't.

Departure does.

"Because you were leaving."

She waited.

More.

"And I didn't want that to become another thing I remembered after it was too late to do anything about it."

Small change in her face.

Enough.

"That's a very old-man answer."

I get those sometimes.

She studied me.

"Are you sick?"

Again.

Not memory now.

Fear.

No.

I don't think so.

"That is an awful answer."

It's honest.

"You keep doing that like honesty is automatically kind."

...

I opened mouth.

Closed it.

Right.

She was right.

"I don't need you to tell me everything."

Surprise.

"I need you not to make me stupid while I don't know it."

There.

I understood immediately.

Don't promise around missing information.
Don't let her build plans around a version of me I can't guarantee.
Don't pretend memory exists because truth is awkward.

I almost explained all that.

Didn't.

"Okay."

"Okay what?"

I won't.

"You can't promise that."

Right.

Again.

Breathe.

"Then I'll tell you when I realize I'm doing it."

Better.

Not perfect.

She nodded once.

Blanket on top of crate.

"Vey runs a packet boat back to Carrow every seventh day."

I looked up.

Letters?

"If somebody writes one."

No smile.

No romance.

A route.

Real route.

"I'll answer."

Her face hardened.

I heard it too.

Promise.

Too easy.

No.

Try again.

"If you write, I want to answer. If I don't, you get to decide what that means without protecting me from it."

She looked down.

Then back.

"That's unpleasantly reasonable."

I have experience.

"Apparently not with letters."

Fair.

Leave it.

We carried first crate downstairs.

Her mother pointed.

Not there. Boatman comes through kitchen.

Of course.

Move it.

Second crate.
Third.

By late afternoon Mara's room looked temporary.

Bare shelf.
Empty hooks.
Map rolled.
Blue cup gone.

A life becoming portable.

Before leaving, I stood in back room again.

Window open.

Closed it old way.

Pull down first.
Press frame inward until latch catches.

My hand knew.

That was enough.

Mara at doorway.

"Tomorrow?"

Tomorrow.

Not stay.
Not leave.

Tomorrow.

Yes.

She looked at me.

I almost made it safer.

Didn't.

"Morning."

Specific enough to fail honestly.

She nodded.

Shoes on.

Her mother handed me heel of bread wrapped in cloth.

"You still don't eat enough when you're busy."

Apparently everybody kept records.

At door I pulled out brass key.

Her mother saw.

"Keep it."

I looked at Mara.

No objection.

Back in pocket.

Outside the river market was closing.

Awnings rolling.
Carts to gates.
Somebody arguing about onions like governance depended on it.

I had turned down two days of easy pay.

Purse unimpressed.

Key clicked against coins as I walked.

Small sound.

Different systems.

Maybe not completely.
