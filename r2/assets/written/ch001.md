I woke because my back didn't hurt.

That was wrong.

Not alarming at first. Waking normally involved a short negotiation with several pieces of me that had outlived their warranties. Lower back. Left shoulder. Right knee. The rib that clicked if I slept twisted.

Nothing dramatic. Just attendance.

This morning nobody showed up.

I waited another breath for something to complain.

Nothing.

I moved my toes. All of them answered.

Right.

One knee bent. Then the other. Too easy.

I sat up fast enough that the room tilted, and my body came with me instead of filing three objections on the way.

That got my attention.

Actually, no. The room got it first.

Cheap plaster. Narrow bed. One chair with my coat hanging off it. Washbasin with the crack down the side. Sword in the corner.

I knew that crack.

Cut my thumb on it once.

I looked at my thumb.

No scar.

Wrong hand, maybe.

The other one was clean too.

Young hands.

That is a stupid phrase until you've spent fifty-nine years watching your own hands get worse at being hands.

My right index finger was straight.

No.

I turned it over.

Still straight.

That finger had not been straight in decades. Contract outside Red Hollow. Bad landing. Broke it. Didn't reset it because we had another descent the next morning and apparently thirty-two-year-old me had considered permanent hand damage a scheduling problem.

Reasonable at the time.

Less reasonable in retrospect.

Most things were.

I bent the finger. Straight. Again. Still straight.

No scar. No ache. No complaint.

My bladder chose that moment to become extremely relevant.

Fine.

Apparently whatever cosmic event had repaired forty years of accumulated damage had not improved the basic administrative demands of being alive.

Afterward I stood in the middle of the room and waited for stiffness.

None.

That was almost obscene.

I crossed the room too fast, corrected without thinking, and stopped.

The correction had been wrong too. Too quick. Too light.

I looked down at my legs.

Both there.

Obviously both there.

I knew that.

Except my body had spent enough years teaching me not to assume anything that I checked anyway. Two feet. Ten toes. Young knees. No swelling. No old pull behind the left one.

I crouched.

Lower.

Stood.

Did it again.

Then, because apparently the first serious thing I would do with a miracle after pissing was test whether I could jump, I jumped.

The floorboards complained.

The man next door objected through the wall with the exhausted fury of someone who had been asleep thirty seconds earlier.

I froze.

I knew the voice.

The name was gone.

That bothered me more than the jumping.

I knew he snored. I knew he borrowed soap. I knew he once threw a cup at someone in the hallway and missed so badly that the cup became my problem.

I could not remember his name.

Forty years of memory and apparently soap theft had survived better than nouns.

Good.

No, not good.

Interesting.

The mirror above the basin caught movement.

Mine.

I leaned toward it.

Nineteen.

I did not know that immediately as a number. I knew the face first.

Mine.

Dark hair. Too much of it. No gray. No line beside the mouth. No scar under the chin. The shoulders under my shirt were broad enough to make nineteen-year-old me optimistic and narrow enough to make fifty-nine-year-old me realistic.

I pulled the shirt up.

Flat stomach.

That was unfair.

The boy in the mirror looked pleased about it.

I checked my teeth. All present.

I grinned.

Stopped because I looked ridiculous.

Then did it again.

For perhaps two minutes I behaved with very little dignity. I flexed one arm. Embarrassing. Still did it twice.

Then the sword in the corner pulled my attention sideways.

Cheap steel. One-handed. Leather grip darkened by use. A nick near the guard from the time I'd used it to split kindling because finding an axe had seemed like more work.

I remembered loving that sword.

That was almost more offensive than the finger.

I picked it up.

Too much weight forward.

I knew that immediately.

Of course I did.

I had spent decades around excellent weapons. Held swords worth more than this building. Watched people cut through plate, stone, enchanted hide, and once, memorably, a dining table because a duke's son had misunderstood how close he was standing.

I knew what good movement looked like.

I raised the sword and cut down.

The blade dragged me farther forward than I wanted. My shoulders tightened late. My rear foot moved after the rest of me had already committed. The tip passed close enough to the chair that my coat stirred.

I stopped.

Huh.

Knowing was apparently still not doing.

Good to establish that before breakfast.

I tried again. Slower. Better.

Again.

This time I concentrated on my feet and made the cut worse.

Of course. Because feet had opinions now.

I lowered the sword.

Interesting.

Not good. Interesting.

I had been a warrior first.

Well. Warrior was generous.

Strong enough. Fast enough. Brave enough, unfortunately.

I made Silver before finally admitting that every swordsman I respected was better at the sword than I was.

Then Barrier happened.

Then support magic.

Then decades of becoming something else entirely.

By the end I had been one of seven S-class adventurers on the continent.

Support.

That word mattered.

I had spent half a lifetime beside people who actually mastered this. I knew pieces of how they trained. I knew what they complained about. What they watched. Where they wasted movement. How elite fighters made violence look smaller while beginners decorated it.

People lifted their shoulders when they were afraid. Held their breath when they were about to do something stupid. Shifted weight before a committed attack. Looked toward the place they intended to move before they lied about moving somewhere else.

I knew all of that.

My first cut had almost hit a chair.

Both facts were going to have to coexist.

The sword could wait.

Magic.

That should have been easier.

I set the blade down, closed my eyes, and reached for mana.

In memory it had barely been a motion. More like deciding to move a hand and discovering the hand had already begun.

Here I got pressure behind my eyes and a little tingling in my palm.

I pushed.

Nothing.

Harder.

Pain sharpened across my forehead.

I stopped.

Shit.

That frightened me.

Cleanly. No joke arrived.

The sword was optional.

Magic was not.

Not to the man I remembered being.

I opened my hand. Closed it. Reached again, gentler.

There.

Maybe.

A thread.

No, less than a thread. A suggestion that a thread might exist if I became very polite about asking.

I almost laughed.

Did not.

I stood in the middle of a cheap room with a nineteen-year-old body, a bad sword, and almost no usable mana answering a mind that remembered shaping barriers across battle lines.

That should have been crushing.

Instead my stomach growled.

Loudly.

Right.

Breakfast.

Apparently existential terror had a schedule.

The fear did not disappear. It just had competition.

And once it had competition, something else slipped in behind it.

Possibility.

A dangerous amount of it.

I checked the room, not because I was calm, but because checking things was easier than having a revelation before eating.

Three copper in the coat.

Bronze guild token in the pocket.

Training leathers in the wardrobe.

Bent lockpick I did not remember owning and probably should not have owned.

Receipts.

Two letters.

Dates.

I stopped.

Dates.

No. Not yet.

I went to the window first.

Carrow was outside.

Younger.

The north side of the street had only one proper stone building. The apothecary was not there. The Guild roof still had red tile. The Exchange was missing completely.

A cart rolled through mud where I remembered paving.

Earlier.

The thought arrived too neatly.

I distrusted it immediately.

Memory loved confidence. Facts were usually less enthusiastic.

So I checked.

Guild notice beneath the bed. Another in the coat. A letter from Mara.

I recognized her handwriting before I remembered whether I liked her.

That felt unfair to Mara.

Assuming she deserved fairness.

I would have to check that too.

The dates matched one another. I compared them against the siege of East Verrel because everybody remembered East Verrel.

Or everybody in my later life remembered it.

That distinction suddenly mattered.

I counted backward.

Stopped.

Counted again.

Nineteen.

I sat on the edge of the bed.

Nineteen.

The room got very quiet.

Forty years.

That was my first real thought.

Not noble. Not grateful. Not frightened.

Forty years back.

My second thought was that I could do everything better.

That certainty should have warned me.

It did not.

I looked at the mirror again.

Young body. Old memories.

I knew which professions became valuable. Which wars happened. Which people mattered. Which mistakes ruined guilds. Which discoveries changed industries. Which monsters were worse than the contracts claimed. Which supposedly brilliant fighters were actually held together by preparation, healers, money, and three very competent people nobody remembered afterward.

I knew magic.

I knew people.

I knew how the world worked.

This was going to be easy.

Then I remembered my mother.

Everything stopped.

I sat back down.

For several seconds I let myself be stupid about it.

Maybe.

Maybe the reset went farther than I thought.

Maybe she was somewhere across the city buying bread. Maybe my father was arguing with someone about tools. Maybe there was a house I could walk to and knock on the door and watch two dead people become alive because I had been given enough time to reach them.

I knew the answer.

I checked anyway.

Of course I did.

My parents had already died.

Years before nineteen.

The miracle stopped exactly short of them.

I stared at the floor.

There are things you forget about grief because grief gets old too. It wears smooth. You stop remembering where the sharp parts were.

Apparently the sharp parts were still stored somewhere.

My throat tightened.

I hated that.

Then hated myself for hating it.

Then the man next door started snoring.

I looked at the wall.

Still no name.

That helped.

Strangely.

Maybe because it was ordinary. Maybe because my mother was still dead but Soap Thief was alive and unconscious six feet away, and the world was apparently willing to be impossible without becoming respectful about it.

I breathed out.

Memory was not a perfect archive.

Good.

If it had been perfect, I might have trusted it.

I found paper.

Old habit.

Three headings.

WHAT I KNOW.

WHAT I REMEMBER.

WHAT I CAN CHECK.

Very professional.

The categories began failing immediately.

Did I know the Northern Gate collapsed in the year I remembered, or did I remember the anniversary people celebrated later?

Did I know a merchant family became rich from river shipping, or remember the version of their history they paid to have repeated?

Did I know Soren died in the Red Winter, or only that I met him afterward with a scar he claimed came from there?

Memory kept handing me outcomes with the causes rubbed off.

Useful.

Dangerous.

Very me.

I wrote one sentence under all three headings.

**USE THE FUTURE AS A LEAD. MAKE THE PRESENT PROVE IT.**

Sensible.

I liked it.

Which made me suspicious.

Good rule.

Probably.

I underlined probably, crossed it out because that was stupid, then stared at the crossed-out word long enough to realize I was avoiding the sword.

Fine.

The sword.

One cut. Worse than I wanted.

Again. Better.

One more. Better again.

That was enough improvement to become dangerous. Not because I was good, but because confidence required almost no evidence when it already had forty years of memories cheering for it.

I tried a side step the way I remembered S-class duelists doing it. Small. Lazy. Barely enough to move the target.

My feet crossed.

Oh, come on.

I nearly fell into the basin. Caught myself on the wall.

The basin cracked.

Not dramatically. Just a tiny sound.

Tick.

Then water began crawling down the outside.

Excellent.

Three copper.

Broken basin.

Almost no mana.

Bronze token.

Bad footwork.

Forty years of useful information.

Probably.

There it was.

That felt familiar.

I grabbed the coat and pressed it to the water.

Wrong choice.

Obviously wrong choice.

The fabric darkened in my hands.

Right.

Now I had a broken basin and a wet coat.

Improvement.

There was a rag under the bed.

That would have been useful first.

Another lesson for the future.

Or the present.

Whichever one I was currently ruining.

I cleaned the floor.

My stomach growled again, louder this time.

Yes. I know.

Talking to my own stomach.

Nineteen was going well.

I dressed. Training leathers first, then stopped.

Too much.

I was going to the Guild, not invading a ruin.

Changed shirts. Put the leathers back.

Picked up the sword.

Set it down.

Picked it up again.

If I walked into the Guild armed, nobody would care. If I walked in unarmed, nobody would care.

I cared.

That was different.

The sword stayed.

At the door I stopped.

Not because I had forgotten anything.

Because leaving made this real in a way the mirror had not.

The room could be a hallucination.

The street would have people.

People would know me.

Or not know me.

Worse.

They would know the boy.

I put my hand on the latch.

Then took it off.

Ridiculous.

I had walked into dragon nests. Held barriers while things hit them hard enough to turn stone into weather. Negotiated with guildmasters, princes, healers, merchants, and one necromancer who insisted on correcting my grammar while threatening to kill me.

Door.

Just a door.

I opened it.

The hallway smelled like boiled grain, lamp oil, damp wood, and somebody else's laundry.

I remembered the smell in pieces. Lamp oil first. Then laundry. Then a woman laughing two floors down, and for half a second the memory of a different laugh forty years later tried to attach itself to it.

No.

Not now.

A door opened across the hall. An older woman looked at me.

I knew her face.

Nothing else.

She nodded.

I nodded back.

She kept walking.

Good.

Apparently I was not required to identify every person from my youth before breakfast.

Useful policy.

I went downstairs.

Each step was too easy.

That became annoying almost immediately.

I wanted the stairs to hurt a little. Not because I missed pain. Because pain would have been proof of continuity.

Instead my knees worked, the building creaked, and somebody was burning onions below me.

Onions won.

Food.

Right.

I could solve metaphysics after food.

Outside, Carrow hit me all at once.

Mud. Carts. Voices. A bell somewhere toward the river. Guild roof above the shops. Morning smoke hanging low. A horse shied at a wheelbarrow and nearly dragged a boy into a fruit stall.

The boy swore.

The fruit seller did it better.

I stopped smiling before anyone noticed.

This city had been dead in my memory for years.

Not destroyed.

Worse.

Changed.

Buildings replaced. Streets widened. People gone. Names moved from doors to plaques, then from plaques to stories, then from stories to nothing.

Here it was noisy and inconvenient and alive.

A wagon blocked half the street. Someone barked at me to move.

I moved.

Good advice.

The Guild roof was visible from here.

Red tile.

That was wrong too.

Later they changed it after the fire.

The fire.

I knew when that happened.

Probably.

No.

Lead.

Present proves it.

Good.

I started walking.

I had spent decades learning that the fastest way to understand a system was to find where it resisted you.

Money resisted.

Bodies resisted.

Magic definitely resisted.

Memory, apparently, resisted being useful on command.

And the Guild?

The Guild had resisted me in both lives.

I smiled.

Not much.

It seemed polite to begin there.
