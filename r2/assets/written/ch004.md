A month later, Sella stopped asking for my guild token.

That was how I noticed the month.

Not the calendar. Not the tally marks beside my bed. Not the increasingly complicated page where I had attempted to track training, debt, food, mana, shale, and several financial decisions that became less defensible when arranged chronologically.

Sella looked up when I entered, reached beneath the counter, and slid the yard register toward me before I had finished reaching for the token.

Habit.

Mine and hers.

That was time.

Jorren was leaning against the end of the counter in new boots.

That was time too.

I noticed the boots because the old pair had split along the inside heel three weeks ago and he had complained about the repair cost for most of a sparring session.

"You got the escort."

He looked down.

"You remember that?"

I had forgotten his name twice during our first week.

Apparently my memory had priorities.

The north-road caravan left in nine days.

Jorren had spent the month earning enough contract credit to get taken on. He had done this while I was busy proving that future knowledge became substantially less impressive when converted into rent, training fees, experimental ceramics, loan interest, and food.

His life had moved without me.

Good.

Annoying, somehow, but good.

Sella tapped the register.

I signed.

My handwriting had gotten worse.

The hard patch under my first finger had gotten better.

I flexed the hand once after putting the pen down.

Jorren noticed.

"Training?"

Everything, really.

The first week had mostly improved my appetite. The second changed my feet. By the third, the overhead cut had stopped feeling like a set of instructions I was delivering to my own joints.

It was not excellent.

It was not Silver.

It was definitely not some hidden S-class inheritance waiting for nineteen-year-old muscles to remember greatness.

It was mine.

That mattered more.

The Guild board mattered more immediately.

One contract had been circled twice in Sella's handwriting.

Old bathhouse near the south wall. Two saltbacks confirmed. Possible third. Acid hazard. Five silver plus salvage.

The listing had been there six days.

Five silver was enough to become attractive around day four.

Vale currently owned part of my week.

Arlo currently owned most of the money I had not given Vale.

The shale experiment had produced one repeatable improvement and several expensive ways to make ugly ceramic.

My mana had improved from almost nothing to a small stable pulse I could hold for several breaths.

That had also developed a price.

Everything useful had.

I took the contract off the board.

Sella watched me do it.

"Guild added hazard money."

Because nobody wanted it?

The bathhouse owner had added three silver.

So yes.

Because nobody wanted it.

Jorren glanced at the paper.

Then at me.

"You going alone?"

No.

That was another thing the month had improved.

I no longer confused knowing more than a beginner with being able to do the work of three beginners.

Usually.

I looked at his new boots.

Then at him.

He had fought saltbacks before. I knew because he had once spent ten minutes explaining that acid in leather smelled worse than acid in cloth.

At the time I had classified this as excessive complaining.

Now it was a qualification.

Interested?

Jorren named his price.

"Two-fifty."

I offered two.

He reminded me that Sella had found the job.

From behind the counter, Sella said, "Correct."

Neither of us had realized she was listening.

Jorren smiled.

"Two-fifty."

Fine.

Sella stamped the contract.

Then did not give it back.

Her other hand came out.

"Acid kit."

Jorren had one.

I did not.

Sella continued holding the paper.

There are several kinds of leverage in the world.

Some of them are armies.

Some of them are capital.

Some of them are a twenty-two-year-old clerk refusing to release your monster contract until you spend four copper on powder and waxed cloth.

I bought the kit.

My expected profit decreased before I left the building.

Adventure.

We walked south.

Jorren's new boots made less noise than the old ones.

My sword grip had darkened where my hand landed every morning.

There were two shallow grooves in a patch of dirt behind the Guild where my feet had repeated the same side step until the ground remembered it better than I did.

The tree I used for the overhead cut had lost bark in one narrow vertical strip.

Hundreds of repetitions.

Possibly more than a thousand.

I had stopped counting when the number began feeling like evidence of competence instead of evidence of repetition.

Arlo had also stopped waiting for me to have ideas.

Two weeks ago he changed the clay mix without asking and improved the filtration result more than two of my suggestions combined.

I congratulated him.

Privately, I was offended.

Then he bought better measuring glass with his own money because the project belonged to his workshop too if I expected his name on the finished thing.

That offended me less.

Mostly because he was right.

The project had begun becoming real the moment it stopped being mine alone.

Jorren nudged my shoulder.

I had drifted toward a cart.

Right.

Street.

Current life.

The bathhouse had been closed since a pipe collapse flooded the lower rooms. The owner had converted the cellar into storage, which apparently meant putting old benches and heating stone somewhere damp until they became archaeological evidence.

Jorren stopped at the cellar door.

The smell got there first.

Mineral. Rot. Something sharp enough to make the inside of my nose feel clean.

Acid.

I knew acid.

Jorren looked at me.

"This kind?"

No.

"Good."

He handed me a strip of waxed cloth.

"Wrap your sword hand."

Why?

"If it spits on the hilt, you'll drop the sword."

I wrapped the hand.

No argument.

That might have been the month's greatest accomplishment.

The stairway went down beneath a low stone arch.

I ducked.

Then looked at my sword.

Jorren saw the look.

"What?"

Nothing.

The ceiling was merely shorter than my best attack.

A month of deliberate practice had met architecture before we had even found the monster.

Excellent.

The first saltback clung to the far wall.

Large dog, if the dog had six legs, mineral plates, and a mouth designed by someone who disliked dogs.

Gray-white crust covered its spine. Two sacs moved beneath the plates near its ribs.

I remembered a larger one.

A shield dissolving along one edge.

Someone screaming for water.

Useful warning.

No useful technique attached.

Jorren's voice came low beside me.

"Don't rush it."

I had not planned to.

Apparently my body had leaned forward without consulting the plan.

The side sacs tightened.

Spit before charge?

Jorren shook his head.

"Usually after."

Current evidence.

Good.

The saltback dropped from the wall.

It hit the stone and came at us much faster than something shaped like a masonry project should move.

Jorren went left.

I went right.

Small.

Clean.

The creature passed between us.

For half a second everything worked.

Its neck seam opened under the raised plates.

There.

Overhead cut.

My feet were already where they belonged.

I raised the sword.

Stone stopped it.

The blade cracked against the ceiling.

Pain ran down my wrist.

The saltback turned.

Jorren shouted.

I moved before I understood the word.

Side step.

Too large.

My heel caught an old bench and I went sideways over it.

Acid crossed the space where my chest had been.

The wall hissed.

My brain arrived half a second later with several observations that would have been much more useful half a second earlier.

Jorren cut at the rear leg.

His sword skidded off mineral plate.

The creature spun toward him.

Wrong room.

That was the problem.

The overhead cut was not bad.

The room had priced it out.

I did not need a better theory.

I needed a taller ceiling.

"Stairs."

Jorren blocked a charge with the bench.

"What?"

"Bring it to the stairs."

He looked up.

Then at my sword.

"Your fucking overhead thing?"

Yes.

The saltback hit the bench hard enough to crack the frame.

Jorren stopped discussing my training philosophy.

He kicked the broken bench into its path and backed toward the stairs.

I moved with him.

The saltback followed.

Side sacs tightening.

"After charge," Jorren said.

Right.

It came.

We split.

Small step.

No crossed feet.

The creature passed.

Jorren slapped its mineral plate with the flat of his sword and made himself the more interesting problem.

It turned toward him.

The stairwell arch rose above us.

Enough room.

The saltback charged again.

Jorren waited longer than I liked.

On purpose.

It committed.

He moved.

The neck seam opened.

My sword was already rising.

No flourish.

No clever angle.

No committee meeting with my shoulders.

Feet where I had put them a thousand times.

Straight down.

The blade struck behind the head.

Impact jarred both arms.

The edge bit.

Not enough.

The saltback screamed.

I tried to recover.

There.

The thing I had not practiced.

The sword stayed low one beat too long.

The creature twisted.

A side sac contracted.

I saw it.

Knew what came next.

My feet moved.

Almost.

Acid sprayed across my coat sleeve.

Heat arrived immediately.

I tore my arm free before the cloth could stick.

Jorren's sword came down sideways into the opening I had made.

The saltback collapsed.

For a moment we stood over it breathing.

My sleeve smoked.

Jorren looked at the dead creature.

Then at me.

"Your cut is stupid."

It had worked.

"You hit the ceiling first."

Different room.

"Then you almost got sprayed."

I pulled the coat off and threw it onto the floor.

The acid kit came out of my pocket.

Neutralizing powder hissed against the sleeve.

Four copper suddenly felt aggressively reasonable.

Recovery.

That was the problem.

Not the cut.

What came after it.

Jorren stared at me.

"There are two more down here."

Right.

Lesson later.

Monster now.

The second saltback was smaller and smarter.

It never gave me the stairwell.

Jorren killed it.

That mattered.

I kept its attention on the side with more room and called the sacs when they tightened. I made it turn once when Jorren needed space. I did not land the finishing blow.

I did not need to.

The third saltback did not exist.

We checked anyway.

Twenty minutes later we came back into daylight with two acid glands sealed in jars and enough mineral stink on us to become socially self-correcting.

Back at the Guild, five silver hit Sella's counter.

Two and a half went to Jorren.

Four copper had already gone to the kit.

My coat was ruined.

The waxed wrap needed replacing.

My wrist hurt where the ceiling had explained architecture to it.

Net profit remained positive.

Barely enough to preserve the fantasy.

Sella looked at my sleeve.

"Kit?"

Used it.

One nod.

No congratulations.

Good.

Jorren pocketed his share and checked the time.

The caravan quartermaster expected him.

Nine days.

Then he would be north.

That landed strangely.

A month ago he had been the annoying Bronze fighter who volunteered to hit me.

Now his absence had a date.

His life continued.

Of course it did.

Still worth noticing.

I looked down at my own share.

Overhead cut.

Side step.

One had killed a monster.

One had kept acid off my chest.

A month ago both had been ideas attached to memories of better fighters.

Now my body could do them.

Not perfectly.

Enough to matter.

That felt different from remembering.

It felt owned.

Sella slid a folded note across the counter.

Arlo's handwriting.

Four words in large angry letters.

**STOP BUYING SHALE. COME SEE THIS.**

I smiled.

Sella saw the expression.

"What now?"

Potentially expensive progress.

She sighed like the answer had confirmed something unfortunate.

I put the silver in my pocket.

My burned coat smelled worse every second.

My wrist hurt.

Recovery had become tomorrow's training problem.

Vale expected money in two days.

Jorren would be gone north next week.

Arlo had apparently moved the project forward without me.

The world had not waited while I learned two things.

Good.

I went to see what it had done.
