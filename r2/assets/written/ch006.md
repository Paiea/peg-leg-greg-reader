Vale counted the money twice.

Not because I was short.

That would have been easier.

He counted it once because that was what lenders did and a second time because apparently I had become interesting enough to verify.

"On time."

I had spent most of the walk rehearsing several good responses to that.

None improved my financial condition.

So I nodded.

Vale looked up.

That almost disappointed him.

Good.

Let him wonder.

He pushed my note across the desk with one finger.

Paid.

Clean.

No balance.

No part of my week belonging to another man.

For the first time in over a month, I owed Antonius Vale nothing.

I had also just reduced my available cash to an amount that made breakfast relevant again.

Freedom had excellent branding.

Expensive product.

I folded the note and put it in my pocket.

Vale did not offer another loan.

He did not offer work.

He did not congratulate me.

He just watched me leave with the expression of a man who had discovered a number did something unexpected and intended to keep the number in the ledger.

Fine.

I had enough systems watching me already.

Arlo's workshop was supposed to be the pleasant problem.

Eight copper.

One customer.

One ugly disk that had actually done something useful outside his bench.

The future, beginning badly and at retail.

I reached the cooper's alley and found Arlo standing outside with a woman whose hands were purple to the wrists.

Not stained lightly.

Purple.

The color lived in the cracks around her nails and disappeared beneath both sleeves.

She held a cloth bundle in one hand.

Arlo had his coat on.

That was wrong.

He almost never left the workshop during working hours unless the building was burning or somebody offered to pay him for leaving it.

The woman looked at me.

"Greg?"

Yes.

She shoved the bundle into my hands.

Ceramic clicked inside.

Several pieces.

Ah.

First customer.

Arlo introduced her as Ivena, the dyer from Mill Street.

Ivena corrected him.

"The dyer whose floor is purple because your filter split."

Specific.

Good start.

I unwrapped the cloth.

The disk had cracked through the center and broken along one edge. Purple residue had dried inside the fracture darker than the rest, almost black.

I looked at Arlo.

He was already looking at the break.

Not defensive.

Angry in the useful direction.

At the object.

"It worked," Ivena said.

That was not the sentence I expected.

I looked at her.

Two batches, apparently.

Better color. Less sludge left in the cloth later. Easier rinse.

Then the third slowed.

The trough backed up.

Her assistant increased the feed because people are extremely consistent across professions.

The disk split.

Concentrated wash went over the side.

Then something in the waste started reacting near the hot line.

I looked back at the crack.

"Reacting how?"

Ivena's eyes narrowed.

"Come look."

Good answer.

We went.

Mill Street smelled better from a distance.

Up close it was wet wool, hot metal, ash, mordant, soap, old water, and enough competing dye to make the air feel colored.

Ivena's shop occupied the ground floor of a long narrow building with vents cut high into the walls. Three large vats sat along one side. Copper troughs carried wash toward a stone drain channel in the floor. Racks of cloth hung overhead in strips of red, green, yellow, and several shades of purple I would previously have described as purple.

Apparently this was wrong.

Ivena had names for all of them.

She used three while pointing out exactly what our disk had ruined.

I remembered none.

The important part was the equipment.

Arlo's workshop tests had used bowls.

Small pour.

Cool enough to hold.

One pass at a time.

Ivena's line used a raised copper reservoir feeding a narrow housing beside the second vat. Warm wash ran through it for long stretches. Not boiling. Hot enough that the copper stayed warm after the flow stopped.

Different volume.

Different duration.

Different temperature.

Different problem.

The housing itself had been wiped clean, but the floor beneath it had not.

Purple stain ran toward the drain in a wide fan.

At the center, the color was nearly black.

One copper fitting above it had a strange pale mark crawling around the edge.

Not corrosion.

I crouched.

The hair on my forearm lifted.

Mana.

Very little.

Enough.

Ivena saw me notice.

"That."

Arlo crouched beside me.

He touched the dry stone near the stain, then stopped before touching the dark center.

Good.

"What is in the wash?"

Ivena listed things I mostly understood.

Pigment. Binding salt. Ash treatment. A cheap mana-set additive used to keep the darker purple from washing gray.

There.

Mana-set additive.

How much?

Not much.

Usually.

I looked at the stain again.

Usually did not matter if the filter was concentrating it.

Arlo heard the same thought in a different language.

"The disk was catching more than the sludge."

Maybe.

Ivena folded her arms.

"Is that bad?"

We did not know yet.

Her expression explained that this answer had limited commercial value.

Fair.

Arlo wanted to see the housing.

Ivena wanted to know whether opening it would make the floor do that again.

Also fair.

We compromised by being careful, which is the traditional stage before discovering how careful was insufficient.

The feed valve was closed.

The reservoir was mostly empty.

The line had cooled.

Arlo loosened the housing bolts one at a time.

I stood to the side instead of directly in front of it.

Current life had improved me.

A little.

The last bolt came loose.

Nothing happened.

Good.

Arlo pulled the housing apart.

The remaining piece of disk came with it.

Purple-black paste filled the ceramic pores along one side.

Not evenly.

The lower half was packed.

Upper half much less.

Flow problem.

Ivena pointed.

"That's where it slowed."

Arlo did not answer.

He had gone very still.

I knew that stillness now.

His version of shouting.

He scraped a tiny amount of the paste onto a metal pick.

The pick warmed in his fingers.

He dropped it.

It hit the wet stone.

Purple light moved across the water.

Not bright.

Fast.

A thin crawling line ran toward the copper trough.

Every later-life instinct I had woke up at once.

Not memories exactly.

Shape.

Failure shape.

Conductive path.

Wet floor.

Metal.

Mana-bearing residue.

People standing inside it.

"Back."

Ivena moved immediately.

Good.

Arlo did not.

He was staring at the pick.

I grabbed the back of his coat and pulled.

The purple line touched the trough.

Copper rang.

One clean note.

Then the entire wet section of trough flashed violet along the seam.

A worker at the far vat turned toward us.

Too close.

"Off the floor."

He froze.

Wrong response.

I pointed at the dry platform beside him.

"Up. Now."

That worked.

He climbed.

The line of light continued along the copper toward the raised reservoir.

I knew what I would have done forty years later.

Barrier under the seam.

Cut the conductive path.

Contain the spill.

Buy everyone ten seconds.

Simple.

I put out my hand.

Reached for mana.

The remembered motion was enormous.

The answer was not.

Pressure behind my eyes.

A weak pulse down my arm.

Then pain.

No barrier.

Not even close.

For one ugly second I was fifty-nine again in my head and nineteen everywhere that mattered.

The copper rang a second time.

Reality did not care.

Fine.

No barrier.

What did I have?

People.

Valves.

Dry wood.

Ash bins.

Drain channel.

A ceramic lid leaning against the wall.

"Ivena, heat."

She was already moving.

Of course she was.

Her shop.

She shut the burner under the second vat and yelled at the worker near the rear line to kill the main feed.

Arlo pointed at the reservoir.

"Pressure's trapped above the housing."

There.

That mattered.

If the line warmed or expanded with the valve closed, the housing could spit the remaining concentrate somewhere new.

Ivena knew the bypass.

"Left wheel. Half turn only."

The worker on the platform reached for it.

Purple light crawled closer beneath him.

Too slow.

I grabbed the ceramic lid.

Heavy.

Good.

I set its edge into the wet channel between the stain and the trough.

Not enough.

Water moved around both sides.

Ash.

I kicked the nearest bin over.

Gray powder dumped across the floor.

Ivena made a sound of personal injury.

Later.

The ash soaked the thin water path and broke it into dark islands.

The crawling light weakened.

Not stopped.

Weakened.

The worker turned the bypass.

Half turn.

The pipe knocked.

Pressure moved.

The housing spat.

A narrow stream of purple-black wash shot sideways.

Toward Arlo.

My hand was already up.

Stupid.

Instinct.

Mana answered this time.

Not a wall.

Not even a proper barrier.

A palm-wide shimmer snapped into existence in front of my hand.

Thin.

Crooked.

Beautiful.

The stream hit it.

For less than a second, the liquid split around my palm instead of through it.

Then the shimmer vanished.

The rest struck the ceramic lid.

I turned my face away.

Hot droplets hit my sleeve.

Not acid.

Still unpleasant.

Arlo stumbled back onto dry stone.

The pipe stopped knocking.

The violet line faded along the trough.

Silence.

Not complete silence.

Dyehouse silence.

Water dripping.

Somebody breathing too loudly.

A vat bubbling because nobody had told the other two vats that the scene was over.

I looked at my hand.

Nothing there.

No barrier.

Of course not.

But it had been there.

One breath.

Less.

Enough to change where the first part of the spray went.

My arm started shaking.

I lowered it before anyone could mistake that for drama.

Ivena walked over.

She looked at the ash covering half her floor.

Then at me.

Then at Arlo.

"You owe me ash."

Good.

I liked her.

Arlo sat on an overturned bucket and examined the ceramic fragments with the expression of a man trying to solve a murder where he had made the victim.

Ivena did not let him retreat into it.

One ruined portion of wash.

One interrupted work cycle.

A stained floor that had, admittedly, already been a stained floor.

A cracked fitting she wanted checked before restarting the line.

And one filter she had paid for that had worked well enough to make the failure more irritating.

That last part mattered.

If the disk had simply been useless, we would refund eight copper and go home embarrassed.

It had improved two batches.

Then scale had changed the problem.

I asked what she wanted.

Not philosophically.

Today.

Ivena wanted the eight copper back.

Reasonable.

She also wanted compensation for the ruined wash.

Less pleasant.

Still reasonable.

And she wanted us to look at the line before she put another one of our disks anywhere near it.

That surprised me.

"Another?"

She looked at the pale second bowl sitting on the work table.

The result from the first batch.

"It worked. Then it broke. Those are two facts."

There.

Outside mind.

Better sentence than mine.

Arlo finally looked up.

He asked about temperature on the first two runs.

Lower.

Third batch had been warmer because they were behind schedule.

Flow rate?

Higher for the same reason.

How long before slowing?

Longer than the first two full tests combined.

Arlo's face changed.

He was no longer defending the disk.

He was collecting the failure.

Good.

Ivena knew her process.

Arlo knew the material.

I knew enough about later systems to recognize that neither of those jobs belonged to me.

My job was somewhere else.

Connections.

What changed?

What became dangerous?

What mattered next?

Heat.

Flow.

Time under load.

Concentration.

Maybe mana salt.

Maybe all of them.

A month ago I would have tried to solve all five before leaving the building.

Current me had recently been introduced to ceilings.

I picked two.

Arlo needed to reproduce the clog under warm continuous flow.

Ivena needed a way to bypass the disk before pressure built if the flow slowed again.

Everything else could wait until those answers existed.

Arlo looked at me.

That was almost approval.

Ivena wanted the fitting checked first.

Correct.

Her shop.

Her order.

We paid her.

Mostly Arlo.

I had just paid Vale and was once again rich primarily in arguments.

Partnership acquired a second meaning.

Shared upside had sounded excellent yesterday.

Shared losses had arrived before lunch.

Good.

Real things did that.

On the walk back to Arlo's workshop, neither of us spoke for half a street.

Then he said, "What did you do with your hand?"

I knew what he meant.

Nothing impressive.

A fraction of a barrier.

Calling it a barrier felt generous.

The old version of me could have covered the entire trough without thinking about it.

The old version of me could have isolated the spill, shielded three people, and probably complained about the pipe layout while doing it.

Current me had made something the size of a plate for less than a heartbeat and nearly given myself a headache doing it.

Arlo had seen the liquid split.

So had I.

That was the problem.

It had worked.

A little.

Which meant I wanted it again immediately.

Dangerous pattern.

I flexed my hand.

No pain.

Just the fading tremor in the forearm.

"Support magic."

Arlo waited.

I had no better explanation that did not require forty years I could not prove.

"Bad support magic."

He accepted that more easily.

We reached the alley.

The workshop door was still open from when we had left in a hurry.

On the bench sat Arlo's notes, the remaining test pieces, and the empty space where the first sold disk had been.

Yesterday, that empty space had looked like success.

Now it looked like liability, evidence, and a second test plan.

Better.

More honest.

Arlo picked up a fresh ceramic blank.

"Warm flow. Longer run."

And a bypass.

He nodded.

The dyer had become part of the experiment whether either of us liked it or not.

Also good.

I sat on the repaired stool and looked at my hand again.

One pulse.

One crooked shimmer.

Enough to move liquid for less than a second.

My sword had taken a month to become mine.

Maybe mana would take longer.

Maybe much longer.

That hurt more than the sword had.

Swordsmanship was something I had respected.

Support was what I had become.

There are failures that tell you what you cannot do.

This one had done something worse.

It reminded me what I wanted back.

Outside, someone rolled a cart through the alley.

The wheel hit a stone and rattled.

Arlo started measuring clay.

I had almost no money.

Jorren left north soon.

Our first customer wanted a refund, a repair, and possibly another filter.

My old magic existed in my body as approximately one badly shaped breath.

Progress.
