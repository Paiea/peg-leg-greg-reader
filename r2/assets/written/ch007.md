# Chapter 7: The Extra Guard

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Batch rehearsal: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/007-010-story-batch.md`

---

Jorren left north in two days.

I knew because his new boots had stopped squeaking.

That and Sella had put his caravan contract beneath the counter instead of back on the board.

Apparently paperwork also understood inevitability.

I was at the Guild because I needed money again.

Not much.

Enough for food, yard time, replacement ash, and whatever Arlo discovered next that would somehow cost silver before it became worth copper.

This was different from debt.

Mostly because Antonius Vale no longer owned part of my week.

I intended to enjoy that distinction for as long as possible.

Sella watched me read the board.

Not openly.

She had forms.

Forms allowed observation without admitting interest.

Road escort.

Cellar clearing.

Night watch at a tannery.

Lost dog.

The dog paid more than the cellar.

Correct market signal.

I kept reading.

A second paper had been pinned over one of the road contracts.

**ONE BRONZE ESCORT NEEDED. FIRST LEG ONLY. DEPARTURE TODAY.**

I read the destination.

North road.

Same caravan as Jorren.

Interesting.

"No," Sella said.

I had not turned around.

"You don't know what I'm thinking."

"Road contract."

"Could be the dog."

"You don't like dogs."

I looked at her.

She had learned too much.

"Why is there an opening?"

The original guard had stepped into a drainage ditch while carrying a crate and twisted an ankle badly enough to miss departure.

Not heroic.

Still unemployed him.

The caravan needed another Bronze body for the first leg to Brell Crossing, six days north if the weather behaved.

There was a southbound return caravan two days after arrival.

Two weeks away, roughly.

Longer if roads were bad.

Arlo would survive.

Probably more efficiently.

That thought was becoming less insulting.

I looked at the listed pay.

Then at the roster.

Jorren.

Four other escorts.

Quartermaster.

Driver count.

Meral, warder.

I read the last name twice.

Sella noticed.

Of course she did.

"You know Meral?"

No.

Not personally.

Not in the future either.

But warder meant working support magic attached to a moving system full of fragile people, animals, wheels, axles, food, money, and bad decisions.

Which was close enough to religion at the moment.

"I know what a warder does."

Sella's face suggested this was not the same thing.

Fair.

Jorren came in from the yard carrying his practice sword over one shoulder.

He saw the paper in my hand.

"No."

Apparently everyone had coordinated.

"Excellent sales culture here."

He took the paper and read it.

Then looked at me.

"You hate walking."

"I hate unnecessary walking."

"It's a road."

"Roads go places."

He considered that.

"You know caravans sleep outside?"

"I have camped before."

"Recently?"

No.

"Still counts."

Sella held out a hand.

Not for the contract.

For my token.

I gave it to her.

She checked my recent contract marks, then the yard register, then me.

A month ago I would have been offended by the inspection.

Current me had been introduced to customer failures, debt agreements, acid kits, and ceilings.

Systems checking things had moved up my list of comforts.

She stamped the paper.

"First leg only."

"I can read."

"Return caravan leaves Brell two mornings after arrival. If you miss it, you're finding your own way back."

"Also readable."

"Do not make me regret this."

There.

That line got through.

Not because of the words.

Because Sella had stopped speaking to me like an unknown Bronze fighter asking dumb questions at the desk.

A month had done that too.

I folded the contract.

"I'll regret it enough for both of us."

She had already reached for another form.

Good.

I told Arlo I was leaving while he was rebuilding Ivena's filter housing out of pieces spread across his bench.

He did not look up.

"How long?"

Two weeks, roughly.

That got his attention.

I explained the contract.

Then the warder.

His expression changed in exactly the wrong direction.

"You're paying someone to teach you magic now?"

"They're paying me."

He stared.

I enjoyed that for three full seconds.

"Road escort."

"Yes."

"You."

"I've been hit by professionals."

"Jorren is not a professional."

"Tell him that."

Arlo returned to the housing.

Warm-flow test tomorrow.

Long run after that if the new bypass behaved.

I told him not to change the ceramic mix and the housing geometry on the same test.

He told me he knew.

I told him because people knowing things had not stopped people before.

He told me to leave.

Partnership.

Healthy.

The caravan was already loading when I reached the north gate.

Nine wagons.

Two passenger carts.

Sixteen draft animals.

Enough people that nobody knew everyone and few enough that mistakes still belonged to specific names.

Jorren was tightening a strap on the third wagon.

He looked at my pack.

Then at me.

"That's all?"

I had clothes.

Food for two days.

Bedroll.

Sword.

Water.

A small pouch of coin I disliked discussing.

What else did civilization require?

Jorren opened his mouth.

A woman behind him said, "If he forgot something, he'll remember it after the gate."

I turned.

Meral.

Thirty-five, maybe forty.

Short dark hair cut with no concern for whether it framed anything. Heavy travel coat despite the heat. Chalk dust on one sleeve. Three narrow cords looped around her belt, each marked at intervals with black thread.

No staff.

No dramatic focus crystal.

A small hammer.

I liked her immediately.

That was usually dangerous.

She looked at the sword.

Then my boots.

Then my hands.

Her eyes paused on the new callus.

"Extra guard?"

Yes.

"Can you walk six days?"

Probably.

"Wrong answer."

"Yes."

"Better."

She moved on.

I watched the cords at her belt.

Jorren saw me.

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're doing with your face."

Sella had infected him.

We left before noon.

Carrow did not disappear dramatically.

No hilltop view.

No last look at the walls beneath a heroic sky.

The road bent around an orchard.

When it straightened again, the city was mostly gone behind trees.

That was it.

Six chapters of my second life and Carrow vanished because the road turned left.

Good.

I kept walking.

The first hour was easy.

The second introduced the pack straps to my shoulders.

The third introduced dust to everything else.

By late afternoon I had learned three useful things about caravans.

First, wagons produced sound constantly and none of it meant the same thing.

Wheel creak.

Harness knock.

Loose chain.

Axle complaint.

Driver swearing because another driver had stopped too quickly.

Second, professional road travel was mostly preventing small errors from becoming large ones.

Third, Meral listened to the wagons more than she looked at them.

That interested me.

We were descending a long grade between two low ridges when one sound changed.

Metal snapped.

Small sound.

Bad consequence.

The rear wagon lurched.

A driver shouted.

One of the left traces went slack.

The wagon's tongue jumped sideways against the team.

The horses surged.

The wheel brake screamed.

Then the loaded wagon started gaining on the wagon ahead of it.

Everyone moved at once.

My old mind supplied a barrier.

Across the road.

Wide enough to catch the wagon.

Angle it slightly.

Spread the load.

Easy.

My hand came up.

Meral hit my wrist with one of her cords.

Not hard.

Enough.

"Don't stop it."

The wagon gained another yard.

"It'll crush the team."

Right.

A wall solved the wagon.

It did not solve the horses attached to it.

Support.

Annoying profession.

Meral pointed downhill.

"Turn it."

There was a drainage bank on the right side of the road.

Shallow at first.

Deeper twenty yards ahead.

The wagon needed to enter before the ditch became a drop.

The driver was pulling uselessly on the reins while trying to work a brake lever that had already become a decorative suggestion.

Jorren ran for the wagon.

I ran for the side.

The rear carried two coils of drag rope.

One was tied down.

The other wasn't.

Current experience rewarded laziness occasionally.

I pulled it free.

"Post!" Meral shouted.

A road marker stood ahead on the right.

Wood.

Thick enough.

Maybe.

I threw the coil to Jorren.

He missed it.

Not his fault.

Running beside a moving wagon while somebody throws thirty feet of rope at you turned out to be a specialized skill.

The rope hit the dirt.

Jorren grabbed the end.

I took the coil and ran ahead.

The wagon was faster.

Excellent.

I reached the marker with perhaps two seconds of dignity remaining.

No time for a proper wrap.

One turn.

Half hitch.

Bad knot.

Good enough if the goal was not stopping the wagon.

I looked at the drainage bank.

We needed the rope to drag the rear right, not arrest the whole load.

"Right rear!"

Jorren understood.

He climbed onto the wagon's back step, got the rope around the corner post, then jumped off before the line tightened.

Smart.

The rope came alive.

No other word for it.

Slack vanished.

Fiber snapped against wood.

The road marker bent.

The wagon's rear shifted right.

Too little.

The rope started climbing the marker.

If it slipped over the top, we got nothing.

If it held too well, the post broke.

Meral was already moving her hands.

Not a wall.

Two flat planes of pale light appeared beside the wagon's front wheel, angled toward the ditch.

Guides.

Not stops.

The wheel hit one.

The ward flexed.

The wagon turned half a foot.

The rope climbed higher.

Greg, fifty-nine, could have pinned the line.

Greg, nineteen, had one badly shaped breath.

Fine.

One breath.

I put my palm against the side of the road marker without touching the rope.

Small.

Not a barrier.

A plane.

Thumb to knuckle.

I had never done it that way.

My mana did not care about tradition.

It cared about whether I could make the shape.

Pressure behind the eyes.

Pulse down the forearm.

A translucent sliver appeared above the rope.

Wrong angle.

The rope hit it and started to slide around.

I twisted my wrist.

The plane twisted too.

Ugly.

Enough.

The rope stayed below the top of the post for one more second.

One second was a great deal of time when several thousand pounds of wagon were moving downhill.

The rear swung.

The front wheel struck Meral's second guide.

The wagon entered the shallow ditch sideways.

Mud exploded.

The wheel dug in.

The whole frame leaned.

Jorren yelled.

Driver jumped.

The team stumbled forward free of the worst angle.

The wagon traveled another ten feet through the ditch and stopped against the bank with a sound that suggested at least one crate had become a different shape.

Silence lasted half a heartbeat.

Then everyone started shouting.

Good.

People who could complain were usually alive.

I looked at the road marker.

The top six inches had split.

The rope smoked where it had dragged.

My tiny plane was gone.

My forearm shook.

Again.

Less than at Ivena's.

Still there.

Meral walked past me toward the wagon.

"Horses first."

Right.

We spent the next fifteen minutes turning excitement back into logistics.

One horse had a scraped flank.

No broken leg.

Driver had bruised one knee.

Two crates of pottery had become archaeological evidence.

One axle bracket bent.

The wagon itself could move after we reset the tongue and redistributed weight.

Nobody died.

Nobody even bled impressively.

Best kind of emergency.

The quartermaster stood over the broken harness pin with the expression of a man discovering that metal had opinions about schedules.

Meral picked up the pin.

Old crack through half the section.

Fresh break through the rest.

She handed it to him without commentary.

That was crueler.

Then she came to me.

"Who taught you to brace?"

Nobody.

Not like that.

"I've done barriers."

Her eyes went to my hand.

"No, you haven't."

Fair.

"Not recently."

That got a look.

I was collecting those.

She pointed at the split road marker.

"Your first thought was to stop the wagon."

Yes.

"Would've killed a horse. Maybe the driver."

Also yes.

"Then you used a brace the size of a dinner plate in the only place it mattered."

I waited.

"Which part do you think I care about?"

The second.

She nodded.

Then took one of the marked cords from her belt and held it between both hands.

"Make a plane between these knots."

The distance was maybe four inches.

I stared.

"Now?"

"You want a lesson or a story about one?"

There.

I put my hand beneath the cord.

Reached.

Nothing.

Again.

A pulse.

No shape.

Again.

My head started to hurt.

Meral lowered the cord.

"Stop."

I almost tried again.

She saw that too.

"That's also part of it."

Annoying woman.

Good.

By sunset the damaged wagon was moving again.

Slower.

The caravan made camp beside a stand of low pines because the schedule had lost the argument.

Jorren dropped his bedroll near mine.

"First day."

I lay back on the ground.

Every part of me had opinions.

"Useful benchmark."

"You almost got run over."

"I did not."

"You ran downhill in front of a wagon."

Different thing.

He laughed.

Across camp, Meral was checking the repaired harness herself.

Not teaching.

Working.

That mattered.

The road had not handed me a mentor.

It had handed me a woman whose job created problems close enough to the ones I wanted to understand.

Much better.

I looked south.

No Carrow.

Just road disappearing into dark.

Arlo would run the warm-flow test tomorrow without me.

Sella would process contracts without me.

Vale would lend money to somebody else.

Jorren would keep going north when my leg ended.

The world had not paused because I left the city.

Good.

My forearm still trembled slightly.

I held one hand above the other.

Thumb to knuckle.

Four inches.

Nothing appeared.

I stopped before the headache returned.

Also progress.
