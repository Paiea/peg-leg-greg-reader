# Chapter 11: The North Gate

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Batch rehearsal: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/011-013-story-batch.md`

---

I spent the morning not buying a sword.

This required more discipline than it deserved.

The balanced one was still at the smith's.

I knew because I had checked.

Not to buy it.

To confirm the price remained unreasonable.

It did.

Excellent merchant.

My road pay sat in my purse with the dangerous dignity of money that had not yet been assigned a purpose.

Support lessons were possible.

The sword was possible.

Arlo could use more capital.

Food continued its long campaign against every other budget category.

I chose none of them.

For one day, I would keep the money.

This felt almost irresponsible.

Then Sella found me work.

The north gate had a traffic problem.

Not a monster.

Not a magical breach.

Not bandits.

Masons were replacing stone above the eastern arch after the rain had opened a seam wide enough for water to reach the inner mortar. The city wanted the gate kept open during repairs because closing one of Carrow's main entries for half a day would apparently cause trade to collapse, citizens to riot, and several officials to experience paperwork.

The Guild needed four Bronze bodies to keep carts away from the work zone, move pedestrians through one side at a time, and stop people from standing beneath suspended stone to watch men repair suspended stone.

The pay was modest.

The risk looked lower than saltbacks.

I took it immediately.

Sella looked at my purse when I signed.

Not literally.

She had developed a way of looking at me that included finances without requiring visual confirmation.

"Saving?"

"Trying something new."

"Does it hurt?"

"Constantly."

She stamped the paper.

That was enough conversation.

The north gate had changed since the last time I passed through it.

Scaffolding covered the eastern side of the inner arch. A timber hoist stood on the wall walk above, its rope hanging through a pulley over the repair face. Two masons were cutting back damaged mortar while another crew sorted replacement blocks on the ground.

The stone was local gray limestone.

Mostly.

One stack near the wall had a warmer color, almost tan where fresh chips showed beneath dust.

I looked at it longer than necessary.

No memory arrived.

Fine.

Present job first.

The foreman was a woman named Vessa with forearms like bundled rope and the conversational patience of a falling hammer.

She divided the gate into three lanes.

Wagons east.

Foot traffic west.

Nothing stopped beneath the arch.

If she shouted clear, we cleared.

If a mason shouted clear, we cleared faster.

If anyone from the city office arrived with a suggestion, we ignored it until Vessa repeated it.

I liked her immediately.

No lesson attached.

She was busy.

My position was outside the gate, where incoming carts had to be slowed before the approach narrowed.

For the first hour, nothing happened.

That was the work.

A vegetable cart tried to pass a brewer's dray inside the narrowed lane.

I stopped both.

The vegetable seller informed me that he had used this gate every morning for eleven years.

I congratulated him.

He did not appreciate the achievement.

A courier ducked beneath the rope line because apparently written messages were immune to gravity.

I pulled him back by the collar before Vessa saw him.

He objected until a mason dropped a fist-sized piece of old mortar where his head had been.

His opinion changed.

Efficient institution.

By midday, I had learned the rhythm.

Hoist up.

Traffic stops.

Stone crosses the approach overhead.

Stone reaches scaffold.

Traffic moves.

Again.

The replacement blocks were not enormous. Two men could shift one on the ground. Suspended ten feet above a road full of horses, they gained authority.

The warm-colored stack bothered Vessa too.

I noticed because she rejected the first block from it.

Not dramatically.

She tapped the edge with her hammer, inspected the grain, and told the city clerk beside her that it was too soft for the outer face.

The clerk said it met the purchase specification.

Vessa said the wall did not care.

They argued quietly.

I wanted to listen.

A wagon arrived.

Work first.

The block went back into the stack.

Another gray one took its place on the hoist cradle.

The rope tightened.

Wood creaked.

Nothing unusual.

Then one of the horses beneath me lifted its head.

Not mine.

The dray driver's.

The animal looked up before I did.

Interesting.

I followed its ears.

The hoist rope had begun rubbing against the side of the upper pulley housing.

Barely.

Each turn moved the line a fraction farther right.

The stone was halfway up.

I raised a hand to stop the next wagon.

The driver swore.

I pointed upward.

He stopped swearing.

One of the masons on the scaffold saw it too.

"Hold!"

The hoist crew stopped cranking.

The rope settled.

For perhaps a second, the problem became smaller.

Then the timber bracket holding the pulley shifted.

Not broke.

Shifted.

A short, ugly movement where wood should not move.

Vessa shouted clear.

People responded at different speeds.

That was the danger.

Workers moved immediately.

Drivers hesitated because horses and carts required agreement.

Pedestrians looked around to discover whether the warning applied to them personally.

It always did.

The bracket shifted again.

The loaded cradle swung away from the wall.

Ten feet above the road.

A woman with two children had stopped directly beneath the western edge of the arch.

Not under the stone.

Under where the stone would swing if the bracket let go.

I ran.

No mana.

Did not even reach for it.

There was no four-inch solution to several hundred pounds of limestone.

One child saw me coming and froze.

The mother turned.

Wrong direction.

"Wall!"

She looked at the wall.

Good enough.

I grabbed the older child by the shoulder and shoved him toward the west pier. The mother followed because children remained a better command system than strangers.

The smaller child stumbled.

I caught the back of his shirt.

Above us, wood cracked.

Not rope.

Bracket.

Vessa shouted something I did not hear.

The stone dropped six inches.

Stopped.

The hoist crew had locked the drum.

The pulley bracket was now tilted out from the wall.

The cradle hung at an angle.

One retaining pin remained through the lower side of the mount.

Everything depended on a piece of iron the length of my hand.

People were still inside the gate.

Not beneath the load anymore.

Better.

Not done.

A cart horse panicked at the cracking wood and backed into the wagon behind it.

The second wagon rolled sideways into the foot lane.

Someone fell.

I left the stone to the masons.

Domain authority.

My problem was now horses and people.

I got to the first horse's head before the driver did.

Bad idea.

Necessary one.

The animal's eyes were white at the edges.

I did not know horses.

I knew frightened things.

Different expertise.

I took the bridle near the cheek instead of the reins and turned its head toward the wall, away from the hanging stone.

The driver arrived and took over without discussion.

Correct.

Behind us, the fallen pedestrian was trying to stand beneath the axle of the sideways cart.

"Stay down."

He looked offended.

The cart rolled another inch.

Offense became cooperation.

Another guard chocked the rear wheel with a timber block.

I pulled the man out by both wrists.

No blood.

Bad ankle maybe.

Enough.

Vessa's voice cut across the gate.

"Greg!"

I looked up.

She knew my name now.

Unfortunate.

The hoist crew could not lower the stone.

The twisted bracket had trapped the rope against the housing. If they released the drum, the rope might tear free instead of running through the pulley.

They needed the weight off the bracket before they could unload it.

Vessa had two masons moving a timber support beneath the cradle from the scaffold side.

The support was six feet long.

The gap was seven.

They could not get enough angle.

I saw the problem because I was standing below it.

"Raise the street end."

Vessa looked down.

I pointed at the timber.

The masons were trying to push the scaffold end upward.

Wrong leverage.

Not wrong exactly.

Expensive.

"Street end up first. Pivot on the rail."

Vessa looked once.

Then told them to do it.

No debate.

The timber rose from the outer end, pivoted over the scaffold rail, and slid beneath the cradle.

The stone settled onto wood.

The bracket stopped carrying the full load.

Everyone breathed differently.

That was all I did.

No spell.

No sword.

Pointed at the correct end of a beam.

Useful profession.

The masons chained the cradle to the scaffold and unloaded the block by hand.

Only after the stone was safe did Vessa climb to the wall walk and inspect the hoist mount.

I stayed below.

Traffic could wait.

The city clerk appeared again.

His face had gained concern without losing paperwork.

Vessa came down holding a wooden wedge in one hand.

Split through the grain.

Fresh break at one end.

Old dark line through the middle.

Not today's failure.

Today's completion.

She put it in the clerk's palm.

"That was reused."

He said the hoist had passed inspection.

She asked by whom.

The answer was not someone currently present.

Naturally.

I looked at the gate.

Really looked.

The repair seam above the eastern arch.

New mortar beside old.

Warm stone stacked below.

Timber scaffolding drilled into temporary sockets.

City traffic waiting on both sides.

A system being kept alive while people repaired one small part of it.

Northern Gate.

The words arrived from forty years later.

Collapsed.

I knew that.

I had written it down on my first morning as an example of something I thought I knew.

Did I know the gate collapsed in the year I remembered, or did I remember the anniversary people celebrated afterward?

I had asked myself that exact question.

Now I was standing beneath it.

I looked at the eastern arch again.

Was this the repair?

No idea.

Was the warm stone important?

No idea.

The split hoist wedge?

Almost certainly not.

Maybe the gate collapsed thirty years from now because of a siege.

Maybe an earthquake.

Maybe neglected drainage.

Maybe a mason not yet born made a mistake on a completely different wall.

Maybe this gate would be rebuilt twice before the one I remembered failed.

Correct future fact.

Missing history.

I felt the old impulse immediately.

Investigate.

Find records.

Identify suppliers.

Trace repairs.

Solve the collapse before lunch.

The injured pedestrian groaned behind me.

Present first.

I helped him onto a cart headed toward a healer.

The gate stayed closed for three hours while Vessa replaced the hoist mount and inspected every temporary fixing on the scaffold.

The city complained.

Trade survived.

By late afternoon, one lane reopened.

Vessa paid the Guild workers herself because the clerk had disappeared somewhere administrative.

She gave me my coins.

Then one extra copper.

I looked at it.

"Beam."

Apparently pointing had a rate.

I put it away.

She was already turning toward the masons.

I almost asked about the warm-colored stone.

Almost asked who supplied it.

Almost asked how often the gate had been repaired.

Not today.

One thing at a time.

I walked through the north gate on my way back into Carrow.

The arch above me looked exactly as solid as it had that morning.

That was the problem with future catastrophes.

Most of them spent years looking like architecture.

I did not know whether I had seen anything important.

For once, I let that remain true.
