# Chapter 14: The Back Room

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

High-heat rehearsal: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/014-mara-performance.md`

---

Sella had work for me.

Of course she did.

Two days watching a bonded warehouse near the river while the regular night crew testified in a theft case.

Easy work, probably.

Which meant the pay was good enough to be irritating and not good enough to justify dying if the word probably turned out to be ambitious.

I read the slip twice.

Two days.

Mara left in two days.

Not complicated.

My brain began complicating it immediately.

Night shifts. Sleep in the afternoon. See Mara in the morning. Pack food. Skip yard time. Maybe the second shift ended early. Maybe I could leave a message. Maybe I could do both if every unrelated person in Carrow agreed to behave on schedule for forty-eight consecutive hours.

I put the slip back on the counter.

Sella looked at it.

Then at me.

"No?"

"No."

She waited.

That was the entire interrogation.

Somehow worse.

"You need money," she said.

"I know."

"All right."

She moved the contract to another stack.

No lesson.

No approving smile because I had correctly identified that human beings occasionally ranked above silver.

The next Bronze fighter took the slip ten minutes later while I was still pretending to read the board.

He looked pleased.

Good for him.

Bad for my purse.

Both could be true.

I left the Guild before I found another contract I could rationalize.

The brass key Mara had given me felt small in my pocket.

Her mother's house stood three streets south of the river market behind a bakery I remembered only after smelling it.

Not the bakery itself.

The corner.

Warm yeast. Burnt flour. Rain on stone.

A memory moved somewhere behind my eyes and refused to become useful.

I kept walking.

The house was narrower than I expected.

Two floors. Green shutters faded almost gray. A brass bell pull beside the door.

I reached for it.

Then looked at the key.

Mara had said to keep it because her mother would hit me with a spoon if I made her explain me to myself.

That sounded like permission.

Possibly a threat.

I tried the key.

The lock turned.

That hurt more than it should have.

Locks were supposed to change when people stopped belonging somewhere.

Apparently nobody had informed this one.

I stepped inside.

"Shoes."

I froze.

A woman's voice from the kitchen.

Not surprised.

Not asking who had entered.

Shoes.

I looked down.

Mud at the edge of one boot.

Some rules survived memory loss.

I took them off.

Mara's mother came into the hall carrying a wooden spoon.

Of course.

She was shorter than Mara, broader through the shoulders, gray beginning at both temples. Flour dusted the front of her apron.

Her eyes reached my face and stopped.

For one second she did nothing.

Then she hit me with the spoon.

Not hard.

Forearm.

Sharp enough to be instructional.

"Ow."

"Six weeks."

I rubbed the spot.

"Mara told you."

"Mara told me enough."

She looked at me again.

The anger did not disappear.

It made room.

Her free hand came up and touched my cheek.

Just once.

I did not move.

"You look wrong," she said.

That was becoming a theme among people who knew me.

I had no good answer.

She seemed to recognize that and turned away.

"She's upstairs. Boxes are in the back room. If you came to make this harder, leave before I waste good flour feeding you."

Reasonable terms.

I followed the hallway.

The back room was at the end.

The door stood open.

I stopped outside it.

Small bed.

Narrow desk beneath the window.

One shelf bowed in the middle.

A nail beside the door with nothing hanging from it.

The room had become storage. Two empty crates. Rolled blankets. A broken chair stacked upside down on the desk.

But underneath that, something fit.

Not memory exactly.

Scale.

I knew where the bed would creak if I sat on the left edge.

I knew the window stuck in wet weather.

I knew there had once been a water stain above the shelf shaped vaguely like a boot.

I looked up.

Paint covered the ceiling.

The stain was gone.

My hand moved to the window latch.

Stuck.

I lifted before pulling.

It opened.

Cold river air came in.

I stood there with one hand on the frame.

Eight months.

I had slept here for eight months after my parents died.

The room knew more about it than I did.

"You found it."

Mara stood behind me carrying folded shirts.

"Apparently."

She put the shirts into one of the crates.

No hug.

No surprise.

Just packing.

Good.

I picked up the second crate.

"Where?"

"My room."

I followed her upstairs.

Mara owned more books than seemed financially responsible.

Ledgers too.

Practice books filled with columns in three different hands. Shipping schedules. River tables. A folded map of western routes pinned crooked above the desk.

Halden had been circled.

Not recently.

The ink had faded at the edge.

I looked at it.

Mara saw me.

"I wanted the job before they offered it."

"How long?"

"Since winter."

I had missed that too.

She handed me a stack of books.

"Heavy ones on the bottom."

I almost said I knew.

Decided against it.

We packed.

That was easier than talking and harder than silence.

Books first.

Then clothes.

Two pairs of shoes wrapped in paper.

A chipped blue cup Mara refused to leave behind despite her mother shouting from downstairs that Halden probably possessed cups.

"Not this one."

She packed it herself.

I did not ask.

After ten minutes, my hands remembered where her room's floorboard dipped near the wardrobe.

After twenty, I remembered that the western window rattled in wind.

After thirty, Mara handed me a small wooden box and my body knew not to open it.

I stared at the box.

She noticed.

"You remember that?"

"No."

"Then why are you holding it like it bites?"

I looked down.

Both hands beneath it. Thumbs nowhere near the lid.

"Apparently I had boundaries."

"Occasionally."

She took the box from me and put it into a crate herself.

I smiled.

She almost did.

We kept going.

At some point her mother brought bread and cheese upstairs without asking whether either of us wanted it.

I ate two pieces before remembering manners.

Mara watched me.

"That part's the same."

"Good."

The word escaped before I could stop it.

She raised an eyebrow.

I chewed.

Fine.

No, not fine.

I swallowed.

"What else?"

"What else what?"

"Is the same."

Mara sat on the floor beside an open crate.

I sat across from her.

She considered me for long enough that I regretted asking.

"You still count exits."

I looked toward the door.

Then the window.

Apparently.

"You still read price boards even when you're not buying anything."

That sounded right.

"You still hate asking for help until the problem becomes embarrassing."

Less flattering.

"You still decide somebody is competent and then immediately become competitive with them."

Sella.

Meral.

Arlo.

I looked at her.

"Was I always this irritating?"

"Worse. You had less evidence."

That got me.

I laughed.

Mara did too.

For a moment the room felt easy.

Then she looked down at the half-packed crate.

"You were coming with me."

The sentence landed quietly.

Not accusation.

Not quite.

"Eventually," I said.

"That's what you told yourself."

I waited.

"You said you'd take contracts west. Halden first. Maybe farther if it was good."

Young Greg again.

Restless.

Planning.

Carrow getting small.

"Did you want me to?"

Mara looked at me.

"Then? Yes."

Then.

A clean word with a wall inside it.

"Now?"

She did not answer immediately.

I let her have the time.

"Now I don't know you well enough to ask you to move cities for me."

That hurt.

It was also sane.

"I don't know me well enough either."

"That's not reassuring."

"I know."

She picked at a loose thread on the blanket beside her.

"Why did you come today?"

I could have said because I promised.

That sounded transactional now.

I came because I had already watched six weeks disappear into things that all felt urgent while I was doing them.

I came because she had become a deadline I could understand.

I came because a locked warehouse would still exist tomorrow and Mara would not.

The warehouse contract was already gone anyway.

"Because you were leaving," I said.

Mara waited.

"And I didn't want that to become another thing I remembered after it was too late to do anything about it."

Her face changed.

Small.

Enough.

"That's a very old-man answer."

"I get those sometimes."

She studied me.

"Are you sick?"

There it was again.

Not memory this time.

Fear.

I shook my head.

"I don't think so."

"That is an awful answer."

"It's the honest one."

"You keep doing that like honesty is automatically kind."

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

She was right.

Mara leaned back against the bed.

"I don't need you to tell me everything."

That surprised me.

"I need you not to make me stupid while I don't know it."

I understood immediately.

No false promises.

No pretending memory existed because the truth was uncomfortable.

No letting her plan around a version of me I could not guarantee.

I almost explained that.

Did not.

"Okay."

Mara looked at me for another moment.

"Okay what?"

"I won't."

"You can't promise that."

Right.

I breathed out.

"Then I'll tell you when I realize I'm doing it."

Better.

Not perfect.

She nodded once.

The crate between us was almost full.

Mara put the folded blanket on top.

"Vey runs a packet boat back to Carrow every seventh day."

I looked at her.

"Letters?"

"If somebody writes one."

There was no smile in it.

No romance either.

Just a route.

A real one.

"I'll answer."

Mara's expression hardened slightly.

I heard myself.

Promise again.

Too easy.

I corrected.

"No. I'll try again."

She waited.

"If you write, I want to answer. If I don't, you get to decide what that means without protecting me from it."

Mara looked down at the crate.

Then back at me.

"That's unpleasantly reasonable."

"I have experience."

"Apparently not with letters."

Fair.

I left that one alone too.

We carried the first crate downstairs together.

Her mother pointed at the wall beside the door.

"Not there. Boatman comes through the kitchen."

Of course he did.

We moved it.

Second crate beside the first.

Third after that.

By late afternoon Mara's room had started looking temporary.

Bare shelf.

Empty hooks.

Map rolled into a tube.

The blue cup gone from the windowsill.

A life becoming portable.

I stood in the back room before leaving.

The window was still open.

I closed it the old way.

Pull down first.

Then press the frame inward until the latch caught.

My hand knew.

That was enough for today.

Mara came to the doorway.

"Tomorrow?"

Not asking me to stay.

Not asking me to leave.

Tomorrow.

"Yes."

She looked at me.

I almost revised it into something safer.

Did not.

"Morning," I added.

Specific enough to fail honestly.

Mara nodded.

I put my shoes back on.

Her mother handed me the heel of the bread loaf wrapped in cloth.

"You still don't eat enough when you're busy."

Apparently everyone had records.

I took it.

At the door, the brass key was still in my pocket.

I pulled it out.

Mara's mother saw.

"Keep it."

I looked at Mara.

She did not object.

So I put it back.

Outside, the river market was closing.

Workers rolled awnings. Carts moved toward the gates. Somebody across the street was arguing about onions with more conviction than the subject deserved.

I had turned down two days of easy pay.

My purse remained unimpressed.

The key clicked once against the coins as I walked.
