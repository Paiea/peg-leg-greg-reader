# Chapter 21: The Reply

---

There was a letter under my door.

I saw the handwriting before I saw the Halden stamp.

That seemed backwards.

It was also true.

I stood in the hallway with one hand still on the stair rail and looked at Mara's name written nowhere on the outside.

She had not needed to write it.

My name was enough.

**Greg. Lower Rook Street. Carrow.**

The last line leaned upward because Mara always became impatient with addresses before they were finished.

Apparently that had survived Halden.

I had spent the afternoon counting grain sacks for a broker who believed arithmetic became more accurate if he watched me do it.

My hands were gray with warehouse dust.

The letter was clean.

I could have picked it up.

Instead I went downstairs, washed my hands at the courtyard pump, came back up, and discovered the letter remained exactly where it had been.

Avoidance had become less ambitious in my second life.

I picked it up.

The paper was heavier than the sheet I had sent.

Not expensive.

Different office stock.

Halden mark on the front.

Vey route mark beneath it.

The date sat four days after the Carrow stamp on my letter.

I counted without meaning to.

Mine had reached Halden.

Mara had lived with it long enough to answer.

Then this had crossed back on a later packet.

Time had happened somewhere I could not see it.

That was becoming one of its favorite tricks.

Inside my room, the support receipt was still tucked beneath the brass key so I would stop checking the date every morning.

It had not worked.

The first Deepwinter appointment was closer now.

Still weeks away.

Arlo and Ivena had not spent those days waiting either.

The tannery had paid through the end of the two-week trial and Ivena had extended the one-unit service arrangement without asking whether I had thoughts about it.

I did.

She had not asked.

Healthy system.

The ordered units were leaving the workshop one at a time as fittings arrived and housings survived firing.

Nobody had needed me yesterday.

Today a grain broker had needed me to count sacks.

And Mara had written back.

I sat on the bed.

The envelope opened badly because I tried to preserve the fold and tore one corner anyway.

Inside were two sheets.

Two seemed excessive.

Then I remembered who owned more books than was financially responsible.

I unfolded the first.

**Greg,**

**No. Halden does not smell better than the lower quay.**

I laughed before I could decide whether the sentence deserved it.

Mara continued.

**It smells different. Wet wool near the east warehouses. Coal smoke when the wind comes off the hill. The cooper under our office burns something every morning that I am almost certain is not wood. Pell eats onions at the ledger desk and claims that does not count as a smell because he likes them. He is wrong.**

Pell.

I did not know Pell.

That was the first thing in the letter that hurt.

Not badly.

Correctly.

Mara had a person in Halden whose onion habits had already become irritating enough to deserve postage.

Her life had acquired Pell while mine acquired Meren Voss and a commercial ward seam full of wool grease.

Reasonable exchange.

I kept reading.

**The job is mostly numbers so far. I knew that before I came. Knowing it and sitting through eight hours of somebody else's corrections are different things. Vey's Halden office is three rooms over a cooper's shop. The west room tilts enough that the ink runs toward the wall if the desk is not level. We share the rear store room with another carrier because apparently a company can own three barges and still argue over shelf space.**

Three barges.

I stopped.

Later Vey Shipping had warehouses in six river cities.

Faster hulls.

Dedicated packet halls.

A scheduling office in Carrow large enough that I once spent twenty minutes trying to find the correct complaint desk and discovered there were two.

Three barges now.

Shared storage.

A tilted room over a cooper.

The future history in my head used words like expansion, consolidation, river network, merchant house.

It had not mentioned the floor.

Of course it had not.

I almost started building a theory from the three barges.

Stopped.

Mara had not mailed me evidence for an investment thesis.

She had mailed me her office.

I returned to the line where I had left her.

**The blue cup survived. Mother was wrong. Halden has cups, but none of them are the blue one.**

I smiled.

**Your rope story was terrible. I read it twice. I am glad you listened when Orra told you to cut it. I am also aware you crossed out `she was right.` Crossing out words does not make them invisible. You used to know that.**

I looked at my copy of nothing.

Right.

Ink remained ink.

Useful technology.

I had sent evidence of self-editing instead of the edit.

Mara had apparently received both.

**Mother told me she gave you Tavin's cards. I do not remember why you burned the seven either. Tavin said you cheated. You said the card was defective. I believed Tavin.**

That sounded unfair.

Probably accurate.

I picked up the deck from the table.

Seven of cups.

Burned corner.

Defective card.

No memory arrived.

The object remained an object.

That was fine.

I put it back.

The next paragraph was shorter.

**You did write something true.**

I read it again.

There were several ways to ruin a sentence like that.

I found three before reaching the next line.

**You also wrote it like someone might grade you afterward.**

I stared at the paper.

There were apparently four ways.

**River mistake. Business update. Cards. Money. Proof that you are trying. Greg, I asked for true. I did not ask for a report proving you had behaved correctly.**

I leaned back against the wall.

The plaster was cold through my shirt.

My first impulse was defense.

That was embarrassing enough that I let it finish.

I had not written the letter to prove anything.

I had written because Mara asked.

I had told the truth.

I had included what happened.

What else was I supposed to include?

The answer was already on the second page.

I turned it over.

**Do not answer this with a worse letter where you explain your feelings correctly. I will throw it in the river.**

I laughed again.

Louder.

The room did not care.

**Next time tell me one thing you would leave out if you were trying to make the letter useful.**

Then, beneath it:

**What you ate. Something stupid Sella said. Whether the room is cold. A person I do not know yet. Something you wanted to tell me before you decided whether it mattered.**

I stopped there.

Not because the letter ended.

Because I had found the problem.

Useful.

Of course.

I had treated truth as another constraint.

No lying.

No false certainty.

No pretending memory existed.

Meet requirements.

Submit.

Mara had asked for contact and I had produced documentation.

The distinction was annoyingly clear once somebody else wrote it down.

There was one more paragraph.

I made myself read it before improving anything.

**I do not know how often I will write. The first week here was longer than it was supposed to be and next week looks worse. If you write again, I want to read it. Those are the two things I know.**

Two things.

I hated the phrase on sight.

Not because of her.

Because somewhere in my room, nineteen-year-old Greg had probably already found a way to make it personal.

Mara signed only her name.

No closing promise.

No `miss you`.

No `come west`.

No answer to a question I had not asked honestly enough to deserve one.

Just Mara.

I set both sheets on the bed.

Then immediately picked them up again because the blanket had a damp corner from laundry.

Important priorities returned quickly.

I put the letter on the table beside Tavin's cards.

The obvious next action was to write back.

I did not.

This was not restraint.

I had no idea what to say.

That was different.

I went downstairs and bought dinner.

Not because Mara had told me to report what I ate.

Because I was hungry.

Still useful to verify.

The cookstall near the south corner had stew with beef in it for two copper if I accepted that the word beef might be aspirational.

I paid.

The woman filled a bowl.

Too much pepper.

Not enough meat.

One potato had given up structurally and become part of the broth.

I ate all of it.

A boy at the next table dropped his spoon, looked at his mother, picked it up, wiped it on his shirt, and continued eating before she turned back around.

Competent field work.

I almost stored that for the letter.

Then realized what I was doing.

I ate.

Carrow moved around me.

A cart with two new ceramic filter housings went past toward East Mill.

Arlo's mark on the crates.

Not mine.

The driver was a woman I did not know.

Ivena had arranged the delivery.

I knew that because she had told me yesterday after the fact.

The tannery service trial had become another week of service.

One unit.

One matched spare.

Nobody had built the network in my head.

Nobody had asked me to.

Across the street, two men in city work coats argued about the North Gate restriction while carrying a ladder south instead of north.

I could not hear the details.

The gate remained standing.

Also moving through the city without me.

I finished the stew.

The pepper remained excessive.

That fact did not improve with analysis.

Back in my room, Mara's letter waited on the table.

I sat in front of it with a blank sheet.

This had happened before.

Different problem.

Last time I needed true content.

This time I had too much.

Meren's ward shift.

The apothecary coupling.

The iron under the drain.

Six copper.

The tannery extension.

Vey's three barges.

Kesra Dain's name in a ledger four weeks away.

The red Guild roof.

A future I still could not prove.

All useful.

I picked up the pen.

Put it down.

Mara had given examples.

What you ate.

Whether the room is cold.

A person I do not know yet.

Something you wanted to tell me before you decided whether it mattered.

The room was cold.

That seemed insulting as correspondence.

Then I noticed my left foot was tucked underneath my right leg because the floorboards near the bed pulled cold through the sole.

I had been doing that for days.

Maybe longer.

Young Greg might have done it too.

No memory.

No historical importance.

Just cold.

I wrote.

**Mara,**

**The room gets cold before dawn. I keep tucking one foot under the other when I sit on the bed. I do not know if I always did that.**

I stopped.

That was small enough to feel ridiculous.

Good sign, perhaps.

No.

Do not grade.

I continued.

**I bought stew tonight. Too much pepper. I ate all of it.**

Then I stared at the page.

My hand wanted to add why I had money for stew.

Ward work.

Six copper.

Support plane.

Progress.

Useful context.

I left it out.

The omission felt dishonest for approximately two breaths.

Then merely unfamiliar.

I added one more line.

**There is a woman who drives Arlo's deliveries now. I do not know her name. That bothered me for a second, then it did not.**

Too interpretive.

I crossed out the second sentence.

Ink remained ink.

Mara would see it.

I looked at the crossing.

Then wrote beneath it.

**Her name is Dena. I asked yesterday and forgot until now.**

That was better.

Not because it proved anything.

Because Dena existed.

I had met her once beside a cart and immediately converted her into delivery capacity.

Mara would probably enjoy that indictment more than I did.

I almost explained it.

Did not.

The letter was very short.

I hated that too.

My first one had taken half a day and a river emergency.

This one contained cold feet, bad stew, and Dena.

I reread it.

No declaration.

No report.

No request that Mara reassure me she had received the relationship correctly.

I signed.

**Greg**

Then I remembered the blue cup.

I turned the page over and added:

**Your mother was still wrong about the cup. I am willing to testify.**

There.

That one I wanted her to read.

The packet office was closed.

I knew because I checked.

Different kind of progress.

I folded the letter and put it beside the Halden envelope.

For once, waiting until morning did not feel like failure.

At dawn, the same clerk from my first letter was behind the counter.

Or maybe not the same clerk.

I had begun respecting the limits of confidence in faces before breakfast.

She took the letter.

"Halden?"

"Yes."

She weighed it.

One copper.

Same rate.

I paid.

The Vey sack beneath the Halden hook was newer than the last one.

Or cleaner.

Again, dangerous confidence.

The clerk stamped Carrow across the fold and dropped my letter in with several others.

Paper against paper.

No ceremony.

This time I did not ask when it would arrive.

The route knew more about that than I did.

Outside, the morning was cold enough that I put both hands in my coat pockets.

The support receipt touched my knuckles on one side.

Mara's Halden envelope was folded in the other.

I left them there and went to breakfast...
