# Chapter 27: The Correspondent

Status: **CHARACTER REBUILD EXPERIMENT / BRANCH ONLY / NOT PUBLIC R2 AUTHORITY**

---

The second letter had spent almost a month under lamp oil.

This was less dramatic than losing it in a fire and more insulting.

I found it the morning after my day off because the table had reached the point where objects were no longer arranged so much as negotiating territory.

Sword oil beside the support receipt.

Waxed thread under the brass key.

Two loose copper on top of Mara's Halden envelope because apparently money became safer when placed on correspondence.

A lamp-oil receipt I had kept for reasons nobody alive could defend.

I moved it.

Envelope.

I stopped.

Not Mara's handwriting.

Cheaper paper. Folded once through the middle before somebody decided it deserved an envelope. My name written across the front without a street.

**Greg**

That was all.

Whoever sent it had expected the packet office to know where I lived.

I disliked that immediately.

Not because it was unreasonable.

Because somebody else apparently knew how easy I was to find before I did.

A freight-yard mark had been stamped in one corner.

South Latch.

River company beneath it.

The name came after the place.

Tavin.

A hand slapping mine away from a bowl of dried beans.

Rain against a small back-room window.

A burned seven.

Mara saying Tavin claimed I cheated.

Then nothing useful.

No face.

No voice.

No reason the name should make my chest tighten anyway.

I sat down.

The chair complained under me.

Good. Current furniture remained reliable.

The letter had arrived eleven days before I woke in this body.

I checked the date twice because arithmetic was easier than opening it.

Eleven days.

Young Greg had received this.

Or should have.

Maybe he had already read it and put it under the receipt himself.

Maybe the delivery clerk slid it beneath the door after whatever happened to him happened.

Maybe I had been carrying a stranger's unopened mail around my room since Chapter One of existence.

I did not know.

That was becoming a crowded category.

I opened it.

The first line solved the voice problem.

**Greg, if you are still pretending Carrow is too small for you, Venn's crew needs another body.**

I laughed.

Then stopped because whoever Tavin was, he had already annoyed me accurately.

I read on.

**Not Guild. Freight company. South Latch to Rell Ford and back, maybe six weeks if the river stays stupid. Mostly wagons beside the water because the shallow barges cannot take the stone cargo this season. Two guards already. They want somebody who can carry and stand watch without inventing problems. I lied about you.**

There he was.

Not remembered.

Present enough to be rude from paper.

The pay was decent.

The food was not.

The foreman was terrible in a way Tavin apparently considered proof of reliability.

Then the letter got worse.

**You said Halden first if Mara goes. If that is still the plan, ignore this. If you are actually serious about getting out on the road instead of talking about it in her mother's back room, write me before the end of the month.**

I read that once.

Then again.

Then I put the letter down because suddenly the room had too many versions of me in it.

Halden first if Mara goes.

Of course.

Nineteen-year-old boy plans to follow the girl west.

Simple.

Embarrassing.

Common enough that entire songs had been written by men who should have known better.

Except that was not what the letter said.

Not only that.

Tavin had a six-week freight lead because I had apparently been talking about roads before Mara left.

Enough talking that another person had become tired of hearing it and tried to make me prove it.

I looked at the field pack beside the door.

Still where I had left it last night.

Cleaned.

Repaired.

Ready to become packed if I chose.

I had thought that was mine.

Not the pack.

The wanting.

Roads.

Work outside Carrow.

Somewhere larger than the room.

I had been careful with that desire because it was easy to explain as contamination.

Old Greg had been an adventurer.

Of course Old Greg wanted roads.

Old Greg remembered contracts and bad inns and support work and rain under armor and the particular stupidity of people who packed six days of food for an eight-day route.

Current Greg could want those things because current Greg remembered them.

Clean explanation.

Young Greg had apparently ruined it before dying.

I was irritated with him.

This was difficult because he was me.

Probably.

Mostly.

Enough for legal purposes.

The dead boy had stolen my excuse.

I picked the letter up again.

Tavin continued as if he had not just complicated my entire theory of myself.

**Do not bring the red blanket unless you finally washed it.**

I looked at the wardrobe.

No red blanket.

One historical problem solved by disappearance.

**Also you still owe me two copper from cards. Mara says you claim the burned seven invalidated the hand. It did not. You burned it after I won. That is not how rules work.**

I stood.

The cards were under Mara's letter.

I found the deck.

Seven of cups on top because apparently I had been arranging evidence subconsciously.

Burned corner.

I held it beside Tavin's accusation.

No memory.

Still defective.

I counted two copper from the table and put them on the card.

This improved nothing and pleased me anyway.

Tavin's last paragraph gave the South Latch office and warned me not to use Kell's packet line because they lost half the mail and charged the other half for the privilege.

Then his name.

No `miss you`.

No careful explanation of our relationship.

No request that I become the person who would understand the joke.

Just Tavin.

I sat there with the letter in one hand and the burned seven under two copper.

The month-end deadline had passed.

Not yesterday.

Enough that Venn's crew was gone or full or halfway to Rell Ford.

My first impulse was to see whether I could catch them.

That embarrassed me.

My second impulse was to calculate whether the pay would justify trying.

That embarrassed me differently.

The job was dead.

The wanting was not.

I almost wrote that sentence down.

Then I heard Mara's voice from four days of packet travel away.

Do not answer this with a worse letter where you explain your feelings correctly.

Fine.

I found a clean sheet.

Paper had become expensive since women started grading my emotional competence.

**Tavin,**

I stopped.

What did he deserve?

Mara knew about the memory problem because she had stood in this room and watched me fail to remember kissing her.

Tavin had sent a freight lead and demanded two copper.

Not the same relationship.

I wrote:

**I found your letter late. The job is probably gone.**

True.

Useful.

Dangerous start.

My hand kept moving.

**I do not remember you properly.**

I stared at the sentence.

Too much.

Not because it was untrue.

Because I could already imagine being on the other end of it.

Hello. Your friend has misplaced you. Thank you for the employment opportunity.

I crossed it out.

Ink remained ink.

Mara had established that technology.

I turned the page over.

Paper had two sides. Emotional honesty could pay rent like everyone else.

**Tavin,**

**I found your letter late. The freight job is probably gone. I am still in Carrow. I have been out on the road anyway.**

Better.

I wanted to add five days west.

Glasshorns.

Road office.

Field pay.

Good sword.

Proof.

There it was again.

Look, Tavin. I had become the person you accused me of only pretending to want to be.

I put the pen down.

That was the part I hated.

I wanted him to know.

Not because the information mattered.

Because I wanted this mostly faceless man to read the page and think, yes, Greg finally did something.

That desire had no professional boundary.

No correct angle.

No five-inch version.

I got up and opened the window.

Cold air came in immediately and made the room worse.

Useful.

Lower Rook was awake below me.

A cart wheel hit the same loose stone twice because apparently the driver had reversed and tried the mistake again.

Someone shouted about onions.

A woman on the second floor shook a rug over the courtyard with personal conviction.

From next door came one hard thump.

Then another.

Noll's voice through the wall.

"You alive?"

I looked toward it.

"Why?"

"You haven't dropped anything in an hour."

"I am becoming graceful."

"No."

Fair.

A pause.

"You got soap?"

I closed my eyes.

"Still."

His door opened.

Mine opened three seconds later because apparently this was our postal system now.

Noll stood in the hallway holding a towel over one shoulder.

He had both boots today.

Progress.

I handed him the soap.

He looked past me at the table.

The two copper sat on the burned card.

"Gambling before breakfast?"

"Debt."

"Yours?"

"Apparently."

Noll leaned around me enough to see the letter.

Not read it.

Just see the paper.

"Mara?"

"No."

He waited.

I waited harder.

He shrugged.

"Dangerous."

"What is?"

"Other people."

Then he went downstairs with my soap.

I stood in the doorway.

That had been either wisdom or theft.

Probably theft.

Back at the table, I read the half-letter again.

I did not finish it.

I did not tear it up either.

That felt important enough that I refused to explain why.

I folded Tavin's old letter and put it beside Mara's.

The unfinished reply went beneath the burned seven.

The two copper stayed on top.

A small paperweight for a debt I could not remember earning.

Outside, Noll shouted from the courtyard that somebody had used all the hot water.

Someone shouted back that water did not become his because he wanted it first.

The argument improved from there.

I closed the window.

The road could wait until after breakfast...
