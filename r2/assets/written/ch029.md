# Chapter 29: The Other Letter

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/029-story-search.md`

---

The second letter had been under a receipt for lamp oil.

Of course it had.

I found it because I was trying to work out whether five copper earned yesterday counted as recovery from one silver spent the day before.

It did not.

This was not complicated mathematics.

I had simply hoped arithmetic might become more supportive if I gave it context.

Three small piles sat on the table.

Money I could spend.

Money I should not spend.

Receipts proving I had already spent both categories incorrectly.

The support-assessment receipt remained beneath the brass key.

That money was gone in the healthiest possible way.

The boot repair had been necessary.

The socks had been necessary.

The sword lesson had been necessary according to Lera and increasingly according to my ribs.

Lunch continued arguing for itself daily.

I moved an old folded receipt aside.

There.

Letter.

Not Mara's.

I stopped.

Right.

Two letters.

Chapter one of my new life, apparently.

Three copper. Bronze token. Bent lockpick. Receipts. Two letters.

I had promoted Mara's handwriting into significance so quickly that the other envelope had become furniture.

Unfair to the envelope.

Potentially unfair to the person.

The paper was cheaper than Mara's old letter.

Creased through the middle as if somebody had folded it into a pocket first and an envelope second.

My name on the front.

Greg.

No street.

No Carrow.

Whoever sent it had assumed the delivery clerk knew where to put me.

That narrowed nothing.

The return mark did.

South Latch Freight Yard.

River company stamp beneath it.

The name arrived before I opened the fold.

Tavin.

Not a face.

A hand slapping mine away from a bowl of dried beans.

Rain against the back-room window.

The burned seven of cups.

Mara telling me Tavin said I cheated.

A person made from three remembered motions and one accusation.

Better than nothing.

I opened the letter.

The first line was:

**Greg, if you are still pretending Carrow is too small for you, Venn's crew needs another body.**

I sat down.

That sounded promisingly rude.

I continued.

**Not Guild. Freight company. South Latch to Rell Ford and back, maybe six weeks if the river stays stupid. Mostly wagons beside the water because the shallow barges cannot take the stone cargo this season. Two guards already. They want somebody who can carry and stand watch without inventing problems. I lied about you.**

I laughed.

There he was.

Not remembered.

Present on paper.

**Pay is decent. Food is not. You would hate the foreman. I hate the foreman. This makes him reliable.**

Also promising.

The next line changed the room.

**You said Halden first if Mara goes. If that is still the plan, ignore this. If you are actually serious about getting out on the road instead of talking about it in her mother's back room, write me before the end of the month.**

I read it twice.

Then once more because memory had developed a habit of hiding behind ordinary sentences.

You said Halden first if Mara goes.

If that is still the plan.

Actually serious about getting out on the road.

Young Greg.

Nineteen.

Before me.

Before the second life.

Before the good sword.

Before Harl and Edda and five wet days on the West Cut.

Before I stood in the Guild yard watching Gold adventurers make hard work look quiet.

He had wanted out too.

Not out of life.

Out of Carrow.

Roads.

Contracts.

Somewhere larger than the room.

I leaned back.

This should not have surprised me.

Chapter 14 had already given me that much. Mara said I had been planning to take contracts west. Halden first. Maybe farther if it was good.

But Mara's version could still have been filed under Mara.

Young man follows girl west.

Common historical event.

Tavin's letter was different.

Tavin had written from south of the city about a six-week freight job because Greg had apparently been talking about roads often enough for other people to become tired of hearing it.

That felt more like me than I was comfortable admitting.

I returned to the page.

**Do not bring the red blanket unless you finally washed it.**

I looked toward the wardrobe.

No red blanket.

Good.

Some histories deserved extinction.

**Also you still owe me two copper from cards. Mara says you claim the burned seven invalidated the hand. It did not. You burned it after I won. That is not how rules work.**

I looked at the deck on the table.

Burned seven on top.

Apparently nineteen-year-old me had already been developing legal theories for losing money.

Continuity strengthened.

The letter ended:

**South Latch. Venn Freight office if I am not in the yard. Do not send anything through Kell's packet line because they lose half the mail and charge the other half for the privilege.**

**Tavin**

No dramatic closing.

No I miss you.

No explanation of who Tavin was supposed to be to me.

Just enough ordinary irritation to prove a relationship had existed without waiting for my permission.

I checked the date.

The letter had arrived eleven days before I woke.

The month-end deadline had passed.

Not by much.

Enough.

The freight crew would already be gone or full or both.

I felt an immediate urge to calculate whether I could catch them.

Then stopped.

Interesting.

Old Greg had forty years of evidence that opportunities multiplied whenever someone wrote the phrase decent pay.

Young Greg had apparently already been preparing to leave.

Neither fact required me to chase a dead six-week job south because a letter survived under lamp-oil accounting.

The useful part was smaller.

The boy had wanted this.

Not exactly this life.

He could not have imagined this life.

But roads.

Work.

Leaving Carrow on purpose.

Coming back maybe.

Finding out what happened outside the streets that knew his boots.

That desire belonged to him before it belonged to me.

I disliked how much that mattered.

I had spent weeks worrying that I was replacing nineteen-year-old Greg with a more competent ghost.

An older man wearing his body and explaining every preference afterward as experience.

Support because Old Greg had become support.

Good sword because Old Greg remembered good swords.

Field work because Old Greg knew how useful field work could become.

But the room kept producing evidence that the boy had been moving too.

Mara.

Halden.

Tavin.

Road work he had not yet taken.

A cheap sword he loved enough to split kindling with.

A Bronze token.

Training leathers.

A bent lockpick I still had not earned an explanation for.

Not blank.

Never blank.

I read Tavin's letter again.

Then I counted two copper from the spendable pile.

Placed them on the burned seven.

Debt settlement, ceremonial edition.

I had no idea whether Tavin still considered it owed.

That was not the point.

The point was mostly that the joke pleased me.

Good enough.

The South Latch return address might still work.

The letter said Venn Freight office if he was not in the yard.

That was current enough to test.

I found paper.

Then stopped.

Mara had trained me badly.

Now every blank sheet felt like an examination in emotional honesty.

Tavin's letter did not require that much dignity.

I wrote:

**Tavin,**

**I found your letter late. The freight job is probably gone. I am still in Carrow. I have been taking work outside it. You were apparently right that I was serious about roads, which is irritating because I do not remember saying it.**

I paused.

Too much truth perhaps.

No.

Tavin could live with confusion.

I continued.

**I have the old deck. The seven is still burned. I maintain that this damages the evidence.**

Then:

**I also have your two copper. This should alarm you more than the memory problem.**

There.

That sounded like somebody Tavin might know.

I considered explaining the reset.

Absolutely not.

Mara had earned the complicated version through proximity and damage.

Tavin had sent me a freight lead and a card accusation.

Start there.

**If Venn's office still gets your mail, write back when you have time. If not, somebody at a freight desk will enjoy this very strange letter.**

**Greg**

I folded the two copper inside.

Then removed them.

Coins in ordinary packet letters were an excellent way to convert friendship into postal theft.

Old Greg knew that.

Current Greg almost needed to relearn it.

I wrote beneath the signature:

**I am not mailing the money because I have become wiser. Collect it later.**

Better.

At the packet office, the clerk read the destination.

"South Latch?"

"Yes. Venn Freight office."

She checked a route sheet hanging behind the counter.

"Company packet goes south tomorrow. Three copper."

Three.

Mara to Halden had been one.

Distance and route ownership remained committed to economics.

"How long?"

"If Venn takes it at South Latch, four days. If they forward it farther, depends where he is."

Reasonable.

I paid.

Recent professional magic had earned five copper.

I had now spent three of them sending a letter to a man I barely remembered because nineteen-year-old me apparently owed him two copper and an explanation.

Economic progress was difficult to graph.

The clerk stamped the fold.

South.

Not west.

For the first time since waking, one of young Greg's relationships left Carrow in a direction Mara had not chosen.

I liked that.

Not because it made Mara less important.

Because it made the boy larger.

Outside, a freight wagon turned toward the southern road.

I watched it long enough to become aware I was watching.

Then kept walking.

The old job was gone.

The road was not...
