# Chapter 31: The Dry Wall

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/031-story-search.md`

---

The hole under East Mill looked smaller when someone else owned it.

The warehouse mason stood beside the broken cellar wall with his arms crossed and the expression of a man hoping professional adventurers would make masonry stop being his problem.

We disappointed him immediately.

Jorren crouched at the opening.

"That's wet."

The mason nodded.

"Water damage."

"I know what wet is."

Good reunion.

I held the lamp closer.

The opening was shoulder-high and perhaps three feet across where bricks had fallen inward.

Behind it, old stone continued into darkness.

Not cellar stone.

Bigger blocks.

Darker mortar.

A passage maybe six feet wide and a little taller than me, which meant it had been built by people with unfair architectural priorities.

Cold air moved out through the gap.

Wet stone.

No rot.

No sulfur.

No animal musk.

No obvious blood.

Excellent start.

The mason handed us a sack of chalk sticks.

"City wants every junction marked."

"We read the contract," Jorren said.

I looked at him.

"You read it?"

"Some of us developed skills on the road."

I put the chalk in my pocket.

The mason's apprentice brought two hooded lamps.

Young. Maybe sixteen.

He looked at the hole like it had personally invited him.

"Can I come?"

Three people answered no.

Useful consensus.

Jorren went through first.

Not because we had discussed it.

Because he had the shorter sword, broader shoulders, and a newer habit of testing floor before trusting it.

I followed with the lamps.

That was also not discussed.

Two steps in, the warehouse disappeared.

Sound changed first.

Above us, carts and feet became dull impacts through stone.

Water ticked somewhere ahead.

The air smelled older than the building.

Not mystical.

Mineral.

Damp lime.

Iron without rust.

The passage sloped down slightly.

Jorren stopped at the first branch.

Left narrow.

Right wider.

We chalked a line on the wall behind us.

One slash toward the warehouse.

Then arrows at both branches.

Simple.

Repeatable.

The city would probably invent a better symbol system forty years from now and claim nobody had ever lived before it.

We took the wider right branch first.

Ten yards.

Twenty.

Old drainage cuts ran along both walls, shallow enough that somebody had cared about seepage without intending the passage to carry water itself.

One section had collapsed inward years ago and been repaired with smaller stone.

Not current repair.

Older than the warehouse.

Maybe older than East Mill as a district.

I searched memory.

Nothing.

No famous Carrow tunnel.

No collapse story.

No future plaque.

Good.

Present city, then.

At thirty yards, Jorren raised one hand.

I stopped.

He pointed near the floor.

Three pale shapes clustered around a wet seam.

Each about the size of my palm.

Low shell.

Too many legs.

White mineral crust across the back.

One moved.

Slow.

I knew several possible creatures this could be and trusted none of them.

Jorren crouched.

"Limebacks."

I looked at him.

"You know them?"

"North culvert job."

Apparently he had been living while away.

"Dangerous?"

"If you put your hand under one."

"Do you plan to?"

"No."

Excellent field doctrine.

The limebacks fed along the mortar seam with tiny scraping mouthparts.

Not the stone itself.

Something growing on it.

Thin blue-gray film I had taken for mineral stain.

One shell pulsed faintly when it crossed a brighter patch.

Mana-sensitive, perhaps.

Or merely rude.

"What do they tell us?" I asked.

Jorren pointed at the wet seam.

"Moving water behind that wall."

"Because?"

"They follow fresh mineral bleed. Pel said if you see more than two in one place, there's a live seep behind it."

Pel.

Forty-year-old ancient master.

Useful man.

I looked at the wall.

No visible flow.

But the mortar at knee height was darker than the rest.

Present evidence agreed with Jorren.

We marked it.

Not dangerous.

Relevant.

The passage bent.

Then the wall changed.

Only for twelve feet.

Same dark blocks.

Same mortar lines.

Except dry.

Completely dry.

I stopped.

Jorren took two more steps before noticing.

Behind us, damp stone reflected the lamplight.

Ahead, damp stone did the same.

The twelve-foot section between them looked as though somebody had cut a rectangle out of another climate and installed it here.

No copper channels.

No ceramic bridge pieces.

No ward plates.

No visible runes.

Just stone.

Jorren touched one block with the back of his fingers.

"Warm."

I touched beside him.

Barely.

Not heat.

Absence of cellar cold.

Interesting.

Old Greg knew civic dampers.

Foundation wards.

Flood seals.

Dry-storage arrays.

Buried heat loops.

This resembled none of them.

Later Carrow could keep a vault wall dry with enough copper, ceramic, annual inspection, and money.

This wall appeared to be doing it while pretending magic had not been invented.

I held the lamp closer.

The mortar had tiny dark flecks in it.

Stone? Glass?

Could not tell.

"You know it?" Jorren asked.

I hated that question today.

"No."

He looked surprised.

Good.

Maybe I had been talking too much.

"Should we touch it?"

"We already did."

"More professionally."

"No."

Better.

The contract asked whether there was active ward infrastructure.

Something here was active enough to keep twelve feet of underground wall dry.

We did not need to discover why with our faces.

I drew a rectangle in chalk around one block and wrote **DRY / WARM / UNKNOWN** beside it.

Jorren added an arrow toward the warehouse.

Team scholarship.

We continued.

At fifty yards, the floor dropped one step.

At sixty, another junction.

Left branch ended after twelve feet at a collapsed stone face.

Right continued.

Chalk.

Arrow.

Distance counted by pace.

No treasure.

No skeleton.

No ancient king's bedroom.

I felt cheated by several decades of tavern stories.

Then the ceiling dropped a brick on Jorren.

Not intentionally.

Probably.

The first warning was grit.

Tiny stones against his shoulder.

He looked up.

Bad instinct.

I saw the cracked support block above him shift.

Not the whole ceiling.

One stone.

Heavy enough.

"Move."

Jorren moved.

The block came down.

My hand was already up.

There.

Plane.

Five inches.

Too small to stop the block.

Enough to catch one lower corner and change the fall.

Impact punched through my forearm.

The plane vanished.

The block rotated half a turn and hit the floor beside Jorren instead of the back of his ankle.

Stone cracked.

Dust everywhere.

We both froze.

Nothing else moved.

Jorren looked at the block.

Then at my hand.

"Fast."

There.

Clean.

"Small."

"Still fast."

I flexed my fingers.

No numbness.

Meren's counting-house doorway had apparently taught something useful outside a counting house.

Good system.

We stepped back twenty feet.

Not because panic.

Because loose masonry had just submitted evidence.

I held the lamp low and looked at the ceiling line.

Crack along one joint.

Water stain above.

Several stones shifted slightly out of plane.

There ended our right to continue.

Contract said obvious structural failure.

Found.

We chalked a wide X before the damaged section.

**UNSTABLE. DO NOT PASS.**

Jorren added a second X because apparently one could be argued with.

We turned back.

The limebacks had moved six inches.

Busy lives.

At the dry wall, I stopped again.

Still dry.

Still warm.

Still unknown.

I wanted to know.

That was not the same as being allowed to find out.

I had recently paid one silver to learn a similar lesson with a sword.

Expensive principle.

Might as well reuse it.

Back at the first junction, we took the narrower left branch.

It descended.

Water sound grew louder.

Twenty yards in, the passage ended at iron bars.

Beyond them ran black water through a stone channel wide enough for a man to lie across.

Not sewer.

Too clean.

Storm drain or overflow route.

Current.

Active.

The flow moved steadily west.

Small white shells clung to the lower wall.

More limebacks.

Jorren pointed.

"Live."

"Yes."

No argument.

We did not open the gate.

A stamped metal plate on one bar carried a city mark so old I did not recognize the office.

Still city.

Good enough.

We chalked **PUBLIC WATER LINE / ACTIVE** at the junction and turned around.

The whole job took less than two hours.

That felt suspicious until I remembered professionalism sometimes shortened stories.

The mason was waiting when we came back through the hole.

So was Sella.

I stared at her.

"Do you live here now?"

"City sent me for the return sheet."

"You personally?"

"No, Greg. I came because I missed you."

Jorren looked at me.

I shook my head.

Dangerous woman.

We gave the report.

Active public water line.

Unknown dry wall that needed a ward specialist or city engineer before anyone disturbed it.

Live seep and limebacks on the east wall.

Unstable ceiling beyond the right branch.

No evidence of occupation.

No evidence of a large den.

No salvage observed.

The mason latched onto that last part.

"Nothing valuable?"

Jorren said, "Not yours if there was."

I was proud of him.

Sella wrote without looking up.

The city could not let the owner seal the passage now.

Public water line changed the property problem.

Unknown active wall changed the safety problem.

Unstable masonry changed the labor problem.

One little hole had successfully become three departments.

Carrow remained itself.

The mason asked what happened next.

Sella finally looked up.

"City inspector. Ward specialist. Then structural crew."

"How long?"

"Different desk."

The phrase contained entire governments.

Our half-day contract still paid the full rate.

Extra distance did not apply because we had stopped before a hundred yards.

Fair.

I signed.

Jorren signed.

Outside, he stretched his shoulders.

"No monsters."

"Limebacks."

"Those barely count."

"Tell them that."

He considered.

"No."

Growth.

We started toward the Guild.

My forearm still remembered the falling block.

Not pain.

Impact.

Fast plane.

Five inches.

Useful.

Behind us, under somebody else's warehouse, twelve feet of stone remained dry for reasons neither of us understood.

Old Greg did not know.

Current Greg did not need to pretend otherwise.

For now, the correct magical technique was chalk...
