# Chapter 33: The Passenger

Status: **CHARACTER REBUILD EXPERIMENT / TEMPORAL A / BRANCH ONLY / NOT PUBLIC R2 AUTHORITY**

---

Dena answered my question with a cart.

This was efficient and physically inconvenient.

I found her at Mill Street before second bell with one clean chamber strapped upright behind the driver's seat and two empty cradles waiting beside it.

Arlo had both workshop doors open.

Ivena was inside arguing with a man about whether `whenever` counted as a service date.

It apparently did not.

Dena was tightening the rear strap with one boot on the wheel hub.

I stopped beside the cart.

"Why fixed days?"

She pulled the strap once.

Hard.

The ceramic chamber did not move.

"Morning."

"Morning. Why fixed days?"

"Get in."

I looked at the seat.

Then at her.

"Is this an answer?"

"No. Route is."

There were more comfortable educational institutions.

Most charged silver.

I climbed up.

Dena took the reins.

The mule looked over one shoulder as if evaluating whether the added weight justified labor action.

"He'll live," Dena said.

"I was not worried about him."

"He was."

She clicked her tongue.

We moved.

Mill Street rolled behind us at approximately the speed of a determined person carrying soup.

The cart had no springs worth respecting.

Every stone in Carrow introduced itself through the bench.

I held the side rail.

Dena noticed.

"Road man."

"Different road."

"Stones know?"

"Apparently not."

She smiled without looking at me.

That was more expression than I had received from her in several weeks.

I let it be that.

The clean chamber behind us carried a black client tag.

Soapworks.

One of Ivena's two customers from the first expansion.

"They called?"

"Yesterday."

"Problem?"

"Dirty chamber."

"That's the service."

"Yes."

I waited.

Dena did not continue.

The cart turned east.

We passed a bakery putting morning loaves into the window, a cooper rolling two barrels across the street without asking traffic permission, and three children using a gutter plank as a bridge over water that was less than an inch deep.

Nobody needed us.

I tried again.

"Why did they call if they have the spare?"

"Because they wanted me yesterday."

"And?"

"I said today."

"Fixed day."

"You learn fast."

"I have gifts."

Dena snorted.

The soapworks sat near the lower bridge in a building that smelled clean from half a street away and terrible once we reached the door.

Lye.

Animal fat.

Herbs trying unsuccessfully to civilize both.

A young man in a stained leather apron came out before the cart stopped.

"Finally."

Dena pulled the brake.

"Morning to you too, Sen."

"We sent yesterday."

"And I told Falla today."

"Flow's down."

"Spare?"

The young man looked toward the building.

That answered more than his mouth did.

Dena climbed down.

I followed.

"You didn't swap it," she said.

"We're busy."

"So was I."

"We pay service."

Dena looked at him.

No anger.

Worse.

Accounting.

"You pay two scheduled collections each week. You want yesterday on top, that's emergency cart."

"For a dirty chamber?"

"For my day."

Sen made a face.

"Falla never said that."

"Falla signed it."

Dena reached under the seat and pulled out a folded service slip.

Ivena's handwriting across the bottom.

Another mark beside it.

Probably Falla.

Dena held it out.

Sen did not take it.

"She's inside."

"Then go ask her."

He went.

I looked at Dena.

"Emergency cart?"

She gave me the same look.

"You signed too."

Right.

Ownership had consequences beyond percentages.

"I read the service terms."

"Did you read my route line?"

I tried to remember.

Pickup schedule.

Matched spare.

Cleaning turnaround.

Replacement liability.

Emergency transport surcharge.

Apparently yes.

Apparently not with my full soul.

"Yes."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I thought that was for actual emergencies."

Dena pointed at the soapworks.

"They always think theirs is actual."

Fair.

Falla came out wiping both hands on an apron.

Older than Sen.

Hair tied back under a scarf.

She saw Dena and sighed.

"He called you yesterday?"

"Twice."

Falla turned her head toward the doorway.

"Sen."

He disappeared farther inside.

Strong management technique.

"Sorry," Falla said.

"Spare still clean?"

"Yes."

"Use it."

"We did after he sent the second boy."

Dena nodded.

No argument left.

We unloaded the clean chamber anyway because their second spare was due to rotate in, then took the dirty one back.

I helped lift because I was present and the ceramic was heavy.

Dena directed the angle.

"Bottom first."

I moved bottom first.

The dirty chamber had a pale crust near the lower collar.

Normal from the soapworks residue.

Probably.

I left it alone.

We strapped it into the rear cradle.

Falla paid Dena for something separate before we left.

Three copper.

I watched the coins change hands.

Dena put them into a small leather pouch tied under her coat.

"What's that?"

"Money."

"For?"

"Barrels."

There were no barrels on the cart.

Dena climbed back onto the seat.

"Later."

I climbed beside her.

The mule resumed its campaign against smooth travel.

"You haul for them too."

"Sometimes."

"Soap?"

"Empty fat barrels back to Tanner Row."

"Today?"

"After filters."

Ah.

The route had other nouns.

We crossed the lower bridge.

River light flashed between the rail posts.

The city smelled colder over water.

Dena turned north instead of back toward Mill Street.

"Where now?"

"East Mill."

"Filter?"

"Wool."

"Dirty chamber?"

"Wool."

I looked at the empty side of the cart.

"Actual wool."

"Eventually."

Jorren's word had apparently become contagious.

At East Mill, we stopped behind a low warehouse with blue chalk marks around the door.

No filter customer.

A woman with both forearms wrapped in cloth came out carrying a bundle of washed wool tied with cord.

Then another.

Then four more men appeared and filled most of the cart with them.

Dena checked each bundle tag before it went in.

Not because she was curious.

Because somebody had written two destinations on nearly identical cloth.

"These four south quay," she said.

The woman nodded.

"Other two Mill Street dyer."

Dena pointed.

"Not my dyer today."

"I thought you were going that way."

"Going a street is not buying the cart."

The woman rolled her eyes.

"One copper."

"Two."

"It's six blocks."

"Then carry it."

The woman looked at the bundles.

Then at Dena.

"One and half."

"Copper does halves now?"

"You know what I mean."

"Two."

She paid two.

Dena tucked the coins away.

I said nothing.

The cart now carried a dirty filtration chamber, six wool bundles, and one clean filter replacement.

"Yesterday?" I asked once we were moving again.

Dena knew which question.

"Yesterday I had three dye bundles to North Market, two empty oil drums, and a stove door from Arlo to a smith. Soap boy sent twice because he didn't want to swap the spare."

"You could've charged emergency."

"He didn't agree to emergency."

"Then say no."

Dena glanced at me.

"I did."

Right.

She continued.

Before fixed days, customers sent runners whenever flow dropped.

Not because the service failed.

Because waiting felt like failure when someone else owned the cart.

Dena had taken three extra trips the first week.

Ivena paid her.

That was not the problem.

The problem was Dena also hauled laundry, wool, barrels, fired pottery, shop stock, and occasionally people who had underestimated distance.

One filter call at midday broke two other promises even when the extra copper covered the trip.

"You lost work."

"Twice."

"How much?"

She told me.

More than the extra filter pay.

Not enormous.

Enough to be insulting.

"So fixed days."

"So fixed days."

"And emergency fee."

"High enough I don't mind losing something else."

That was less elegant than the schedule in Ivena's ledger.

More convincing.

The cart hit a hole.

My teeth met.

Dena did not apologize.

We delivered two wool bundles to a dyer I did not know.

The remaining four stayed aboard.

Then we stopped at the wool washer who actually used our filter.

Their dirty chamber was tagged, wrapped, and waiting by the door.

Nobody had sent a runner.

Dena swapped the clean one in under five minutes.

A worker helped.

They were back on the wash floor before we finished the straps.

Dena looked at me.

"Boring?"

"Beautiful."

"You're strange."

Established fact.

We headed south.

The packet office appeared one street ahead before I remembered the letter in my coat.

I had put it there that morning because the burned seven looked stupid without the two copper on top.

That was not a good reason.

It had still worked.

My fingers touched the folded paper through the cloth.

Dena noticed.

"What?"

"Packet office."

"I know."

"Can we stop?"

"Why?"

"Letter."

"How long?"

"A minute."

"That's a lie."

"Two."

She pulled the cart to the curb.

"Two."

I climbed down.

Inside, three people were ahead of me.

Of course.

One had a parcel large enough to contain regret.

The clerk argued about weight.

I looked through the front window.

Dena sat on the cart with both arms folded.

Time had become visible.

I took Tavin's letter out.

It was no longer the half-letter from two days ago.

I had added the line after Jorren left.

**You were right that I wanted them. I am less sure now what I mean by that.**

Still dangerously polished.

Still true enough to survive embarrassment.

Below it:

**I still dispute the two copper.**

Better.

I considered adding that the copper had been spent on pork.

Tavin did not need Noll's accounting.

Probably.

The parcel argument ended.

My turn.

"South Latch," I said.

The clerk looked at the address.

"Company yard?"

"Venn freight. Tavin."

"Family name?"

I did not know.

Excellent.

"No."

She looked at me.

"Big yard."

"Helpful."

"Could mark hold for Venn crew."

"Do that."

She weighed the letter.

One copper.

I paid.

Then stood there one second too long after she stamped it.

The clerk looked toward the next customer.

Right.

No ceremony.

She dropped the letter into the South Latch sack.

I went outside.

Dena looked at the office clock through the window.

"Four minutes."

"Three and some."

"Four."

"You rounding emotionally?"

"Get in."

I got in.

The cart moved before I had fully settled.

The packet office disappeared behind wool.

I felt nothing dramatic.

That annoyed me slightly.

Tavin's letter was gone.

Not gone.

Moving.

Somewhere between Carrow and a man I still could not picture properly.

Dena clicked her tongue at the mule.

"Bad news?"

"No."

"Money?"

"Two copper."

She looked at me.

"Letter cost one."

"Separate problem."

"You have many."

Correct.

She did not ask again.

At South Quay we unloaded the four wool bundles.

Dena collected five copper.

Then we returned to the soapworks for three empty fat barrels, because apparently the route had been folding back on itself with more intelligence than I had noticed.

Sen helped load them.

He did not mention yesterday.

Dena did not either.

One barrel smelled bad enough that I moved to the far side of the seat.

Dena noticed.

"Road man."

"Roads smell better."

"Some."

"Most."

"You ever hauled hides?"

"No."

"Then shut up."

Fair.

We reached Mill Street after midday.

Dirty chambers went inside.

One clean chamber remained for the afternoon lower-bridge stop.

The empty barrels stayed on the cart for Tanner Row.

Dena climbed down and stretched her back.

I did the same because the bench had reorganized my spine.

Ivena came out with the ledger.

"Soapworks?"

"Used spare after being told twice," Dena said.

"Emergency?"

"No."

"Good."

Ivena wrote something.

Dena pointed at me.

"He cost four minutes."

Ivena looked up.

"How much?"

"One copper."

"For what?"

"Emotional freight."

I stared at her.

Dena's face stayed perfectly flat.

Ivena did not.

She laughed.

"Charge him."

"Absolutely not."

"Owner surcharge."

"I own part of this."

"Then you can afford it."

The business had found a new method for stealing my money.

I paid Dena one copper.

Not because the contract required it.

Because she had waited four minutes with a loaded cart while I sent a letter that had nothing to do with filters.

Dena bit the coin once.

"Good."

"You're not supposed to actually test copper with your teeth."

"You want it back?"

"No."

"Then works."

She pocketed it.

I looked at the cart.

Filter chamber.

Fat barrels.

Wool fibers caught in the rear slats.

A smear of blue dye on one rail.

Yesterday the fixed service days had been three lines in Ivena's ledger.

Today they had been Sen wanting yesterday, Falla using the spare, two wool deliveries, a packet stop that cost me a copper, four more bundles to South Quay, and barrels that smelled like death.

I did not summarize any of that aloud.

Dena was already climbing back onto the seat.

"Where now?"

"Tanner Row."

"Filters?"

"Barrels."

I looked at the bench.

My body declined before my mouth had to.

"I'm walking."

Dena nodded.

"Smartest thing you did all day."

She drove off.

The cart turned south with someone else's goods, someone else's schedule, and one copper of mine in her pocket.

I walked home.

The room next door stayed quiet until well after dark.

Noll was working his first trial shift.

Tavin's letter was in a sack heading south.

Jorren was somewhere east with millstones.

I made dinner badly and ate it anyway...
