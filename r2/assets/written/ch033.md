# Chapter 33: The Share

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/033-story-search.md`

---

Arlo handed me nine copper and told me not to spend it.

This was not how payment was supposed to work.

I looked at the coins.

Then at him.

"Whose are these?"

"Yours."

"Then the second instruction seems weak."

Ivena closed the workshop door behind me.

"Sit down before you turn ownership into gambling again."

Unfair.

Historically supported.

I sat.

The workshop had changed again.

Not enough to become a different room.

Enough to prove nobody had asked permission.

One new shelf along the kiln wall.

Two finished housings marked with customer initials.

A crate of matched ceramic chambers beneath the bench.

Three service tags hanging from nails.

Dena's handwriting on one.

Arlo's on another.

The third belonged to somebody whose letters leaned so hard right they looked eager to leave the card.

No Greg handwriting.

Healthy system.

Ivena put a ledger on the table.

Not Arlo's workshop book.

Separate.

Filtration.

Three names on the inside cover.

Good.

"Nine copper," I said.

"Yes," Arlo said.

"From what?"

Ivena opened the ledger.

"That is why you're here."

I liked her less every time she made accounting sound like medicine.

The numbers were not complicated.

That made them worse because there was nowhere to hide.

Two unit payments had cleared since I last checked.

Tannery service paid again.

Soapworks paid for one chamber replacement because somebody there had dropped the original while cleaning it.

Not warranty.

Important distinction.

One wool-washer payment remained outstanding but inside agreed terms.

Current orders already had enough clay paid for.

Brass fittings were partly covered.

Kiln time for the next batch was reserved.

Then came the things I wanted to call expenses until I noticed they were mostly consequences of promising people a functioning product.

Replacement seals.

Two spare bypass tongues.

Dena's delivery pay.

One cracked housing Arlo had replaced without charging because the crack had appeared inside the trial period.

I pointed at that.

"Cause?"

Arlo knew immediately.

Uneven firing near the lower collar.

Not customer fault.

Good replacement.

Bad housing.

Useful evidence.

"And this?"

A line marked **service reserve**.

Ivena answered.

Money they had not spent.

Yet.

"How much?"

She told me.

More than I expected.

I looked at Arlo.

He shrugged.

"She won."

"What was your number?"

Lower.

"Much lower?"

He looked away.

Excellent governance.

Ivena crossed her arms.

"If three units fail in the same week, I want enough money to keep promises without deciding which customer gets lied to."

There.

Support.

Different uniform.

"Three failures at once is unlikely," I said.

"Yes."

"So why three?"

"Because two is plausible and three is the number that hurts."

I considered that.

Annoyingly good.

Old Greg had built contingency plans around exactly that kind of arithmetic.

Not the average day.

The day where two normal problems arrived holding hands with a third.

"Keep it," I said.

Arlo pointed at me.

"See?"

Ivena did not look impressed.

"He has been here forty seconds."

Fair.

The ledger continued.

After current materials.

After delivery pay.

After service reserve.

After one small amount Arlo had insisted stay available for workshop damage caused specifically by filtration work.

There was money left.

Not much.

Enough.

"Distribution," I said.

Arlo nodded.

There were three numbers.

Not equal.

Ownership shares.

My line ended at nine copper.

Arlo's a little more.

Ivena's less by percentage and then more again because her referral margin on one customer sat below it.

I read the whole page twice.

Ivena waited.

Arlo began cleaning a file while I read because apparently he had already learned what waiting for me looked like.

"You're comfortable with this?" I asked Ivena.

"No."

Good answer.

"Which part?"

"We should keep more."

Arlo groaned.

There it was.

I looked at him.

"You want to distribute more?"

"I want to buy a better small kiln."

That was not the direction I expected.

He pulled another page from beneath the ledger.

Used kiln.

Not new.

Small.

Better temperature control than his current repair kiln.

Seller wanted enough that buying it would consume almost all money currently available beyond reserve.

I looked at the number.

Then at the kiln against the wall.

Current one worked.

Mostly.

Arlo followed my eyes.

"The lower collar cracks are the problem."

"Sometimes."

"Three out of the last eleven housings."

There.

Real number.

Ivena tapped the service reserve line.

"And if we spend everything on a kiln because three housings cracked, then four customers decide to need seals next week?"

"We buy seals from new sales."

"That works beautifully unless new sales happen later than next week."

Arlo looked at me.

Absolutely not.

I held up both hands.

"Why am I suddenly the adult?"

"Age in the face," Ivena said.

Disturbing.

I read the kiln offer.

Future Greg remembered production floors full of specialized ceramic equipment.

Temperature-controlled firing.

Replaceable molds.

Standardized housings.

This tiny used kiln was probably primitive compared with all of it.

That did not answer whether it was worth buying today.

Later equipment had been purchased by businesses with later money.

Important distinction.

"Can we rent kiln time with tighter control?"

Arlo made a face.

Yes.

Expensive per batch.

Not always available.

"Enough to test whether the crack rate drops?"

He stopped making the face.

Ivena looked at me.

Good.

Maybe.

"Two batches," I said. "Same clay. Same collar. Better kiln. If cracks drop enough to matter, then we know what we're buying."

Arlo objected.

"The used kiln might sell."

"Then another kiln exists."

"Not at that price."

Future knowledge stirred.

Equipment market.

Opportunity.

Scarcity.

Possible value.

I felt the old reflex.

Buy before somebody else understands.

The same reflex that had purchased an entire cart of Kestrin shale when one bucket could have proven the idea.

I looked at Arlo.

"Do you know this kiln fixes the collar problem?"

"No."

"Do you know tighter firing fixes it?"

"Probably."

"Then we're trying to buy a machine before we buy the answer."

He hated that.

Which made three of us.

Ivena sat.

"Two rented batches."

Arlo looked betrayed.

"You already wanted more reserve."

"I contain multitudes."

I laughed.

Arlo did not.

Then he did.

Fine.

Decision.

Two controlled batches before equipment purchase.

Current reserve stays.

Small distribution goes out.

If the test materially reduces cracks, we revisit the kiln market with evidence.

Arlo wrote it down.

Ivena made him write **materially** and then forced us to define it.

Cruel woman.

We settled on no more than one collar crack across two batches unless another variable clearly explained it.

Not perfect.

Useful.

Then the coins came back into focus.

Nine copper.

Mine.

Not repayment of an investment.

That had already happened.

Not wage.

I had not scraped anything.

Had not stood watch.

Had not held a plane.

Had not carried a case.

Had not even been in the workshop while most of the money was earned.

Dena drove crates.

Arlo fired housings.

Ivena handled customers.

Workers cleaned units.

Somebody at the soapworks dropped a chamber.

The system moved.

Then nine copper reached me.

I disliked how good that felt.

Not because I had done nothing.

I had done something.

Earlier.

Money.

A remembered lead.

Negotiation.

Work.

Risk.

Ownership.

Then other people had continued.

Old Greg had earned extraordinary money later in life.

Contracts.

Ranker work.

Consulting.

Things rich people paid S-class adventurers absurd sums to make stop happening.

This felt stranger.

Nine copper from absence.

I turned one coin over on the table.

Arlo noticed.

"You're doing the face."

Apparently everyone had unionized around this observation.

"I got paid while I was underground."

"You were underground?"

Right.

We had not reached that part.

"Yesterday."

Ivena stared.

"And you opened with the coins?"

"There was a dry wall."

"A what?"

Report instincts finally won.

I told them about East Mill.

Not every step.

Old passage.

Limebacks.

Public drain.

Falling block.

Dry warm stone.

Unknown mechanism.

Arlo became interested at exactly the wrong point.

"No copper?"

"Visible? None."

"Ceramic?"

"None visible."

"Can I see it?"

"No."

His face fell.

"City owns next access."

He looked personally offended by municipal procedure.

Ivena tapped the nine copper.

"Good."

"What?"

"You have enough hobbies."

Fair.

I put the coins into my purse.

All nine.

Did not reinvest them.

Did not buy anything on the way out.

This required more discipline than the amount justified.

Outside, I stopped at the corner stall.

Stew smelled good.

Two copper.

I considered.

Then bought it.

Seven copper remained.

Ownership had paid for lunch while I was not looking.

I could live with that...
