# Chapter 36: The Listener

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/036-story-search.md`

---

The uphill line was dead because somebody had killed it on purpose.

We learned that after breakfast.

Breakfast was oat cake, hard cheese, and the remains of yesterday's apples eaten inside a relay hut cold enough to make chewing feel administrative.

Meral finished first.

Jorren finished second because Jorren approached food as a task with measurable completion.

I was still deciding whether the cheese had always been this hard or had achieved something overnight when Meral closed the diagnostic case.

"Pack."

Apparently the cheese would not receive an appeal.

Outside, frost silvered the uphill grass.

The storm had scrubbed the sky clean. Carrow was gone behind the ridge below us, hidden by distance and folds of land, but the courier line continued upward in irregular little pieces.

Stone post.

Gap.

Wood post.

Field wall.

Another ceramic head catching morning light.

We tested the station again before leaving.

Downhill pulse answered.

Uphill did not.

Meral marked the time.

Then we walked.

The first post above the station worked.

So did the second.

Third too.

That was worse than broken.

Broken was polite.

Broken introduced itself.

A line that worked in pieces made you earn the location of the lie.

Meral clipped her cord to the third head.

Blue trace.

Answer from behind us.

Nothing ahead.

"Next segment," she said.

Jorren took the path first.

Not because Meral ordered him to.

The grade narrowed and his eyes were already on the ground.

Storm water had cut shallow channels through the dirt. One section of trail had slumped toward the ravine, leaving enough width for a person and not much enthusiasm.

Jorren stepped across, tested the edge with one heel, then looked back.

"Stay uphill."

Useful advice.

I followed.

Meral came last because she was carrying the expensive things and believed in priorities.

The next relay point should have been on a low saddle between two hills.

I saw the house first.

Small stone place below the saddle.

Smoke from one chimney.

Goat shed attached to the lower wall.

Three fenced patches of winter vegetables arranged on terraces that had clearly lost arguments with goats before.

The relay post stood in the boundary wall beside the upper gate.

Its ceramic head was intact.

Copper strap intact.

No fallen tree.

No cracked stone.

No obvious reason for the line to be dead.

Then I saw the connector.

Loose.

Not broken.

Pulled free from the ceramic head and bent away from the wall.

A folded wool sack had been tied around the end.

Jorren stopped.

"Someone did that."

"Yes," Meral said.

No outrage.

Just fact.

A goat answered from the shed.

Another goat answered the first goat because apparently infrastructure inspection had become public entertainment.

The house door opened.

A woman came out carrying a bucket.

Thirty-five, maybe forty. Thick brown braid. Wool coat over a work dress. Boots white with dried mud along the sides.

She saw us.

Saw Meral's Guild tag.

Looked at the relay post.

Then sighed.

Not fear.

The sigh of somebody whose morning had acquired paperwork.

"I was going to send word today."

Meral stopped outside the gate.

"You disconnected the relay?"

"Yes."

No hesitation.

"When?"

"Storm night."

"Why?"

The woman put the bucket down.

"Because it started throwing blue at my gate."

Meral looked at the gate.

Iron hinges.

Iron latch.

Iron reinforcing strap along the lower edge.

Wet frost now.

Much wetter two nights ago.

My future memory began raising its hand.

I told it to wait.

Meral did not ask whether the woman was sure.

Good start.

"Show me."

The woman opened the gate.

"Tess Orin."

Meral gave her name.

Jorren did too.

I followed.

Tess looked at us in the order people usually did when three strangers arrived to inspect something expensive.

Lead.

Sword.

Other sword.

My good blade bought perhaps one second of attention before she moved on.

Correct market valuation.

"It was near midnight," Tess said as she led us toward the post. "Rain sideways. Wind from the west. The head was flashing wrong."

"Wrong how?" Meral asked.

"Not the usual pulse. Fast. Little snaps."

She pointed at the gate latch.

"Then that started answering."

Interesting.

Not theory.

Observation.

"You touched it?" Jorren asked.

Tess gave him the exact look the question deserved.

"No."

Good local.

"Hoe was dry."

She pointed toward the shed.

A wooden-handled hoe leaned beneath the eave.

"Knocked the connector out. Wrapped it once the rain eased."

Meral crouched beside the wall.

"Anybody hurt?"

"No. One goat objected."

A white goat pushed its face through the fence at that exact moment.

Possibly the witness.

"Anything catch?"

"No."

"Smell?"

Tess thought.

"Hot iron. Wet wool."

Meral nodded.

That was the first thing she wrote down.

Not **unauthorized disconnection**.

Hot iron.

Wet wool.

Good Silver.

I crouched on the other side of the post.

The old ceramic head had dark scoring near one contact.

Small.

Easy to miss if you wanted the problem to be the civilian who had pulled the wire.

The copper strap below it looked normal until it passed within a handspan of the gate hinge.

Later relay installations isolated that distance aggressively.

Later.

Different heads.

Different grounding plates.

Different everything.

Current proof.

I touched the stone beside the strap.

Dry now.

Jorren touched the soil beneath the gate with two fingers.

Still damp.

He rubbed them together.

"Drain runs this way."

He pointed downhill along the wall.

Tess nodded.

"Whole saddle drains through the gate when it pours."

Useful.

Meral looked at the ground.

The gate had been installed after the relay post.

Probably.

Different stone around the hinge anchors.

Cleaner cuts.

I pointed.

"Gate newer?"

"Six years," Tess said.

"Relay?"

She shrugged.

"Older than me."

Excellent dating system.

Meral scraped frost from the lower copper strap.

"No one ever tested after the gate went in?"

Tess shrugged again.

"Men looked at the post."

That was not an answer.

It was probably the correct one.

Meral opened the diagnostic case.

"Keep the relay disconnected."

Tess looked relieved.

There.

Important.

The Guild had arrived and had not immediately turned the dangerous thing back on because the diagram wanted continuity.

Meral pulled out two ceramic test tabs and a short copper lead.

She looked at me.

"Plane."

I put my pack down.

"Where?"

She pointed between the relay contact and the gate-side strap.

Five inches.

Useful size.

I formed it.

Faster than I would have a few weeks ago.

Not instant.

Close enough that Meral had already begun positioning her lead before the pale surface finished appearing.

Flat.

Angle slightly away from the gate.

"Hold."

I held.

She attached the test lead to the relay head without reconnecting the main strap.

Jorren moved Tess back two steps without touching her.

Tess moved because he said, "Give her room."

No fuss.

Meral fed a diagnostic pulse.

Blue light crossed the ceramic head.

Touched the test lead.

My plane received a faint pressure.

Not much.

Then something tugged sideways.

Toward the gate.

There.

I felt it before I saw anything.

"Gate side."

Meral cut the test.

"Off."

The plane disappeared.

Clean.

No trailing edge.

That satisfaction was mine alone.

Meral moved the lead farther from the hinge.

Test again.

I formed again.

Less sideways pull.

Again, with the lead raised.

Almost none.

She sat back.

"Wet path."

Tess folded her arms.

"Meaning?"

Meral pointed at the relay post, then the gate.

"When everything soaked through, charge found the iron. Your gate became part of the return."

Tess looked at the gate with new dislike.

Reasonable.

"So I should remove the gate?"

"No."

Meral looked at the relay head.

"Relay is older. Relay gets fixed."

There.

Also good.

Infrastructure did not get moral seniority because it arrived first.

Meral took one of the spare ceramic caps from her case.

Different base shape than the old head.

Not newer in the way I remembered later systems.

Current newer.

Enough.

"Need separation," she said.

She pointed to the strap line.

"Move the lower return away from the hinge. Add ceramic stand-offs. Replace the scored head."

Jorren looked at the wall.

"How far?"

Meral measured with her hand.

"Two spans minimum. More if the stone lets us."

The stone did not particularly want to.

Of course.

The easiest path crossed the top of the gate opening and would interfere with the latch post.

The second easiest path required drilling new anchors into old boundary stone.

Tess objected to that one.

Not sentimentally.

"That stone cracks, the upper wall shifts."

Meral looked at it.

"You know?"

"Rebuilt that corner twice."

Meral stood.

"Then not there."

No argument about professional authority.

Tess knew her wall.

Meral knew her relay.

Apparently two facts could survive the same morning.

We walked the boundary together.

There was a third route around the inside of the post, farther from the gate iron and beneath the top stone.

Longer copper run.

Ugly.

Meral liked it less.

Tess liked it more.

I liked that it did not set someone's gate on fire.

Strong coalition.

Jorren found an existing notch behind the wall cap where an old wooden brace had once sat.

"Could carry the strap through there."

Meral crouched.

Tested the stone.

"Yes."

Three people had now improved the repair.

None of them me.

I survived.

We worked.

Jorren removed the old scored ceramic head.

I held the replacement while Meral seated the new contacts.

Tess brought a dry stool from the house because our knees had apparently offended her.

Then she went back to feeding goats.

Important.

Her life had not become the relay repair.

We rerouted the copper strap through Jorren's notch.

Ceramic stand-offs every few feet where the stone permitted.

Two spans from the nearest gate iron at the tightest point.

Meral checked each one.

I carried tools, held tension, formed a plane twice when she wanted the live contact isolated during adjustment, and released both times without being told twice.

That was the kind of progress nobody applauded because applause would have slowed the repair.

Good.

By late morning, the new head was seated.

Main strap still disconnected.

Meral called Tess back.

"We're testing."

Tess put the feed scoop down and came to the gate.

She stayed behind the wall without being told.

Also good.

Meral connected the low diagnostic source first.

One pale pulse.

The ceramic head answered.

No snap at the gate.

Again.

Nothing.

She wet the ground around the hinge deliberately with half a bucket from Tess's well.

Tess stared at her.

"You want it wet?"

"I want to know before the next storm."

Better than hope.

Another pulse.

No gate response.

Meral increased the test charge.

Still clean.

Then she reconnected the relay line.

Downhill signal arrived from the station behind us.

The head flashed once.

Up the hill, the next post answered.

There.

Alive.

Meral watched the gate through three more pulses.

Nothing.

Tess exhaled.

I had not noticed she was holding it.

"If it snaps again?" she asked.

Meral pointed at the connector.

"Do exactly what you did. Dry wood. Knock it free. Don't touch copper or gate iron. Then send word when the route is open."

Tess blinked.

"That's allowed?"

Meral considered the question.

"It is now."

Maybe there would be a form.

Civilization advanced.

Meral wrote Tess's name beside the post number and added a note about local emergency isolation.

Not punishment.

Procedure.

I liked that too.

Dangerous morning.

We ate lunch sitting on Tess's boundary wall.

She sold us goat cheese because giving things away to adventurers was apparently not one of her agricultural practices.

Correct.

Two copper for enough to split three ways.

Jorren paid and informed me I owed him later.

Debt returned in manageable forms.

Tess asked where we were headed.

Meral pointed uphill.

"Next station, then back if continuity holds."

"Path washed near the birches. Take the goat cut above it."

Meral changed route immediately.

No map consultation.

The person living here knew the hill.

We followed the goat cut.

It was steeper.

It also existed.

Useful advantage.

At the next relay post, Meral tested.

Pulse good.

Next one.

Good.

Next ridge station.

Good.

The dead line had been one deliberate disconnect.

Not sabotage.

Not mystery.

Not a hidden enemy.

Someone had noticed a dangerous machine behaving dangerously and stopped it.

By midafternoon, Meral closed the diagnostic case.

"Line's restored."

Jorren looked back toward Tess's saddle.

"So we're done?"

"With the relay."

There was still the walk home.

Cruel distinction.

We returned to the first station before dark, packed the emergency bedding, and started downhill the next morning after one more clean continuity test.

Two-day contract.

Two days.

A concept I increasingly respected.

At the Guild, Sella read Meral's completion note.

Her eyes stopped on one line.

"Local disconnected the post?"

Meral nodded.

"Correctly."

Sella read the rest.

"You want an emergency isolation note added to that station?"

"Yes."

"Resident named?"

"Tess Orin."

Sella wrote it down.

That was all.

No hearing.

No lecture.

No fine for touching Guild equipment.

The dangerous thing had happened.

The person nearest it had acted.

The professionals had checked.

The procedure got better afterward.

I looked at Jorren.

He was looking at the pay line.

Different priorities.

Also healthy.

Sella slid our coins across separately.

Meral signed one final box.

Then the three of us became three people with different afternoons again.

No ceremony.

Outside, Jorren jingled his purse once.

"Food?"

"Yes."

"You paying your cheese debt?"

"I was hoping history would forget."

"I didn't."

There was probably a lesson in that.

I declined to find it...
