# Chapter 39: The Owner

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/039-story-search.md`

---

The better kiln worked.

This was inconvenient.

Ivena had arranged the evidence on Arlo's bench in four rows.

Eight chambers from the first ordinary firing.

Eight from the borrowed kiln.

Eight more ordinary.

Eight more borrowed.

Thirty-two pieces of fired clay, brass collars, pressure tongues, and the accumulated financial consequences of heating mud correctly.

Arlo stood behind the bench with both hands braced against the edge.

He looked proud.

Ivena looked armed.

Dena was somewhere in the back washing a dirty matched spare and singing the same four notes badly.

Normal workshop.

I had come because Ivena had sent a boy to Lower Rook with a folded note reading:

**Test done. Need third owner before Arlo buys a building.**

This was unfair to Arlo.

He wanted to buy only the kiln inside the building.

Probably.

I touched the first chamber.

Hairline split beneath the collar.

Reject.

Second had warped enough that its matched spare would have required optimism as a fitting method.

Third looked fine.

Fourth looked fine.

Five through eight contained one more crack, one bubbled firing scar near the pressure tongue, and four usable bodies.

I looked at the borrowed-kiln row.

Seven clean.

One shallow glaze blister nowhere structural.

"That one passes?" I asked.

Ivena nodded.

"Arlo says yes. Tannery agrees."

Arlo lifted one finger.

"Because it is cosmetic."

"You said that already."

"He asked."

"He asked me."

Ownership had improved nobody.

I moved to the second pair of rows.

Ordinary kiln: six usable.

Borrowed: eight.

Not subtle.

"So," Arlo said.

I held up one hand.

"Let me enjoy being wrong slowly."

He smiled.

The used kiln he had wanted in Chapter Thirty-Three had been larger than his current one, better sealed, and built by a tileworks that had gone out of business after its founder discovered debt could become hereditary if handled creatively.

I had liked it.

Not emotionally.

Capacity did not require affection.

But I understood capacity.

More reliable heat meant fewer rejected chamber bodies.

Fewer rejects meant less wasted clay, less brass refitting, less kiln time repeated, more matched spares available, more units shipped, more service customers supported.

Good system.

Simple.

Which was why the kiln had sold six days ago.

"To who?" I asked.

Arlo's expression became morally offended.

"Brewer."

I looked at him.

"For pottery?"

"For malt drying."

That was worse somehow.

A perfectly respectable kiln had escaped into beer.

Markets were cruel.

"Paid asking?"

"More."

Ivena slid a small paper across the bench.

The final sale price sat at the bottom.

I whistled.

"We're not matching that."

Arlo did not answer.

Good sign.

Ivena tapped the rows of chambers.

"Test still matters."

"Yes."

"He thinks it means buy another one."

Arlo straightened.

"It means our kiln wastes work."

"That is not the same sentence."

"It becomes the same sentence eventually."

I looked at him.

Arlo looked at me.

Dangerous alliance possible.

Later-life me knew this shape.

A production constraint proves real.

You solve it before demand grows into the constraint.

If you wait until every customer is screaming for capacity, equipment gets expensive, lead times become stupid, skilled people leave, and someone discovers your entire operation depends on a single kiln whose hinge has been making a noise for three months.

Buy before pain.

That instinct had made me money before.

It had also made me own things I later paid other people to regret.

Current evidence first.

Ivena had another page.

Of course she did.

"What is that?"

"Last three weeks."

Not sales.

Service.

Matched-spare swaps.

Cleaning returns.

Missed windows.

Customer holds.

Breakage.

Replacement bodies.

Dena came out of the back carrying a chamber dark with tannery solids.

She saw us looking at the page.

"Tell him Tuesday."

Ivena did not look away from me.

"Tuesday."

I looked at Dena.

"What happened Tuesday?"

"North dye house had a clean spare ready. I had the dirty one off in twelve minutes. Then I spent forty-five waiting behind a wool cart because South Lane froze into one long mistake."

She set the chamber near the wash trough.

"Kiln wouldn't have helped."

Then she went back to singing.

Ivena's eyes said this evidence had been prepared in advance.

"You rehearsed her."

"I employ people who remember days."

Fair.

I read the sheet.

Two customer delays from route congestion.

One from Dena being at another swap.

One from a cracked matched spare.

That one the kiln might have prevented.

One customer bypassed the unit for half a day because nobody had a clean chamber ready.

Cleaning capacity.

Not firing.

Two new orders waiting on bodies.

That was firing.

But not many.

Not yet.

"How many usable bodies do we have unassigned?"

Arlo answered.

"Five."

"Matched?"

"Two. Three raw."

"How many customers could sign tomorrow before we start lying about service?"

Ivena answered this time.

"Two. Maybe three if nobody breaks anything."

"And how many are trying?"

"One likely. Two asking price."

There.

Capacity mattered.

Capacity was not yet strangling us.

I touched one of the borrowed-kiln chambers.

"Where did you fire these?"

Arlo frowned because I knew the answer mattered now.

"Bell Tile."

I knew the name only because their blue roof could be seen from the east market.

"Price?"

Ivena slid another paper.

Of course.

Per shelf.

Night firing only.

We delivered shaped dry bodies by fourth bell.

They loaded them around their own work.

Collection next morning if the firing ran on schedule.

No guarantee during roof-tile season.

Breakage terms favored Bell Tile because reality favored people who owned kilns.

I read it twice.

"How many chambers per shelf?"

"Ten if I hate myself," Arlo said. "Eight if I want the pressure tongues to survive."

"And how often will they give us one?"

"One night a week now. Maybe two if we pay more."

I looked at the test rows.

Eight better-fired bodies a week was not a factory.

It was also more than our current order pressure required.

"What do you want?" I asked Arlo.

He did not say kiln immediately.

Good.

He picked up one ordinary-fired reject.

"I want to stop throwing this away."

Different sentence.

Better one.

"I shape it. Dry it. Fit collar. Fire it. Find the split. Then I shape another one."

He placed the reject down.

"Bell Tile proves heat is part of the problem."

"Part."

"Yes."

He did not fight the word.

"I don't want to rent forever."

There.

Also reasonable.

Ivena leaned against the wall.

"I don't want to own equipment before we can keep the customers we already have."

Also reasonable.

They both looked at me.

Third owner.

This had sounded impressive when I was nineteen for the second time and desperate for money.

Nobody had explained the main function was becoming the tie-breaking idiot.

I walked to the wash trough.

The dirty chamber Dena had dropped there smelled like leather, lime, and something that had died professionally.

Two clean matched spares sat drying on a rack.

One had a tiny chalk mark near the collar.

Customer identifier.

Not interchangeable.

Still.

Weeks later and our clever replacement system remained a collection of married objects.

Later factories would standardize these.

Later firing control would make today's kiln argument embarrassing.

Later route schedules, seal compounds, replaceable liners, and cleaning rigs would turn this whole shop into a museum exhibit.

None of that helped current Arlo.

But later Greg knew something simpler than technology.

Capacity purchased too early became rent with a chimney.

I went back to the bench.

"How long will Bell Tile hold the weekly slot?"

Ivena answered.

"They won't hold it. We book two days ahead."

"Have we lost one yet?"

"No."

"Roof-tile season?"

"Spring."

Months.

Not forever.

Enough.

I looked at Arlo.

"I vote we don't buy a kiln."

His face tightened.

I kept going.

"Yet."

Small difference.

Important.

"One Bell shelf a week. Two when orders justify it. Track what the better firing actually saves us in clay, brass refitting, time, and rejects."

I looked at Ivena.

"And track how many customer failures came from not having enough bodies versus not cleaning or moving them fast enough."

She nodded.

Already doing it, probably.

Annoying woman.

Arlo touched the clean borrowed-kiln row.

"And when Bell Tile says no?"

"Then we learn what a lost firing night actually costs us."

He did not like that.

Neither did I.

Useful tests rarely came with emotional support.

"And if another good used kiln appears?"

That was the real question.

I thought about the purse under my coat.

My own money.

Assessment paid.

Instruction still waiting.

Nine copper distribution from this business once.

Three copper more rent every week now.

Different pools.

Same nervous system.

"If another one appears," I said, "we look at current cash and current orders that day. We don't buy it because this one got away."

Arlo sighed.

Not agreement.

Close enough to continue living.

Ivena looked at him.

"Vote?"

He stared at the test rows for another long second.

Then lifted one hand.

"Rent Bell. One shelf. Keep looking."

Not unanimous joy.

Unanimous decision.

Ivena wrote it down.

Of course.

"Do we have to document that I was responsible?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Can you spell my name wrong?"

"No."

Institutions everywhere.

Dena came back out.

"Did he buy a building?"

"Not today," Ivena said.

Arlo pointed at both of them.

"I never said building."

Dena looked at me.

"He said building?"

"Repeatedly."

Arlo swore.

Good meeting.

We broke for lunch because ownership did not suspend hunger.

The workshop stove had a pot of something thick enough to support small tools.

Dena called it stew.

I respected her confidence.

While we ate, Arlo talked about Bell Tile's firing profile.

Not purchase.

Actual heat.

They brought temperature up slower through the middle range.

Held longer.

Cooled with vents cracked instead of sealing overnight.

Maybe some of the improvement could be copied in his current kiln without spending silver.

There.

He had already started moving.

Not toward the answer I had voted for.

Toward his own problem.

"Can you try it?" I asked.

"Some. Current kiln leaks heat on the north seam. Slow ramp costs charcoal."

"How much?"

He gave me an estimate.

Not terrible.

Ivena immediately asked how many extra usable bodies would pay for it.

Arlo immediately looked betrayed.

They did not need me for this part.

Good.

I ate the stew.

It contained turnip.

Less good.

Before I left, Ivena handed me no money.

This was correct and still disappointing.

"No distribution?"

She looked at me.

"You voted for reserve."

Consequences had become personal.

"I wanted responsible ownership and cash."

"Invent it."

I considered.

"Later."

She had already turned away.

Outside, Deepwinter made the lane smell like wet stone and smoke.

I could have gone to the Guild.

The support block price was folded in my pocket with Kesra's assessment.

One field-support block.

Available.

Useful.

Expensive.

Instead I walked home.

Not because I had decided against it.

Because the decision did not become more correct merely because the paper remained warm from my hand.

At Lower Rook, my new room still felt slightly like someone else's.

I hung my coat.

Put the assessment on its shelf.

Put the workshop note beside the nine-copper distribution record.

One business had just improved because we chose not to own a better machine.

One support path had just improved because I was now allowed to spend more money on it.

Progress was developing an expensive sense of humor.

I moved the field pack away from the stove so the leather would dry slowly.

Then I practiced one cut across the empty floor.

Arrive.

Recover.

No second cut.

No plane.

No new purchase.

For one evening, owning more options was enough...
