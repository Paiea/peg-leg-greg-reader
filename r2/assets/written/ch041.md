# Chapter 41: The Cordon

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/041-story-search.md`

---

Edda found me before I found work.

This was becoming a professional hazard.

She stood beside the Guild board with one glove tucked into her belt and a work slip already folded in half.

No Harl.

No Pate.

No road survey.

Good start.

"Free today?" she asked.

"Depends what happened."

"Shardback. North road."

Less good.

"Alive?"

"No."

Better again.

Edda handed me the slip.

**SITE CORDON / PROCESSOR ESCORT.**

Two Bronze.

One licensed appraiser.

Two carcass hands.

Road office observer if available.

No salvage rights.

The Guild had underlined that line once.

Maturity.

"Who killed it?"

"Gold crew yesterday."

I looked up.

Edda had already moved to the next useful fact.

"Charged a wool string north of the reopened gate. One mule broke a leg. Driver got thrown. Nobody died. Golds were passing south."

"Same crew from the yard?"

She looked at me.

"Which yard?"

Right.

Normal people did not catalog strangers by training session.

"Never mind."

"You taking it?"

"Jorren?"

"East road. Grain escort. Back in three days."

There.

Jorren had employment without me.

Healthy.

I read the pay.

Three copper base.

One more if the site ran past dusk.

Nothing heroic.

Nothing dangerous enough to justify heroics, assuming the dead animal respected its status.

"Yes."

Edda nodded once.

Decision complete.

Sella took my Bronze token, copied the second name onto the slip, and pointed at a man waiting near the door.

He was perhaps fifty.

Which made him ancient according to Jorren.

Heavy leather apron rolled under one arm. Short gray beard. Two narrow cases strapped across his back and a city salvage license hanging from his neck.

"Mesk," Edda said.

The man looked at me.

"Greg?"

"Yes."

"You cut anything before I mark it, I charge you for it."

Excellent introduction.

"I wasn't planning to."

"Everyone says that near valuable dead things."

He held out one hand.

I shook it.

"Licensed carcass appraiser?"

"Today."

"Other days?"

"Married."

Edda started walking.

Conversation ended through leadership.

The two carcass hands waited outside with a narrow two-wheeled handcart, hooks wrapped in cloth, wedges, saws, oilskin rolls, and enough rope to restrain something that no longer needed opinions.

Brother and sister, according to Mesk.

Rin and Del.

They worked for a renderer north of Carrow and had been arguing since before we met them.

"You brought the short saw," Rin said.

"Long saw catches plate," Del said.

"Short saw takes twice as long."

"Then you'll have twice as long to regret being wrong."

Professional family.

We went north.

The reopened gate changed the walk immediately.

No east detour.

No lower-quay crowd spilling onto the road.

Light carts moved through the controlled lane one at a time.

A clerk checked our work slip and waved us through on foot.

Yesterday's excitement had already become today's procedure.

Beyond the gate, the road carried fresh wheel tracks through sleet-soft dirt.

We passed a wool cart coming south with one axle wrapped in new rope and one driver who watched the tree line more than the road.

Shardback residue.

Two miles out, the smell reached us first.

Blood.

Wet hair.

Broken earth.

Then mineral heat.

Faint.

Wrong in cold air.

I knew the smell.

Not exactly smell.

Heat changed wet stone and blood in a particular way around a shardback.

The animal lay in a ditch below the road.

Large.

Boar-shaped if you described mountains as rocks.

Shoulder nearly to my chest.

Dark winter coat stiff with mud and blood.

Tusks curved forward beneath translucent gray sheaths clouded like bad glass.

Across the shoulders and upper spine, overlapping mineral plates rose through the hair.

Not armor in the crafted sense.

Growth.

Pale stone-glass ridges, each one thick near the base and thinner toward the edge.

Several were shattered.

One had been driven almost flat against the body.

The road above it was worse.

A freight wagon sat tilted on one repaired wheel.

Wool bales had been restacked under oilskin.

Blood marked the ditch edge where the mule had gone down.

No mule now.

Good.

Either alive somewhere else or removed before processing.

A city cord already ran between two stakes around the carcass.

Gold work.

So did four chalk marks on the road.

Positions.

I recognized the spacing before I recognized the people.

Spear lane.

Shield set.

Cord anchor.

Support angle.

Then I saw the broad shield leaning against the wagon.

Three repaired edge plates.

Same shield.

The Gold party from Lera's yard was still there.

Not dramatically.

They were eating breakfast.

The spearwoman sat on a wool bale with a tin cup in one hand. Her spear leaned within reach.

The shieldman had one boot off and was wrapping two toes.

The short-sword woman with the coil of cord was talking to the freight driver while writing something on a Guild incident sheet.

The unarmed man crouched beside the repaired wagon wheel with both gloved hands around a metal fitting.

A pale line pulsed once between his fingers.

Then stopped.

Gold.

Breakfast.

Foot care.

Paperwork.

Apparently excellence did not eliminate mornings.

Edda noticed my attention.

"Know them?"

"Seen them train."

"Useful?"

"To watch."

"Good. Don't."

Lera would have liked her.

Mesk ducked under the cord.

The short-sword Gold looked over.

"Appraiser?"

"Mesk."

She came down from the road and handed him the incident sheet.

No introduction to us.

Correct priority.

"Animal crossed from east scrub at second bell yesterday," she said. "Freight string was stopped for axle binding. Driver shouted. Shardback turned toward sound, charged wagon. We intercepted after first impact."

Mesk read while she spoke.

"Mana discharge?"

"Shoulder plates twice. Low after death. We didn't cut."

"Good."

No reverence for rank.

Domain authority.

"Poison?"

"None observed."

"Other animals?"

"No."

"Mule?"

"Guild stable. Leg set. Healer thinks usable if the joint holds."

The unarmed Gold looked up from the wagon fitting.

Probably his work too.

Mesk signed one line and handed the paper back.

"You're clear once road office copies position."

The Gold woman nodded.

Then looked at Edda.

"Cordon yours?"

"Yes."

"People have already tried for bristles."

Edda looked toward the roadside.

Three local boys standing much too casually near a hedge suddenly found the weather interesting.

"Of course they have."

The Gold woman smiled.

Small.

Then returned to her paperwork.

Nobody asked my name.

I survived.

Mesk walked the carcass slowly.

He used a short brass rod to tap each plate and listened to the sound.

High clear note on the intact ones.

Duller where cracks ran beneath the surface.

Rin and Del followed with chalk.

One mark for plate salvage.

Two for structural break.

Cross for discard.

Tusk sheath separate.

Bristle bundles measured by patch.

Meat inspection later.

Glands before gutting.

Nothing became coins just because someone pointed at it.

Ownership first.

Condition.

License.

Processing.

Transport.

Buyer.

Then maybe money.

I liked it.

Dangerous.

Edda put me on the east side of the cordon.

"People, dogs, scavengers."

"In that order?"

"Whichever gets stupid first."

Clear procedure.

She took the road side where carts and curious drivers were more likely to create work.

I stood in sleet beside a dead magical boar while professionals argued about saw length.

Adventuring had improved.

The first trespasser was a dog.

Medium brown.

One white foot.

No respect for licenses.

It approached downwind with its body low and nose working.

I stepped toward it.

"No."

The dog stopped.

Considered rank structure.

Went around me.

I moved again.

The dog sighed through its nose.

Then sat.

This felt manipulative.

I had dried meat in my pack.

That was exactly how institutions failed.

I threw one piece away from the carcass.

The dog chased it.

Success through corruption.

Edda saw.

"That comes out of your pay."

"One bite."

"Still."

The second trespasser was one of the boys from the hedge.

He waited until Mesk and the carcass hands had all moved to the animal's far side.

Then he slid toward the cord near a patch of loose bristle.

Not even valuable bristle.

Young crime lacked research.

"Don't."

He froze.

"It's outside the line."

I looked.

Three black bristles lay six inches beyond the cord.

Technically.

"Then wait for Mesk to say they're outside the claim."

"They're hair."

"So is mine. Still attached to legal trouble."

The boy looked at my head.

Long enough to become insulting.

Then Mesk called without turning around.

"Loose bristle outside cord is road waste. He can take it."

Domain authority again.

I stepped aside.

The boy grabbed the bristles and ran.

"Good work," Edda said.

"I protected public waste."

"Bronze."

Fair.

Processing started with the tusks.

Del had been right about the short saw.

Rin did not acknowledge this because family law operated separately from evidence.

Mesk marked cut lines well behind the translucent sheath.

The inner tusk was ordinary ivory-colored bone.

The sheath was not.

Clouded mineral around it held faint threads of pale charge even dead.

Useful for heat-tolerant fittings, cheap ward spacers, and several crafts I remembered becoming boring later.

Current value was already decent.

No unfair fortune waiting for me.

Good economy.

Then Rin moved to the left shoulder.

I stopped watching the road.

Not completely.

Enough.

She set a wedge beneath the lowest cracked plate.

Wrong place.

Not obviously.

Mesk had marked the plate for removal.

The crack ran outward.

Cold air had tightened the surface.

The plate beneath it had been driven inward during the charge.

Stored mechanical load.

Maybe charge too.

I had seen one open like a thrown handful of knives thirty years from now.

Different winter.

Different carcass.

Same shape.

"Wait."

Rin stopped.

Good professional.

Mesk looked at me.

"What?"

I pointed without crossing the cord.

"That lower plate took inward load. See the base?"

Mesk crouched.

The plate root disappeared beneath hair and frozen blood.

He pushed the brass rod under the edge.

Not prying.

Feeling.

The rod clicked against something beneath.

He changed angle.

Tapped.

The sound came back high.

Too high for a cracked plate.

Mesk's face changed.

Not fear.

Attention.

"Rin. Back."

She stepped away.

Del did too without being told.

Mesk used a hooked scraper to clear hair from the base.

The lower plate had folded partly beneath the one above it.

Compressed.

Spring waiting for permission.

"Cold-set," he said.

Current name.

I knew a later one.

Did not improve it.

"How long?" Edda asked from the road side.

Mesk checked the air.

Then the plate.

"Warm the root before wedge. Slow."

He looked at me.

"Seen it?"

"Yes."

"Shardback?"

"A few."

Not a lie.

Not enough truth to become useful socially.

Mesk nodded anyway.

"Good catch."

Clean.

No hidden reversal.

The carcass hands packed oilcloth around the plate root and poured warm water into it in small amounts.

Not boiling.

No magic.

Twenty minutes later the plate relaxed enough that the brass rod sounded dull again.

Rin reset the wedge from a safer angle.

One strike.

Two.

The plate lifted.

No explosion.

Excellent outcome.

I returned to watching dogs.

The Gold crew left before noon.

The road-office clerk arrived, copied positions from their chalk marks, signed the incident closeout, and released them.

The four packed efficiently.

Shield strapped.

Cord checked.

Case latched.

Spear wrapped against sleet.

The unarmed man spoke briefly to Mesk about the mule's set leg.

Then they walked south.

The spearwoman passed within ten feet of me.

She glanced at my Bronze mark.

Then at the cordon stake.

Then kept walking.

That was all.

Current Gold had somewhere else to be.

By midafternoon, half the shardback had become labeled bundles.

Plate.

Sheath.

Bristle.

Hide sections.

Gland jars packed in sawdust.

Meat marked for inspection before sale.

Waste separated for burning because one parasite species liked the gut wall in winter.

I did not know that one.

Mesk did.

Good.

The world remained larger than my old expertise.

The dog came back.

I had no meat left.

It judged me correctly and left.

We finished before dusk.

Mesk signed the processor sheet.

Edda signed the site clear.

I signed beneath her.

Second Bronze.

Again.

At the Guild, Sella paid three copper.

No bonus for preventing a plate from opening someone's face.

That had apparently been included under **competence**.

Reasonable.

Edda took her pay and tucked it away.

"Next time Mesk asks for field hands, he'll know your name."

"Is that good?"

"Depends what died."

She left.

I stood at the board for another minute.

The Gold party had killed the shardback.

The healer had saved the mule if the joint held.

The road clerk had documented the site.

Mesk had owned the carcass.

Rin and Del had turned a dead animal into usable material.

Edda had owned the cordon.

I had stopped one bad wedge and bribed a dog.

All of it counted as the same incident.

That felt more like adventuring than the songs usually managed...
