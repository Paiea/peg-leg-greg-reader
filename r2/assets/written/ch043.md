# Year Chapter 01: The Date

Status: **TEMPORAL YEAR REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

`Y312 / D001 / FIRSTDAY OF SPRING`

`DAYS SINCE PRIOR CHAPTER: opening`

`LOCATION: Carrow`

`RANK: Bronze`

---

I wrote the date down because otherwise I could pretend I had never said it.

Firstday of Spring, Year 313.

The ink bled slightly into cheap paper.

I stared at it.

Then wrote underneath:

**One year from today, I am going to stand at the Black Stair.**

Noll read it upside down from the second chair.

He had entered my room while I was still deciding whether the sentence needed `probably`.

It did not.

He chewed something that smelled like onion and looked at the paper again.

"Black Stair."

"Yes."

"Far."

"Yes."

"Why?"

This was a rude question because I had prepared for difficulty, danger, expense, Guild regulations, monsters, winter roads, old magic, and potentially death.

I had not prepared for why.

"I want to go."

Noll waited.

Apparently this answer passed.

"Okay."

He took another bite.

I looked at him.

"That's all?"

"You wrote it."

"Most people would point out that I'm Bronze."

"You know."

"That I don't have a party."

"You know."

"That Westreach does not usually take random nineteen-year-olds with a sword and an unreliable memory."

Noll swallowed.

"Do you know?"

"I assume."

"Then ask."

Annoying.

Useful.

I folded the paper once and put it inside my coat.

The room had changed over the last few weeks in ways small enough that I only noticed when leaving. Two chairs instead of one. Tavin's letter beneath the burned seven. Mara's old note tucked under the room key. My field pack leaning open beside the bed because I had not decided whether wanting a road meant keeping it ready.

Outside, spring rain had stopped sometime before dawn.

The street still held it.

Water sat in wagon ruts. Roofs dripped. A woman across Lower Rook courtyard beat a blanket against railing while cursing weather as if spring had arrived specifically to inconvenience laundry.

Noll followed me downstairs.

"Work?"

"Guild."

"Same."

"Cruel."

Maud stopped me at the front room.

"You eating?"

"Guild has food."

"Guild has lies."

She put a heel of bread into my hand anyway.

I ate it walking.

Carrow on Firstday of Spring did not look transformed by the season. The city had apparently failed to receive the ceremonial notice. Gray sky. Wet stone. Carts. People stepping around puddles with personal resentment.

The Guild had changed its front board.

That was enough.

Three old winter notices had been stripped. Fresh postings occupied the right side beneath a painted green stripe.

Road season.

I stopped before entering.

One large sheet hung behind glass.

**WESTREACH EXPEDITION, YEAR 313**

There.

A full year away and already taking up wall space.

The sheet was not recruitment exactly. It was an information notice.

Field applications open now.

Preliminary sponsor declarations due by late autumn.

Skills registry review during winter.

Final crew selection before departure.

Departure: late winter.

Target arrival: Black Stair, Firstday of Spring.

My stomach tightened.

Not because I had learned anything impossible.

Because the date on my paper existed outside my room.

Someone else had printed it first.

I went inside.

Sella sat behind the contract desk with a mug and a stack of route tokens.

She looked at my face, then at the Westreach notice through the door.

"No."

"I didn't ask yet."

"Still no."

"Excellent service."

She drank.

"What do you want?"

I took out the folded paper and put it on her desk.

She read the date.

Then the sentence.

Her expression changed very slightly.

Not amusement.

Worse.

Professional evaluation.

"You want a Westreach slot."

"I want to stand at the Black Stair."

"Same road."

"Not necessarily same job."

"Almost certainly same expedition unless you plan crossing western passes alone in winter."

"I don't."

"Good."

I heard the evaluator word and let it pass.

"What do they require?"

Sella leaned back.

"Depends what you are."

"Tall."

"That is already overrepresented."

"Bronze."

"Currently."

There.

I hated how quickly one word affected me.

"Usually Silver minimum for adventuring roster," she continued. "Gold leads some field sections. Bronze gets on through trade, animal handling, freight, medicine, mapping, specialist craft, sponsor labor, or somebody deciding they are worth the problem."

"Specialist magic?"

"Possible."

"Ward work?"

"Possible."

"Force?"

"What force?"

Fair.

My current magic existed in a category I understood better from fifty-nine-year-old memory than nineteen-year-old execution.

"Small support. Load redirection. Some sensing."

"Documented?"

"Not much."

"Then not much."

Clean.

She pulled a blank Westreach interest card from a drawer.

Not application.

Interest.

The distinction offended me.

"This puts your name in the seasonal registry. It does nothing else."

"Cost?"

"One copper."

"For a piece of paper?"

"You can make your own expedition."

I paid.

Sella wrote my name.

`GREG` looked unimpressive in black ink.

"What should I do?"

"Work."

"Specific."

"Get field marks from people who are not your friends. Take roads. Take jobs where someone can say what you actually did. Stop treating your sword like private evidence. If you want Silver, ask what Silver needs instead of imagining it. If your weird magic is real, make it useful where somebody else owns the problem."

That last part landed because she had either become wiser or more targeted.

"Timeline?"

She turned the Westreach sheet toward me and tapped four lines.

"D274, candidate office wants a real path. Rank, sponsor, trade, whatever. D304, crew trials start. D316, final freight and role commitments. D326, west gate departure if weather holds."

Four dates.

I copied them onto the back of my paper.

My one-year goal now contained smaller teeth.

Sella watched.

"You know writing dates does not do them."

"I had suspected."

"Good."

Again.

I folded the paper.

"Contract board?"

She pointed.

I turned toward it.

Road jobs lived right.

My eyes went there first this time.

Progress perhaps.

Most excluded me quickly.

Silver escort north.

Two-person river patrol requiring local endorsement I did not have.

Five-day freight run leaving tomorrow, but team wanted prior mule experience.

A mapping crew wanted a ward reader and accepted Bronze, but seven days out and I had no idea if my sensing met their definition.

Then left side caught me.

**STONEHOOK FREIGHT, WEST DISTRICT**

**DAY LABOR / LOAD WARD ASSIST / THREE DAYS POSSIBLE**

**BRONZE ACCEPTED**

Pay ordinary.

Work ugly.

Exactly the sort of thing Sella meant.

"Stonehook?"

She nodded.

"River machines. Kiln clay. Metal fittings. Crane yard."

"Danger?"

"Everything with six men and a suspended crate is danger if they get creative."

"Ward assist?"

"Their old reader is sick. They have a rigger who can tell you what not to touch."

Good enough.

I took the token.

Sella wrote `Bronze / sensing` on it.

Then, after a pause, added `sword` in smaller letters.

I noticed.

She noticed me noticing.

"Don't become unbearable."

"Too late."

I left.

Stonehook sat west of fish market where the streets widened for freight carts and every building looked like it had been hit by a wagon at least once.

The yard announced itself through sound.

Chain.

Timber.

A hammer striking iron pin.

Men shouting numbers.

Something heavy hitting ground hard enough that pigeons left three roofs at once.

I stopped at the open gate.

A woman with a gray scarf and a short-handled mallet looked up from a hoist base.

"Guild?"

I held token.

"Greg."

"Mira."

She took it, read Sella's notes, and snorted.

"Sensing. Sword. Which one lifts?"

"Neither, mostly."

"Then hands."

She pointed at a stack of wheel chocks.

"Move those under shed. Then come back."

I moved chocks.

There was something satisfying about an ambitious one-year expedition beginning with carrying blocks of wood twenty feet.

I did not say that because it sounded like a chapter ending and it was barely morning.

Stonehook did not care about my narrative structure.

By midday I had learned three things.

First, river clay became heavy when packed wet.

Second, every crane crew believed every other crane crew tied bad knots.

Third, Mira owned the yard whenever something hung above a person's head.

She was not a wardwright.

She was a rigger with twenty years of not dropping things often enough to stay employed.

That counted.

My job was mostly looking at old bronze load plates fixed near hoist heads and telling her whether charge stayed even while crews moved test weights.

The magic was simple.

Simple did not mean easy.

Each plate carried a low field across pulley supports. It did not hold the load. It warned when tension shifted beyond a set relationship.

Old Greg remembered systems that actively compensated.

These did not.

Current Stonehook used cheap reliable warning because cheap reliable things survived accountants.

One plate on north crane pulsed when the empty hook swung in wind.

"Bad?" Mira asked.

I watched again.

"Maybe too sensitive."

"Maybe?"

"Empty hook isn't enough evidence."

"Better answer."

She had apparently attended the same school as everyone else in Carrow.

We loaded a half-weight test crate with broken brick.

I held tag line.

The crane took strain.

Plate glowed pale green.

Steady.

Mira pushed the crate sideways with a pole.

Plate flashed amber.

Then green.

"Reads lateral," I said.

"Supposed to."

"Then maybe fine."

"Maybe again."

I ignored her.

By second bell my shoulders hurt from handling rope and my boots had clay in the seams.

Mira signed the token for one day.

One field mark.

Tiny.

Real.

"Tomorrow?" she asked.

I looked at the yard.

A crew was securing a crate taller than me beneath south hoist.

"Yes."

"First bell. Eat before."

I nodded.

On the walk home I took the folded paper out once.

Not because I needed the date.

Because the back now had four deadlines and the front had one sentence.

One year from today.

The Black Stair was still impossibly far.

But Stonehook's ink was on my Guild token.

That was smaller than a dream and heavier than one.

At Lower Rook, Noll sat in my second chair again.

"Work?"

"Yes."

"Black Stair?"

"Not today."

He nodded.

"Tomorrow?"

"Also probably not."

"Slow."

"Extremely."

He handed me a bowl.

I ate.
