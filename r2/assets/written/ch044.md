# Chapter 44: The Swordsman

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/044-story-search.md`

---

The cart held medicine, lamp glass, and absolutely nothing worth dying over.

This became relevant around noon.

The job had looked ordinary enough at the Guild.

One Bronze.

One light commercial cart.

Carrow to Wicker Ford and back before dark if the road stayed hard.

Cargo belonged to a north-road apothecary cooperative with the kind of inventory list that made theft sound medically disappointing.

Bandages.

Dried willow bark.

Lamp chimneys.

Six sealed bottles of fever tincture.

Three jars of something called bitterroot salve.

The driver was Orrin Kesh, a square man with a wool hat, a scar through one eyebrow, and no interest in becoming my friend before breakfast.

I liked him immediately.

Not emotionally.

Operationally.

He checked my Bronze token.

I checked his axle pins.

We both approved of this arrangement.

"Road's open," he said.

"One lane."

"Enough for me."

His cart was narrow enough to qualify.

One horse.

Low load.

Canvas tied tight.

Nothing stacked above the sideboards.

The North Gate clerk weighed nothing because the city had not yet invented a scale large enough to humiliate carts efficiently.

He checked the manifest, looked at the wheel width, and waved us through.

Orrin clicked his tongue.

The horse moved.

I walked on the left rear quarter.

Not because anyone told me.

Because that gave me the road behind, the tree line, the driver's hands, and enough of the cargo to know when somebody else's problem became mine.

Good position.

Current body agreed.

That was happening more often.

The road north of Carrow looked healthier with traffic on it.

Not beautiful.

Used.

Farm carts.

Couriers.

One butcher wagon smelling aggressively of success.

Two Guild riders passed south before second bell.

No one waved.

Professional civilization.

Orrin spoke after an hour.

"You always walk?"

"Usually."

"Could sit."

He nodded toward the rear board.

I looked at the road.

Frozen ruts beneath a thin skin of melt.

"I like my spine."

"Fair."

Conversation complete.

Wicker Ford was barely a town.

Twenty houses.

A cooper.

Two sheds pretending to be warehouses.

A bridge that was mostly wood and optimism.

The apothecary storehouse took half the cargo.

Orrin signed three lines.

I watched a clerk count bottles twice and learned nothing useful.

Good.

We ate bread and onion beside the cart.

Then started south.

The first sign of trouble was not a person.

It was the horse.

Her right ear turned before the rest of her did.

Toward the trees.

Then back.

Not fear.

Attention.

Orrin noticed too.

He shortened the reins.

I moved closer to the cart.

No magic.

No sword yet.

Road narrowed ahead where a drainage culvert crossed beneath the packed dirt.

Ice had formed along one side where water seeped over the stone lip.

A reasonable place to slow.

That was the problem.

Two men stood near the ditch.

Not hiding.

One had a broken-handled shovel.

The other held his left arm against his chest and looked cold enough to become sympathetic.

Maybe actual road trouble.

Maybe not.

Orrin did not stop.

Good driver.

He slowed because the ice required it.

Different thing.

The man with the shovel lifted one hand.

"Wheel cracked! Need a pull!"

No wagon.

No wheel.

I looked behind them.

Brush.

Ditch.

No vehicle hidden nearby unless somebody had become ambitious about concealment.

"Keep moving," I said.

Orrin already was.

The injured man stepped into the road.

Not far enough to be struck.

Far enough to force a decision.

"Please."

Wrong word.

Not because criminals could not say please.

Because his injured left arm changed position when the horse came close.

Elbow opened.

Hand dropped.

Nothing wrong with it.

Knife appeared.

There.

I drew.

The good sword cleared cleanly.

No branch.

No scabbard drag.

No cheap balance pulling my wrist forward.

Worth the money again.

"Road," I said.

The knife man looked at the blade.

He had expected one Bronze.

He had perhaps not expected six feet of steel-adjacent irritation to become real this quickly.

Behind him, the shovel man moved toward the horse.

Not me.

Better criminal judgment.

Orrin saw it.

"Don't touch her."

The shovel hooked toward the reins.

Orrin yanked back and snapped the brake handle loose from its catch.

Not a weapon originally.

Now a length of hardwood with iron on one end.

The shovel man reconsidered.

Knife man did not.

He came at me.

Fast enough.

No formal stance.

Blade low.

Left shoulder forward.

He wanted inside my reach.

Correct idea.

I stepped off the cart line.

He followed.

Also correct.

My first cut went toward his knife hand.

Not deep.

Not theatrical.

He jerked back.

The edge caught sleeve and skin along the outside of his forearm.

Blood.

His knife stayed in hand.

My sword finished the cut.

I did not admire it.

Feet arrived.

Weight settled.

Blade came back between us.

He expected the opening that used to live after my strike.

It was smaller now.

His second step stopped.

There.

Not victory.

Space.

Behind me, wood cracked against metal.

Orrin.

I did not look.

Driver owned horse side until something changed.

Knife man feinted right.

I saw eyes before shoulder.

Left.

He lunged.

I moved outside.

Good body.

Current body.

My hilt struck his wrist.

Knife dropped.

Not because I had planned a beautiful disarm.

Because wrists disliked iron when hit correctly.

He grabbed for me with the cut arm.

Bad choice.

I put the sword point under his chin.

Stopped.

He stopped too.

Excellent communication.

"Back."

He backed.

One step.

Two.

Then his eyes moved past me.

I heard the third man before I saw him.

Brush behind the cart.

Of course there were three.

I turned enough.

Young man.

Club.

Running toward the rear board.

Cargo.

Not me.

Orrin had the shovel man on the ground beside the horse.

Brake handle across his chest.

Driver busy.

The club man reached the cart.

I could have chased.

Bad geometry.

Knife man still in front.

Club behind.

Horse between chaos and road.

The cargo was not the objective.

The moving cart was.

"Orrin! Go!"

He looked once.

Understood.

Dropped the brake handle.

Snapped reins.

The horse lunged.

Cart rolled.

Club man grabbed the rear board.

For one second he succeeded.

Then road ice turned the wheel sideways six inches.

The cart jerked.

He lost one hand.

Orrin kicked the brake handle backward from the driver's bench.

It struck the man's knuckles.

He let go.

The cart kept moving.

Good driver.

Knife man used my glance.

He ran.

Also good.

Not toward us.

Into the trees.

The shovel man rolled away from the road and followed badly.

Club man joined them after deciding medicine was not worth being dragged to Carrow beneath a wheel.

Nobody chased.

Orrin looked at me.

I looked at him.

"You hit him with the brake."

"It's removable."

Apparently this answered everything.

I walked beside the cart until the culvert disappeared behind us.

Sword still out.

No one returned.

After two hundred yards I sheathed.

Then checked my hands.

No blood.

Checked coat.

No cuts.

Checked breathing.

Fast.

Settling.

The old Greg in my head had already begun cataloging errors.

Too much attention on knife hand during first cut.

Could have angled farther from cart.

Third man surprised me.

No call before driver moved because driver did not need magical support.

Stop.

Not because analysis was wrong.

Because the road remained.

Orrin spoke first.

"You cut him shallow on purpose?"

I thought about it.

"Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"I wanted the hand unavailable, not the man dead."

"Worked."

Clean.

I accepted it.

Five minutes later he added:

"You missed the third one."

Also clean.

I accepted that too.

"Yes."

Orrin nodded.

Balanced review.

We stopped at the next occupied farm because road procedure after attempted robbery remained less glamorous than the robbery.

Farmer sent his oldest son toward Carrow on a mule with our description.

Three men.

One cut forearm.

One possibly bruised chest.

One damaged hand.

No horse.

No known bows.

No stolen cargo.

Orrin wrote the location on his manifest margin.

I signed beneath it.

The farmer's wife gave us hot water and no sympathy.

"Road's open," she said. "Idiots come back with it."

There.

Independent economics.

Not prophecy.

We reached Carrow before dusk.

The gate clerk took our report.

Sella took another copy.

The apothecary representative checked the cargo seals before asking whether either of us was hurt.

Correct commercial priority.

Then she looked embarrassed and asked.

"No," Orrin said.

"No," I said.

One lamp chimney had cracked.

Orrin blamed the culvert.

I blamed civilization.

The representative deducted nothing from our pay.

Good employer.

Sella counted my escort rate onto the counter.

Then looked at the incident sheet.

"Three?"

"Yes."

"Any recognized?"

"No."

"Magic?"

"No."

"Injuries?"

"One cut. Two bruised, probably."

"Dead?"

"No."

She made one mark.

No praise.

No promotion.

No sudden realization that Bronze had become too small for me.

Just a completed escort with attempted robbery attached.

I liked that more than I should have.

Outside the Guild, I drew the sword halfway.

Checked the edge.

One tiny mark near the middle where it had caught a buckle or knife spine.

Nothing serious.

I bought oil on the way home.

The first real blood the sword had drawn in this life belonged to a man whose name I did not know and whose biggest historical contribution might be learning that fever tincture was guarded.

That felt ordinary enough to be true.

Upstairs, I cleaned the blade.

One cut.

Arrive.

Recover.

The words did not need saying anymore.

That was probably the point...
