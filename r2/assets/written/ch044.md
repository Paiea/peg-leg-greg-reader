# D126: The Second Chair

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The second chair cost three copper and created a problem immediately.

It did not fit.

This was technically my fault.

I had measured the space beside the table.

I had measured the chair at the market.

I had not measured the distance required to open the door while a person sat in it.

Architecture remained adversarial.

Noll stood in my doorway holding the chair by one back leg.

I held the other.

We stared at the door.

"Turn it," he said.

"Which way?"

"Other."

"You haven't picked a way."

"Then both work."

Strong engineering.

We turned the chair.

The arm caught the table.

Noll looked at me.

"Bad room."

"Chair is oversized."

"You bought it."

"Market fraud."

He lifted his end higher.

We moved the table six inches toward the window.

The chair passed.

The door closed.

Nothing collapsed.

Current domestic triumph.

Noll sat in the new chair immediately.

"Good."

"That's mine."

"You have one."

He pointed at the old chair.

The old chair had a cracked back, one repaired rung, and a cushion whose stuffing had migrated to two corners sometime before I was born the second time.

"That one is terrible."

"Then why keep it?"

I looked at the chair.

No answer improved me.

"History."

"Trash."

"Different discipline."

Noll leaned back in the new chair.

It did not creak.

The quality offended me.

One hundred twenty-six days after Mara left, I could afford a chair without checking whether doing so endangered rent.

That was new.

Not wealth.

Not even comfort, apparently, because Noll had occupied it first.

But the calculation had changed.

Three copper no longer required a meeting.

The room had accumulated around that fact.

A kettle sat on the shelf above the basin.

Mine.

Two good field shirts hung beside the old ones.

A small box under the table held spare cord, chalk, two ceramic wedges, and a brass clamp Kessa had told me to stop carrying loose in my pocket after it tore through the lining.

My good sword had a second scuff on the sheath and no longer looked expensive enough to frighten me.

The Vey slip still lived under my room key.

604 days.

I did not count every morning.

Mara occasionally counted for me.

When she wrote.

She had not written in twenty-three days.

This was also new.

Not the absence.

The amount of attention I had given it.

The first week had been ordinary.

Mara's letters had never arrived on a perfect schedule.

The second week had produced irritation.

Vey could move timber, grain, ceramics, account books, people, and apparently goats across half the western routes, but a folded piece of paper had defeated the institution.

By the third week, irritation had become worry and worry had become embarrassing enough that I visited the packet office twice in one day.

The clerk charged me one copper for holding a Doss parcel and offered no emotional services.

Good boundary.

I had written Mara on day seventeen.

Not a good letter.

Not terrible.

The dangerous middle.

**If Vey can keep track of six crates of lamp fittings through three warehouses, I assume they can locate one clerk long enough to confirm she is alive.**

I had almost crossed out **alive**.

Did not.

Then spent the next six days regretting the entire sentence.

Noll knew none of this.

He knew a letter had not come because the packet runner had stopped asking which room was mine and started leaving Halden paper with Maud.

He also knew I had been walking downstairs faster when the front door opened.

My life remained private only by mutual laziness.

"Why two chairs?" he asked.

There it was.

"Because one was insufficient."

"For what?"

"Sitting."

"You sit already."

"Other people also have knees."

Noll looked toward the bed.

Then at me.

"Mara?"

"People."

"Mara is people."

"Technically."

"So Mara."

"She lives in Halden."

"Chair can wait."

I hated him.

"You are in it now."

"Exactly. Useful."

He stood before I could throw him out.

His Brass Spoon shirt was cleaner than the one he had worn two months ago.

Same cheap cloth.

Better laundering.

He paid the woman downstairs to wash his kitchen clothes now because five and sometimes six shifts a week had turned soap and time into different costs.

Noll had also stopped borrowing copper from me.

Not announced.

Just stopped.

Last week he had lent Jorren four.

That had bothered me for reasons too stupid to investigate.

"Work," he said.

"Lunch?"

"Night. Vessa moved me."

"Again?"

"Domm back lunches."

"You like nights?"

Noll shrugged.

"More money."

"Why?"

"Drunk people."

Excellent compensation model.

He went downstairs.

I sat in the new chair.

It was better.

I resented that too.

Someone knocked five minutes later.

Not the wall.

Door.

I opened it.

Maud held a packet.

Vey seal.

Halden mark.

My chest did the stupid thing anyway.

Maud saw.

"Rent's paid," she said.

"I know."

"Then whatever this is, don't break anything."

"Supportive household."

She handed it over.

The seal was scuffed.

One corner of the packet had dried mud under the string.

Not recent mud.

Old enough to crack when I bent it.

I checked the route marks before opening it.

Halden.

Kelm turn.

North road relay.

Back to Halden.

Then Carrow.

The paper had traveled more than I had this month.

I opened it standing up.

Four pages.

Mara's handwriting started larger than usual.

**I am alive. This is not Vey's fault, which is irritating because I had prepared a better complaint.**

I sat.

The second chair earned its keep immediately.

Mara had been sent north with Len and a route auditor named Sera because three warehouse accounts disagreed about damaged wool.

The job was supposed to take six days.

It took fifteen.

A bridge inspection closed the south road on day four, so the Vey party went north to Kelm turn, then west, then back down along a route with no regular packet exchange.

Mara had written on day seven.

That letter was apparently still somewhere north of Kelm being improved by weather.

She had written again on day twelve and given it to a merchant who promised to hand it to a river runner.

The merchant reached Halden before the letter did.

Mara described him as **a man with excellent boots and no relationship to cause and effect.**

I laughed.

Then reached the paragraph I deserved.

**Your letter arrived yesterday. I understand why you wrote it. I am also annoyed by it. Both can be true. I do not need to report alive on a schedule because you are frightened by silence. You are allowed to be frightened by silence anyway. Those are also both true.**

I stopped.

Read it again.

No clean winner.

Unfortunate.

She continued.

**If I disappear for twenty-three days, you can ask if I am all right without filing an indictment against my employer.**

Fair.

**Also Vey absolutely could have moved one of those letters if Len had not handed the first one to a grain factor going the wrong direction. So your institutional complaint has partial merit. Do not become pleased.**

Too late.

I leaned back.

The chair held.

Good purchase.

The rest of the letter was not about my worry.

That helped.

Mara had seen Kelm from a hill and disliked the roofs.

Sera slept with one boot on because she had once lost both to a lodging-house thief and apparently considered asymmetry safer than trust.

Len had stopped humming when he was wrong because Mara noticed, then started humming when everyone else was wrong because he was petty.

She had earned enough travel allowance to buy a wool coat before winter.

Her room ceiling leaked once near the washstand and not over the bed, which improved Vey housing beyond projection.

At the bottom:

**You asked in your last good letter whether Halden feels like mine yet. Not yet. I know where bread is cheap, which stairs ice first in rain, and which clerk lies about tea. Maybe that's the beginning.**

Then:

**604, because you bought yourself this problem.**

I put the letter down.

Twenty-three days had contained an entire piece of Mara's life I had not witnessed.

Road closures.

Wet wool.

Sera's one boot.

Len becoming more annoying.

A coat purchase.

Cheap bread.

None of it had waited for me to know it happened.

That was obvious.

Still required practice.

I found paper.

Started with:

**I was frightened by silence.**

Stopped.

No joke yet.

Good.

**I was also being an ass about Vey. Partial withdrawal only because Len remains criminally negligent with letters.**

Better.

I told her about the needlebacks.

Not heroically.

Mostly about Savi hitting one with a shovel and making the entire Guild profession look overequipped.

I told her about East Quay.

Kessa's ladder dispute.

The eight-copper shift.

Noll's nights.

Jorren lending him four copper and thereby destabilizing several household assumptions.

Then I looked at the second chair.

Absolutely not.

I wrote:

**I bought furniture. This may be the most serious development.**

Mara could discover the rest later.

If she came.

When she came.

No.

If.

I left the word where it belonged.

At fifth bell I carried the letter to the packet office myself.

The clerk weighed it.

"Halden?"

"Yes."

"Regular packet goes tomorrow."

"Any chance Len gets involved?"

The clerk looked at me.

"Who?"

"Excellent. Keep it that way."

I paid.

Outside, the air had cooled enough that the market sellers were pulling cloth over late fruit.

Summer was not gone.

It had started losing arguments.

I stopped at the Guild on the way home.

Sella had two contracts left on the board.

One city.

One road.

The road paid better.

Four days north-east with a quarry crew, load support requested by name.

I read the line twice.

Then signed.

Not because Mara had written.

Not because she had not.

Because four months had happened and I had work tomorrow.
