# Year Chapter 02: The Yard Hand

Status: **TEMPORAL YEAR REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

`Y312 / D004 / SPRING`

`DAYS SINCE PRIOR CHAPTER: 3`

`LOCATION: Carrow west freight district`

`RANK: Bronze`

---

By the fourth morning, Stonehook had taught my palms where rope lived.

Two small blisters had flattened into sore pink skin beneath the fingers. My shoulders hurt in the exact places Old Greg's body had stopped complaining about decades later. Clay had worked permanently into the welt around my right boot.

Mira had signed three day marks on my Guild token.

Not endorsements.

Marks.

I had learned the distinction because I asked.

"An endorsement means I would put my name next to yours when something expensive is hanging," Mira had said.

"And three marks?"

"Means you showed up three days."

"Glowing praise."

"Show up thirty."

So I came back.

Spring had changed in three days too.

The rain stopped.

Morning light reached the west yard before first bell, weak but actual. Fish market smelled worse in warmth. One of the river carts had shed its winter canvas. A boy outside Stonehook sold lemon water instead of hot root tea and looked personally betrayed by the new inventory.

Inside the yard, south hoist was already wrong.

I knew because Mira was standing under nothing.

She never stood under a live hoist line unless the hook was down and tied.

Today the hook rested on paving beside her boots.

The bronze load plate above it glowed faintly despite no load.

"Morning," I said.

"Look."

I looked.

No dramatic pulse.

A weak amber shimmer along the lower edge.

Three days ago I would have reached for it immediately.

Today I looked at the mechanical parts first.

Progress could be embarrassingly fast when the lesson involved not being crushed.

"Hook line has tension?"

"Only its own weight."

"Wind?"

"None."

"Did it start overnight?"

"Night crew wrote it at fourth bell."

She handed me the log slate.

The mark had flickered twice while lowering empty rig. They tied off. No load since.

"Plate could be bad," I said.

"Could."

"Bracket could be moving."

"Could."

"Line could be running wrong through sheave."

Mira waited.

"I hate this game."

"Good."

There it was.

I crouched beside the hook and opened mana sense.

The plate above was thirty feet away, but its field ran through bronze pins in the hoist frame and down a thin warded thread braided into the main line. Not enough to hold anything. Enough to tell the plate what tension felt like.

The field did not feel damaged.

It felt pulled.

Very slightly.

Left.

I stood and walked around the hoist base.

"Can we turn the upper wheel by hand?"

Mira waved to Hobb at the crank.

"One quarter."

Hobb moved it.

The hook rose six inches.

Amber brightened.

"Again."

Another six.

A sound came from above.

Not crack.

Tick.

Mira heard it too.

"Ladder."

We climbed to upper platform one at a time.

She went first.

I followed after she clipped a short safety line to my belt because apparently future S-class memories did not exempt current nineteen-year-old me from falling off industrial structures.

The upper sheave looked fine until Mira put both hands on its axle housing and pushed.

It moved.

Not much.

A thumbnail.

Enough.

One retaining pin had backed halfway out of its seat.

"There," I said.

Mira looked at me.

"What?"

I almost said `good`.

I stopped myself.

"Pin."

"I have eyes."

Fair.

She touched it with hammer head, then stopped.

"Why backed?"

That was better question.

The pin had a locking wedge.

Wedge still present.

But the seat around it showed bright metal where repeated motion had polished the edge.

"Something is walking the housing sideways," I said.

"Load?"

"Maybe line alignment."

Mira looked toward yard floor.

South hoist had been modified last year with longer swing arm to reach carts farther from wall.

Old structure carrying new geometry.

Familiar problem in every century.

We came down.

For two hours Stonehook stopped using south hoist.

This cost money.

The owner, Pell Ardin, arrived in a clean vest and made sure everyone knew.

"We have three kiln-clay crates waiting."

Mira said, "Then they can wait."

"North hoist is booked."

"Then those can wait too."

Pell looked at me.

"Guild says ward?"

"Ward is reporting real movement."

"Can you fix?"

"I can tell you it isn't lying."

Not the answer he wanted.

Mira liked it enough not to fire me.

A carpenter and ironworker arrived. We stripped side cover, found the real cause after another hour: the extended swing arm pulled the sheave housing off-axis whenever load moved beyond a certain yard angle. The retaining pin was not defective. The modification had changed the path enough that every heavy lateral swing worried it loose a fraction.

"New bracket," ironworker said.

"Today?" Pell asked.

He laughed.

"No."

"Brace?"

Mira looked at him.

"Temporary. Half-load max. No outer swing."

Pell argued.

Stonehook continued being a business.

By midday we had a temporary timber brace and secondary tie around the housing. Mira let us test empty.

Then quarter load.

Then half.

No amber.

No movement.

Pell wanted full load.

Mira said no.

He looked at me.

Three days ago I might have offered the technical argument for full test.

Today I said, "Her hoist."

Mira did not smile.

Later she handed me the good end of a sausage at lunch.

Possibly equivalent.

The yard resumed using south hoist only for light crates.

North hoist took the heavy kiln-clay queue.

That should have ended the interesting part.

It did not.

Second bell, north yard.

A six-man crew was moving a crate of iron kiln bands from cart to shed.

Not huge.

Eight hundred pounds maybe.

The crate hung four feet off paving while crew walked tag lines.

I was on the west line with a man named Senn who had three missing teeth and a habit of humming when nervous.

Mira stood near crank.

"North two feet."

We pulled.

Crate shifted.

"Hold."

We held.

A cart entered yard behind us.

Horse snorted.

Then screamed.

Something in fish market alley had spooked it. Dog maybe. Dropped pan. I never saw.

Horse lunged sideways.

Cart shaft struck our tag line.

Line snapped tight.

The crate swung.

Fast.

"Release!" Mira shouted.

Senn released.

I did too.

The loose line whipped across paving.

The crate kept moving because eight hundred pounds had developed an opinion.

A loader named Fen was crossing from shed with pry bar.

He froze exactly where the swing would take him.

"Fen!"

He looked up.

Wrong choice.

I moved.

Not toward him.

Toward the crate.

Old Greg's body supplied a technique before current brain approved.

Force plane.

Small.

Angled.

Do not stop the load.

Change the arc.

I planted my right foot and threw both hands toward the lower corner.

Mana caught.

For one instant it felt like pushing a wall with my wrists.

The crate struck the invisible plane.

My elbows buckled.

Pain ran from palms to shoulders.

The plane slid sideways because current control had almost no shear.

But the crate moved too.

Six inches.

Maybe eight.

Enough.

Fen finally dropped and rolled.

The crate passed over his boots instead of his ribs.

Then hit a stack of empty pallets.

Wood exploded.

Iron bands inside shifted with a sound like a church falling into cutlery.

The hoist line held.

Mira had stopped crank and locked brake.

The horse cart slammed wall thirty feet away.

Someone grabbed bridle.

For three seconds the whole yard was noise.

Then orders.

Mira owned it.

"Clear load!"

"Fen, stay down!"

"Hobb, brake locked?"

"Locked!"

"Cut cart free if horse won't settle!"

I stood with hands hanging uselessly at my sides.

My wrists hurt.

The force plane was gone.

Fen sat up.

"Fuck."

"Yes," I said.

He looked at the crate.

Then me.

"Was that you?"

"Partly."

"Don't say partly."

Mira reached us.

She checked Fen first.

No head hit. One boot scraped. No obvious injury.

Then she looked at me.

"Hands."

I showed them.

No burn.

No cut.

Wrists shaking.

"Magic?"

"Yes."

"You try to catch it?"

"No. Redirect."

"Could you?"

"Apparently."

Mira's eyes narrowed.

"How much?"

The honest answer felt good.

"Eight inches. Maybe."

She looked at where Fen had been.

Eight inches was enough to have an argument about for the rest of my life.

"Sit."

"I'm fine."

"Sit."

I sat on an overturned bucket.

Growth remained humiliating.

A city stable worker helped settle the horse. The cart driver apologized to everyone, then started explaining why the animal never spooked, which was a useless fact now.

The crate stayed suspended until crew rigged second line.

Then they lowered it onto broken pallets.

One iron band inside had bent.

Pell Ardin appeared and immediately asked who paid.

Mira told him to wait until people finished having bones.

I liked her more.

After twenty minutes, my wrists stopped trembling.

Pain remained when I flexed.

Not injury I thought.

Overload.

Current body had accepted force that Old Greg considered trivial and charged interest.

Fen sat beside me with one boot off.

"You Bronze?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

This was the first time anyone had asked like rank itself was suspicious.

I enjoyed it.

No lesson followed.

"Paperwork," I said.

Fen laughed.

Mira did not.

At end of shift she took my Guild token.

I expected fourth mark.

She wrote longer.

`STONEHOOK, 4 DAYS. LOAD SENSE USEFUL. ACTED UNDER SWING FAILURE. CONTROL LIMITED. RESPONSE GOOD.`

Then signed her full name.

I read it twice.

"Endorsement?"

"One."

My chest warmed in an embarrassing way.

Mira handed token back.

"Don't make me regret it."

"When would that be measured?"

"Every day."

Fair.

Walking home hurt more than morning.

Not legs.

Wrists.

I bought cold cloth from apothecary and wrapped both after dinner.

Noll came in, saw them, and pointed.

"Black Stair?"

"Stonehook."

"Same?"

I looked at the paper on my table.

D274.

D304.

D316.

D326.

Then D365.

"Maybe," I said.

Noll stole one dumpling from my bowl.

I let him because both hands were wrapped and combat would have been undignified.
