# Chapter 46: The Buyer

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/046-story-search.md`

---

The city sold the North Gate's bad decisions by the pound.

Not all of them.

Some were still installed.

The surplus tables filled half the municipal yard behind the Guild with iron strap, cracked ceramic heads, old brace timber, bent brackets, copper offcuts, three damaged hand winches, and enough obsolete support hardware to prove that infrastructure eventually became shopping.

I had not come to shop.

This was my first mistake.

I had come because the notice outside the Guild said **PUBLIC SURPLUS, NORTH WORKS PHASE ONE**, and later Greg remembered several generations of Carrow hardware that had stopped existing quietly.

Curiosity.

Cheap.

Usually.

The sale clerk handed me a chalk token with the number thirty-two.

"Bid or browse?"

"Browse."

He looked at the sword.

Then at me.

"Everyone browses until lot twelve."

"What's lot twelve?"

"Copper."

Fair.

I walked the tables.

Most of it was exactly what current people thought it was.

Scrap.

Iron straps with bolt holes stretched oval from years of load.

Ceramic ward caps cracked through their conductive glaze.

A timber brace foot blackened by rot.

Three old anchor plates whose etched channels had been scraped away badly enough that restoring them would cost more than new work.

No secret relic.

No forgotten king's sword hiding beneath municipal bolts.

Good city.

A contractor bought the copper lot for enough money that I stopped pretending scrap was cheap.

Two carpenters fought politely over straight brace timber.

One woman bought all three winches without testing them.

Either expert or optimist.

Hard to tell at distance.

Lot nineteen was ceramic relay heads.

I recognized the older pulse shape from Meral's hill line.

Three usable.

Five cracked.

One missing its copper contact entirely.

A road-office man bought them as repair stock.

Correct buyer.

I kept walking.

Then lot twenty-four made me stop.

Not because it looked valuable.

Because it looked familiar in the way obsolete things sometimes did.

Six metal rings lay in a shallow crate.

Four iron.

Two brass-faced.

Each was slightly larger than my palm, thick around the rim, with three movable tabs spaced roughly around the outside.

Inside the ring sat a segmented ceramic seat.

Temporary brace rings.

Old ones.

Older than the versions I had used later, though the family resemblance survived.

You clipped them to rope, timber, wagon frame, scaffold, whatever would hold.

The ceramic seat gave a support practitioner a known connection surface instead of asking raw wood, wet rope, or somebody's improvised ironwork to behave consistently.

Not a power source.

Not a spell.

A good place to put one.

Later field kits carried lighter versions with standardized bite teeth and load stamps.

These had thick tabs, crude locking pins, and enough metal to survive being thrown at a problem.

I loved them immediately.

Dangerous.

The lot card read:

**6 BRACE RINGS. OLD CITY PATTERN. DECOMMISSIONED. TWO CRACKED SEATS. ONE INCOMPLETE. NOT RATED FOR STRUCTURAL REUSE. SCRAP / TRAINING / PARTS.**

Accurate.

I picked up the incomplete one.

The sale clerk shouted from twenty feet away.

"Don't remove from table."

I kept it above the crate.

"I have very long arms."

He stared.

I put it down.

Municipal humor had limits.

The missing seat segment left perhaps a quarter of the inner ceramic bare.

One outer tab was bent.

Lock pin moved.

Barely.

Corrosion but not failure.

I rotated the ring.

Maker stamp near one hinge.

Not famous.

No historical thrill.

Just a city forge mark I remembered from old maintenance kits.

This pattern had one useful flaw.

It was overbuilt.

Later standardized rings handled rated loads better and packed smaller.

These old ones tolerated ugly anchors.

Rope too thick.

Timber not square.

Cart frame at a stupid angle.

The three tabs had more travel than later safety standards allowed.

Bad for untrained structural use.

Excellent when an experienced support hand needed a modest known connection on whatever the field had provided.

Experienced support hand.

Current Greg qualified intellectually.

Physically, less so.

Still.

A five-inch plane did not need a bridge anchor.

It needed consistency.

I looked at the lot card again.

Six sold together.

Problem.

I did not need six.

I did not need one.

Different problem.

A man beside me picked up one of the iron rings.

"Boat mooring?"

He asked nobody in particular.

I looked at the ring.

"Wouldn't."

He looked at me.

"Why?"

"Ceramic seat. Shock load through the tabs."

He turned it over.

"Could knock the ceramic out."

"Then it's an awkward iron ring."

He considered.

"I need an awkward iron ring."

Fair.

He put it back anyway.

The lot started at three copper.

For all six.

Cheap.

Then a contractor bid four.

Someone else bid five.

I did nothing.

Good.

Six.

Contractor again.

Seven.

I counted my purse without touching it.

Rent paid.

Food fine.

Support block already paid.

No next block needed.

Business reserve not my purse.

Field cash adequate.

Tavin postage recently insulting.

Seven copper for six rings was still cheap if two could be repaired.

I needed one.

That sentence remained important.

Eight.

The contractor hesitated.

His interest was probably metal weight.

At nine, scrap value began arguing with utility.

I raised my chalk token.

"Nine."

The clerk called it.

Contractor looked at me.

Not angry.

Calculating whether I was stupid enough to save him money.

He let it go.

"Nine, thirty-two."

I had bought six obsolete damaged brace rings.

Excellent restraint.

The sale clerk collected my copper.

"You know they're not rated?"

"Yes."

He pointed at the card.

"City takes no responsibility if you hang anything living, expensive, public, load-bearing, sacred, or litigious from them."

"Very complete list."

"It became complete."

Experience.

He signed the receipt.

I carried the crate out.

It was heavier than my decision had felt.

Nine copper.

Not ruinous.

Not nothing.

At the Guild entrance, Sella saw the crate.

She looked at me.

Then the crate.

Then me again.

"No."

"I didn't ask anything."

"Good."

"Do you know anyone who repairs old brace rings?"

She closed her eyes.

Very briefly.

"You bought North Gate scrap."

"Surplus."

"Scrap with paperwork."

"Six rings."

"Why six?"

"Lot structure."

"That is not an answer adults use."

I put the crate on the floor.

"I need one."

"Better. Why?"

I showed her the incomplete brass-faced ring.

"Old pattern accepts bad anchor angles. Good for temporary field connection under low load."

Sella took it.

Not delicately.

She knew hardware.

Turned one tab.

Tested the lock pin.

Pressed a thumbnail against the broken ceramic seat.

"Not with that gap."

"I know."

"Bent tab."

"I know."

"Pin is rusted."

"I know."

She handed it back.

"Then why are we talking?"

Fair.

"Repairer?"

She pointed at a notice board near the rear desk.

Not support instructors.

Certified equipment repair.

Harness.

Ward fixtures.

Field iron.

Two names.

One had **temporary anchors / old city hardware** written beneath it.

Useful institution.

I copied the address.

"Do not bring it to Neris and ask her to teach you around broken equipment," Sella said.

I looked at her.

"I wasn't going to."

"You have a face."

Apparently this diagnosis had spread.

The repair shop belonged to a man named Pell Orst.

Small place near the cooper lane.

Half forge, half bench work.

He had three harness buckles open under a lamp when I arrived.

I showed him one ring.

Pell looked at it for less than ten seconds.

"Old city three-tab."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Popular question.

"Temporary field connection. Low load."

He looked at my Bronze mark.

Then at my hands.

"Support?"

"Some."

"How low?"

Good question.

I described the plane honestly.

Five inches.

Short holds.

Moving load, modest.

No structural brace.

No body suspension.

No pretending one repaired ring turned me into an engineer.

Pell nodded.

"Could make it safe for that."

There.

Not rated.

Safe for stated use.

"Cost?"

He named it.

Four copper for ceramic segment, straighten tab, clean pin, check seat continuity.

More if the inner groove cracked when he cleaned it.

Purchase plus repair.

Thirteen copper minimum.

I thought about the field-support block price folded in my recent memory.

Different expense.

Still.

"What about the other five?"

Pell glanced into the crate.

"Two good enough for parts. One iron ring might clean up. Brass one there has seat crack through half. Scrap."

"Would you buy the parts?"

He looked at me differently.

Business.

"Maybe."

We went through them.

Not quickly.

He valued ceramic segments.

Lock pins.

One good tab set.

Metal weight.

In the end he offered five copper for four rings and loose salvage rights from the cracked brass one.

I kept the incomplete brass-faced ring and one iron ring with a sound seat.

Nine copper out.

Five back.

Net four before repair.

Then four to repair the one I actually wanted.

Eight copper total for one repaired brace ring plus one ugly spare.

Not a miracle.

Good purchase.

Pell took the target ring.

"Three days."

"No rush."

He looked up.

Suspicious.

"You sure?"

"I don't currently need it."

That seemed to offend the laws of buying things.

"Then why buy it?"

I thought about that.

Because later field kits used worse versions for ugly work.

Because I knew the old pattern.

Because options mattered before emergencies.

Because nine copper had become an amount I could spend without the room disappearing.

Because sometimes future knowledge should make money instead of producing anxiety.

"It was cheap," I said.

Pell shrugged.

Good enough.

I carried the remaining iron ring home.

Did not test it.

Did not form a plane through it.

Did not spend the evening inventing a reason I had needed it today.

I put it on the equipment shelf beneath my field cord.

Empty ceramic circle.

Three tabs.

No magic happening.

One repaired ring would come back in three days.

Maybe I would use it next week.

Maybe next month.

Maybe sell it to someone smarter.

The useful thing about buying an option was that it remained an option until I did something stupid with it...
