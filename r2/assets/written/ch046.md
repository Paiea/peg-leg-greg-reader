# D218: Thirty-Six Hours

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Mara came to Carrow while I was gone.

This was how I learned the second chair worked.

I came back from North Quarry after eleven days with slate dust inside one boot, a split thumbnail, and enough dried mud on my cloak that Maud made me take it off before climbing the stairs.

"Floor," she said.

"The floor has survived worse."

"Not for this rent."

She held out one hand.

I gave her the cloak.

Then she said, "Your girl was here."

Everything in me stopped badly.

"What?"

Maud looked at my face.

"Maybe not your girl. The ledger one."

"Mara."

"That."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

I looked toward the stairs.

Ridiculous direction.

As if Mara might still be on the third floor because Maud had used past tense carelessly.

"She's here?"

"No."

There it was.

Eleven days of quarry work disappeared behind one word.

"When did she leave?"

"Morning."

I closed my eyes.

Not helpful.

Opened them.

"Why was she here?"

"Vey."

Of course.

"How long?"

"Day and a half."

Thirty-six hours.

Maybe less.

I had been ninety miles north arguing with a buried drainage room while Mara occupied my city for thirty-six hours and left again.

The world had developed timing as a weapon.

Maud handed me my room key.

"She paid no rent."

"Thank you, Maud."

"Didn't stay here."

"I assumed."

"Could have. Cost extra."

Warm household.

I climbed.

My right calf cramped on the second landing.

Eleven days of quarry stairs and bad camp ground had shortened something in the leg enough that the building's stairs now objected.

I stopped.

Stretched it.

Kept going.

The fourth stair squeaked.

Mara had remembered the third.

Still wrong.

Her absence made the correction mean something different.

I disliked that.

Noll's door was open.

Empty room.

Brass Spoon lunch service today.

His schedule lived in my head now because the building had taught me through silence.

Jorren's door was shut.

He had left three days before me on a south freight run and might already be back.

Might not.

No one maintained a board for my convenience.

I unlocked my room.

The second chair had moved.

Three feet.

From beside the table to near the window.

That was enough.

Mara had been here.

Not because Maud said so.

Because no one else in the building would move a chair toward light unless they intended to read.

Probably.

No.

Evidence first.

A folded paper sat on the seat.

Vey seal broken already because it was not a mailed packet.

My name on the outside.

Under it:

**Your furniture remains badly planned.**

I sat on the bed.

The room looked exactly like it had eleven days ago except somebody I wanted had passed through it without me.

That felt unreasonable.

I opened the paper.

**Greg,**

**I had thirty-six hours in Carrow. Before you decide Vey did this specifically to hurt you, they gave me forty-eight and the north packet lost twelve to rain. I am writing this first because I know you.**

I stared at the sentence.

Hostile accuracy.

**I asked Maud where you were. She said hole. I asked what kind. She said expensive. I went to the Guild and Sella said North Quarry. Apparently this is enough information for everyone except me.**

I laughed once.

Then didn't.

The quarry job had started as four days.

Old structure under the third cut became six days when the city claimed the upper rooms.

Then nine when a second passage appeared behind a collapsed wall.

Then eleven because Kessa refused to let the quarry reopen the east face until a buried retaining channel had been mapped properly.

I could have left on day nine.

Technically.

The work did not own me.

But Kessa had needed someone on the lower brace shifts who could hold shaped load while they reset the temporary frame.

Someone else could have done it.

Not as cheaply.

Maybe not as cleanly.

I had stayed.

That mattered now in a way it had not when I signed the extension.

Mara continued:

**I considered coming north. Then I learned the quarry is not three streets past the gate, which is apparently what Carrow people mean when they say up north. I had to be back on the west packet this morning.**

**I was angry.**

I stopped.

Read that line again.

No apology attached.

Good.

**Not at you exactly. This did not improve the anger. I had pictured finding you here. That was my mistake, not your contract. Both things can be true and I continue to resent this phrase because it keeps being useful.**

I smiled despite myself.

**Noll fed me at the Spoon. Vessa charged me for wine and refused to charge for dumplings because she said I knew you, which appears to be a local tax category. Your second chair is better than the first one. I moved it because whoever placed it beside the table hates knees.**

There.

Proof.

**I found the Halden slip under your key. I did not read anything else. Before you become offended that I looked, it was under the key I needed to move so I could put this letter where you would find it.**

I looked at the table.

The Vey slip was still there.

Different angle.

Mara had touched it.

I knew that should not matter.

It did.

**512 days.**

**I still intend to be there.**

That line was worse.

Not romantic.

Not a declaration.

Just current intent.

The thing I had wanted from her without having the right to ask for it.

**I assume you do too. If not, tell me before I spend another year and a half making jokes about your punctuality.**

I put the page down.

My hands were dirty.

Still.

Quarry dust in the knuckles.

Slate under one nail.

I had washed at camp that morning and somehow brought North Quarry home anyway.

Mara had sat in the new chair while I was holding a timber frame seventy miles away.

No villain.

No mistake large enough to fix.

Just two lives failing to occupy the same room.

I hated it.

That was useful information.

I went downstairs without answering.

Not avoiding.

I needed food.

Also Noll.

Different systems.

The Brass Spoon lunch crowd had thinned by the time I arrived.

Noll was carrying bowls from the kitchen hatch.

He saw me.

Looked at my boots.

Then my face.

"Back."

"Apparently."

"Mara gone."

"I noticed."

He put two bowls down at a table.

"You look bad."

"Thank you."

"Quarry?"

"Mara."

Noll nodded as if these were comparable workplace categories.

"Sit."

"I'm not hungry."

He looked at me.

"Fine. Moderately hungry."

I sat at the rear table with the short leg.

The menu-paper shim had been replaced by a folded piece of thin wood.

Improvement.

Time everywhere.

Noll brought soup.

Not dumplings.

Punishment.

He sat across from me for exactly as long as Vessa allowed before pointing toward the kitchen with two fingers.

"Five minutes."

"Mara was here."

"Yes."

"You had dinner with her."

"She ate. I worked."

"Did she seem angry?"

Noll considered.

"Yes."

My stomach tightened.

"At me?"

"At road. At Vey. At quarry. At you. At chair."

"Chair?"

"You put it bad."

Traitor.

"How angry at me?"

Noll looked toward the kitchen.

"You got four minutes."

"Noll."

"She asked if you knew she was coming."

"I didn't."

"I said no."

"Good."

"Bad word."

Everyone had learned that too.

He continued.

"Then she asked if you could've come back."

I waited.

"What did you say?"

"Didn't know."

Accurate.

"And?"

"She said same."

There it was again.

No one could tell whether I should have been here.

Including me.

Noll leaned back.

"She liked room."

I looked at him.

"What?"

"Said it looks more yours."

That hit somewhere lower than the missed visit.

"She said that?"

"Something like."

"Something like what?"

"Three minutes."

I hated employment.

"Noll."

He thought.

"Said you have more stuff but still bad curtains."

"That is not the same sentence."

"Feels same."

Impossible witness.

Vessa called his name.

Noll stood.

Then stopped.

"She was sad."

No joke.

He went back to work.

That was enough.

I ate the soup.

It was good.

Annoyingly.

On the walk back to Lower Rook, I passed the Guild.

Sella was outside talking to a courier.

She saw me.

"Quarry signed the extension."

"I know."

"They want you again next month."

Of course.

The sentence should have made me happy.

It did.

That was the problem.

Not Mara versus work.

Too simple.

I wanted both.

Work that had started asking for me.

A woman three cities of paperwork away who had occupied my room for an hour and left before I came home.

Neither had done anything wrong by continuing without the other.

"Pay?" I asked.

Sella told me.

Better again.

I laughed once.

She misunderstood.

"Too low?"

"No."

"Then what?"

"Nothing."

Sella looked at me long enough to decide the Guild did not pay for this kind of problem.

Correct.

"Answer by Three-day."

"I will."

Back upstairs, I moved the second chair where Mara had left it.

Near the window.

It was better there.

Of course.

I found paper.

This letter was harder than the worried one.

No institution to accuse.

No Len to blame.

I wrote:

**I am angry too. At the quarry. At your packet. At myself for not somehow knowing you were coming. None of those are especially reasonable.**

Stopped.

Then:

**I could have left on day nine. I did not know you were here. If I had known, I think I would have come back. I am not completely sure. That is worse to write than a lie, so I assume it is the useful version.**

I crossed out useful.

Everyone was infecting me.

Wrote:

**the honest version.**

Then I told her why I stayed.

Not because I was needed uniquely.

Because I was useful enough that leaving would have made the work harder.

Because the buried rooms interested me.

Because Kessa trusted me with the lower brace shift.

Because the pay mattered.

Because I wanted to be asked back.

All of it.

At the bottom:

**I found your note under the chair. I still intend to be there.**

I considered writing 512.

Didn't.

Then added:

**The chair stays by the window now. You were right. This is intolerable.**

Better.

I folded the letter.

Before I took it downstairs, I picked up the Halden slip.

Mara had put one tiny pencil mark beside **TERM CLOSE**.

A check.

Nothing else.

I looked at it for too long.

Then put it back.

Tomorrow I had to decide whether to take another quarry contract.

Tonight I had a letter to send.

Neither decision cancelled the other.

That was apparently adulthood.
