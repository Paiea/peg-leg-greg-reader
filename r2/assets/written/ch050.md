# Year Chapter 08: Blackglass

Status: **TEMPORAL YEAR REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

`Y312 / D069 / SPRING`

`DAYS SINCE PRIOR CHAPTER: 14`

`LOCATION: Old canal salvage works, Carrow`

`RANK: Bronze`

---

By D069 the tusker bruise had disappeared, my new sword belt had stopped feeling expensive, and Mara had finally bullied a date out of me.

D128.

I had written:

**If Vey has not eaten you, I can come west around D128. Warning enough?**

Her reply took nine days.

**Barely. Green Finch still exists. I do too.**

Then three lines about a river clerk I did not know and one complaint that Halden spring had turned hot early.

Fifty-nine days remained before I might see her.

That number was long enough to feel real.

Tavin was somewhere south of Venn according to a packet from D061. Noll now opened Brass Spoon one morning and closed three nights every week. Jorren had been given his own route ledger at Meln's yard and had stopped referring to the desk as temporary.

My Guild file had grown thick enough that Sella no longer found endorsements immediately.

She hated this.

"Folder," she said on D067.

"You already have one."

"Bigger folder."

"Promotion?"

"Stationery."

Two days later Vaska found me at Bell Tile.

I was there helping Dena test a filter frame after someone over-tightened a ceramic collar.

Vaska stood in doorway with waterproof coat despite blue sky.

"Salvage."

"Greeting?"

"No."

She handed me a city token.

Old canal pumping house Number Three had been exposed during demolition of a tannery annex. City records said the lower machinery was stripped forty years ago.

Workers found enough metal still inside to prove city records optimistic.

They also found one active magical reading around a collapsed counterweight frame.

"Ward?" I asked.

"Maybe."

"Danger?"

"Obviously."

"Pay?"

"Eighteen. Salvage share after city claim."

I looked at Dena.

She waved me away.

"Go touch old wall."

My reputation had become narrow but useful.

Pump House Three sat below a demolished brick building near old canal where water had been diverted generations earlier. The entrance was a hole in street beneath temporary timber roof.

Harl the mason was there.

I knew him from one Bell Tile repair, mostly as an old man who distrusted anything built after he turned thirty.

He looked down shaft.

"Bad stairs."

"How bad?"

"City stairs."

Vaska ignored him.

Our team included Ilan, a rigger from city list, two salvage laborers named Pera and Oss, and a young clerk responsible for writing down anything the city wanted before workers could sell it.

Ilan put everyone in harness.

No argument.

The shaft descended twelve feet into a chamber half filled with rusted machinery.

Old pump rods ran through ceiling. Bronze pipes green with age. One stone channel still held six inches stagnant water.

The smell was metal, mold, old tannery, and something sweet I refused to identify.

Mana sense caught the strange reading immediately.

Not a field spread through room.

A point.

Behind a collapsed frame at north wall.

"There," I said.

Vaska marked location.

"We clear around. Nobody pulls."

The counterweight frame had once supported two vertical iron chains running up through pulleys. One beam had collapsed sideways, pinning a box-shaped bronze housing beneath.

Pera reached for crowbar.

Ilan stopped her.

"Load first."

He tested chains.

One slack.

One still held tension.

Forty years and something above remained attached.

"What?" I asked.

Harl looked upward.

"Maybe old gate stone."

"Maybe roof."

"Same if drops."

Good point.

We rigged secondary support from modern tripod over shaft. Ilan ran chain through block and took partial load before anyone moved timber.

I helped where told.

Not enough to satisfy curiosity.

Enough to remain alive.

When first beam shifted, magical point brightened in my senses.

Not visually.

Pressure.

Like a held breath behind teeth.

"Stop."

Everyone stopped.

I crouched closer.

The bronze housing had three arms bolted into surrounding frame.

At center, beneath grime, something black reflected lamp.

Glass maybe.

Not opaque.

Dark enough that depth disappeared.

I brushed dirt with gloved finger only after Vaska said yes.

The object was oval, slightly larger than my palm, held inside bronze cage with three mounting eyes around rim.

Black glass shot through with faint silver threads.

No inscription.

No Guild mark.

Old Greg memory reached for it and found nothing.

That made me more interested than recognition would have.

"Active?" Vaska asked.

"Yes. Very low."

"Doing what?"

I touched bronze cage.

The answer hit through hand.

Not power.

Relationship.

Tension from the two old chains entered the housing unevenly.

The black glass spread it.

Not equally exactly.

Balanced against three frame anchors.

"It's sharing load."

Ilan looked over.

"Mechanical?"

"Magical. Maybe both. The frame arms are feeding tension into it."

"Can disconnect?"

"Not until you own load."

He smiled.

"Finally useful mage."

I was not a mage.

I accepted compliment.

We spent an hour rigging around it.

That was the right amount of respect for an object nobody understood.

Ilan took tension from old chains onto modern block.

Harl braced cracked masonry.

Vaska had clerk copy every bolt before removal.

Pera and Oss cleared debris.

I watched magical response while each arm loosened.

First bolt came free.

The black glass warmed slightly.

Second.

Field shifted.

Third arm still loaded more than expected.

"Hold."

Ilan stopped wrench.

"How much?"

"More."

"Useful unit."

"Enough that I don't like it."

We added another line.

Then masonry above us cracked.

Small sound.

Harl swore.

Everyone looked up.

One old pump rod moved downward half inch.

The supposedly stripped machinery above still contained weight.

Ilan shouted, "Back!"

We moved.

Pera did not.

Her boot caught under broken beam.

She fell.

The pump rod dropped another inch.

Old chain snapped tight.

Our modern line took load.

Tripod creaked.

Pera pulled at boot.

Stuck.

I started toward her.

Ilan shouted, "No!"

Why?

Then I saw tripod leg shifting on wet stone.

If I crossed under line, I entered collapse path.

Pera screamed, "Get me out!"

Need another way.

The blackglass housing was still attached by one arm.

Its field surged as old chain loaded.

It was trying to spread tension into broken frame.

That frame had one anchor still in sound masonry.

Maybe enough.

Not to hold everything.

To buy seconds.

"I can feed load into wall."

Ilan stared.

"How?"

"That thing."

"Know it?"

"No."

Bad answer.

"What happens if wrong?"

"We still have your line. I add support, not replace."

Better.

Vaska looked at Pera.

Then tripod.

"Do it."

Permission.

I put both hands on bronze cage.

Opened mana.

The black glass did not accept command like a ward plate.

It accepted pressure.

My force plane touched one arm.

Instead of resisting, the object pulled the plane's load sideways through silver threads.

I almost lost it.

Adjusted.

Not push.

Connect.

I built a static plane under old chain bracket and let its force enter the cage.

The blackglass spread it across three points, including the last masonry anchor.

Tripod eased.

Not much.

Enough that Ilan saw.

"Now!"

Oss crawled low outside direct line and cut Pera's boot laces.

She yanked foot free, leaving boot trapped.

They dragged her back.

I released.

Blackglass field snapped down.

Tripod took full load again.

My arms shook.

No collapse.

No heroic glow.

Pera sat barefoot swearing at everyone.

Harl laughed from relief.

"Boot died."

"Fuck boot."

"Good boot?"

"Was."

After that, city closed chamber until additional rigging arrived.

Nobody cared that artifact remained partly exposed except me.

Pera's ankle was bruised, not broken.

We ate lunch above ground while second rig crew set heavier tripod.

I could not stop thinking about the black glass.

Old Greg had no memory of it.

That was rare enough to become gravitational.

By fourth bell we safely transferred old pump load, removed final arm, and lifted housing to street.

Without frame pressure, blackglass became almost inert.

The oval weighed perhaps four pounds.

Heavier than glass should.

Clerk inventoried:

`ONE BRONZE-FRAMED MAGICAL LOAD REGULATOR, AGE UNKNOWN, DAMAGED MOUNT.`

"Load regulator," I said.

"Need a noun."

"Fair."

City appraiser arrived near dusk.

She knew nothing more.

Old public works component. No identifying maker. Active but low. No current municipal use. City claimed copper pipes and pump bronze, not the small regulator unless archive found ownership by next day.

Salvage rules allowed crew shares after city release.

I wanted it immediately.

This was suspicious.

Not because prophecy.

Because I had felt it do something my current magic wanted to learn.

Pera wanted compensation for boot.

Ilan wanted old pulley bronze.

Harl wanted nothing except two stone wedges he said were better than modern.

Next afternoon, city released the regulator to salvage pool.

We held an ugly little bid in Stonehook side shed because Ilan knew the owner.

Only me and a metal dealer cared.

He wanted bronze frame.

I wanted the glass.

Price climbed.

Twelve copper.

Eighteen.

Twenty-four.

I nearly stopped.

My money band had improved. Ridge tusker and weeks of work put me comfortably above ordinary daily needs.

Twenty-four still hurt.

The dealer said twenty-six.

I said thirty.

Silence.

He looked at me like I had become irrational.

Maybe.

He stopped.

Thirty copper, minus my salvage share credit of six.

Twenty-four net.

Almost the cost of my boots.

Again.

I owned an object I could not name.

Noll stared when I set it on Lower Rook table.

"Rock."

"Artifact."

"Rock."

"Expensive rock."

"Worse."

He lifted it.

"Heavy."

"Yes."

The black glass caught lamp light and returned almost none.

Silver threads inside looked like cracks until I tilted it.

"What does?" Noll asked.

"Shares load. Maybe charge."

"Useful?"

"I think."

"Twenty-four?"

I stared.

"How do you know?"

"Maud."

Of course salvage economy had reached landlord.

"Maybe."

Noll put it down.

"Name?"

The appraiser had called it regulator.

Too generic.

Black glass.

Three anchor eyes.

I said, "Blackglass Anchor."

Noll shrugged.

"Rock."

I kept the name.

That night I mounted it temporarily to a scrap board with three bolts and spent an hour pushing force into one edge.

The first attempt slid the board off table.

The second sent a spoon onto floor.

The third made the silver threads brighten faintly as pressure moved from one bolt to the other two.

Not more power.

Distribution.

I smiled.

Then my left wrist cramped hard enough to end practice.

The Anchor sat on table, black and silent.

It had not chosen me.

I had overpaid for it.

That felt much safer.
