# D347: Closed

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The Brell bridge was still closed when we came back.

This created two problems.

The first was geography.

The second was Kessa.

She saw the scaffolding before the rest of us saw the red flag.

"Stop."

Vell stopped the lead wagon.

Five days had passed since the ice release.

The mill shaft now lived at Rellin Cross, where twelve men had spent most of a day lowering it through a roof opening because the mill had been designed by people who apparently hated future repairs.

Our wagons were lighter coming back.

One carried old bearings for recasting.

One carried flour.

Two carried nothing but tarps, tool chests, and the accumulated smell of animals.

The fifth carried six passengers who had paid Vell for the return road and were now learning why road schedules were a form of fiction.

Brell bridge sat ahead with barriers at both ends.

Timber scaffolding wrapped the south footing.

Two men stood on a work platform just above water level.

A third was waist-deep on the bank beside them with a shovel.

Kessa climbed off the wagon before Vell finished swearing.

"Wait," he said.

She did not.

Second problem.

Jorren looked at me.

"Go get her?"

"You go."

"Silver."

I hated rank immediately.

Edda stayed with the wagons because six passengers had already started asking whether the bridge looked safe.

It did not.

Useful visual communication.

I followed Kessa.

The repair camp occupied the south side of the crossing now.

Canvas shelter.

Stone blocks stacked beside the road.

Timber braces.

Coils of rope.

A portable hand winch anchored to two driven posts.

The bridge keeper recognized Kessa.

His face improved.

"You're back."

"What moved?"

No hello.

Professional affection.

He pointed beneath the bridge.

"South-east corner. Bed washed out behind the footing."

Kessa stopped.

"How much?"

"Three feet under the outer edge. Maybe more upstream."

There was the word.

I heard Jorren making the noise in my head even though he was thirty yards away.

The keeper continued.

"Stone itself is sound. Ground isn't."

Kessa crouched near the bank.

The river had dropped since the ice release, exposing the lower edge of the stone footing.

A dark gap showed behind it where packed gravel should have been.

Not large from above.

Enough.

Water had scoured material from beneath the approach-side corner until part of the footing rested over a void.

The bridge had not failed.

It had started becoming a bridge that could.

"Plan?" Kessa asked.

A man climbed up from the work platform.

Mason.

I knew because he wore stone dust in places river water had not removed it.

"Pack the void with rubble and lime bags, drive two temporary piles upstream, brace the footing until spring. Full rebuild when water's low."

Kessa looked at the scaffolding.

"Who designed the brace?"

The mason pointed at another man.

Kessa's expression did not change.

This was worse than if it had.

"What's wrong?" I asked.

She pointed to the diagonal timber running from the bank anchor down toward the footing.

"Angle's too shallow."

The mason said, "Bank won't take steeper."

"Then move the bank anchor."

"Ground's frozen."

"So thaw it."

"Takes a day."

"Bridge has been closed five."

Different priorities met.

Neither man looked stupid.

That helped.

The mason crossed his arms.

"We need it open before market day."

"Then you need it standing after market day."

He looked toward the road.

Saw Vell's wagons.

Commerce had arrived to make his argument visible.

Kessa looked at the current brace again.

Then at me.

I knew that look.

"No," I said.

"I didn't ask."

"Your face did."

She almost smiled.

Growth infected everyone.

"How long can you shape under steady side load now?"

"Depends on load."

"Good answer."

"That sounded like approval."

"Don't ruin it."

The mason looked between us.

"What's he do?"

Kessa pointed at me.

"Temporary directional load. Small area. Short duration."

I appreciated the absence of magic poetry.

The mason looked at my Silver tab.

Then at the footing.

"Could hold the brace while we reset it?"

"No," Kessa said.

Immediate.

Correct.

"Could unload one connection long enough to move a pin."

The mason's expression changed.

That was more useful.

"How long?"

"Minutes? No. Breaths? Yes."

He crouched beside Kessa.

Now three people looked at the same bad angle.

I stayed standing because too many knees did not improve structure.

Vell eventually came over.

"Detour?"

The keeper nodded.

"South ridge. Adds a day if road stays dry."

Vell swore.

"Road?"

"Passable this morning."

"Passengers?"

"Same."

Vell looked at Kessa.

"You coming?"

Kessa did not answer immediately.

There.

Decision.

The repair needed a rigger.

Not necessarily her.

The current crew had one.

Maybe competent.

Maybe not enough for a footing already moving.

The detour needed no rigger if nothing else went wrong.

Vell had hired her for the movement, not the bridge repair.

Kessa looked at the scaffolding.

Then at Vell.

"I'll stay."

He nodded.

No guilt.

"Contract ends here?"

"My part."

"Fair."

He looked at me.

I became annoyed before he spoke.

"You?"

There it was.

I looked at the wagons.

Jorren stood beside the lead team.

Edda was talking to the passengers.

Return road.

One extra day.

Known job.

Then at the bridge.

Two days of repair, maybe three.

Silver support rate.

Kessa.

A problem I understood enough to be interested and not enough to be bored.

Also higher pay.

Money remained spiritually persuasive.

"What does repair pay?" I asked.

The mason told me.

Vell laughed.

"Traitor."

"Commerce."

"You learned."

Jorren came over when he saw the negotiation finish.

"Staying?"

"Probably."

"Mara?"

I looked at him.

"What about Mara?"

"Nothing."

Hostile man.

"Why say her name?"

"Wanted to see face."

I considered pushing him into the river.

Too cold.

He handed me a small packet from his coat.

Halden mark.

"Was on Vell's mail bag. Forgot."

I stared.

"You forgot my letter for five days?"

"Four. Got it Rellin second day."

"That is not better."

"You were there."

"Give me that."

I took it.

Jorren smiled.

"Now staying?"

"Yes. Leave."

He did.

No emotional support.

Perfect friend.

Vell's wagons turned toward the south-ridge detour before noon.

Edda lifted one hand as she passed.

Jorren shouted, "Don't hold bridge!"

Kessa shouted back, "He won't!"

I shouted nothing because both were correct and this felt unfair.

Then the road quieted.

Five wagons gone.

Six passengers gone.

Repair camp remained.

I opened Mara's letter at lunch sitting on a timber pile.

The first line said:

**Silver? You absolute asshole. Congratulations.**

I laughed hard enough that Kessa looked over.

"Good?"

"Mostly."

"Then eat."

Mara had heard through Noll, who had apparently heard through Edda before Edda left Carrow.

Packet networks remained more dangerous than drakes.

Mara wrote about work.

Len had transferred to route accounts for three months and now hummed in two buildings.

Sera had taken a promotion Mara wanted and Mara was angry about it while also admitting Sera deserved it.

Her housing allowance had finally begun.

She moved to a better room with a window facing west and discovered the chimney next door smoked directly into it on still mornings.

Progress.

Near the bottom:

**You have 384 days. I know because I am better at counting than you and because Vey puts the term date on every pay sheet. I still intend to be on the steps.**

Then:

**Do not become Gold before I see Silver in person. I refuse to learn two new levels of arrogance at once.**

I folded the letter.

No mention of coming to Carrow.

No plan to see me sooner.

Not sad.

Slightly sad.

Both.

I put the letter inside my coat and went back to work.

The bridge repair did not become dangerous that afternoon.

That mattered.

We unloaded stone.

Drove two test stakes into frozen bank.

Built a charcoal trench around the new anchor position to thaw ground slowly.

Kessa rejected one timber because a split ran too close to the end.

The mason complained about wasting good wood.

She made him cut the split section off and use the shorter piece elsewhere.

Nothing failed.

No one got hurt.

At dusk, the river was lower than morning.

The temporary brace still held.

The footing had not moved according to Kessa's chalk marks.

We ate stew in the canvas shelter.

Bad stew.

I missed Noll's food enough to become insulting about it.

The mason told me I could cook tomorrow.

I withdrew the criticism.

I slept on a straw pallet beside three repair workers with my boots near the stove.

Mara's letter stayed inside my coat.

Outside, the Brell moved in the dark against a footing we intended to fix.

Everything was exactly as dangerous as people already knew it was.

That was why we were there.
