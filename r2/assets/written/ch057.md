Twelve days changed Halden from Mara's city into somewhere I could get lost without being surprised.

That was progress.

It was not competence.

I still took the wrong stair twice.

The first week I slept at Green Finch and worked wherever Halden Guild would accept a visiting Bronze with too many Carrow endorsements and one Silver road lead's signature.

That turned out to be more places than before.

Furniture move.

Two warehouse watches.

One escort for a municipal clerk carrying tax seals nobody wanted to steal.

Three river-yard shifts under Ora, who had remembered me from the barge rescue and immediately made that memory less flattering by putting me on rope inventory.

"You throw one line well and think you're river-born," she said.

"I did not say that."

"Your face did."

The disease had spread west.

Mara and I saw each other four evenings in twelve days.

Not twelve.

Not every night.

That mattered.

The first evening was noodles and kissing and three months of letters collapsing into words spoken too fast.

The second was twenty minutes on Vey steps because her shift ran late and I had early Guild work.

The third she came to Finch after dark, fell asleep sideways across my bed while still wearing one stocking, and woke angry because I had covered her with my coat instead of waking her.

"I had a room," she said.

"You were unconscious."

"Sleeping."

"Deeply."

"Not dead."

"Evidence was limited."

She threw my coat at me.

The fourth evening had been yesterday.

We did not fight.

Almost worse.

She cooked badly in her room. I repaired one loose chair rung. We talked about Westreach, her rent, Siva's review, and whether Blackglass Anchor needed a better carrying frame.

At one point Mara said, "You sound happier."

I had no defense ready.

"Do I?"

"More tired too."

"Road."

"Work."

"Same thing some weeks."

Carrow.

Home anyway.

"D181 packet maybe."

"That's forty-one days."

**And eat. Sera says you forget when excited.**

The problem sat three berths down.

A river crane had not failed.

That would have been simpler.

Its load wards were disagreeing.

Two side plates showed normal green. The upper rotation collar flashed amber whenever boom crossed east. No visible strain. No rope slip. No bearing noise.

The crane had been closed since dawn because Heli, the rigger from my earlier Halden work, trusted amber more than production targets.

Good woman.

Vey had six waiting loads and one barge paying berth fees.

Sera was therefore professionally furious at physics.

"We tested empty," Heli said. "Fine west. Amber east. One-quarter load, same. Half, worse."

"Wind?" I asked.

"Changed twice. Same."

"Foundation?"

"Survey says sound."

Three lines.

Side load.

Rotation stress.
