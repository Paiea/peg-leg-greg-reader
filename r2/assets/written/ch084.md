# D000: The Date

Status: **SELECTED R2 WRITTEN PROSE**

---

I arrived at the river stairs early.

Not very early.

Seven minutes.

Mara had specifically forbidden this.

I had spent a year crossing half a continent toward a date I wrote myself. Apparently punctuality had become a disease.

The first Halden barge crew was already loading the forward deck.

Crates went down the ramp in pairs. One man called numbers. Another repeated them. A third appeared employed specifically to disagree with both.

River work still contained the correct amount of yelling.

I stood beside a post with both hands in my coat pockets and tried not to look toward Vey's office every ten seconds.

I managed twelve.

Progress remained narrow.

The Blackglass Anchor sat under my coat in its rebuilt harness. I had considered leaving it home.

Then remembered it cost too much to become ceremonial.

Mara appeared at the top of the stairs carrying the travel bag she had brought into my room two nights earlier.

The blue cup knocked against something inside.

She saw me.

Stopped.

Looked at the river clock.

Then at me.

"You are early."

"Seven minutes."

"You counted."

"I am trying to improve."

"At the wrong thing."

She came down.

I took her bag.

She let me, mostly because she had already made me carry route books for Vey the day before and the symbolic boundary had been destroyed by paperwork.

"Heavy," I said.

"Books."

"Still?"

"Carrow continues to know three things Halden doesn't. We are trying to reduce it to two."

"Dangerous project."

"Emmet labeled them."

"Execution."

"You have been saying that about Emmet for a year."

"Slow courts."

Mara smiled.

That was different now too.

I knew Emmet.

I knew Sera.

I knew the Green Finch, the east desk stairs, the noodle stall Mara claimed was good and I claimed was salt with structural support, the window in her room that smoked on still mornings, and the Vey yard clerk who called me Tall Greg because institutions resisted dignity.

Halden was no longer a city Mara had gone to.

It was one of the places she lived.

The barge clerk shouted for west freight.

Mara set her bag beside the post instead of boarding.

"I got it," she said.

I looked at her.

"The desk?"

"The real version."

There it was.

For most of the last year Mara had handled whatever Vey threw between road accounts and river accounts because she was good at finding where two ledgers described the same crate differently.

Temporary assignments became repeat assignments.

Repeat assignments became responsibility.

Responsibility had now become paper.

"Halden east desk," she said. "Mixed route accounts. Two-year appointment."

"Promotion?"

"Vey refuses the word because then people ask for more money."

"More money?"

"Yes."

"Promotion."

"I agree."

That mattered more than the title.

"Do you want it?"

Mara looked toward the river.

Not because she needed time to invent an answer.

Because she wanted to say the right one.

"Yes."

Simple.

I nodded.

"Congratulations."

She looked back at me.

"That's it?"

"I have been trained."

"By who?"

"Repeated injury."

Mara laughed.

Then her expression changed.

"It is two years. Fixed term."

"I heard that part."

"You heard appointment and started calculating pay bands."

Unfairly accurate.

"When does it start?"

"Today."

"Of course."

"Vey enjoys paperwork that becomes true before you finish reading it."

"And when does it end?"

Mara looked at me differently.

"First bell, two years from today."

The words landed harder than they should have.

Maybe because my old paper was still in my room.

Firstday of Spring.

Black Stair.

One year.

A stupid sentence had turned into wet roads, Silver, forty days west, a crew, bad oats, and my hand on black stone.

I had learned not to worship dates.

I had also learned they could hold a lot of ordinary life together without owning it.

Mara continued.

"Last morning I sign out the east-desk keys, close whatever accounts are still open under my name, and then I choose what comes next. Another Halden term. Route office. Transfer. Something else."

"You have to be there."

"Unless I'm dead."

"Still aggressive policy."

"Vey remains Vey."

The barge crew rolled a cask down the ramp badly.

Everyone shouted.

The cask survived.

No one apologized.

"Will you stay in Halden the whole two years?" I asked.

"No. Audits travel. Carrow obviously. Northbank sometimes. Probably Rell. Maybe coast if Sera loses her mind."

"So I can go to Halden and miss you."

"You already know this."

"I continue to object."

"City existed before your convenience."

"Allegedly."

Mara's smile softened.

Then she said, "You don't have to make this into another Black Stair."

There.

She knew me too well now.

"I wasn't going to."

"You had the face."

"Everyone says that."

"Because your face leaks planning."

I looked at the working river instead.

Two years.

Not a quest.

Not an expedition requirement.

Not a rank deadline.

Mara's job.

Mara's choice afterward.

Her date existed whether I did anything with it or not.

That made it cleaner.

And worse.

"Where will you definitely be that morning?"

Mara went still.

"Greg."

"I'm asking a logistics question."

"That is how your dangerous questions dress."

"Please."

She sighed.

"Halden river office. East side. Stone steps. Before first bell."

I knew the steps.

I had waited on them twice during the last year while Mara finished work.

One of them chipped at the west corner.

Pigeons liked the upper rail.

There was a coffee seller across the lane who charged too much because Vey employees were trapped nearby.

Current place.

Not imagined destination.

"Two years from today," I said.

"Don't."

I stopped.

Mara rubbed her forehead.

"You just got home from spending a year doing exactly this."

"Not exactly."

"Greg."

Fair.

I thought about the Black Stair paper.

I thought about D360 when I had recommended the slower road even though it could have cost me the date.

The useful part of the promise had not been arriving exactly on time.

It had been giving future me somewhere to answer from.

Mara was not a staircase.

She would not wait in one place for two years.

Good.

"I'm not asking you to wait for me," I said.

"Good, because no."

"I'm not asking where you'll live after."

"Also good."

"I'm not even promising I won't come to Halden before then."

"You better not."

That made me smile.

"I'm saying I will be there that morning."

Mara looked at me for a long time.

"Why?"

Because young Greg had once said eventually and then I had inherited the sentence without the memory.

Because I had spent a year learning that places became real when people worked in them.

Because Mara had become more than the ordinary life my broken memory failed to hold.

Because in two years she would be deciding something that belonged to her.

The answer that survived all of those was smaller.

"Because I want to show up."

Mara looked away.

The goat from the freight line chose that moment to pull free of a boy holding its rope.

Three people moved.

The goat lost immediately.

Professional river staff remained undefeated.

"Two years is stupid," Mara said.

"Yes."

"You don't know what you'll be doing."

"No."

"I don't know what I'll be doing."

"That's the point."

She looked back.

"You might hate me."

"Possible."

Her eyebrows rose.

"You could pretend."

"You might hate me too."

"Currently plausible."

"Then we meet efficiently and confirm."

Mara laughed hard enough to look down.

When she looked up, her eyes were wet.

I chose not to inspect that evidence.

"All right," she said.

My breathing changed.

"All right?"

"Stone steps. Two years. Before first bell."

"Not too early?"

"Especially not too early."

She took a folded Vey slip from her coat and borrowed the pencil behind my ear.

I had forgotten it was there.

She wrote on the blank back.

**HALDEN EAST OFFICE**

**STONE STEPS**

**TERM CLOSE**

**FIRST BELL**

Then underneath:

**TWO YEARS. DO NOT BE EARLY.**

I read it twice.

"Legally binding."

"Only if Carrow's courts are worse than I remember."

"Dangerously possible."

I folded it and put it in the inside pocket of my coat.

Mara watched me do it.

"If you lose that, I am not writing another."

"Cruel."

"Memory training."

The barge clerk shouted, "Passengers west!"

Mara picked up her bag.

This time I did not reach for it.

We walked down the last section together.

At the ramp she hugged me.

Long enough that the flour porter behind her became visibly hostile.

"Write," she said against my coat.

"Yes."

"Open mine."

"Hostile clarification."

"Historically necessary."

"Yes."

She stepped back.

"And do not spend two years planning the trip."

"I would never."

Mara stared.

"Fine. I would."

"Live somewhere in between."

That hurt enough to be useful.

"You too."

She boarded.

The barge pushed away from Carrow slowly enough that leaving looked ridiculous for the first thirty feet.

Mara found a place near the middle rail where there was less spray.

She lifted one hand.

I lifted mine.

Then the current caught the hull.

Distance became real again.

I stayed until the barge disappeared behind the warehouse corner.

Only then did I take the Vey slip out.

Halden.

Stone steps.

Two years.

I put it away before the date could become the whole day.

Mara had specifically warned me.

I went to work.
