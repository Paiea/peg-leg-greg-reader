# Chapter 84: The Caravan

Jorren asked me to take six days north-east with a three-cart caravan.

He had already accepted.

That mattered.

He found me at the Guild board before I found the contract.

"Six days," he said.

"Threat?"

"Road. Cargo. Drivers."

"Which is the threat?"

"Ask tomorrow."

He handed me the client sheet.

Three carts.

Lamp oil, nails, flour, two crates of ceramic drain fittings, and ordinary return cargo if the receiving office had any.

Two Bronze escorts requested because thaw had reopened the north-east road faster than some of the road crews had reached it.

Nothing dramatic.

Small delays were simply becoming expensive again.

"You need a second?" I asked.

"Client wants two."

"Why me?"

Jorren folded his arms.

"Because I like knowing which mistakes I'm getting."

That was friendship, apparently.

"When?"

"First bell. East freight yard."

I checked my schedule.

The scabbard repair would be ready after we returned if the leatherworker took all three days. Neris had not booked another block. The filtration workshop had no owner meeting scheduled.

Six days fit.

Probably.

"Yes."

Jorren nodded as if we had just decided where to eat.

"Bring rain layer."

"Forecast?"

"Road."

Fair.

The east freight yard smelled like wet rope and horse before sunrise.

Our lead driver was a narrow man named Kett who had already decided the second driver loaded badly.

The second driver had decided Kett complained recreationally.

The third driver was a woman named Mora who said almost nothing and had the best wheel chocks.

I trusted her immediately.

Jorren walked the rear cart with Kett while I checked the lead.

Lamp oil sat in sealed jars under canvas.

Nails occupied two low barrels.

Flour rode high enough to stay away from wheel splash.

The ceramic drain fittings had enough straw around them to suggest somebody had already paid for one broken shipment.

The client clerk watched me looking.

"Fragile."

"I noticed."

"Carrier pays transit damage if seals hold."

"Good to know."

"Better not to use it."

Also good to know.

Jorren came forward.

"Rear axle has play."

Kett followed him, already annoyed.

"It has tolerance."

"It moves when I push it."

"Axles do that."

Mora crouched without joining the argument and put two fingers against the hub.

"Pin's seated. Collar's worn. It'll make the trip. Grease it before hill country."

Kett looked at her.

"That's what I said."

"No," she said.

Then she walked back to her cart.

I looked at Jorren.

"One driver solved."

"Two remain."

We left Carrow under a thin rain that never committed enough to become weather and never stopped enough to remove the rain layer.

Jorren took the rear cart.

I took the front.

That put enough distance between us that we could each annoy different professionals.

The first half day passed in the usual caravan rhythm.

Walk ahead on narrow ground.

Ride when the road widened.

Check the oil seals after hard ruts.

Get down again because the road narrowed immediately after deciding to sit.

At midday Kett wanted to push past a slower timber cart on a soft shoulder.

I looked at the wheel tracks disappearing into mud.

"Wait."

"We lose ten minutes."

"Or a wheel."

He looked back at Jorren as if hoping for another opinion.

Jorren shrugged.

"I like our wheels."

We waited.

Nine minutes.

Kett complained for eleven.

The road climbed after second bell.

Mora stopped us before the grade, greased the worn collar, and made Kett hold the lantern while she worked.

The axle remained attached to the cart afterward, which felt like a successful negotiation.

By dusk we had developed arguments with two drivers, one axle, and a roadside innkeeper who claimed three carts required four stable bays because lamp oil made horses nervous.

"Does lamp oil make horses nervous?" I asked Mora.

"Some horses."

"These?"

She looked at the team chewing hay without visible philosophical distress.

"No."

Kett argued the fee down by half a bay.

I did not understand the accounting method but supported the result.

Jorren and I ate stew near the kitchen door.

"Good choice?" I asked.

"You?"

"The job."

He tore bread.

"Ask on day six."

Reasonable standard.

The second morning the road turned toward country I remembered badly.

Not unknown.

Worse in some ways.

Familiar after too much time had passed.

I remembered a stone bridge at the river town ahead. Wide enough for freight wagons. Toll shed on the south bank. Grain towers beyond it.

The route sheet showed none of those things.

It showed a ferry.

I checked twice.

Jorren noticed.

"Later?"

"Bridge."

"Now?"

I tapped the map.

"Boat."

"Hope it likes lamp oil."

The road bent north-east through wet fields.

By late afternoon we could smell the river before we saw it.

No stone bridge crossed the horizon.

Only a ferry mast, a rope line, and smoke from a cluster of buildings along the bank.

Current map wins again.

We kept walking.
