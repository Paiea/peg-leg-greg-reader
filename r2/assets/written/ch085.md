# Chapter 85: The River Camp

The ferry stopped at dusk because the river had turned silver.

Not the color.

The behavior.

We came down the last muddy grade expecting to cross before dark and found the ferry half out of the water, its upstream rope pulled tight around a higher post.

The ferryman stood on the landing watching the current.

Thin bright lines moved across the river against the wind.

At first I thought they were reflections from the lowering sun.

Then one curved around a patch of darker water and kept going upstream.

Kett climbed down from the lead cart.

"We crossing?"

The ferryman shook his head.

"Not tonight."

"We've got three carts."

"I can count."

Kett looked at the ferry as if capacity had been the disputed fact.

Jorren came up beside me.

"Ice?"

"No," the ferryman said.

Another silver line passed near the landing. This one broke into three smaller streaks, vanished, then appeared again farther upstream.

"What is it?" Jorren asked.

"Lightfin. Spawn run."

I knew lightfin.

Later towns served them smoked, fried, salted, and once in a soup so full of pepper that identifying the fish had become theoretical.

I had never seen this.

A second merchant from the caravan walked down the bank.

"How long?"

The ferryman was already moving one of the mooring lines.

"Morning, probably."

"Probably?"

"They move when they move."

"Can we cross empty carts and bring the loads after?"

The ferryman stopped tying long enough to look at him.

"No."

"Why?"

He pointed at the upstream cable.

I followed the line with my eyes.

The river looked calm enough. Fast, but ordinary.

Then a cluster of silver passed beneath the cable.

The water around them seemed to flatten for half a breath.

The cable jerked sideways.

Not much.

Enough that the ferry bumped once against the landing post.

The ferryman went back to the knot.

"That's why."

The merchant had more questions.

Mora interrupted from behind us.

"Where do the carts wait?"

Useful person.

The ferryman pointed to a packed shelf above the bank.

"Up there. Keep oil uphill from the fire pits. Horses at the south rail."

We moved the caravan.

No dramatic decision. No vote.

The person who moved loaded boats across this river had said the river was closed, so the river was closed.

By the time the carts were chocked, darkness had settled over the water.

The silver became easier to see.

Hundreds of lines moved through the black current, some narrow as scratches on metal, some broad enough to show a pale body beneath the surface before they disappeared again.

One of the drivers whistled.

"Worth anything?"

The ferryman's daughter was carrying kindling past us.

"Fine if you like fines," she said.

"Protected?"

"No nets during spawn. No traps in the crossing reach. Hand line below the bend if you're stupid enough to stand there tonight."

The driver looked at the dark bank below the bend.

"Not that hungry."

Local conservation achieved through pricing and fear.

We made camp beside the carts.

Not much of one.

The ferry settlement already had three stone fire rings for delayed travelers, a roofed water barrel, and a board listing charges for hay, feed, and repairs.

Apparently the river had inconvenienced people before us.

Kett read the board.

"No delay fee."

"Why would there be?" the ferryman asked.

"Most places charge for breathing near a stable."

"Hay costs. River doesn't."

That was either philosophy or accounting.

I liked it.

We ate beside the second fire ring.

Lamp oil stayed twenty yards uphill under Mora's supervision.

Jorren had bread, hard cheese, and half an onion he claimed was still good.

I disagreed after smelling it.

"You knew about this?" he asked.

"Lightfin? Yes."

"This?"

I looked at the river.

Silver lines crossed one another without touching.

"No."

He tore the onion in half anyway.

"Later?"

"Food. Mostly."

"Useful education."

"I remembered the important part."

"Salt?"

"Exactly."

A merchant at the next fire ring complained that the receiving office would charge delay.

His partner pointed out the contract had a river clause.

They argued about whether spawning fish counted as river conditions.

The ferryman did not participate.

He and his daughter walked the cable line with lanterns, checked the lower post, and moved a stack of loose boards farther from the landing.

Work continued around the closure.

That was the thing later memory had left out.

I had remembered a bridge here.

Stone, toll shed, wagons moving straight across.

I had not remembered the nights when the crossing belonged to fish, or the family that knew which rope to move when it did.

Near midnight I woke because Jorren shifted beside the fire.

The silver was still moving.

More slowly now.

No one was watching us.

No monster had approached camp. No thief had tested the carts.

The river itself was simply busy with something that had nothing to do with our delivery schedule.

I pulled the blanket higher and went back to sleep.
