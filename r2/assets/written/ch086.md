# Chapter 86: The Ferry

We crossed after sunrise when the lightfin had moved upstream.

The ferryman did not take our word for that.

He walked the landing before breakfast, crouched beside the upstream cable, and watched the water long enough that Kett started making noises about daylight.

Nobody joined him.

The ferryman's daughter came down with a mug and handed it to her father without asking whether he was done watching fish.

He drank half, gave it back, and pointed at the boat.

"Empty first."

The merchant who had complained overnight opened his mouth.

Mora looked at him.

He closed it.

Progress.

The ferryman poled the empty boat away from the landing with his daughter on the stern line.

No cargo. No horses. Just the hull, the cable, and the river.

They crossed once, came back, and talked quietly beside the upstream post.

Jorren stood next to me with both hands around a cup of bad camp tea.

"Good?" he asked.

"They're still talking."

"Professional answer."

Eventually the ferryman waved the first ordinary cart forward.

Not ours.

A local hay cart that had been waiting since before dawn.

One horse. Light load. Driver who knew exactly where to stop.

The boat settled lower.

The ferryman checked the cable twice during the crossing.

Nothing silver broke the surface.

The hay cart reached the north bank.

Then he took a heavier two-wheel freight cart across and came back empty again.

Only after that did he point at our caravan.

"One at a time. Oil last."

Kett looked at the three carts.

"Why oil last?"

"Because if I learn something on the first two, I'd rather not learn it with lamp oil."

Kett accepted this immediately.

So did I.

Our flour and nail cart went first.

Jorren crossed with it while I stayed on the south bank beside the second cart.

The ferry looked smaller once a loaded wagon occupied it.

Horse forward, cart centered, driver holding brake, Jorren near the rear wheel where he could see how the load sat.

The ferryman gave three short instructions before pushing off.

Nobody answered unless asked.

The boat crossed cleanly.

Jorren stepped onto the far bank and lifted one hand.

Second cart.

Ceramic drain fittings.

Mine.

I walked beside the near wheel as Mora eased it onto the ferry.

The boards flexed under the weight.

Not dangerously.

Enough that I noticed.

The ferryman positioned the cart by inches.

"Brake."

Mora set it.

"Horse still."

She touched the rein.

The ferryman looked at me.

"You ride?"

"Escort."

"Then stand where I tell you."

Excellent arrangement.

He put me near the downstream rail, forward of the cart axle.

His daughter took the stern line until we cleared the landing, then released it.

The river carried us sideways before the cable took the load.

I had crossed ferries before.

Later ferries.

Different boats, different rigging, different rivers.

I knew enough to know that familiarity was not authority.

So I watched the ferryman.

Halfway across, a silver line appeared upstream.

One fish, maybe several. Hard to tell beneath the surface.

The ferryman saw it before I did.

"Hold."

Mora tightened the brake.

The line passed under the upstream cable.

Water flattened strangely around it.

The cable kicked.

The ferry yawed.

Fast enough that my body reacted before my understanding did.

I widened my stance.

My hand went halfway toward the rail.

"REAR LEFT!" the ferryman shouted.

Jorren was on the far bank.

Wrong crossing.

Mora shifted her weight left instead, still holding the brake.

The ferryman drove one long pole into the riverbed and leaned his whole body against it.

The boat continued turning for another ugly half second.

I saw three possible things I could support.

Cable.

Cart.

Rail.

I did none of them.

No objective had been called.

I did not know the load path.

I did not know what changing one point would do to the ferry.

The pole bent.

The bow slowed.

"BRAKE HOLD!"

Mora held.

The silver line broke apart behind us.

The cable settled.

The ferry straightened.

The ferryman pulled the pole free.

Nobody spoke until the boat touched the north landing.

Then Mora breathed out.

I realized I had been doing the same thing badly.

Jorren took the horse as we rolled off.

"Looked fun."

"You missed your instruction."

"What instruction?"

"Rear left."

He looked across the river at the ferryman.

"I wasn't there."

"Mora was promoted."

Mora climbed down from the driver's seat.

"No."

Fair.

The ferryman checked the cart wheels, then looked back at the river.

Kett called from the south bank.

"Good?"

The ferryman raised one hand.

"Wait."

So we waited.

Five minutes.

Maybe ten.

No one complained.

Current evidence had become expensive enough to respect.

The ferryman crossed empty again before taking the lamp-oil cart.

That one went clean.

Kett stepped onto the far bank afterward looking personally vindicated by survival.

The merchant from the night before came across with the final passengers.

When the ferryman tied off, he said, "Good crossing."

The merchant stared at him.

"That was good?"

"You arrived."

Professional standard.

We checked the cargo before leaving.

Flour dry.

Oil seals intact.

Ceramic crates unbroken as far as the slats let us see.

The caravan re-formed on the north-east road.

Before we moved, I looked back once.

The ferryman and his daughter were already talking to the next driver.

Later, there would be a stone bridge here. Traffic would cross without waiting for spawning lightfin, and people like me would remember the bridge instead of the family who knew when the river could carry weight.

Current world had restored the missing profession for one night and one morning.

Then Kett shouted that we were losing daylight.

I turned north-east and walked with the cart.
