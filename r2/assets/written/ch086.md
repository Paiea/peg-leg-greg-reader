# D047: The Trainee

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

By the forty-seventh day after Mara left, I had learned three things about Meren Voss.

She charged for waiting.

She hated ladders owned by other people.

And if she said, "Hold that," the correct question was not what.

It was how long.

"Six breaths," she said.

Better.

I stood on the East Quay warehouse floor with my left palm angled toward a brass guide rail and tried not to look at the suspended crate above my head.

Not directly above.

Professionally adjacent.

This distinction had been explained twice.

The crate held ceramic ward plates packed in straw. Forty of them. Meren had told me the replacement cost and thereby made the entire object more threatening.

The crane hook above it creaked.

The rigger on the platform shouted, "Ready."

Meren looked at my hand.

"Plane."

I reached.

Mana gathered against my palm.

The shape came easier now.

Not easy.

Easier.

A flat pressure surface formed between my hand and the guide rail, nearly invisible except where warehouse dust found the edge and refused to pass through it.

Four inches wide.

Maybe five.

Angle down.

Load toward the stone post.

Meren put two fingers against my wrist.

"Too high."

I lowered it.

"There."

The crane operator eased the suspended crate sideways.

The guide rail took the first touch.

My plane took the second.

Pressure arrived through my arm without actually touching it.

The sensation still offended anatomy.

One breath.

Two.

The crate moved another inch.

Three.

The brass rail flexed.

Four.

Meren said, "Enough."

I dropped the plane.

The rigger locked the crane.

I stepped back.

My hand tingled to the elbow.

"That was four," I said.

"I know."

"You asked for six."

"I changed my mind."

"Mid-contract?"

"You want me to keep going because the first number was prettier?"

No.

Unfortunately.

"No."

"Then drink water."

Meren turned away.

This was what praise looked like in commercial warding.

I drank.

East Quay had spent the last month becoming familiar in pieces.

Warehouse seven had the bad south door.

Warehouse three had a foreman who lied about inspection dates until Meren made him produce the book.

The alley between five and six smelled like fish even on days no fish arrived.

The tea seller near the packet office remembered I took mine without milk and charged me one copper more than the woman beside me anyway.

I had not confronted her yet.

Long strategy.

My work had changed too.

The first time Sella handed me to Meren, I carried cases and held a four-inch plane for several useful breaths.

Now Meren asked for me by name twice a week when she had old seams, awkward angles, or jobs where an extra hand who could shape load was cheaper than renting a second brace frame.

That was less glamorous than adventuring.

It also paid better than being glamorous badly.

I approved.

Jorren called it wall magic.

Noll called it standing near expensive things.

Both descriptions had survived argument.

Meren climbed the warehouse stair to the crane platform.

I stayed below with Kessa Dorn, the rigger who owned the ladder Meren had complained about for three consecutive mornings.

Kessa was broad across the shoulders, gray at both temples, and had one missing fingertip on her left hand.

I had noticed it on the first day.

I had not asked.

This counted as growth.

She looked at my water cup.

"Done?"

"Apparently."

"Good. Get under the south beam."

I looked up.

"Under?"

Kessa smiled.

"Not directly."

Professional adjacency again.

The job was not the crate.

The crate was in the way of the job.

Warehouse seven's old lift rail had begun pulling away from the wall because one anchor plate had cracked behind the plaster. The ward line sharing the same masonry meant Kessa could not simply hammer new iron through the block without Meren isolating the section first.

So the morning had become:

Move valuable ceramics.

Open wall.

Separate ward line.

Replace anchor.

Put wall back.

Move valuable ceramics again because commerce disliked empty floors.

Ordinary work had excellent pacing.

Kessa handed me a pry bar.

"Stone facing only. Don't touch copper."

"Meren said the same thing."

"Meren assumes everyone is an idiot until proven expensive."

"Have I been promoted?"

"No."

Fair.

We opened the wall.

The cracked anchor was worse than expected.

Not broken.

Pulled.

One bolt had elongated its hole through the stone over years of crane vibration. The plate still sat flat if no load moved through the rail.

Which explained why every inspection done while the crane was idle had found nothing useful.

Kessa swore.

Meren came down the ladder.

Looked once.

Swore more efficiently.

"How long?" she asked.

Kessa touched the edge of the plate.

"Could have been moving for a year."

"Rail?"

"Maybe sound. Maybe not."

Meren looked at the warehouse foreman.

He looked at the floor.

An entire confession occurred without paperwork.

"It knocked," he said.

Kessa closed her eyes.

"How long?"

"Sometimes."

"That is not time."

"Couple months."

There.

Old problems became new problems when people admitted dates.

The foreman started explaining that the crane still worked.

Meren stopped him with one hand.

"It works now."

"Yes."

"That is when maintenance happens."

He did not enjoy this philosophy.

Kessa crouched beside the plate.

"Need the load off the rail completely."

"It is," the foreman said.

Kessa pointed upward.

The suspended crate waited six feet away, locked on the crane line.

"Hook weight. Trolley weight. Rail weight."

"We can lower the crate."

"Then the trolley still sits on the bad span."

Meren looked at the platform.

"Move trolley north."

Kessa shook her head.

"Past the splice. Rail dips."

"How much?"

"Six inches."

Six inches had become the enemy.

I looked from the exposed plate to the rail.

Then to the stone post beside me.

I could hold four inches of plane reliably.

Five on good attempts.

Width was not distance.

The trolley needed to cross a six-inch section where the rail had sagged enough that moving it might load the cracked anchor sideways.

Meren saw me thinking.

"No."

I had not spoken.

"I can angle into the post."

"No."

"Not hold the rail. Reduce side load."

Kessa looked at me.

Meren did not.

"How much?" Kessa asked.

"I don't know."

Excellent sales pitch.

I pointed.

"Plane between guide wheel and stone. Trolley pushes into it instead of pulling the bad plate for the six inches."

Kessa stood.

Walked under the rail.

Looked at the geometry.

Meren finally did too.

No one said clever.

Good.

Kessa touched the stone post.

"This takes it?"

"Stone does," Meren said. "His shape just gives the load somewhere else to complain."

I liked that description enough to keep it.

"How many breaths?" Kessa asked.

Meren looked at me.

"Four useful."

I wanted to object.

Did not.

She was right.

Kessa climbed to the platform.

Meren stood beside me.

"If the plane slips, let it slip."

"Then the plate takes the load."

"Yes."

"Bad."

"Less bad than you trying to catch a crane with your hand."

"I wouldn't."

Meren looked at me.

"I would consider it briefly."

"That's why I'm here."

She put one hand on my shoulder and positioned me half a step left.

"Post takes it. You don't."

There.

Kessa called ready.

The warehouse went quiet around the job.

Not completely.

Somewhere near the front, men still rolled barrels.

A clerk argued about a receipt.

Outside, gulls screamed at fish.

But everyone near the lift stopped pretending not to watch.

I set my feet.

Reached.

Plane.

Four inches.

Angled into stone.

Kessa moved the trolley.

The first wheel crossed the dip.

Pressure struck the plane.

Harder than the crate.

One breath.

My forearm tightened.

Two.

The cracked anchor gave a small sound behind the open wall.

Tick.

Meren's hand came up.

Not touching me.

Ready.

Three.

The trolley wheel climbed.

The rail flexed toward the stone.

My plane held.

For one stupid fraction of a second, I wanted to make it bigger.

Show I could.

Old Greg knew better.

Current Greg still wanted to.

Useful disagreement.

Four.

Kessa shouted, "Clear."

I dropped the plane.

The trolley rolled onto sound rail.

Nothing broke.

No one died.

The warehouse foreman exhaled like he'd contributed.

Kessa climbed down.

She looked at the exposed anchor.

Then at me.

"Again on the return move?"

Meren answered before I could.

"No. We repair first."

Kessa nodded.

No argument.

That was the part I noticed.

Not the magic.

Not the six inches.

Kessa had asked because it worked.

Meren had said no because repeating a successful temporary solution after the danger changed was stupid.

Both were competent.

Neither needed to win the conversation.

I stored that somewhere old memories would hopefully fail to erase.

The actual repair took four hours.

Of course.

We drilled the elongated hole wider.

Set a sleeve.

Reset the plate with a larger stone bite.

Meren isolated the ward seam in sections while Kessa installed the new anchor.

I carried tools.

Held a lamp.

Mixed seal compound badly once and better the second time.

At midday we ate cold meat and bread sitting on an overturned packing crate.

Kessa told Meren her ladder was fine.

Meren said the left foot was shorter.

Kessa said the floor was crooked.

I stayed out of it because I wanted future employment.

At fourth bell, the repaired rail took the trolley back across the dip without my magic.

Better result.

Kessa loaded the ceramic crate again.

Meren signed the warehouse book.

The foreman looked at the invoice and developed a spiritual objection to maintenance.

I got paid eight copper.

More than my first full ward shift.

Not enough to buy a kingdom.

Enough to buy dinner without checking the board price first.

That felt suspiciously powerful.

Outside, Meren stopped before turning toward Copper Street.

"Tomorrow free?"

"Until fifth bell."

Noll's schedule had taught me to answer like a person with one.

Meren nodded.

"Guild's got a mill spillway inspection. Old brace gates. Two hands short."

"You?"

"No."

Interesting.

"Then why me?"

"Kessa will be there."

Kessa, ten feet away, said, "Unfortunately."

Meren ignored her.

"They need somebody who can shape under load and knows when to stop."

The second half landed harder than the first.

I looked at Meren.

"You told them that?"

"I told Sella you were becoming less expensive to supervise."

There.

Promotion.

"How much?"

Meren told me the pay.

I tried not to react.

Failed.

Kessa laughed.

"He'll be there."

"I was going to negotiate."

"No, you weren't."

Correct.

I walked back toward Lower Rook with eight copper in my pocket and my left hand still tingling faintly from the rail.

At the packet office, a Halden envelope waited under my name.

Mara's fourth letter.

The clerk handed it over.

"She writes a lot."

"You read the outside?"

"I can see your name."

"Suspicious skill."

"Costs one copper to hold after three days."

Commerce returned immediately.

I took the letter home.

Did not open it in the street.

Restraint remained inconsistent.

Upstairs, the Vey slip still sat under my key.

683 days left.

I knew because Mara had written the number on the outside of her previous letter just to annoy me.

I had refused to count since.

Mostly.

I opened the new one.

The first line said:

**The useless key opens the roof door. I am furious.**

I sat on the bed and laughed until my shoulder hurt for an entirely different reason.
