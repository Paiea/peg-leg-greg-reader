# Chapter 87: The Receiver

The first person to be angry about the ferry delay was not the merchant who had complained at the river.

It was a woman with a counting board.

We found her outside a long, low warehouse two hours after the crossing, standing beside three empty handcarts and looking at the sky as if weather had personally misfiled something.

"Vennor Drain?" our lead driver called.

"You are late."

"River."

"I have a river too."

Different river, probably.

Same argument.

The town sat on the north-east bank road where later memory wanted a stone bridge, two grain towers, and a customs shed that did not exist yet.

Current town had timber wharves, a ferry bell, one muddy market lane, and a woman named Ressa Dorn who apparently owned the right to be disappointed in our arrival.

She checked the lead cart seal.

Then the second.

Then mine.

"Drain fittings first," she said.

One driver objected.

"Flour's forward."

"Drain crew leaves at third bell. Flour can eat lunch."

The driver looked at me as if Bronze status included warehouse arbitration.

I looked away.

Ressa had already won.

We unloaded the ceramic fittings.

Not glamorous work.

Heavy crate down.

Hands clear.

Next.

Jorren took the opposite end of one crate and grunted.

"You said this job was mostly sitting on carts."

"I was wrong."

"Historic."

We set it down.

One corner had cracked during travel.

Not the crate.

The ceramic fitting inside.

Ressa heard the sound before I saw it.

She crouched, opened the slats, and pulled packing straw aside.

Hairline fracture along a bell mouth.

The driver started explaining the ferry.

Ressa held up one hand.

"No."

He stopped.

"Seal intact when you received?"

"Yes."

"Any cart upset?"

"No."

"Then I document transit damage and charge the carrier claim. I do not need a fish story."

"Lightfin."

"Still fish."

Fair.

She marked the crate with red chalk and moved on.

No one asked Greg from the future to determine when the ceramic had cracked.

Evidence did not support it.

Good system.

The rest of the fittings passed.

Flour went inside next.

Lamp oil to a locked rear bay.

Nails to a cooper who arrived with his own cart and complained that the barrels were heavier than nails should be.

"That's because barrels weigh something," Ressa told him.

Current commerce remained educational.

By midday our three carts were half empty.

The lead driver produced the outbound closeout sheet.

Ressa signed only after counting the sealed oil jars herself.

Then she handed the board back.

"Return load is wool and two machine frames. Frames stay dry."

There.

The caravan had another direction.

Jorren looked at me.

"Still six days."

"Unless the river develops another profession."

The ferryman had called lightfin normal.

I was trying to respect local standards.

We ate beside the warehouse before loading return cargo.

Ressa sat on an overturned crate with us because apparently anger had lunch breaks.

"Stone bridge coming?" I asked.

She looked at me.

"What stone bridge?"

Right.

Later history had reached for the town again.

I corrected.

"Anyone talking about one?"

"Always."

"Funded?"

She laughed.

Answer received.

"Ferry family hates the proposal," she said. "Road office loves it. Merchants love it until someone says tax."

I looked toward the river.

Later bridge memory had made the ferry obsolete enough that I had forgotten the profession entirely.

Current ferry employed four families, a rope yard, two repair hands, and probably everyone who sold food to delayed merchants.

Infrastructure did not replace emptiness.

It replaced people doing something else.

Useful fact.

Not mine to solve.

We loaded wool under canvas.

The machine frames went high and dry.

Ressa checked the tie-downs herself.

Then she looked at Jorren and me.

"Bronze?"

"Two," Jorren said.

"Good. South road has ditch thieves after dusk. Mostly hungry boys. Don't kill anybody for wool."

Professional instruction from client.

"Wasn't planning to," I said.

"Everyone says that before someone bleeds."

Also fair.

We left before third bell.

Behind us, Vennor Drain's crew rolled the good ceramic fittings away on handcarts.

The cracked one stayed under red chalk.

Our delay had cost them hours.

It had not stopped their world.

By evening the town was behind us.

The river was ahead again.

This time, silver stayed inside the fish...
