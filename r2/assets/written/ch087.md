# D083: The Hunter

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

By day eighty-three, Sella had stopped explaining why she was offering me work.

This was flattering until I understood the alternative.

"North quarry road," she said.

I looked at the contract.

"What happened?"

"Needleback."

"One?"

"Reported one."

That was a better answer.

The Guild hall smelled like wet wool and chalk. Morning rain had driven everyone indoors, which meant the contract board had three people standing in front of it pretending not to read over each other's shoulders.

I had come in for a six-hour warehouse seam job.

That job still existed.

It had simply become less expensive than the paper Sella had put in front of me.

"Pay?"

She tapped the bottom line.

I read it again.

"That's for one day?"

"If it is one."

"And if it is two?"

"Second day is half again."

"Generous."

"Quarry wants the road open."

There was always money when somebody else's stopped moving.

I looked at the names already written beneath the contract.

Edda Vorr.

Good.

Jorren Pell.

Better.

A third name I did not know.

Savi Tor.

"Who is Savi?"

"Local hunter."

"Guild?"

"No."

"Then why is he on Guild paper?"

Sella looked tired enough to make the answer personal.

"Because he knows where the animal sleeps and you don't."

Excellent institution.

I signed.

Noll had left Lower Rook before me that morning.

The Brass Spoon had added lunch service two days a week because Vessa believed Carrow possessed a previously undiscovered need for soup at noon.

Noll disagreed with the theory and accepted the extra pay.

His white work cloth had become two.

His second pair of kitchen shoes no longer lived under the cookroom table because Maud had threatened to charge rent for them.

Jorren had been back from Doss for four days and already taken this contract before I knew it existed.

Mara's sixth letter sat unopened in my room because it had arrived while I was sleeping and I had decided opening it before dressing would establish an unhealthy standard.

Three months ago I would have opened it in the hallway.

Time had improved me in extremely narrow ways.

Edda waited outside the Guild with her short spear under one arm.

She looked at my sword.

Same look she had given it in the tannery yard the day I bought it.

"Still have that thing."

"Paid too much to lose it."

"Used it?"

"Laundry."

Edda stared.

"Long story."

"Bad one?"

"Three shirts survived."

Jorren came around the corner carrying two wrapped bundles of bread.

One hit me in the chest.

I caught it.

"Breakfast?"

"Second breakfast."

"What happened to first?"

"Ate it."

Stable system.

We met Savi at the north gate.

He was perhaps fifty, narrow enough that his heavy coat looked borrowed, with a gray beard cut short and a hunting bow unstrung inside an oilskin wrap.

He carried no Guild token.

No visible armor.

A long knife at one hip and a shovel strapped to his pack.

I trusted the shovel more.

"You Greg?" he asked.

"Unfortunately."

Savi looked at Jorren.

"He always answer like that?"

"Gets worse."

Good introduction.

The rain stopped before we cleared the outer farms.

Not because the sky had improved.

It had simply moved its argument east.

The quarry road climbed north through low wooded hills, then bent along exposed gray cuts where old slate had been taken out close to the surface before deeper work moved farther upslope.

I had passed the lower road once in another life.

Maybe.

The later road was wider.

Or somewhere else.

I no longer trusted familiarity enough to argue with maps.

Current road first.

Mud at the edges.

Fresh wheel ruts.

One wagon stopped beside a farmhouse with its team unhitched.

The driver sat on the porch drinking something hot and looked offended by stillness.

"Road closed since yesterday," Savi said.

"Anyone hurt?" Edda asked.

"Horse."

"Bad?"

"Leg opened. Lived."

Savi did not dramatize it.

That made the injury worse in my head.

Needlebacks were not dragons.

That was the first useful thing about them.

No one wrote songs about needlebacks.

They were low, broad animals that lived in rocky scrub and dug shallow dens under warm cuts. Adults came to a man's waist at the shoulder. Thick front legs. Narrower rear. A ridge of hard quills laid flat along the spine until the animal became frightened or angry, which appeared to be most of its public life.

They ate roots, eggs, carrion, small animals, and apparently horse feed when commerce became available.

The dangerous part was not the quills.

It was the head.

Wide wedge of bone with two short forward horns meant for turning soil and breaking things that objected.

"Why road?" I asked.

Savi pointed uphill.

"Quarry opened new waste pile."

"Food?"

"Warm stone. Rats. Men leaving scraps."

"So we built it housing."

"Pretty much."

Edda sighed.

Human progress remained collaborative.

We found the first tracks half a mile from the road closure.

Savi found them.

I found mud.

He crouched beside a shallow print near the ditch.

Three thick toes.

Deep front edge.

"Big?" Jorren asked.

"Female."

"How know?"

"I saw her yesterday."

Strong method.

Savi stood.

"She came down at dusk. Took grain. Team horse kicked. She hit it. Driver got clear."

"And now?" Edda asked.

"Den's above the second cut."

"You sure?"

"No."

I liked him.

We left the road before noon.

The hillside was worse than the road in every way boots measured.

Loose slate shifted under weight. Wet grass covered holes. The old cut created shelves of exposed stone six to ten feet high with scrub growing between them.

Savi moved slowly.

Edda moved where he moved.

Jorren and I stopped pretending we knew better after the third patch of loose rock.

The den was not a cave.

A low hollow beneath a collapsed shelf, half screened by thorn brush.

Fresh dirt outside.

Broken eggshell.

Two pale quills caught in the brush.

Savi held up one hand.

We stopped.

He pointed left.

A trail curved around the shelf toward open ground.

"Wind's wrong," he said.

I felt almost no wind.

Another useful difference between professionals.

Edda crouched.

"Drive or wait?"

"Drive. Quarry wants today."

Savi looked at me and Jorren.

"You two stay downhill. Don't stand together."

"Why?" I asked.

"It chooses gaps."

Good reason.

We spread.

Edda went right with Savi.

I took the lower trail.

Jorren moved twenty feet left among scrub and broken slate.

For several minutes, nothing happened.

Birds.

Water dripping from leaves.

A quarry hammer somewhere farther north striking stone at a patient rhythm.

Then Savi shouted.

Not a word.

Sound.

Brush exploded above us.

The needleback came downhill faster than an animal that shape had permission to move.

Gray hide.

Mud on the shoulders.

Quills lifted high enough to make the back look twice its size.

It did not charge me.

Good.

It charged the gap between Jorren and me.

Savi had been annoyingly accurate.

I drew the sword.

The animal hit loose slate and slid half a step.

Recovered immediately.

Jorren moved left.

I moved right.

Wider gap.

The needleback adjusted toward me.

Apparently I had won selection.

"Angle it!" Edda shouted from above.

Useful instruction.

Difficult implementation.

I did not want to stop the animal.

That was old instinct.

Barrier.

Wall.

Take impact.

Meral's voice returned instead.

Plane.

Direction.

Load.

I shaped low.

Not in front of the needleback.

Beside its left forefoot.

A slanted plane no wider than my hand.

The foot struck it.

Not hard enough to stop.

Enough to change where hard became ground.

The animal stumbled right.

One step.

That was all Edda needed.

Her spear came in from the side behind the front leg.

Deep.

The needleback screamed.

Turned toward her.

Bad choice.

I was already moving.

My sword entered behind the jaw.

Old body knew the line.

Young body almost followed it correctly.

Almost was enough because the blade was good.

It cut deeper than my cheap sword would have.

The animal dropped hard onto its front knees.

Edda pulled the spear free and drove again.

Then it was done.

Fast.

Ugly.

Quiet after.

I stood with the sword low and breathing louder than I wanted.

Jorren looked at the animal.

Then my hand.

"Wall magic."

"Direction magic."

"Tiny wall."

"You are committed to being wrong."

Savi came downhill.

He did not look impressed.

He looked at the den.

Then at the dead female.

Then uphill.

"Back."

Edda moved before he finished the word.

So did Jorren.

I did not.

Not immediately.

That nearly mattered.

A second needleback came over the shelf above us.

Smaller.

Male, maybe.

I had not heard it.

No dramatic roar.

Just stone moving where stone should not.

Savi's shovel left his shoulder.

Not the knife.

The shovel.

He swung the flat of it into the animal's face as it came down.

The strike did not hurt it much.

It changed the head angle.

Again.

Direction.

Edda stepped past me.

Her spear caught the shoulder.

Jorren went wide instead of closing.

I finally moved.

Not toward the animal.

Out of Edda's line.

The second needleback tried to turn downhill.

Savi shouted, "Let it!"

We did.

It ran.

Straight through the gap we gave it.

Across the lower trail.

Into scrub.

Gone.

I looked at Savi.

"We're letting it leave?"

"Yes."

"Road?"

"Not its den now."

"It might come back."

"Might."

Edda wiped her spear on wet grass.

"Contract says road hazard. Not extinction."

There was the professional limit.

I disliked how often limits made sense.

Savi went to the den.

We followed.

Two shallow nests.

Old quills.

No young.

Food scraps from quarry workers.

A broken grain scoop stamped with the quarry mark.

Savi held it up.

"This is why."

Jorren looked at the dead female.

"Quarry feeding them?"

"Not on purpose."

"Still counts," Edda said.

The return walk was slower because we carried part of the animal.

Needleback hide had value.

Quills too.

Fantasy became much less glamorous when salvage needed straps.

I took the rear left corner.

Jorren took rear right.

Edda and Savi carried front.

Halfway down, my forearms started burning.

No magic.

Just weight.

I preferred the magic.

At the road, the stopped wagon driver came down from the farmhouse porch.

He looked at the hide.

Then at the four of us.

"Road open?"

Savi said, "For now. Quarry needs to bury scraps and move the waste pile."

The driver frowned.

"So open?"

"Yes."

Commerce heard what it needed.

By third bell, carts were moving again.

The quarry factor paid the Guild rate and complained about the cleanup order.

Savi told him he could either move the scraps or pay us again next week.

That ended the complaint more effectively than ecology.

Back in Carrow, Sella read the field note.

"Two?"

"Reported one," I said.

She looked at me.

"That's why the paper says reported."

Excellent institution.

Edda signed beneath my name.

Then tapped the contract with one finger.

"His shape was useful."

I looked at her.

She did not look back.

Sella wrote something in the margin.

That was it.

No ceremony.

No rank bell.

No one announced that I had become dangerous.

I went home with mud to both knees, needleback blood dried near one cuff, and enough pay that I stopped at the market for meat without doing subtraction first.

Noll was leaving for work when I reached Lower Rook.

He looked at the cuff.

"Laundry again?"

"Monster."

Noll considered.

"Laundry worse."

"Three shirts."

"Exactly."

He went to The Brass Spoon.

Upstairs, Mara's sixth letter waited on my table where Maud had pushed it under the door.

I washed my hands before opening it.

This was not romance.

Needleback blood smelled terrible.

The first line read:

**Len hums only when he's wrong now. I may have trained him accidentally.**

I smiled.

The second page included a complaint about Halden rain, a sketch of the east-office roof door, and a note that she had taken her first route audit outside the city for three days.

No permission requested.

No reason to.

At the bottom she had written:

**647. I know you are pretending not to count.**

I looked toward the folded Vey slip under my key.

I was not pretending.

I had genuinely stopped counting every morning.

Now I counted.

647.

Then I put the letter away and ate dinner because tomorrow existed too.
