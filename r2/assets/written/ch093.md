# D341: Brell Again

Status: **SELECTED R2 WRITTEN PROSE**

---

The Brell bridge had acquired rules.

This was progress.

A board stood at the south approach with six lines painted in black.

**ONE LOADED WAGON AT A TIME**

**KEEP CENTER**

**NO CROSSING DURING ICE RELEASE**

**RIDERS WALK HORSES**

**REPORT RAIL DAMAGE AT BRELL CROSSING**

**DO NOT REMOVE THIS BOARD**

The last rule suggested history.

The first time I crossed Brell I had been Bronze, wet, and considerably less qualified to have opinions about bridges.

Since then I had crossed stranger things.

Westreach spans.

Snow bridges.

The Black Stair approach plate with an old blackglass system under it and twenty-five wagons waiting behind me.

Brell remained a wooden bridge over a dark winter river.

It did not care about my resume.

Good.

Jorren read the sign beside me.

"Board's new."

"Bridge isn't."

"Still here."

"Excellent inspection."

He kicked my boot lightly.

Five wagons waited behind us carrying the expensive parts of a grain mill.

One replacement shaft wrapped in oiled cloth.

Brass collars.

Bearings.

Machined fittings that had taken three months to arrive.

Vell had hired Edda for front guard, Jorren for second, Kessa for rigging, and me for Silver structural support because replacing one crate would cost more than all four of us.

My newly cleared municipal-support line had existed thirty-six days.

Sella had already found ways to monetize it.

The Blackglass Anchor sat in its harness under my coat.

Kessa had not asked for it.

That mattered.

She went beneath the bridge first.

Not directly beneath.

Professional adjacency.

A maintenance path followed the south bank to the first stone footing. Kessa walked it with a hammer, sounding rod, and waist rope because frozen banks punished confidence.

I stayed above and watched the river.

Thin ice clung to shaded stones near shore. The middle channel ran dark and fast, carrying flat pieces of broken ice that turned slowly under the deck.

The bridge keeper had told us the upper river released shelf ice twice that week.

Not flood.

A short surge of water and broken ice.

Enough to justify rule three.

Kessa returned after fifteen minutes.

Mud on both knees.

Expression not worried.

Important distinction.

"South footing sounds clean," she said. "Deck repairs are holding. Third east rail post loose. Doesn't matter for load."

Vell looked upstream.

"Ice?"

"No release since yesterday afternoon. Water steady."

"Cross?"

Kessa looked at me.

Not permission.

Second set of eyes.

I touched the Anchor through my coat and sensed nothing useful from here.

Good artifact limitation.

"I want one unloaded walk," I said.

Kessa nodded.

We crossed on foot.

The deck spoke in ordinary wood complaints.

At midspan I crouched long enough to feel the east rail post wobble when a wind gust hit it.

Nothing under my boots suggested active magical support.

This bridge trusted timber, stone, iron, and maintenance.

Old-fashioned remained functional.

On the north side Kessa checked the opposite footing.

Sound.

We walked back.

"Light wagons first," she said. "Mill shaft last."

Vell frowned.

"Why last?"

"If anything moves under the first four, the expensive one never touches it."

"And if the first four weaken it?"

Kessa looked at him.

"Then I'm wrong and we stop."

No defensiveness.

That was why people paid her.

Wagon one crossed.

Edda walked the team.

Jorren watched rear wheels.

Kessa watched the bridge.

I watched all three and tried not to become an additional profession.

The deck flexed within expectation.

Wagon two followed.

Then three.

On four, an empty freight wagon with tool chests, a sharp crack sounded upstream.

Not bridge.

Ice.

Kessa's head turned before mine.

"Hold north!"

The wagon driver stopped after clearing the span.

The bridge keeper rang the release bell.

Three fast strikes.

Nobody moved onto the bridge.

Thirty seconds later the river changed.

Water rose maybe six inches, carrying plates of ice, branches, and one whole dead sapling sideways.

Nothing dramatic until the first ice slab struck the south footing.

Stone did not move.

Second struck the timber fender upstream.

Crack.

It held.

Third went underneath.

The bridge shuddered once beneath my boots on the approach.

Kessa crouched.

"That was ground."

Not timber.

I felt it too.

Small.

A shift through the south bank under stone.

Then the water dropped almost as quickly as it rose.

The release passed.

No collapse.

No broken deck.

The bridge looked exactly like it had five minutes earlier.

Worse.

Kessa went down the maintenance path again.

I followed this time because the contract now included structural support and because she said, "Greg."

Clear enough.

The south footing itself looked sound.

Stone joints tight.

No fresh crack.

The bank behind it had changed.

A thin line of muddy water seeped from where packed gravel met the footing.

Kessa shoved the sounding rod into ground beside it.

Six inches.

Twelve.

Eighteen.

Then the rod dropped suddenly another foot.

Void.

She stopped.

"Scour."

The river had been eating behind the footing where nobody could see.

The ice release had not caused the problem.

It had announced it.

Vell came down far enough to hear.

"Can we cross the shaft?"

Kessa looked at the bridge.

Then at me.

I could probably use the Anchor to redistribute some deck shock.

That thought arrived immediately.

Old S-class brain loved tools and temporary solutions.

Current Greg had spent enough time around Kessa to ask the next question.

"Would reducing deck shock make the ground less missing?"

Kessa looked at me.

"No."

"Then no."

Vell swore.

Correct.

The bridge keeper closed both approaches.

Red flag up.

Mill shaft stayed south.

Rellin Cross stayed north.

The detour added most of a day.

Nobody died.

Commerce suffered.

Acceptable ordering.

We took the south-ridge detour and delivered the mill shaft after dark the next day.

The Anchor remained unused.

That felt almost as professional as using it well.

Five days later, on the return road, Brell was still closed.
