# D349: The Footing

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The bridge moved after we stopped touching it.

That was the part I kept thinking about later.

Not during.

During, there was no room for philosophy.

Morning began safely.

Annoyingly safely.

The river had dropped another four inches overnight. Kessa checked the chalk marks on the south footing before breakfast and found no movement.

The new bank anchor had gone in yesterday afternoon after the charcoal trench softened the frozen ground enough to dig and drive properly.

Steeper brace angle. Better line. Two temporary piles upstream of the footing. Rubble bags ready to pack the scoured void once the brace transferred load.

The mason, Pell Orst, had stopped arguing with Kessa about the original brace sometime after she proved the new angle used less timber.

Economics continued improving professional relationships.

I ate bad porridge in the repair shelter and read Mara's letter again.

Not all of it.

The part about Sella finally killing my third line.

Then the part about her room.

Then **384 days**, because apparently I enjoyed being attacked by arithmetic before work.

I folded it and put it back inside my coat.

Kessa came through the shelter entrance.

"Ready?"

"Emotionally?"

"No."

"Then yes."

We went outside.

The work plan had been discussed three times.

Once the evening before. Once at breakfast. Once again beside the footing, because repetition was cheaper than funerals.

The old shallow brace still carried part of the footing's side load. The new steeper brace sat beside it, fitted but not fully loaded.

We needed to transfer pressure from old to new, remove the old brace, then open enough working room to pack the void behind the stone footing.

Ordinary rigging could do most of it.

My part was small.

Kessa pointed at the connection where the old brace met the iron shoe bolted into the footing.

"You shape here."

I nodded.

"Direction?"

"Into new brace. Not into stone."

"Duration?"

"Three breaths first. We see what moves."

"If nothing?"

"We do it again."

"If something?"

"Depends what."

Pell looked over from the bank.

"Maybe."

Jorren was not there to suffer.

I did it for him.

Kessa smiled once.

The lower work platform stood between the bank and the footing, three planks wide, lashed to driven posts and one bridge crossbeam. Brell repair workers had built it after the river dropped.

Not elegant.

Solid enough that Kessa had spent twenty minutes hitting it before stepping on.

I found this reassuring.

We climbed down.

Pell stayed above with the hand winch. Two workers stood at the new bank anchor. The bridge keeper watched the river from upstream.

Nobody rushed.

The old brace creaked when the river pushed against the footing.

Not loudly.

Wood under load.

Normal sound until it wasn't.

Kessa put one hand on the new brace.

"Take it."

I shaped.

Five-inch plane. Angled from the old connection toward the new brace shoe.

Pressure arrived immediately.

One breath.

Pell took one click on the winch.

The new brace loaded.

Two.

Kessa watched the old shoe.

"Half."

Pell clicked again.

Three.

I dropped the shape.

Nothing broke.

Kessa touched the old brace, then the new one. Listened with her fingers because apparently riggers had extra professions hidden in their hands.

"Again."

I rested.

Not long.

Enough.

Second shape.

Two clicks.

The old brace relaxed visibly.

Kessa nodded.

"Good."

I pretended the word did not affect me.

Third transfer.

One click only.

Old brace loose.

Done.

That was my dangerous magical contribution.

Nine breaths total.

No collapse. No strain beyond a warm ache in my forearm.

Pell locked the winch.

Kessa checked both shoes.

Then checked them again.

"Off load," she said.

One repair worker came down with a hammer and drift pin.

We knocked the old connection pin free slowly.

The brace settled an inch when the pin came out.

Expected.

Two men above took its weight on a rope. We guided the timber away from the footing.

No incident.

The space behind the stone opened enough to see the scour clearly.

It was worse than the probe had suggested.

Not deeper.

Wider.

Water had eaten a channel behind the footing toward the bank. Dark gravel curved into shadow beyond my lantern.

Kessa crouched.

"Pell."

He leaned over the bank rail.

"What?"

"Void runs west."

"How far?"

"Can't see."

"Probe it?"

Kessa looked at the stone above us.

Then the new brace.

"From bank. Nobody goes in."

We climbed off the lower platform.

All of us.

Pell brought a long iron probe and drove it into the softened bank from above.

First position hit packed earth after two feet.

Second after three.

Third kept going.

Four feet.

Five.

Pell stopped.

No one spoke.

The hidden channel ran farther into the bank than expected.

Kessa looked at the charcoal trench from yesterday.

Not guilt.

Calculation.

The thawed strip had made the new anchor possible. It had also made the upper soil easier to read now.

Water seeped into the probe hole.

Slow.

Clear.

"We don't pack from platform," Kessa said.

Pell nodded.

"Bag chute from above."

"Yes."

"Brace enough?"

Kessa looked at the new timber, then the chalk marks.

"For current load."

The bridge keeper called from upstream.

"Water steady."

Kessa nodded.

"Then we stop changing structure and fill the void."

That was the decision.

We spent the next hour building a wooden chute from the bank down behind the footing so rubble bags could slide into the void without anyone crawling under stone.

Boring.

Safe.

I carried boards. Held one end while Pell nailed. Cut rope. Made a small shape once to keep a board from sliding while a worker reset his grip.

Nothing heroic.

At midday, we ate bread and hard cheese on the road.

The bridge remained closed.

A merchant arrived with two carts, complained for five minutes, then took the south-ridge detour after the keeper showed him the footing.

After lunch, the first rubble bag went down the chute.

Then the second.

Each weighed perhaps eighty pounds. Stone chips wrapped in coarse sacking, followed by smaller lime-gravel bags meant to fill gaps.

Pell watched the footing marks after every third load.

No movement.

Six bags.

Nine.

Twelve.

The void swallowed them.

"Hungry," one worker said.

"River ate first," Pell answered.

At fifteen bags, packed rubble became visible near the outer edge.

At eighteen, water coming from behind the footing turned cloudy.

Kessa stopped the line.

"Why?"

Pell crouched and watched.

Fine gray sediment in the seep.

Not much.

Enough to mean new material was moving somewhere we could not see.

"Settling?" I asked.

Kessa said, "Maybe."

I made Jorren's noise again.

Pell laughed.

Then the chalk mark on the west edge moved.

Not dramatically.

A fraction downward.

Kessa saw it at the same time I did.

"Back."

Everyone stepped away from the bank.

No debate.

She pointed at the lower platform.

"Nobody down."

Already empty.

"Winch?" Pell asked.

"Leave it."

"Brace?"

"Leave everything."

We stopped.

Completely.

That was when the bridge moved.

A sound came from beneath the bank.

Not a crack.

Not a snap.

A low grinding, like stone being dragged across stone somewhere underground.

The new brace dropped at its bank end.

Six inches.

Maybe eight.

The anchor did not pull out.

The ground beneath it sank.

Kessa shouted, "Clear!"

I was already moving.

So was everyone.

The road surface between the anchor and river folded inward.

Not a hole opening.

A section of earth slumping as the hidden scour channel collapsed under its own softened roof.

The new brace pivoted. Its footing end stayed fixed. The bank end dropped.

The long timber swept sideways as the geometry changed.

I saw it.

Had time to understand it.

Not enough time to leave.

I stepped back.

My right foot found road.

My left found the edge of the collapsing bank.

It went down to the ankle.

Then the brace hit me.

Not hard enough to throw me.

Worse.

It caught my lower left leg and drove it against the stone edge of the old anchor trench.

There was a sound.

Wood.

Stone.

Maybe me.

Then I was on the ground.

No pain.

That arrived later.

First came pressure.

Enormous.

My left leg existed from hip to knee.

Below that, something had become architecture.

Kessa was shouting.

Pell was shouting too.

Different directions.

I tried to sit up.

The world narrowed so fast I stopped.

"Don't move!"

Kessa.

I looked down.

The brace crossed over my shin just below the knee, not touching directly because my leg disappeared beneath it against collapsed earth and stone.

My boot was visible farther down.

Wrong angle.

Not dramatically wrong.

Enough.

"Greg."

Kessa was beside me now, kneeling on stable road, one hand on my shoulder.

"Look at me."

I did.

"Can you feel your foot?"

I considered.

"Boot."

"Your foot."

"No."

Her face did not change.

That was information.

The pain arrived.

Not sharp.

Deep enough that sharp had become irrelevant.

My body tried to curl around it.

Couldn't.

I made a sound I did not recognize.

Kessa put more weight through the hand on my shoulder.

"Stay."

"Wasn't planning travel."

Bad joke.

She ignored it.

Pell had three workers on the hand-winch line.

"Brace is still loaded!" he shouted.

They could not simply lift the timber.

The footing still pushed against it. The bank anchor had dropped but held. If they pulled the brace free wrong, the footing might shift into the remaining void.

Part of my mind started calculating.

Kessa saw.

"No."

"I can shape."

"No."

"Unload shoe."

"Greg."

"Three breaths."

She leaned closer.

"Your job is done."

The sentence hit harder than the pain for one second.

Not permanently.

Just now.

My job is done.

I stopped trying to move my left hand into position.

Kessa looked toward Pell.

"Secondary brace from road side. Use wagon jack under timber. Do not lift until I say."

Pell repeated it.

People moved.

Not panicked.

Fast.

A worker ran to the repair wagon for the screw jack. Another brought two short timbers.

The bridge keeper had already rung the emergency bell toward Brell Crossing.

Three fast.

Pause.

Three again.

I knew that pattern now.

Healer.

Or fire.

Something people ran toward.

The sky above me was aggressively normal.

Gray. Bare branches. One cloud shaped like nothing.

My coat had ridden up beneath my shoulder and cold road pressed through my shirt.

The leg hurt enough that the rest of my body had become background.

Kessa kept talking to me.

Not comfort.

Questions.

"Name."

"Greg."

"Where?"

"Brell."

"Which leg?"

I almost laughed.

"The bad one."

"Left or right?"

"Left."

"Good."

Bad word.

I did not correct her.

The jack arrived.

Pell built the cribbing beside my leg where I could not see.

I heard wood stack.

Hammer.

Kessa looked away from me only when she needed to inspect the setup.

"One inch," she said.

The workers turned the jack.

The brace lifted.

Pressure changed.

Pain became white.

Everything disappeared for some amount of time.

When I came back, someone was holding my jaw.

Not Kessa.

Older man. Dark green coat. Hands warm against my face.

"Stay with me."

"Busy day."

He did not appreciate the joke.

Behind him, Kessa was still beside the brace.

My leg was free.

Mostly.

The boot was off.

I did not remember that happening.

I looked down.

The healer moved his body into the way.

That answered enough.

"Don't," he said.

"Bad?"

No one answered immediately.

There are silences that exist to be kind.

They are often terrible at it.

The healer touched my knee.

"Feel that?"

"Yes."

Lower.

"That?"

"Yes."

Lower again.

Nothing.

Maybe pressure.

"No."

He looked at another healer beside him.

She had both hands around my lower leg beneath a blood-soaked wrap.

I had not noticed the blood until then.

There was a lot.

Not enough to become cinematic.

Enough to make everyone efficient.

The woman said, "Pulse at knee. Nothing below crush."

The first healer nodded.

I understood the words.

Refused the sentence they formed.

"Can you fix it?"

My voice sounded normal.

That was offensive.

The healer looked at me.

Not the leg.

Me.

"We can get you to Brell."

Not an answer.

I knew that kind.

"Can you fix it?"

He held my eyes.

"I don't know yet."

Honest.

Worse.

They put me on a board.

The movement hurt enough that I vomited beside the road.

Pell apologized because apparently it landed on his boot.

I told him to bill the bridge.

Then the world broke into pieces.

Cart roof.

Kessa's face.

Brell bell.

Cold air.

Somebody cutting my trouser leg.

A room with whitewashed walls.

Hands.

Magic that felt warm and uselessly shallow compared with the pain.

A woman saying my name twice.

Not Mara.

Kessa.

Then a man I had never seen before pressed two fingers behind my knee and asked whether I could move my toes.

I tried.

Nothing happened.

"Again."

Nothing.

He uncovered the lower leg.

This time no one blocked my view.

I looked.

Old Greg had seen worse.

That did not help current Greg at all.

The boot was gone.

The lower leg from mid-shin down had swollen into shapes a leg did not use. Skin split along one side beneath a healer's closure band. Blood flow had returned to some tissue and not others.

My foot looked pale.

Wrong.

Still mine.

That was the problem.

The healer covered it again.

"Greg."

I looked at him.

"I'm Tovan. Surgeon here."

Surgeon.

Not healer.

Different profession.

"Can you save it?"

He exhaled through his nose.

"Maybe the leg. Not the foot."

My chest stopped.

He continued before I could decide whether that sentence meant anything.

"The bones below the crush are broken badly. More important, the vessels are gone. We restored some flow above the ankle. The foot isn't taking it."

I looked at the blanket covering me.

Both feet should have made two shapes at the bottom.

One did.

The other barely.

"Magic?"

"Helps close. Helps blood. Helps infection. Doesn't rebuild a crushed artery three handspans long."

Specific.

I hated him for being specific.

"Wait?"

Tovan looked at the woman beside him.

Then back at me.

"Hours, not days. If the foot stays dead, waiting makes the rest of you sick."

I knew what that meant from another life.

Different medicine.

Same body logic.

I closed my eyes.

Mara's letter was inside my coat.

No.

My coat was somewhere else.

Kessa probably had it.

384 days.

No.

382 now.

Two days had passed since the letter number.

Why was I counting that?

Because the alternative was looking at the blanket.

Tovan said my name.

I opened my eyes.

"You don't have to decide this second."

"But today."

"Probably."

Maybe.

Everyone's favorite word.

I looked toward the window.

Brell Crossing roofs.

Winter afternoon.

The bridge was somewhere beyond them.

Still closed.

My left foot was still attached to me.

For now.

Everything after that had not happened yet...
