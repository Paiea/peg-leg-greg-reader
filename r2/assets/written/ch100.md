# D379: The Tester

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The first job after losing my leg paid less than the one that took it.

Reasonable.

Still offensive.

Sella put eight ceramic anchors on the bench in front of me.

"Test, mark, stack."

I looked at them.

"That's the job?"

"That is the job."

"Eight."

"There are forty-two more behind you."

Better.

I turned carefully in the chair.

Crutches against the wall.

Left leg wrapped below the knee, stitches out three days ago, no socket yet.

Fifty anchors in wooden trays.

Commercial ward pieces from three warehouses being refitted after winter damp warped the old mounting frames.

Not glamorous.

Not road work.

Not even standing.

Paid.

I looked back at Sella.

"Rate?"

She told me.

"Per shift?"

"Per completed lot if the reject marks are correct."

There.

I liked the job more.

Sella noticed.

"Do not turn this into a competition."

"With what?"

"Time."

"Time started it."

She left before I could improve the sentence.

The anchors were palm-sized ceramic plates with copper teeth set into one edge and a narrow silvered groove across the back.

Nothing mysterious.

A good one took a stable load without the groove flickering.

A cracked one leaked force sideways.

A warped tooth made the plate sit badly and turned a good anchor into a bad installation.

The standard test used a hand clamp, a marked bar, and a warder's pressure pulse.

Clamp.

Set.

Pulse.

Watch groove.

Release.

Turn.

Repeat at second angle.

Mark.

Stack.

First anchor took ninety-four breaths.

Second took eighty-eight.

Third took eighty-nine because I dropped the chalk.

I wrote the times down.

The Guild apprentice assigned to move trays looked over my shoulder.

"Why?"

"Because this is slow."

"It's always slow."

"That is not a defense."

He shrugged.

Young enough to accept bad systems as weather.

I tested five more.

Same problem.

The ward pulse was not the slow part.

My magic took perhaps six breaths to form and settle.

The clamp was the slow part.

Open jaws.

Set plate.

Align bar.

Tighten.

Test.

Loosen.

Rotate.

Tighten again.

Half the job was asking metal to hold still.

I stared at the bench.

Then at the apprentice.

"What's your name?"

"Tero."

"Tero, find me two wood blocks, one leather scrap, and a hand drill."

He looked toward the door.

"Sella said I move trays."

"You can move those things here."

He considered this.

"Fair."

Good man.

Ten minutes later I had two blocks and discovered my first design was stupid.

Good.

The second was less stupid.

Two blocks made a shallow V with leather between them.

Anchor sat in the angle.

One copper pin stopped lateral slide.

My force-plane pressed the plate down instead of the hand clamp squeezing from the sides.

No moving jaw.

No retightening.

One flat plane.

Load.

Release.

Rotate.

Load again.

Tero watched.

"Is that allowed?"

"Excellent question."

I looked toward Sella's office.

"Get Meren."

"Why Meren?"

"Because she does commercial seams and will tell me if this is idiotic before Sella tells me it is unauthorized."

Tero grinned.

"Good system."

"Learning."

Meren arrived twenty minutes later, still wearing the gray work coat she had worn the first day I held a commercial ward plane for her.

She looked at my left leg first.

Quick.

Then at the blocks.

"What is that?"

"Potentially unauthorized efficiency."

"So you called me."

"Before Sella."

"Coward."

"Professional."

She picked up an anchor.

Checked the leather.

Checked the pin.

"Plane pressure?"

I showed her.

"Too much."

"How much?"

She pressed two fingers against my wrist and made me lower it.

"There. You're testing the groove, not proving you can break ceramic."

Right.

I adjusted.

She watched six anchors.

One cracked unit leaked sideways exactly as expected.

One bent tooth shifted under the plane and exposed itself faster than the clamp had.

Meren took the chalk from my hand and marked it herself.

"Again."

We ran another six.

Average time fell under forty breaths.

Meren looked at Tero.

"Get Sella."

Tero left at a run.

I said, "You could have just approved it."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because if this becomes Guild test procedure I want her name on the mistake."

Experienced worker.

Sella arrived irritated by motion.

"What did he do?"

Meren pointed at the fixture.

"Useful thing."

Sella's irritation deepened.

Apparently useful was more paperwork than dangerous.

I demonstrated.

Sella watched four anchors.

"Does it change failure detection?"

Meren answered.

"Not that I've seen. Better on bad teeth. Same groove read. Needs pressure mark so another ward hand doesn't crush plates."

Sella looked at me.

"How many have you done?"

"Twenty-three."

"Correctly?"

"Twenty-two."

She looked down.

One anchor sat alone.

"That one?"

"I marked a leak that was chalk dust in the groove. Rechecked it. Good."

Sella looked almost disappointed that I admitted it.

"Finish the lot. Meren signs the process. No one else uses the fixture until she writes pressure limits."

"Rate stays per lot?"

"Yes."

Excellent.

She started to leave.

"Sella."

She turned.

"If I finish early?"

"Go home."

"Second lot?"

"There is no second lot."

"Tomorrow?"

"Greg."

There it was.

The look people used when they thought they saw the problem I was avoiding.

I hated that look.

"I'm asking about inventory."

"You're fourteen days out from losing a leg."

"And sitting."

Meren said, "Your shaping arm is shaking."

I looked down.

It was.

Slightly.

I had not noticed.

That annoyed me more than Sella.

"How many before that started?" I asked Meren.

"Maybe thirty."

I wrote it down.

Sella watched me.

"You are impossible."

"Thirty is useful."

"Thirty is today."

"Agreed."

That surprised her.

I finished thirty.

Not fifty.

At anchor thirty, the plane edge fluttered once.

I stopped.

Meren signed the tray.

Sella paid me for the partial lot at a rate I argued about successfully enough to improve by one copper.

Not much.

Mine.

Outside the Guild, Kessa waited against the wall with a folded roof plan under one arm.

I had not known she was coming.

"Sella told you?"

"Tero told everyone."

Of course.

"Unauthorized efficiency travels fast."

Kessa looked through the window at the test bench.

"You built a jig."

"Two blocks and leather."

"And cut test time in half."

"More."

Her eyes narrowed.

"You look pleased."

"I got paid."

"That's not the face."

I knew the face.

I had worn it after logistics problems broke open.

After bad maps became good maps.

After finding twelve wagons where everyone else saw six.

The world had become tractable for an hour.

That feeling was dangerous because I loved it.

"I found leverage," I said.

Kessa looked at me for a long moment.

"There you are."

The phrase hit wrong.

Jorren had said something similar in Brell.

I stiffened.

Kessa noticed.

"Not like that."

"Then how?"

She shifted the roof plan under her arm.

"You looked miserable yesterday. Today you look like you want to invade the furniture."

Better.

I laughed.

Then pointed at her plan.

"Roof job?"

"Warehouse. Flat floor."

"You said I wasn't invited."

"You aren't."

"Then why bring it?"

Kessa held the roll out.

"Because I need someone to tell me whether I can test six brace points from one seated position."

I looked at the plan.

Then at her.

Paid usefulness had lasted less than an hour before reproducing.

Good.

"My rate changed," I said.

Kessa smiled.

"There you are."
