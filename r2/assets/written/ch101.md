# D402: The First Leg

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

My first new leg was disappointing.

This was probably good medicine.

It was still disappointing.

Bera Tonn put it on the workbench between us.

Wooden lower shaft.

Leather socket.

Two straps.

No articulated ankle.

No clever spring.

No brass joint.

No hidden mechanism worth admiring.

A shaped piece of ash ending in a broad foot that looked like somebody had described a boot to a carpenter from memory.

I stared at it.

"That's it."

Bera looked at the leg.

Then at me.

"For today."

"It has no ankle."

"You currently have no tolerance."

"Different problem."

"Same customer."

I disliked her immediately.

Excellent sign.

Bera was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with leather dust on both sleeves and a shaved strip of gray hair above one ear where a scar crossed the scalp.

Her workshop smelled like oil, old boots, and hot glue.

Finished legs stood against one wall.

Not many.

Some simple pegs.

Some shaped feet.

One knee mechanism for an above-knee amputee, heavy enough that I understood why Tovan had celebrated keeping mine.

On another shelf sat sockets in different stages of failure.

Cracked leather.

Warped wood.

One stained dark where pressure had clearly gone wrong.

I looked at that one longer than the pretty leg.

Bera noticed.

"Good."

"What?"

"You looked at the bad one."

"Why did it fail?"

"Owner wanted tighter fit because the leg felt loose at speed."

"Too much distal pressure?"

Bera's eyebrow rose.

"Where'd you learn that phrase?"

"Tovan."

"Poor Tovan."

"Agreed."

She took my temporary socket from the bench.

"Up."

I reached for the crutches.

"No. Hands on bench first."

"Why?"

"Because if you fall I want you to ruin my bench, not my floor."

Strong craft philosophy.

I stood.

The stump had healed enough for controlled socket pressure but still changed shape during the day as swelling rose and fell.

Bera had wrapped it in layers of soft cloth before fitting the leather cup.

The first time she tightened the upper strap I nearly punched her.

Not from pain.

From sensation.

Pressure where there should have been shin.

Weight where the foot used to be.

My phantom heel pushed against an imaginary floor while the wooden foot touched a real one three inches farther forward than my brain expected.

Everything disagreed.

"Weight through it," Bera said.

I did.

Badly.

My hips shifted right.

"No."

"I am standing on it."

"You're escaping it."

I looked down.

She was right.

Most of my weight stayed on the right leg.

The prosthetic existed as decorative evidence.

"Again."

I centered.

Pressure built around the socket.

Not on the end.

Around.

Good.

The design was crude but not stupid.

I had spent three weeks drawing load paths with Arlo and imagining clever answers.

Bera had solved the first stage with leather, wood, and forty years of other people's bad ideas.

That irritated me because experience remained annoyingly difficult to skip.

"Step," she said.

I moved the wooden leg forward.

Too far.

"Shorter."

I brought it back.

"Again."

Forward.

Set.

Weight.

Right leg through.

My body wanted ankle motion that did not exist.

The wooden foot rolled nowhere.

I pitched forward.

Caught the bench.

Bera did not touch me.

"Again."

Second step worse.

Third better.

Fourth bad because I started thinking about the third.

By ten, I was angry enough to become precise.

That was familiar.

I shortened the left step.

Kept the socket under the hip.

Let the right leg pass without rushing.

No ankle.

Fine.

Move from hip.

The gait was ugly.

Ugly could travel.

"Better," Bera said.

I took another step.

Then felt the socket shift slightly against the outer side.

I corrected with a tiny force-plane at the hip.

Barely anything.

A pressure wall.

Enough to stop the lateral drift.

The next step was cleaner.

Bera slapped the bench.

"No."

I stopped.

"What?"

"Magic off."

"It improved alignment."

"It lied to me."

I looked at her.

"Explain."

"I'm fitting the leg to your body. If you secretly hold yourself straight with magic, I fit to a body you don't have when you're tired."

I stared.

She stared back.

Correct.

Infuriating.

"Fine."

I dropped the plane.

The next step got worse again.

Good.

Real information.

We walked the length of the bench twelve times.

Not continuously.

Bera stopped me whenever the skin reddened in one place too quickly.

She loosened the outer wall once.

Added a thin leather pad at the upper inner socket.

Took it away after three passes because it pushed me sideways.

No design survived contact with flesh merely because it looked sensible on paper.

I knew that from armor.

I had somehow hoped legs would be more cooperative.

At the end of the first hour, I had crossed six feet without touching the bench.

Bera looked unimpressed.

I felt exhausted.

That combination offended me.

"Again."

"No."

"Skin is fine."

"Skin is pink."

"Pink is not broken."

"Yet."

"Ten more passes."

"Five."

"Eight."

"Five."

"Seven."

Bera reached for the straps.

"Zero."

"Five."

She stopped.

"You negotiate like a landlord."

"I learned from a criminal."

Five.

On the fourth, I made the entire length without touching the bench.

On the fifth, I tried to turn too fast.

The wooden foot stayed planted while my hip rotated.

The socket bit hard into the outer stump.

Pain flashed white.

I grabbed the bench.

Bera said nothing.

I stood still until the pain settled.

"Good," I said.

"Do not say good."

"Now I know."

"You knew before. I told you."

"Now my body knows."

She looked at me for a long moment.

"That's the problem with you."

"Which one?"

"You trust information after it hurts you more than before."

I almost dismissed it.

Then did not.

Old me had survived because pain was reliable.

Orders lied.

Maps lied.

People lied.

Bodies lied less.

That habit did not become intelligent merely because it had worked.

I sat.

Bera removed the leg.

The stump looked wrong immediately without it.

Then the wooden leg looked wrong sitting on the floor without me.

Apparently we had become mutually disappointing very quickly.

"Wear schedule," she said.

"Numbers."

"Twenty minutes twice today. Thirty twice tomorrow if skin stays clean. Add slowly. You check after every session."

"Distance?"

"No distance target."

"Why?"

"Because you'll chase it."

Correct.

"Time is also chaseable."

"Yes, but your skin doesn't know miles."

I wrote it down.

Bera looked at Kessa's notebook.

"You write everything?"

"Important things."

"You wrote where the privy is."

"Important."

She laughed.

Then pointed at one of my drawings.

The Havel warehouse hinge.

"This is too heavy."

"I know."

"And you don't need ankle articulation yet."

"Yet."

"Maybe never."

I looked at her.

"Why not?"

"Because a simple foot that survives mud can be better than a clever one that jams in gravel. Depends what life you want."

There.

Wrong question again.

I had been asking what leg was best.

Bera was asking what the leg had to survive.

Road.

Combat.

Warehouse.

Stairs.

Long standing.

Mud.

Water.

Weight.

Repair away from Carrow.

Cost.

The answer might not be one leg.

That thought opened something.

Bera saw my face.

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You invented three legs."

"Two."

"Go home."

I took the training leg under one arm and the crutches under the other.

Awkward.

Bera watched me fight the door.

"You know," she said, "most people let someone carry it the first day."

I looked at her.

"Most people are sensible."

"You could try it."

"Later."

Outside, I sat on the shop step and stared at the wooden foot.

No ankle.

No magic.

No brilliance.

It had carried me six feet.

I had spent fifty-nine years learning not to confuse simple with weak.

Apparently I needed the lesson again.
