# Chapter 110: The Caller

The moving-plane practice became useful two weeks later in a dye house that smelled like vinegar and wet wool.

No crate.

No chalk line.

No Neris telling everyone when I was about to be stupid.

Meren had me on a second-hand rate for a ward inspection near South Dye Lane. The building was old enough that every floor seemed to have been added by a different carpenter with a personal theory about stairs.

Our problem lived between the second and third floors.

A ceramic channel carried a draft ward around the upper drying rooms. One section had started pulsing under load instead of holding a steady feed.

Meren owned the ward.

Two dye-house workers owned the channel handling because they moved those pieces every cleaning season and knew exactly how brittle expensive ceramic became when somebody tried to prove strength.

I owned whatever support Meren called for and staying out of everybody else's job.

Progress already.

The upper worker was named Tessa. The lower was a man called Rol who had blue dye permanently living in the cracks of both hands.

They loosened the channel section while Meren isolated the feed.

"Weight's yours," Meren said.

"We know," Tessa answered.

Apparently this conversation had happened before.

The ceramic was longer than my forearm and narrow enough to make its price invisible until someone dropped it.

Tessa backed down first.

Rol guided the lower end.

One stair.

Two.

The staircase turned sharply at a landing where somebody had stored three bundled drying frames because buildings hated simple work.

"Move those?" I asked.

Rol shook his head.

"Can't. Wet frames. Lean them wrong, they warp."

So the workers turned the ceramic instead.

Meren stood above us watching the disconnected ward line.

I stayed below and to the side, where I could see both hands and enough floor to leave if they lost it.

Tessa stepped backward onto the landing.

Her left boot hit a smear of something purple.

One foot slid.

Not a fall.

Enough.

The channel dipped toward Rol.

His hands tightened.

"LOW END!"

Call first.

Rol heard me.

He shifted his right hand away from the underside.

I formed beneath the ceramic edge.

One inch.

The load touched.

Pressure arrived behind my forehead.

Tessa recovered her boot, but the channel was now one stair lower than where I had formed.

Old habit wanted release and rebuild.

Instead I moved.

One stair.

The plane came with me.

Not perfectly.

Enough.

Rol stepped down.

The ceramic edge slid across the support by less than a finger width.

Another stair.

I moved the plane again.

This time it lagged.

"HOLD," Rol said.

We held.

Tessa changed her grip.

Meren came down two steps but did not touch the channel.

"Greg?"

"Still clean."

That was nearly true.

My forehead had started tightening faster than in practice because real ceramic had a price and every other person on the staircase had opinions I could not control.

Rol looked at the next step.

"One more."

"One."

He moved.

Tessa moved.

I moved the plane.

The lower end cleared the landing corner.

"GONE."

I released.

Rol took the weight fully.

No drop.

No crack.

Tessa looked at the purple smear behind her.

"Who left that?"

A voice from the drying room called, "Probably you."

"Helpful."

They carried the channel the rest of the way without me.

Meren waited until it rested on padded trestles downstairs.

Then she looked at me.

"New?"

"Practicing."

"Moving support?"

"About an inch. Sometimes while moving."

Rol flexed his hands.

"It felt bigger than an inch."

"Ceramic has anxiety."

"So do workers."

Fair.

Meren inspected the channel before continuing the actual job.

No crack from the stair incident.

The pulsing fault turned out to be one worn ceramic link at the upper junction. She replaced it, tested the line twice, and made me stand clear during both tests because my useful five seconds on the stairs had not transformed me into a ward specialist.

We finished near dusk.

At closeout Meren paid ordinary second-hand rate.

I counted the coins.

No tool fee.

No specialist premium for using my own magic.

I considered arguing.

Then remembered Hess getting two copper for being professional educational furniture.

"What?" Meren asked.

"Nothing."

"You have a money face."

"I have several."

"Use one outside."

I did.

The stair support had become work.

The pay had remained the pay.

Both seemed correct enough to be irritating.
