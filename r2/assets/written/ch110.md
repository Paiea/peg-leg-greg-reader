# D646: The Seeker

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

I recognized the artifact because nobody else cared about it.

That felt appropriate.

Hearthline's contract was not for treasure.

Private warehouse survey.

Old river quarter.

Owner wanted three floors cleared for renovation before winter storage moved in.

The building belonged to Orsen Harrow, a collector with more inherited money than useful shelving.

Paintings.

Old armor.

Broken temple furniture.

Three cases of coins Nesha said were mostly fake.

Sixteen boxes labeled **SURVEY / OPTICS / MISC.**

The label nearly killed me.

Daro and I were checking a warped upper gallery while Venn argued downstairs about whether a carved stone basin counted as architecture or inventory.

"Inventory," Harrow said.

"It weighs eight hundred pounds," Venn said.

"Still inventory."

Private wealth had categories.

I stood in the north storage room wearing the field peg and holding a narrow plane against a shelf upright while Daro checked the ward strip behind it.

Nothing dramatic.

Then Nesha opened one of the survey boxes.

"Glass," she said.

One word.

I looked.

Small brass frames.

Magnifying disks.

Two cracked sighting lenses.

A folded tripod hinge.

And one smoke-gray oval of glass set into a blackened copper rim.

No handle.

No chain.

Thumb-sized.

Clouded at the edge.

I stopped breathing.

Not metaphorically.

Daro said, "Greg?"

I breathed.

The room returned.

Nesha held the disk between two fingers.

"This anything?"

Yes.

Too much yes.

My first life knew the object forty years older.

Polished rim.

Silver mounting.

Leather eye cup.

Iven Marr wore it over his left eye at Red Gate while forty men tried not to become part of a collapsing fortification.

Later A-ranker.

Before that, Gold support specialist.

Before that, according to a story he told every time alcohol made him generous, a broke surveyor who bought an ugly piece of glass in Carrow for fourteen copper from an estate lot nobody wanted.

Harrow estate.

Yes.

Fuck.

Marr called it Faultglass.

Not its original name.

Nobody knew the original name.

He said it showed him where things were already arguing with themselves.

Stone.

Metal.

Wards.

Mana structures.

Sometimes people.

Especially people if they were stupid enough to reinforce muscle with magic.

I had seen him use it once.

A fortress gate looked intact.

Marr looked through the glass for three breaths and pointed six feet above the hinge.

We hit there.

The whole left frame folded.

He laughed for an hour.

Then spent the evening unable to tell red wine from water until he smelled both.

Faultglass had costs.

Color wash.

Afterimages.

Headaches.

More if you stared through active magic.

Marr once vomited after looking at an unstable barrier too long.

He still carried it for thirty years.

Nesha turned the disk toward the window.

"Cloudy."

I crossed the room before deciding to.

Too fast.

The peg struck hard.

Daro looked at my face.

"You know it."

Wrong question from the right man.

"Maybe."

Nesha stopped turning it.

Gold scout.

She heard the lie immediately.

"That's not maybe."

I held out my hand.

She did not give it to me.

Good.

"Owner's object," she said.

Right.

Important category.

I lowered my hand.

"Can I inspect it without activating anything?"

Daro's eyebrows moved.

"Activating?"

Too late.

Nesha put the lens back into the box.

Not because she feared it.

Because now it mattered.

Venn came upstairs.

"What happened?"

Nesha pointed at me.

"Greg discovered religion."

"Unlikely."

"Glass."

Venn looked into the box.

Then at me.

"Dangerous?"

I could lie.

If I said worthless, the box might disappear back into Harrow storage.

If I said dangerous, Hearthline would involve Daro, maybe Harrow, maybe Guild artifact protocol.

If I said valuable, Harrow would price it like a small kingdom.

Four possible routes opened immediately.

Five.

The window had no exterior bars.

The upper gallery lock was old.

Nesha had shown me the night watch path downstairs without meaning to.

The survey box was small enough to move under one arm.

Stop.

There.

The thought arrived whole.

Not fantasy.

Plan.

Window.

Lock.

Watch.

Weight.

Exit.

I hated how easy my brain made it.

Venn was still waiting.

"Potentially valuable," I said.

Everyone became quieter.

Correct answer.

Daro asked, "How potentially?"

I looked at the smoke-gray disk.

Iven Marr had once told me he would sell his house before selling Faultglass.

At the time his house had contained three children and a wife who hated me.

Useful scale.

"Very."

Nesha closed the box.

"Explain."

"Not here."

"Why?"

I looked toward the stair.

Harrow downstairs.

His warehouse.

His property.

"Because I don't know what he knows."

Venn studied me.

Then nodded.

"Finish structure first."

Gold.

Of course.

We did.

That was the worst part.

For two hours I knew Faultglass sat fifteen feet away while we measured gallery sag and shifted two cabinets off an overloaded beam.

I did not touch the box.

I thought about it constantly.

Current Greg had spent a year learning to respect present facts.

Old Greg knew one present fact nobody else in the building understood.

That disk would become extraordinary.

Not maybe.

Would.

Unless I changed it.

There was the poison inside future knowledge.

Knowing value before the market did not create ownership.

It created temptation.

At lunch Venn brought Harrow upstairs.

Orsen Harrow was perhaps sixty, soft around the middle, dressed too well for dust, with one cuff already ruined by the warehouse.

He looked into the survey box.

"That lot?"

"Greg thinks one lens may be significant," Venn said.

Harrow looked at me.

"Which?"

No route left where I pretended ignorance.

I pointed.

He picked it up casually.

Too casually.

My whole body wanted to take it from him.

"This?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I know of a similar piece."

True.

"From where?"

"Later collection."

Bad phrase.

Daro looked at me.

I corrected.

"A private collection I knew."

Also true in the most criminal possible direction.

Harrow held the glass to the light.

"My father bought that box from a surveyor's widow. Twenty years ago, perhaps. None of it works."

There.

Current acquisition history.

Not Marr's story.

Older.

"Has anyone tested this piece with mana?"

"Probably."

"Who?"

Harrow frowned.

"I don't inventory servants from twenty years ago."

Of course.

"Is it for sale?" I asked.

Venn looked at me.

Nesha looked at me.

Daro stopped pretending to inspect the shelf.

Harrow smiled.

Collector smile.

The moment someone wanted an object, it became more beautiful.

"No."

"You said it doesn't work."

"Then why do you want it?"

Fair.

"Because I think it might."

"Then definitely no."

Private wealth remained efficient.

He returned the lens to the box himself.

Closed the lid.

"If you determine what it is, send me a note."

Then he left.

Nesha waited until his footsteps disappeared.

"You are making a face."

"I have one."

"The expensive face."

Daro said, "How much?"

"I don't know."

"You do."

"Not in current money."

Silence.

Fuck.

Venn looked at me carefully.

Not suspicion exactly.

He knew I had strange knowledge.

Not why.

Most people around me had learned not to pull every loose thread.

"Is it dangerous enough that Harrow needs warning?" he asked.

Good question.

I thought about Marr's color wash.

The nausea.

The possibility of staring into unstable magic.

"If activated without knowing what it does, yes. Probably not explosively."

"Then tell him that much."

I nodded.

Nesha asked, "You want it?"

There was no useful lie left.

"Yes."

"Badly?"

I looked at the closed box.

My mind still held the window route.

The lock.

The watch path.

"Yes."

Nesha smiled without humor.

"Good to know."

That sentence followed me home.

Not accusation.

Inventory.

At Lower Rook I took off the walking leg, washed the socket liner, ate Noll's fish stew, and did not open Kessa's notebook for almost an hour.

Then I did.

One clean page.

I wrote:

**Faultglass. Orsen Harrow warehouse. Survey box.**

Then everything I remembered.

Iven Marr.

Fourteen copper estate lot.

Color loss.

Stress visibility.

Ward strain.

Living reinforcement.

Headaches.

Unknown activation method.

Unknown original limits.

Then, beneath it:

**Not mine.**

I stared at those two words.

Added a third line.

**Yet?**

That was worse.

I did not cross it out.
