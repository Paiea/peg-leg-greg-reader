# Chapter 112: The Lime Road

Lime dust got everywhere.

This became the defining fact of the escort before any danger appeared.

Three carts left Hollen Brick's north yard under canvas before first bell.

Covered barrels on the first two. Bagged lime and tools on the third.

Two drivers, a spare teamster riding the rear board, Jorren at the back, me at the front.

The yard foreman had given us one final instruction while checking the seals.

"If thieves want bags, let the drivers decide cargo. If they want horses, make noise. If they want people, do your actual job."

Specific enough.

"And lime?" Jorren asked.

"Do not breathe it."

Also useful.

The first mile proved the canvas was decorative.

Every rut shook a pale line from somewhere inside the second cart. By breakfast the rear driver's coat had a white shoulder. By midday my boots looked older.

Jorren wiped dust from his sleeve.

"Invisible cargo would be better."

"Harder to inventory."

"Worth it."

The road south-east stayed busy enough that thieves would have needed ambition.

We passed two grain wagons, a wool cart, one road crew replacing marker posts, and a family moving three goats in a vehicle designed for fewer goats.

The goats objected to us personally.

No one else did.

At midday we stopped beside a toll shelter and checked the carts.

The drivers checked wheel hubs first.

Jorren checked rear tie-downs.

I walked the road edge because the ditch provided better cover than any hypothetical thief deserved.

Nothing.

We ate bread with lime dust on it anyway.

"Crunchy," Jorren said.

"Minerals."

"You are why people distrust Guild advice."

We camped the first night at a roadside inn whose stable roof leaned enough that the carts stayed outside under their own canvas.

One driver slept beside them.

Jorren took first watch because he claimed I snored after road days.

This was false.

Probably.

Nothing happened.

No thieves tested the contract. No creature investigated the lime. One fox crossed the far ditch and decided we were boring.

Second morning, the road narrowed through low fields where thaw water still sat in wheel ruts.

The carts slowed.

Lime dust became mud paste along the rims.

Near midday I smelled something hot.

Not fire exactly.

Wood and iron rubbed too hard.

I looked toward the lead cart.

Nothing obvious.

Jorren called from behind me.

"STOP."

The drivers stopped all three carts.

By the time I reached the second, a thin line of smoke had started at the left rear wheel.

The driver was already crouched beside it.

"Rim."

He touched near the hub with the back of two fingers, swore, and pulled his hand away.

The iron rim had loosened against dry wood and started heating the wheel around one section.

I had noticed the smell.

Jorren had noticed the smoke.

The driver understood the wheel.

Reasonable division of labor.

"Water?" I asked.

"Not yet."

He looked at the cargo.

"Need weight off."

Two covered barrels sat almost directly above the bad wheel.

The spare teamster untied the canvas.

"These two."

Jorren and I moved to help.

The driver stopped us.

"Barrel hooks first."

Right.

He carried two short iron hooks for exactly this problem.

We worked one barrel down onto a timber skid, then the second.

Heavy enough that my shoulders immediately remembered the north-east millstone job and complained about comparison.

Neither barrel leaked.

The cart rose slightly on its springs.

The driver checked the wheel again.

"Now water. Slow."

The spare teamster poured from a bucket in small amounts instead of throwing the whole thing on hot iron.

Steam lifted.

The wheel smelled worse before it smelled better.

"Can we tighten it here?" Jorren asked.

The driver shook his head.

"Not properly. Need wheelwright."

Nearest hamlet was less than two miles ahead.

The road behind us held three carts, two barrels on the shoulder, and enough pale dust to advertise exactly where we had stopped.

"I'll go," the spare teamster said.

He took one horse and rode ahead.

Then we waited.

That became the job.

The drivers worked around the wheel without pretending they could finish it.

Jorren stayed where he could see the road behind us.

I took the forward ditch and the field gate opposite.

A farm cart passed after twenty minutes.

The farmer slowed.

"Wheel?"

"Rim," our driver said.

The farmer nodded with the sympathy of someone who had paid for wheels before and kept going.

An hour later a pair of boys came down the ditch carrying fishing poles.

They looked at the unloaded barrels.

I looked at them.

They kept walking.

Ditch thieves, technically.

Wrong ditch.

Wrong career stage.

Jorren caught my eye and shook his head before I could say anything.

We ate the rest of the bread.

The lime dust found that too.

The escort fee had not purchased a fight.

It had purchased two people who remained with the cargo while the drivers solved a wheel problem that belonged to drivers.

By midafternoon the wheel had cooled enough to stop smoking, but the rim was visibly wrong against the wood.

No one suggested moving anyway.

That may have been the most professional decision of the day.

The wheelwright arrived near fourth bell in a small cart full of clamps, wedges, and the expression of a person who had been promised an easy roadside job by someone who did not do roadside jobs.

He climbed down, looked once at the wheel, then at the two barrels sitting beside us.

"Good. You unloaded."

The driver pointed at the rim.

"Can you save it?"

The wheelwright crouched.

"Probably."

Nobody asked him to define probably.

He opened his tool chest.

Jorren settled onto one of the unloaded skids and watched the road.

I moved back to the forward ditch.

The client had lost half a day.

We still had three carts, all the lime, all the horses, and no reason to hurry a man holding our second wheel together.

So we waited while he worked.
