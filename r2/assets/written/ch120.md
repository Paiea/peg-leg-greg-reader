# Candidate Chapter 120: Dead Inventory

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Pera said my walking leg was fine.

Then she charged me to fix it.

I considered this inconsistent.

She did not.

"Walk."

I stood from the fitting bench.

Six steps to the front door.

Turn.

Six back.

The shop floor was dark wood polished smooth by people learning where their weight went now.

My walking foot struck harder than my right shoe.

Wood.

Leather.

Wood.

Leather.

Pera watched my hips instead of the leg.

I had learned this was rude only socially.

Professionally, apparently, people were allowed to stare at whatever part of you was making their work harder.

"Again," she said.

I walked again.

She stopped me halfway back.

"Slow."

"I am walking."

"You're escaping."

"From what?"

"Me."

Reasonable.

I slowed.

The outer edge of the socket pressed earlier than I wanted when I loaded left.

Not pain.

Not even discomfort yet.

Just information.

Pera saw my mouth change.

"There."

I said nothing.

"There," she repeated.

"You enjoy being correct."

"Pays better than being pleasant."

She crouched beside the socket.

Two fingers pressed the leather edge while I shifted my weight.

"You're rolling out."

"Wet stone."

"Your wet stone isn't here."

"Started in rain two days ago."

"Then your body kept it."

"Bad habit."

"Yes."

I disliked professionals who answered too quickly.

Pera stood and pulled a stick of pale chalk from behind one ear.

Her shop smelled like leather, boiled glue, lamp oil, and the bitter wood treatment she used on feet that had to survive sweat, rain, stairs, mud, and owners with bad judgment.

Three finished legs hung from pegs behind the counter.

One was plain ash with a dark leather socket.

One had a metal ankle joint more complicated than mine and probably more expensive than my room.

One was short, child-sized, painted green from knee to foot.

A matching brace sat half-laced on the worktable beside it.

I looked away before interest became inspection.

Pera marked the outer cup of my socket.

"Liner is fine. Cup is catching late."

"How late?"

She looked up.

"Enough that you noticed."

"Useful measurement."

"You want numbers, buy measuring tools."

"You have measuring tools."

"They're mine."

Fair.

She drew another chalk mark.

"I widen this. Add a little more capture outside. Maybe shave here."

"Maybe?"

"Leather changes when I cut it."

"Concerning philosophy."

"So does skin."

More concerning.

"Cost?"

Pera named it.

I stared at her.

"Robbery."

"You came back."

"Extortion through customer retention."

"Exactly."

She did not smile.

Pera almost never smiled while negotiating. This made every joke feel like I had paid extra for it.

"How long?"

"Two hours."

"Two?"

"Could be one if you want it wrong."

"No."

"Then two."

I looked at my walking leg.

At the chalk.

At the street beyond the window.

"What am I supposed to do for two hours?"

Pera followed my eyes.

"Halden exists."

Suspicious claim.

She pointed toward the rack behind me.

"Use the field leg."

"City stone."

"It survives rocks."

"It announces itself."

"So do you."

Unnecessary.

I changed behind the curtain.

Field peg.

Shorter foot profile.

Harder contact.

Less graceful on flat stone.

Much better when the ground stopped pretending to be flat.

When I came back out, Pera had already taken the walking leg apart enough that asking for it back would require surrendering dignity.

"Two hours," she said.

"I heard you."

"Come back in two."

"Still heard."

"Not one."

I left.

The field peg made Halden sound different.

No soft heel roll.

No imitation of a normal shoe.

The wooden end met stone with a compact knock that traveled up through the socket.

Knock.

Right shoe.

Knock.

Right shoe.

People looked sometimes.

Most didn't.

Halden had river crews, furnace workers, glass shops, veterans, caravan guards, dock accidents, crush injuries, burns, bad winters, and enough people carrying visible evidence of work that one missing leg did not qualify as public entertainment.

Good city.

Annoying hills.

Halfway down the slope, a cooper rolled two barrels across a plank laid over three shallow steps. I waited for him, then used the same plank.

He saw me coming and shifted one barrel aside.

"Leave it?" he asked.

"Please."

That was the whole conversation.

The plank bowed under me less than it had under the barrel.

Someone had painted narrow green lines along both edges, probably a cheap traction charm because the wood felt dry despite the street damp.

I did not stop to inspect it.

Halden had apparently survived without my analysis for several hundred years.

Promising.

I followed the market slope because it was there.

Not because I needed anything.

This remained true for almost a block.

A woman sold copper cooking hooks from a tray hung across her chest.

A boy had three charm beetles in glass jars, all of them producing different colors when he tapped the lids.

A cobbler displayed six left boots and two right.

I stopped.

"Why?"

The cobbler looked at the boots.

"Right feet buy faster."

"That cannot be the entire explanation."

"People with two feet wear the right one out more."

"Do they?"

"No."

I stared.

He shrugged.

"Shipment error."

Better.

I kept walking.

The Lantern Rows started where two old warehouses had been joined by an iron roof.

Ward lamps hung from the trusses overhead.

Most were dark in daylight.

A few burned blue-white over stalls selling things that apparently became dangerous if inspected under ordinary sun.

Glass tubes.

Fine ward needles.

Copper inscription strips.

Bone pins.

Powdered shell in stoppered jars.

Monster teeth sorted into little trays by size.

One crate held black feathers beneath a sign painted in aggressive red letters:

**NOT WYRM**

I stopped.

The vendor looked up from his stool.

"They're not."

"I didn't ask."

"You looked."

"What are they?"

"Marsh drake."

"Then why not write marsh drake?"

"People stop for the sign."

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

I had stopped.

"Sound business," I said.

"Two copper a bundle."

"I don't need feathers."

"Neither did you need the answer."

Also sound.

I moved on.

A woman two stalls down sold cracked scrying mirrors by the piece.

Not repaired.

Not useful as mirrors.

She had sorted them by color.

Someone else sold military buckles, old shoulder plates, ward stakes, three short blades without grips, and a box marked **CHEAP BECAUSE SOMETHING IS WRONG**.

I appreciated clarity.

I looked inside.

Bent clasps.

Miscast rings.

Two charm housings with the cores removed.

A little iron disk that buzzed when I touched it.

"Don't," the vendor said.

I removed my finger.

"What is it?"

"Buzzes."

"I established that."

"That's why it's cheap."

He was reading a broadsheet and clearly had no interest in improving his business.

I respected that too.

Then I saw the cuff.

Dark brass.

Six narrow bands nested inside one another.

Old enough that the hinge had gone almost black in the corners.

The inner edges were polished bright from use.

Not decorative.

Nothing about it wanted to be decorative.

I picked it up.

The vendor lowered his paper.

"That one costs more."

"Because something isn't wrong?"

"Because enough things are right."

The bands shifted when I turned the cuff in my hand.

Each inner ring carried a shallow groove.

Mana guide.

Pattern reinforcement.

Not a casting tool exactly.

Training architecture.

I had seen the type before.

Not this cuff.

The type.

Structural corps.

Siege crews.

People who needed the same brace, same catch, same load plane fast enough that creativity became less valuable than certainty.

Setter's cuff.

Useful.

Dangerous mostly in the boring way.

Do one thing often enough and the body starts helping.

Do one thing through guided channels for years and sometimes the body becomes very helpful.

Then less helpful when asked for something else.

I had known people in my first life who loved them.

I had known people who refused to touch one.

I had belonged to the second group.

The vendor watched my face.

"You know it."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know the price makes sense."

"Those statements are unrelated."

He named the price.

I laughed.

Not politely.

He waited.

"You can keep it."

"Old corps piece."

"Obsolete."

"Rare."

"So is plague."

"Plague doesn't come with inspection."

"Inspection?"

"Guild bench-tested last winter. Bands answer. Takes charge. Hinge holds."

"Card?"

"Extra."

"Then it doesn't come with inspection."

"It comes with the knowledge that inspection exists."

"Very generous."

I set the cuff down.

The metal left a faint warm line across my palm.

Probably sun.

Probably.

"Half."

"No."

"Then enjoy owning history."

"Three quarters."

"Card included."

I stopped.

Different problem.

"Three quarters."

"Why are you negotiating if you aren't buying?"
