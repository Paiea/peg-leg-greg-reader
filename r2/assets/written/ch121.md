# Candidate Chapter 121: Six Millimeters

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

The Guild board had seven jobs I could do and three that would pay enough to matter.

This should have simplified things.

It did not.

One was a two-day road survey north of Halden. Good pay. Bad timing with Mara's five-day decision window.

One was dock reinforcement after a barge strike. Better pay. Required a licensed water-ward specialist I did not have.

One was a warehouse wall that had moved six millimeters since spring.

I took the wall.

The clerk behind the board looked at the slip, then at me.

"You sure?"

"Why?"

"Owner has been in three times."

"About the wall?"

"About everything."

Ah.

Risk premium not listed.

"Still taking it."

She stamped the assignment.

"River Glass Storage, south warehouse row. Ask for Bren Tal."

"Owner?"

"Unfortunately."

Promising.

The walk took twenty minutes with the new socket fit and thirty because I stopped for food.

This was responsible planning.

The bread stall near the lower bridge sold flat rounds filled with onion, egg, and something green the vendor called riverleaf.

I bought one.

The riverleaf tasted like wet parsley had developed ambition.

Acceptable.

I ate half while walking and put the rest in paper inside my bag.

River Glass Storage occupied an old stone warehouse two streets back from the docks.

Three floors.

Tall loading arch.

Blue-painted shutters.

A line of padded wagons outside waiting for crates.

The building looked fine from fifty yards.

This was why buildings were dishonest.

A thin crack started above the west loading window and disappeared under the eave.

At ground level, the south pier sat a fraction out from the wall plane.

Not six millimeters from here.

Maybe four.

Close enough to be irritating.

A man in a good brown coat met me before I reached the door.

"Guild?"

"Greg."

"Bren Tal."

His handshake lasted exactly as long as required to establish that both of us regretted it.

"The wall moved six millimeters," Bren said.

"I read the slip."

"Since spring."

"Also on the slip."

"It isn't supposed to move."

"Everything moves."

Bren stared at me.

I looked at the crack.

"Walls shouldn't."

"Slowly is usually allowed."

His stare improved.

I pointed up.

"This is not slowly enough."

That helped.

Bren led me inside.

The warehouse smelled like straw packing, damp stone, lamp oil, and hot glass from somewhere farther down the block.

River glass sat in padded wooden crates stacked three rows high along the interior wall.

Some held flat window sheets.

Others carried thick green bottles nested in straw.

One crate had a red painted hand on the side.

"What is that?" I asked.

Bren followed my eyes.

"Unstable color batch."

"Meaning?"

"If it breaks, don't lick it."

I looked at him.

"Was that happening enough to require instruction?"

"Dockworkers exist."

Fair.

A foreman came down the aisle carrying a ledger.

Shorter than Bren. Rolled sleeves. Gray beard cut close enough not to catch on work.

"This the Silver?" he asked.

"Greg," I said.

"Tovan Bell."

Not my Tovan.

Different face. Different city. Common names remained inconvenient.

Tovan Bell looked at the walking leg, then my short staff, then the chalk case on my belt.

"You want shelves cleared?"

Bren said, "He hasn't inspected yet."

Tovan said, "Shelves are the problem."

I looked at him.

"Why?"

"Started moving after they went in."

Bren's mouth tightened.

"They passed inspection."

"I did not say they were installed wrong," Tovan said. "I said the wall moved after."

Better distinction than most clients managed.

I liked him immediately.

Bren noticed.

"You two done?"

"No," I said.

Tovan smiled.

Bren did not.

Excellent start.

I spent the first twenty minutes not using magic.

Crack above the west window.

Mortar dust below the south pier.

Loading floor slightly low toward the river side.

No new water stain.

No heat damage.

No visible ward line failure.

I pressed the staff butt against three floor seams and listened through wood.

Mostly because it was easier than kneeling repeatedly.

Solid.

Old.

The floor had settled before the shelves existed.

That mattered.

"When were these installed?" I asked.

Tovan looked at his ledger.

"Late winter."

"Who?"

"Brosan's crew."

"Bolted where?"

Tovan pointed.

The new shelves were iron framed, tall enough for three crate levels, and anchored into the wall every six feet.

I put one hand on the nearest upright.

"Unload this bay."

Bren said, "Why?"

"Because I want to see behind it."

"How long?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"What I see behind it."

"How long to see that?"

I looked at him.

Tovan turned away before his face became evidence.

"Longer if you keep asking me how long."

Bren inhaled through his nose.

He left.

Tovan waited until the office door closed.

"He owns the building."

"I guessed."

"His father built half the river row."

"That explains the personal relationship with masonry."

"Don't say that where he can hear you."

"I assumed walls had already told him."

Tovan laughed once and called four workers over.

They unloaded the top crate row first.

No heroics.

Two people per small crate.

Four for the heavy green bottle loads.

One worker checked the red-hand crate and said, "Not me."

Tovan assigned someone else without argument.

Apparently the warning system worked.

When the shelf bay cleared, the anchor pattern became obvious.

New iron straps entered old mortar rather than the deeper stone joints.

That was not automatically wrong.

Cheap, maybe.

The problem was lower.

The floor had settled toward the river.

The shelf frames followed the floor.

The wall anchors did not.

Every loaded cycle turned the shelving into a slow lever.

Down at the front.

Inward at the wall.

Tiny movement.

Repeated hundreds of times.

Six millimeters eventually.

I ran my fingers over one anchor plate.

Mortar had powdered around the bottom edge.

"There."

Tovan crouched beside me.

"Thought so."

"You knew?"

"Knew enough to stop stacking fourth row. Not enough to convince Bren to pay anyone."

"Why fourth row?"

"Because it made the wall complain."

"Sound?"

"Little scrape after loading."

"When?"

"Only when three bays were full."

That was better information than the Guild slip.

I stood.

"We need the top rows down on the next three bays."

Tovan nodded.

Bren reappeared from nowhere.

"All three?"

"Yes."

"We have outgoing freight."

"Then it will leave later."

"How much later?"

Tovan coughed into one hand.

I ignored him.

"I can make it faster if you'd like the wall to move another six millimeters while we work."

Bren stared.

"No."

"Then three bays."

Workers started moving crates.

I waited until the second bay cleared enough to test.

Feathered plane beneath the first shelf run.

Small.

Broad enough to spread load across two floor points.

Thin at the edges.

I pushed just enough to take pressure off the wall anchors.

The shelf rose less than a finger width.

Stone scraped behind it.

Every person in the room stopped.

Including me.

The sound traveled farther than expected.

Not crack.

Shift.

Bren came through the office door at professional panic speed.

"What was that?"

Tovan said, "Wall talking."

Bren looked at me.

"Is that bad?"

"It was already moving."

"That is not an answer."

"Then yes, generally."

His face went pale enough that I regretted the efficiency.

"It also told us where the load is," I added.

Color returned slightly.

Tovan looked at me.

"Can you hold all three bays?"

There it was.

The old question.

Could I hold more?

Probably.

For a while.

That was not the useful answer anymore.

"I can unload one at a time and keep the others from worsening while we brace them."

"How long?" Bren asked.

I pointed at Tovan.

"Ask the foreman how fast his people move."

Bren actually did.

Progress arrived in strange forms.

We spent the next three hours turning a magical problem into carpentry.

Temporary crib blocks under the shelf feet.

Crates down.

Old anchors loosened.

New floor braces cut and seated.

Tovan owned his crew.

I owned the load transitions.

Bren owned standing close enough to worry and far enough not to help.

The first brace went in badly.

Not dangerous.

Just wrong.

The carpenter cut the seat too shallow, and the shelf frame kissed it on one corner before taking load across the full face.

I felt the pressure spike through the plane.

"Back out."

Tovan raised one hand.

Nobody moved except the carpenter.

He pulled the brace.

Shaved it.

Tried again.

Better.

No speech about failure.

Work continued.

By the second hour, the new socket edge started rubbing.

The same outer spot Pera had adjusted.

Not because she had done it wrong.

Warehouse floor sloped toward the loading doors, and I had spent two hours resisting that slope with every left step.

I noticed.

Ignored it.

Ten minutes.

Fifteen.

Twenty.

Then I stopped.

Skin did not care whether the reason was professional pride or bad scheduling.

"Five minutes," I told Tovan.

He looked at the shelf.

Then me.

"Need help?"

"No. Office?"

He pointed.

Bren's office had one chair, one desk, and three framed sketches of warehouses that all looked more emotionally stable than the current one.

I shut the door.

Trouser cuff up.

Strap loose.

Socket off.

The skin along the outer residual limb was red.

Not broken.

No blister.

No heat that worried me.

I changed to the thinner liner from my bag and waited.

Someone knocked once.

Tovan entered before I answered.

He stopped when he saw the walking leg on the chair.

His eyes went to my face.

"Bren thinks you're dying."

"Tell him six minutes."

"You said five."

"He needs growth."

Tovan smiled.

"Need anything?"

"Water."

"I'll send it."

He left.

That was the whole event.

No story about the leg.

No question about Brell.

No observation that I was brave for having skin.

Five minutes later, a worker left a cup of water outside the door and kept going.

Excellent warehouse.

I put the leg back on.

The thinner liner changed the fit slightly.

Less pressure on the red spot.

Enough for the last hour.

When I returned, Tovan had not waited for me to continue the carpentry.

Also excellent.

He had the third floor brace half fitted and two workers shifting crates away from the next anchor.

I resumed the planes where they were needed.

By late afternoon, the new braces carried down into the floor instead of dragging the wall inward.

One shelf run at a time, I reduced the magic.

First bay.

Nothing moved.

Second.

A faint settling sound from the timber crib.

No mortar dust.

Third.

Still.

Tovan checked the crack above the window.

Bren checked my face.

"That's it?"

"For today."

"The wall is fixed?"

"The loading problem is fixed. The wall still owns six millimeters of history."

He frowned.

"Do I repair the crack?"

"After someone checks it again in a week. If it stays where it is, repair it. If it moves, call the Guild."

"You?"

I looked around the warehouse.

"If I'm here."

That answer surprised both of us slightly.

Bren paid the Guild rate and signed the completion slip.

Tovan added a note about the three new floor braces and made Bren initial it.

I approved of the bureaucracy.

Outside, I sat on the warehouse step and counted my share.

Once.

Then again because optimism was not accounting.

Pera's adjustment recovered.

Two pairs of socks recovered through remarkable commercial strategy.

Enough set aside for Harrow's next payment.

Some left.

Money had become boring again.

I liked boring money.

I had just put the coins away when a boy in a Guild runner's blue sash came around the corner at a speed incompatible with dignity.

He saw me.

Slowed badly.

Nearly hit a handcart.

Recovered.

"Greg?"

"Depends."

He pulled a folded slip from his belt.

"Greg. Silver structural. Carrow file."

"Unfortunately."

The boy looked too worried to decide whether that was a joke.

"West quarry wants a specialist."

I looked at the light.

Late afternoon.

"Tomorrow?"

"Now."

There it was.

The useful part of life developing terrible timing.

"What happened?"

The runner swallowed.

"Blast opened a hollow under the limestone. Old walls inside. Three men went in after they heard shouting. They didn't come back."

The warehouse behind me suddenly felt very ordinary.

Tovan came out carrying the signed Guild copy.

He heard enough.

"You going?"

"Probably."

The runner said, "They said twenty minutes."

I looked down at the walking leg.

Wrong tool.

Sword at the inn.

Field peg there too.

Mara would finish at sixth bell.

I pulled the notebook from my bag and tore out a small corner of blank paper.

Tovan offered charcoal before I asked.

I wrote:

**Quarry call. West cut. Guild. I may miss sixth. Not dead unless someone competent says so. Greg.**

I read it.

Added:

**Do not choose Northbank because of this.**

Too much.

I crossed it out until the words became black.

Tovan watched.

"Relationship?"

"Administrative hazard."

"Ah."

He gave the note to the runner.

"Vey east office," I said. "Mara. Hand it to her or a clerk who knows her."

The boy nodded.

"Then quarry?"

"Then quarry. Tell them forty minutes."

"They said twenty."

"Tell them thirty."

"You said forty."

"I am negotiating with geography."

He stared.

"Thirty," he said.

"Good."

The boy ran.

I stood carefully.

The socket spot complained once.

Information.

Not prohibition.

I had money for Harrow in my pocket, a wall behind me that had stopped moving, and a field peg waiting across town.

Seven hours ago I had wanted a normal problem.

Apparently I had been too specific about only wanting one...
