# Candidate Chapter 131: Six Days

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Mara packed badly on purpose.

I knew this because I had seen her pack well.

Two years earlier, moving to Halden had required lists, crate weights, duplicate route labels, waxed document sleeves, three colors of twine, and enough contingency planning to survive a small war.

Northbank got one open trunk.

One closed trunk.

Three piles of clothes.

A blue cup sitting inside a boot.

I picked up the cup.

"Explain."

Mara looked over from the bed.

"Padding."

"For the cup or the boot?"

"Yes."

"Those are different objects."

"Both need protection."

"From each other?"

"Greg."

I put the cup on the table.

"Documents first."

"No."

"They crease."

"So do clothes."

"Clothes recover."

"Not from you."

Hostile room.

Six days before departure.

The number sat at the back of everything now.

Not as loud as the two-year promise had been.

Worse in some ways because six days could fit inside ordinary life.

You could waste six days accidentally.

One shift ran long.

One job turned into two.

One rainstorm delayed a river packet.

Then a person was leaving.

I picked up a folded gray skirt.

"Where?"

"Trunk."

"Which?"

"The open one."

"Everything is going in the open one."

"That's why it's open."

I looked at Mara.

She was holding three shirts against her chest while trying to decide whether any of them had become Northbank shirts through geography.

"You are doing this badly."

"I know."

That stopped me.

Mara put two shirts on the bed and one into the trunk.

"You know?"

"Yes."

"Then why?"

She sat beside the clothes.

The room had changed around us over the last week.

Books missing from the shelf.

Two framed prints already wrapped in brown paper.

Desk drawers open.

A stack of Vey forms near the door marked RETURN TO OFFICE.

The blue cup was one of the few things still exactly where I expected it.

Until the boot.

Mara rubbed one thumb across the shirt in her lap.

"Because if I do it properly, I'll finish."

There.

I sat on the edge of the chair opposite her.

My walking leg fit badly under the little desk, so I turned the knee outward.

"You are still leaving if the cup remains in a boot."

"I know."

"Bad system."

"I know."

I disliked conversations where both people had already accepted the facts.

No correction available.

Mara looked at me.

"You can help."

"I have been trying."

"You have been auditing."

"Related profession."

"Help without making the packing plan."

I stared at the open trunk.

At the clothes.

At the cup.

"Define help."

Mara laughed.

"Pick up things when I ask."

"That's labor."

"Correct."

"No routing authority?"

"None."

"No labels?"

"Maybe later."

"Inventory?"

"Greg."

"Labor only."

"Thank you."

I hated it for almost eight minutes.

Then she asked for the books on the high shelf.

Simple.

I stood.

Walking foot under me.

Right boot.

One hand on the shelf edge because the chair was positioned badly for my balance and I refused to turn retrieving a ledger into a medical event.

Three books down.

Mara chose two.

Put one back.

No explanation.

I did not ask.

Growth remained humiliating.

We worked like that for an hour.

Mara decided.

I carried.

She folded.

I tied when asked.

She put the blue cup into the same boot again.

I allowed the crime.

At some point the room started looking less like Mara lived there and more like Mara had lived there.

I did not like that either.

A knock came at the door.

Mara called, "Open."

Tali from the breakfast place came in carrying a paper sack.

I looked at her.

"Why do you know where Mara lives?"

"Halden exists," Tali said.

Everyone had become unbearable.

She put the sack on the desk.

"Sera sent food because Mara skipped lunch."

Mara frowned.

"I ate."

"Tea is not lunch."

"There was bread."

Tali looked at me.

"Fix her."

"Not my authority."

Tali paused.

Mara looked at me too.

I immediately regretted saying anything unusual enough to earn witnesses.

"He's learning," Mara said.

"Terrifying," Tali said.

She left before I could defend myself.

The sack held two meat pies, sliced pear, and a little jar of mustard strong enough that opening it counted as aggression.

We ate on the floor because the table had become documents.

Mara leaned against the bed.

I sat with my left leg extended enough to keep the socket edge from digging into the floor angle.

"What are you doing after I leave?" she asked.

"Work."

"Obviously."

"Carrow eventually."

"Eventually?"

I bit into the pie.

Too hot.

Waited.

"I don't know yet."

Mara raised both eyebrows.

"Again?"

"Do not make it a thing."

"Twice in one week."

"Temporary illness."

She smiled.

I looked at the open trunk.

"Red Quay will be gone another ten days at least. Maybe more."

"You're waiting for them?"

"No."

The answer came clean.

That surprised me enough to check it.

Still true.

"I liked working with them," I said. "That's different."

"You asked if the same people were going to the next job."

"Renn told you?"

"Veya."

I stopped chewing.

"Why are you speaking to Veya?"

"She came to Vey to ship something before they left."

"Rod thief."

"She says you have her rod."

"Temporary custody."

"She called it collateral."

"Illegal contract."

Mara laughed.

Then she studied me for a second.

"You like them."

I considered lying.

Too much effort.

"Some."

"Which?"

"Professionally?"

"Greg."

"Mett is excellent. Veya is irritating. Korr is expensive to feed. Asha is terrifying. Renn makes decisions before they become meetings."

"That wasn't the question."

"It was an answer."

Mara let me keep it.

Good woman.

Outside, somebody dragged a crate down the hall and apologized to the building after hitting the wall.

The afternoon light through Mara's window had gone yellow.

Six days.

"First visit," I said.

Mara went still.

There.

I had apparently been planning after all.

"What about it?"

"I should come to Northbank after you're settled."

"How settled?"

Dangerous question.

"You tell me."

Mara smiled slowly.

"That hurt you."

"Deeply."

"I don't know either."

"Excellent system."

"Maybe three weeks. Maybe five. I start almost immediately after I arrive. Housing is temporary for the first month. I don't know what the office actually feels like yet."

I wanted dates.

I wanted a packet departure calendar.

I wanted room dimensions and work shifts and whether Northbank's west road still flooded near the lower terraces like it had in my first life.

Instead I said, "Write when you know."

Mara looked at me long enough that the sentence became embarrassing.

"Okay."

"Do not reward it."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

"This is my face."

Theft.

I pointed at her.

She smiled wider.

We went back to packing.

Near fifth bell, we reached the desk drawers.

Mara pulled out letters.

Mine, mostly.

Tied in bundles.

Not all of them.

Enough.

I recognized the packet paper by route year.

Black Stair letters rougher because I wrote them in field rooms and wagons.

Carrow paper after.

Halden paper newer.

One bundle had a blue thread around it.

"What is that?"

Mara moved it away from me.

"Mine."

"They are my letters."

"You sent them."

"That does not answer ownership."

"It does in Northbank."

"You are already adopting local corruption."

She put the bundle in the trunk.

Not the boot.

Better.

I found one letter loose beneath the drawer lining.

My handwriting.

Eleven months into Black Stair.

I remembered writing it after a bridge inspection in rain.

Three pages about a cracked pin.

Mara saw what I held.

"Interesting bridge?"

"Very."

"Keep it."

"Why?"

"Because you were right."

I looked at her.

"About the pin?"

"No idea. I stopped reading that part."

Cruel.

She took the letter and folded it back into the blue-thread bundle.

"You wrote that you were starting to understand why people specialized."

I did not remember that sentence.

Mara did.

"Did I?"

"You said being broadly useful had started feeling like spending yourself in every direction."

That sounded like me.

Annoying.

"And?"

"And then you wrote two more pages about the pin."

"Important pin."

"Obviously."

She closed the trunk halfway.

Not enough to latch.

Clothes bulged at one side.

The boot with the cup prevented the other.

I could have fixed both in under a minute.

Mara looked at me.

I looked at the trunk.

"Ask," she said.

I hated how much work one word could contain.

"Do you want me to fix the trunk?"

"Yes."

Finally.

I took the boot out.

Wrapped the cup in a shirt.

Moved three folded layers.

Rotated the books so the spines carried against the side instead of the lid.

Closed.

Latched.

Mara stared.

"You enjoyed that too much."

"Correct."

"One trunk."

"One victory."

"The other stays mine."

"Hostile negotiation."

"Six days."

There it was again.

I looked around the room.

Half packed.

Half lived in.

Six days was enough time for both.

For once I decided not to make it smaller by counting hours.
