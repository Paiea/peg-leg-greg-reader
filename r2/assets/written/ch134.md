# Candidate Chapter 134: The Traveler

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Mara's blue cup survived packing.

This was the first thing I checked.

Not because it mattered most.

Because the rest was obvious.

Two trunks.

One document case.

One canvas travel bag.

Gray coat.

Northbank packet ticket tucked inside the left glove because Mara had apparently decided that once she left Halden, organization could become experimental.

The cup sat inside the smaller trunk wrapped in a shirt.

Correctly.

Mostly.

"Stop inspecting," Mara said.

I closed the trunk.

"I was checking the latch."

"You opened it."

"Latch inspection became internal."

"Greg."

I stepped away.

Departure morning had arrived without asking whether either of us had finished with the previous five days.

The room looked wrong.

Bed stripped.

Desk empty except one ink stain Mara had spent two years threatening to remove.

Nothing on the shelf.

The wall where the framed river print had hung was slightly darker around the edges.

Rental-room archaeology.

Mara stood in the middle of it wearing travel clothes and trying to decide whether she had forgotten something.

"You have the office papers?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Northbank acceptance?"

"Yes."

"Route vouchers?"

"Yes."

"Temporary housing letter?"

"Yes."

"Money split?"

"Yes."

"Emergency cash separate?"

"Greg."

"Question."

"Yes."

"Good."

She stared at me.

I corrected myself.

"Excellent."

Worse.

Mara laughed anyway.

A knock came at the door.

Tali entered without waiting for permission, carrying a paper bag and a small wrapped package.

"Breakfast."

Mara looked at the bag.

"I ate."

"Tea is not food."

"There was bread."

"Bread beside tea is still tea if you're packing."

I nodded.

"Sound law."

Tali pointed at me.

"You don't get a vote."

Hostile jurisdiction.

The wrapped package contained a new blue cup.

Mara stared at it.

Then at Tali.

"I have one."

"Northbank doesn't."

"That isn't how cups work."

"Now you have two."

Tali hugged her before the argument could improve.

Mara held on longer than either of them commented on.

I looked toward the window.

Observation rights did not extend to everything.

Tali left after making me promise to carry the heavier trunk down the first stair only.

"Why only first?" I asked.

"Because I have met you."

Terrible breakfast provider.

We ate standing because there were no chairs left in useful positions.

Then the landlord arrived with a handcart and two boys who moved trunks for a living and were visibly better at it than I was.

I let them.

Mostly.

The heavier trunk caught the doorframe.

I moved the frame-side strap six inches.

One boy nodded.

No speech.

Good.

We followed the cart toward the river.

Halden was bright enough to be rude about departures.

Clear sky.

Cool wind.

Glass windows catching morning sun.

The city had offered rain for three of the previous five days and apparently saved perfection for the one morning I wanted weather dramatic enough to blame.

Mara walked beside me with the document case under one arm.

"You hate the sky."

"No."

"Planning face."

"Weather has become smug."

"That's not a thing."

"Current evidence disagrees."

At the packet dock, Vey had assembled an ambush.

Sera from Mara's office.

Two clerks I recognized from the first morning.

Tali again somehow.

A route analyst named Benn who had corrected Mara's transfer figures for two years and looked offended that Northbank had acquired her first.

Even the potato-peeling man from the breakfast place was there.

I stared at him.

"Why?"

He shrugged.

"Tali said come."

Mystery solved.

Mara looked at all of them and immediately became angry enough to cry.

"You people have work."

Sera said, "Not for twelve minutes."

Benn held out a narrow ledger.

"Northbank route notes."

Mara took it.

"You made me route notes?"

"Half warnings. Half insults."

"Perfect."

One clerk gave her a little tin of ink cakes.

The other gave her nothing and said, "I already paid for lunch yesterday."

Mara hugged both.

No one gave a speech.

This improved everything.

The river packet sat low against the dock, longer and narrower than the tourist ferry.

Cargo under deck.

Passengers above.

Blue Vey stripe along the rail.

Northbank was not written anywhere visible.

Good.

For another few minutes it was just a boat.

The port clerk called first boarding.

Mara's trunks went on.

She checked the claim tags herself.

Obviously.

Then she looked at me.

The crowd around us became less relevant without actually leaving.

"You have the table," she said.

I blinked.

"What?"

"The comparison table."

"Why?"

"I want to see it."

"You rejected the table."

"Before I chose."

"Correct."

"I chose."

Dangerous legal reasoning.

I had the table.

Of course I had the table.

Folded into the back of my notebook beneath three pages I had added after Mara's decision and therefore could not ethically claim were part of the original document.

I took out the original sheet only.

"No commentary."

Mara held out her hand.

"Greg."

I gave it to her.

She unfolded it.

Nine columns despite our unresolved methodological dispute.

Carrow.

Halden.

Northbank.

Salary.

Housing.

Route cost.

Contract term.

Advancement.

Staff authority.

Effective distance.

Family proximity.

Existing network.

Fine.

Maybe more than nine depending on whether grouped measures counted independently.

Mara read slowly.

I watched her face.

"You scored Northbank highest," she said.

"Professionally."

"Before I told you about the final route-desk authority."

"Yes."

She looked at Carrow.

"And Carrow highest overall."

"Because the table was biased."

"Toward you?"

"Toward things I understood how to measure."

Mara looked at me.

That sentence had escaped without inspection.

Unfortunate.

She looked down again.

"You gave Halden four points for food."

"Strong category."

"Northbank three."

"Expensive bread."

"You haven't been there in this life."

"Bread persists."

She laughed.

Then folded the table once.

Not along my original crease.

Criminal.

"Can I keep it?"

"Why?"

"Because it's ridiculous."

"Then no."

Mara folded it again and put it into her document case.

Theft.

Apparently our relationship had developed themes.

Second boarding call.

Sera hugged Mara first.

Benn after.

Tali told her to eat lunch in Northbank like the city was specifically opposed.

Then everyone found reasons to look at cargo, water, sky, anywhere else.

Mara stepped closer to me.

"Three to five weeks," she said.

"You said write when you know."

"I know enough to give range."

"Dangerous generosity."

"Do not arrive in two because you optimized the river."

"I would never."

She stared.

"Fine. I would consider."

"Wait for the letter."

"Yes."

"Actually wait."

"Yes."

"Greg."

"Mara."

She smiled.

Then stopped smiling.

"I love you."

No clever response arrived fast enough.

Good.

"I love you too."

She kissed me.

Not short.

Not theatrical either.

The dock remained a dock.

Somebody behind us complained about blocking the boarding lane.

Mara laughed against my mouth.

"We're being operationally inefficient."

"Finally."

Third call.

She stepped back.

I took the document case while she climbed the packet steps, handed it up, then let go.

No reason to keep holding something once the other person had it.

Mara found a place at the rail.

Her coworkers gathered beside me.

The packet clerk released the forward line.

Engine charm deep under the deck hummed once.

Water churned.

The boat moved away from the dock slowly enough to make departure rude.

Mara raised one hand.

I raised mine.

No running beside the boat.

No last-minute instruction.

No shouted route plan.

The packet turned with the current.

Blue stripe smaller.

Then glass glare off the river hid part of the deck.

Then the first bridge took the whole boat behind stone.

Gone.

Tali exhaled beside me.

Sera said, "Well."

Benn looked at his watch.

"We have work."

Of course.

They left in pieces.

Tali squeezed my arm once before following.

No advice.

Bless her.

I stayed at the dock.

Not because I expected the packet to come back.

The river kept moving.

Another freight boat arrived ten minutes later and occupied the same berth.

Workers rolled barrels down the ramp.

A porter told me to move.

Life remained aggressively scheduled.

I moved.

At the top of the dock steps I stopped.

Walking leg.

Good fit.

Field peg back at the inn.

Copper rod in my bag.

Wooden tourist bird in my pocket because I had forgotten to put it anywhere else.

Red Quay somewhere near Esven.

Mara somewhere beyond the first bridge on the way to a city I disliked mostly on principle.

No promise clock.

No twelve-day clock.

No job waiting with my name on it.

That should have felt empty.

It did.

Also open.

I distrusted both.

I could make a list before reaching the inn.

Carrow.

Guild board.

Pera.

Northbank route research I was explicitly not supposed to perform yet.

Find new work.

Write Noll.

Write Jorren.

Check whether the setter cuff was still at Lantern Rows.

Then I smelled fried dough from a corner stall.

Purple honey.

Glassberry.

Mara had liked it.

I bought one.

Not because it meant anything.

Because I was hungry.

Good enough for now.
