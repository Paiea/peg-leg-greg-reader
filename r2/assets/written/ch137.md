# Candidate Chapter 137: The Freight

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

The cheap wagon had six wheels, nine passengers, four crates of lamp glass, two sacks of dried riverweed, one goat, and no respect for knees.

Excellent value.

I boarded after second bell with the walking leg on and the field peg tied beneath my bench.

This was correct for the first hour.

By the third, I hated every decision that had led to seated travel.

The wagon master was a woman named Dema Orr.

Not Guild.

Not interested in becoming Guild.

She owned the wagon, the two draft horses, the route permits, and apparently our continued right to breathe near her cargo.

"Glass doesn't like arguments," she said before departure.

One passenger asked what that meant.

"Means don't start one near the crates."

Clear policy.

I liked her.

The road south from Halden followed the river for most of the first day.

Not scenic enough to justify the bumps.

Wet fields.

Low stone walls.

Farm tracks cutting toward reed country.

Every few miles a ward post marked the maintained route boundary with blue paint and a copper cap.

One leaned badly.

I looked at it.

Then kept looking as we passed.

The man beside me noticed.

"Broken?"

"Maybe."

"You fixing it?"

"No."

He seemed disappointed.

Good.

I was learning to disappoint strangers efficiently.

His name was Foll, a cloth merchant returning to Carrow with samples nobody had bought in Halden.

He had three cases beneath the rear tarp and enough opinions about dye tariffs to survive several wars.

Across from us sat a farm woman named Nemi with a hammer in her bag, though I did not discover this until later.

The goat belonged to nobody currently admitting responsibility.

Dema claimed it was cargo.

The goat disagreed through volume.

By noon, people had started asking about my leg.

Not rudely.

Mostly.

Foll asked first.

"Battle?"

"No."

"Monster?"

"No."

"Magic accident?"

"Work."

He looked cheated.

"Just work?"

"Very committed work."

Nemi laughed.

Foll tried again.

"Where?"

"Brell."

That satisfied him slightly because Brell had enough reputation to restore genre.

I ate bread and cheese.

No further biography offered.

At the first road stop, I changed to the field peg.

Dema watched from the horse line.

"Problem?"

"Wagon."

"Wagon is fine."

"My knee disagrees."

"Knee not listed on manifest."

"Administrative oversight."

The field peg made getting in and out easier and sitting slightly worse.

Tradeoffs remained loyal.

Pera's washer softened the road shock when we walked hills beside the wagon.

On flat stretches I rode.

At steep ones everyone except Foll walked because Dema said the horses had better labor contracts than we did.

Foll complained.

Dema charged him an extra copper for "horse resentment."

He paid.

I respected her enormously.

We made camp near an old roadside shrine.

Not active temple.

One stone figure under a cedar roof, face worn smooth by rain, hands cupped around an empty bowl.

Someone had left three yellow flowers in it.

Someone else had left half a biscuit.

The goat ate the biscuit.

Religious dispute unresolved.

Dema parked the wagon with the glass side away from the road and made everyone move bedrolls twenty feet from the wheels.

"Why?" Foll asked.

"Because people sleep stupid."

Fair.

Dinner came from one pot.

Bean stew.

Salt pork.

Nemi produced pepper from a little tin and became the most important person at camp.

Afterward Foll brought out cards.

I should have refused.

I had work to do.

No.

I had nothing to do.

That remained harder.

We played.

Local game called Three Roads.

Simple rules.

Draw three.

Commit one.

Hold one.

Pass one left.

Except half the cards altered who counted as left.

I lost the first game because I assumed the best card should be kept.

Nemi laughed.

"You hold too much."

Everyone had become thematically malicious.

Second game I passed the best card.

Lost worse.

Foll won and became unbearable.

Third game the goat ate a discard.

Dema declared the goat neutral and made us continue.

I won that one.

Questionable legitimacy.

Good enough.

Night passed without monsters.

This felt statistically rude after paying for sword maintenance.

Day two rained.

Not heavy.

Persistent.

The wagon cover leaked exactly above Foll.

Dema said this improved the dye samples.

At midday, Mara would have been reaching Mill Junction or already beyond it depending on transfer speed.

I did not calculate beyond that.

Mostly.

The road left the river and climbed through low wooded hills.

More stone.

Better ground.

Field peg excellent.

Washer noticeable only when I thought about it.

Best kind of equipment.

Late afternoon, the wagon stopped with a sound that did not belong to horses.

Wood crack.

Not catastrophic.

Still expensive.

Dema was down before anyone asked.

Rear left wheel sat wrong.

Not broken wheel.

Axle cap split where an iron strap had worn into old wood.

Foll said, "Can you magic it?"

I looked at him.

"Probably."

Dema said, "Don't."

Correct answer.

She crawled beneath the wagon with a pry bar.

Nemi opened her bag.

Hammer.

Of course.

Another passenger, a cooper named Den, knew the knot Dema wanted for a temporary wheel bind.

I had rope too.

Nobody cared.

Dema pointed at the wagon body.

"Can you take some weight off left rear while we reset?"

There.

Specific ask.

"Yes."

I put two low planes beneath the frame.

Not lift the wagon.

Just shave enough load that the wheel stopped biting the rut.

Dema jacked the axle with wood.

Nemi hammered a spare cap plate flat around the damaged end.

Den bound it.

Foll held the goat.

This was more useful than anything else he had done.

Twenty minutes.

Done.

Dema tested the wheel.

"Gets us to Brin yard."

"Then?"

"Real repair."

Foll looked at me.

"You could've held it all the way."

"Probably."

"Why not?"

Dema answered before I did.

"Because I don't pay him enough to become an axle."

Excellent woman.

We reached Brin before dark.

The wheelwright looked at the temporary bind and said three separate insults about Dema's route maintenance, then copied Nemi's plate shape almost exactly.

Professional respect had dialects.

Day three was mostly road.

No fire.

No monster.

No ancient ruin.

I slept badly in an inn where the mattress sloped toward the wall and the soup contained a vegetable nobody identified confidently.

The wooden tourist bird came out of my bag while I searched for clean socks.

Foll saw it.

"Your child?"

"No."

"Gift?"

"Prize."

"You won that?"

His tone suggested injury.

"Technically."

"At what?"

"Fish."

I refused further context.

Day four, Carrow appeared after sunset.

Not dramatically.

First the south watch marker.

Then tile roofs through trees.

Then Bell Tile smoke against the evening sky.

My body knew the road before I decided how I felt about it.

Walking leg back on for the last miles because I had changed at lunch.

Right boot.

Wooden left.

Familiar grade.

I knew which cobbles near South Gate held water after rain.

Still did.

The city did not notice I had returned.

Someone shouted about onions.

A wagon blocked half the gate.

Two children ran past carrying a glowing hoop.

Normal.

Good.

Dema stopped at the freight yard.

We unloaded.

I helped with the lamp-glass crates because after four days I had become emotionally invested in their survival.

Foll collected his unsold fabric.

Nemi shouldered her bag.

The goat changed ownership through a conversation I did not understand.

Then everyone left.

No vows.

No future contract.

Dema handed me a small receipt.

"You travel again, ask south freight desk."

"Discount?"

"No."

"Then weak recruitment."

"You paid cheap and complained expensive."

Fair.

I started toward Lower Rook.

My room was maybe still mine.

Noll had not replied.

That seemed dangerous.

Halfway there I passed the road leading uphill toward Hearthline.

I slowed.

Could have gone.

Daro might be working.

Pell definitely was somewhere nearby.

No.

Not tonight.

Home first.

I kept walking.
