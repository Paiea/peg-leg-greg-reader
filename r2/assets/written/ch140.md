# Candidate Chapter 140: The Deputy

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Jorren came home wearing a clean coat.

Noll had warned me.

I still wasn't prepared.

"Take it off," I said.

Jorren stopped in the doorway.

Three ledgers under one arm.

Road dust on both boots.

Clean gray coat with a narrow dark stripe at the cuff.

Not expensive.

Official enough to become dangerous.

"Hello to you too."

"The coat."

"Work coat."

"Exactly."

He looked at Noll.

Noll kept stirring soup.

"I told him," Noll said.

"Traitor."

Jorren put the ledgers on the table and finally smiled.

Then hugged me.

Quick.

Hard enough to count.

He stepped back and looked down at the walking leg.

Not long.

"Better?"

"Than no leg?"

"Than last one I saw."

"Yes."

"Good."

He looked at my face.

"You look same."

"Cruel."

"Older somehow."

"Worse."

Noll pointed the spoon at both of us.

"Shoes."

Jorren looked at the mud on his boots.

Then at Noll.

"I pay rent."

"Shoes."

Route authority apparently ended at the kitchen floor.

Jorren took them off.

The coat stayed.

I decided civilization could survive one thing at a time.

Lunch turned into reports without anyone calling them reports.

Jorren had been on Leth road six days.

Two freight delays.

One bridge crew short three workers.

One merchant who insisted axle width was a philosophical question.

"It isn't," Jorren said.

"Correct."

"He disagreed."

"Then he has philosophy now."

Jorren laughed.

His route-deputy role covered two road segments and one transfer yard under a senior factor who apparently believed training meant allowing mistakes to approach the cost ceiling before intervening.

I liked the senior factor immediately from description alone.

"You have people?" I asked.

"Not mine."

"Workers."

"Two clerks on rotation. Yard crews depending shift. Road crews belong to site leads."

Good answer.

No little kingdom.

"And they listen?"

Jorren ate soup.

"Sometimes."

"When?"

"When I sign the delay sheet."

Power through paperwork.

Terrifying.

He looked toward my field bag beside the wall.

"Noll says you stole another thing."

I put down my spoon.

"I did not."

Noll said, "I said maybe."

"You asked stole."

"Same family."

"No."

Jorren grinned.

"What is it?"

I showed him Veya's copper rod.

He held it by the middle.

"This all?"

"For her technique, yes."

"What does it do?"

"Marks one point of a straight cutting line."

"And she gave it to you?"

"She says I count as a spare fixed point."

Jorren looked at me.

"That sounds like flirting."

"Everyone keeps being wrong about this."

Noll said, "Who Veya?"

"Nobody."

"Red Quay cutter," Jorren said.

"Traitor again."

Jorren handed the rod back.

"You have a party now?"

"No."

"Crew?"

"No."

"People you keep taking jobs with?"

"Sometimes."

"That's crew."

"Temporary repeated labor association."

Noll looked at Jorren.

"He worse."

"Halden damage."

I finished soup alone morally.

Jorren stood after lunch and put his boots back on.

"Come with me."

"Where?"

"South transfer yard."

"Why?"

"I forgot one signature."

"You need help signing?"

"No. You keep asking questions. Come ask them somewhere else."

Fair.

We walked.

I wore the walking leg because Carrow streets remained mostly civilized.

Jorren moved faster than he used to.

Not physically.

Purpose.

He knew which alley avoided market carts at this hour.

Which gate clerk would be on duty.

Which yard entrance was blocked by timber deliveries.

He no longer asked me whether a route choice looked right.

He simply took it.

The south transfer yard sat inside a wide stone square behind the freight sheds.

Six wagons waiting.

Three unloading.

One being shouted at.

Jorren saw the shouted one and sighed.

"That's mine."

"Congratulations."

We crossed.

A merchant in a brown coat was arguing with a yard lead beside a four-wheel freight cart loaded high with ceramic tile.

The right rear wheel had sunk half a hand into the soft shoulder beside the loading lane.

Not stuck yet.

Trying.

The merchant saw Jorren.

"Deputy. Good. Tell him we can move."

Jorren looked at the wheel.

Then the load sheet clipped to the cart.

"You added weight."

"Six crates."

"Sheet says forty-two."

"Now forty-eight."

"Then sheet wrong."

"Road dry."

"Shoulder isn't."

"We stay on lane."

Jorren pointed at the rear wheel.

"You're already off lane."

The merchant looked at me.

Maybe because Silver clasp.

Maybe because people searched for alternate authority when current authority became inconvenient.

"You structural?"

"Sometimes."

"Will it move?"

I looked at Jorren.

His face did not ask for rescue.

Good.

"Ask the deputy," I said.

Merchant frowned.

"I am asking you."

"Then yes. Everything moves eventually."

Jorren covered his mouth with one hand.

Not coughing.

Traitor.

The yard lead turned away openly.

Merchant's patience died.

"Can the cart leave?"

Jorren answered.

"Unload six crates or wait for the shoulder to dry."

"That's a day."

"Maybe."

"Contract penalty."

"Your contract."

"You're costing me money."

Jorren held out the load sheet.

"You changed the load after route clearance. I'm costing you six crates of unloading. You chose the rest."

No speech.

No anger.

The merchant stared at him.

Jorren waited.

Eventually the merchant snatched the sheet.

"Unload six."

Yard lead called workers.

Problem ended.

I looked at Jorren.

He started walking toward the office.

"That's it?"

"What?"

"No explanation about shoulder failure?"

"He doesn't care."

"He might if he understood."

"No. He cares about leaving. I gave him the condition for leaving."

I stopped.

Jorren took three more steps before noticing.

Turned.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That face."

"Everyone needs new language."

He shook his head and continued.

Inside the yard office, he signed one delay sheet, corrected a clerk's route number, and moved a transfer slot because another wagon had a cracked wheel.

No one asked me anything.

I sat on a stool and watched.

Jorren did not look like a commander.

Good.

He looked like someone who had been doing the work long enough that decisions no longer required performance.

One clerk called him "Deputy" twice.

He ignored the title both times.

On the walk back, I said, "You got better."

Jorren looked offended.

"I work there."

"That's how it happens."

"You sound sixty."

"Fifty-nine."

He looked at me sideways.

Right.

Old number.

Still weird.

"Mara Northbank?" he asked.

"Yes."

"You going?"

"Later."

"When?"

"She tells me."

He stopped walking.

Actually stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Jorren."

"You let someone else pick timing."

"I have always done this."

He laughed so loudly a woman across the street looked over.

"Fuck you."

"Healthy relationship."

We kept walking.

At Lower Rook, Noll had put bread out.

Mesi was at the table copying a tax appeal.

Jorren changed out of the clean coat before eating.

Finally.

Underneath, same old shirt with one repaired cuff.

Balance restored.

He dropped a folded notice beside my bowl.

"Guild posted these."

I opened it.

Three-day open field exchange.

Practical demonstrations.

Sparring.

Team problem course.

Equipment tables.

Mixed-rank entry.

No prize amount listed yet.

Interesting.

Bad word.

I looked at Jorren.

"You entering?"

"No. Working second day. Freight demonstration."

"Demonstration?"

"How not to lie on load sheets."

"Advanced discipline."

"You should go."

"Why?"

Jorren took bread.

"Maybe somebody there can teach you to stop calling temporary coworkers temporary labor associations."

Mesi looked up from the tax appeal.

"What?"

"Nothing," I said.

Jorren smiled.

The field-exchange notice stayed beside my bowl.

I did not sign anything that night.
