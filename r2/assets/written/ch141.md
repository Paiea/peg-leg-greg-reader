# Candidate Chapter 141: The Entrant

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Jorren did not make me sign the field-exchange form.

He only stood beside the registration table holding bread until refusal became inconvenient.

Different crime.

"You can eat after," he said.

"Coercion."

"Professional development."

"Worse."

The Carrow field exchange occupied most of South Yard and enough surrounding streets that three food carts had already violated normal licensing boundaries by sunrise.

No tournament banners.

No nobles in raised boxes.

No champion's purse large enough to attract masked rivals.

Guild had organized something more dangerous.

Practical learning.

Spar circles on the east side.

Technique lanes under canvas awnings.

A ward court built from removable posts.

Equipment tables along the freight sheds.

One problem course disappearing through an old loading yard and into the dry drainage cut behind it.

Silver and Bronze tabs everywhere.

Some Golds too, mostly judging or demonstrating.

One woman carried a fishing spear and a basket of boiled eggs.

Nobody questioned either.

I respected the event slightly more.

The clerk pushed a form toward me.

"Rank?"

"Silver."

"Standing crew?"

"No."

"Primary discipline?"

I paused.

The line waited.

Jorren bit my bread.

I looked at him.

"Payment for delay."

"Structural support," I told the clerk.

She wrote it.

"Secondary?"

"Sword. Field work."

"Ward?"

"Some."

"Rigging?"

"Some."

"Healing?"

"No."

Excellent certainty.

The clerk turned the sheet around.

Three boxes.

**SPAR.**

**TECHNIQUE.**

**TEAM COURSE.**

I checked all three.

Jorren watched.

"You know you don't get paid."

"I know."

"And you checked spar."

"Sword exists."

"So do bad decisions."

"You wear an official coat now. Your authority has limits."

He looked down.

Today the coat was already dusty.

Better.

The clerk glanced at my walking leg.

Professional look.

Not pity.

"Course has gravel, stairs, one wet section."

"I brought the field peg."

I tapped the bag.

"Change where marked. No equipment swap inside timed section unless declared before start."

"Fine."

She stamped the form.

"Technique first. Spar after midday. Team draw tomorrow evening for third-day course."

"Random?"

"Within rank band unless you register a standing team. Prior shared-work records can be used to reduce duplicate orientation."

That sounded bureaucratically dangerous.

"I have no standing team."

"Then the Guild will disappoint you creatively."

Good clerk.

Jorren finally gave me the bread.

Then left because he had a freight demonstration to prepare on the second day.

"What are you demonstrating?" I asked.

He walked backward three steps.

"How to read load sheets."

"Crowd favorite."

"Go lose at something."

Unkind.

I ate while walking the yard.

This was not a tournament.

That mattered.

People were good at things without needing elimination brackets.

A Bronze woman demonstrated a climbing charm that worked only on painted stone and looked ridiculous everywhere else.

A Gold healer had six people lying on mats while teaching splint speed.

Two men argued over whether a three-hook river rig counted as one tool or four.

At an equipment table, somebody had built a shield handle that rotated under impact and seemed designed specifically to remove fingers from careless owners.

Pell would love it.

Then I found Pell.

Of course.

He had half a table in the equipment lane beside Kesta and three heel-block samples.

The grazer tendon was not among them.

Good.

"You entered," Pell said.

"Apparently."

Kesta looked at my form.

"Sustained barrier at first bell."

I looked down.

The technique clerk had assigned categories beneath my check mark.

**SUSTAINED BARRIER**

**PRECISION HOLD**

**OPEN UTILITY DEMO**

"I did not request sustained barrier."

Pell said, "You wrote structural support."

"That is not the same thing."

"Tell Guild."

I looked toward registration.

Longer line now.

No.

Fine.

Sustained barrier remained something I could do.

Maybe finding out exactly how badly was useful.

Bad word.

I went to the technique lane.

The evaluator was a Gold caster named Hessa Tor who had forearms like a dock worker and no patience for people trying to turn a measurement into theater.

"Flat plane," she said. "Chest high. Six feet wide. Weight sled increases by hundred. You may alter thickness but not geometry after load begins. Stop on command."

Simple.

Almost offensive.

Five entrants ahead of me.

I watched.

Bronze at three hundred.

Silver at six.

Another Silver at five but cleaner.

One woman failed two hundred because her plane kept curling around the edges and laughed harder than anyone else.

Then my turn.

I planted the walking foot.

Right boot.

Even stance.

No need for field peg on packed yard dirt.

Opened the plane.

Broad.

Flat.

Old shape.

One hundred pounds.

Nothing.

Two.

Fine.

Three.

Shoulders engaged.

Four.

Plane held.

Hessa looked at my hands.

"Stop feathering. Fixed geometry."

I had not noticed.

Of course.

My habit now wanted softer edges automatically.

I tightened the plane.

Five hundred.

Pressure climbed through arms and upper back.

Not dangerous.

Just expensive.

Six.

Plane trembled.

I corrected.

Wrong correction.

One edge thickened.

Other thinned.

Hessa raised a hand.

"Stop."

I released immediately.

The sled settled.

No dramatic failure.

No crack.

No collapse.

Score clerk wrote **500 CLEAN / 600 STOP**.

Respectable.

Hessa looked at me.

"You use broad planes often?"

"Less now."

"Shows."

Not insult.

Measurement.

I stepped out.

A Silver man two entrants later held seven hundred clean.

Eight.

Nine with visible effort.

At ten, Hessa stopped him when his knees bent.

He released.

People clapped because ten hundred pounds was objectively cool.

I clapped too.

Annoyed.

But honestly.

He was better at this.

No hidden cost appeared.

No secret reason his big flat wall was less sophisticated.

He had trained broad holding and could broad-hold the shit out of things.

Good for him.

I went to precision hold next.

Different apparatus.

Three hanging brass rings swinging independently on cords.

Keep a small wooden bead centered inside one ring for ten breaths while the evaluator disturbed the support.

That felt more familiar now.

First ring clean.

Second clean.

Third moved diagonally and I nearly fixed it relative to the room before Pell's voice arrived in memory.

Move with surface.

I shifted the pocket.

Bead stayed inside.

Nine breaths.

Ten.

Hessa marked the sheet.

Better score.

Not best.

A Bronze girl with thread-thin white magic held two beads simultaneously because apparently children existed only to ruin perspective.

She was probably eighteen.

Current body disliked old language.

Fine.

I watched her finish.

Excellent control.

No need to convert it into a lesson about me.

At open utility, people did strange things.

One caster used force pulses to clean grain husks from cloth without tearing fabric.

A dock mage moved six floating hooks through an obstacle rack.

Someone made a tiny stable shelf in midair and balanced tea on it while the evaluator tried to distract him.

He spilled at the final second.

Crowd applauded anyway.

I demonstrated a feathered catch on a moving crate.

Simple.

The evaluator shoved a weighted box down a short ramp.

I did not stop it.

I changed the angle of its final slide and let it settle inside the chalk square without tipping.

Clean.

Small.

The score clerk wrote something.

I did not ask.

That surprised me more than the barrier result.

Lunch carts opened fully at midday.

I bought a meat bun and something fried that claimed to contain cheese.

The cheese had left before service.

I sat on a low wall near the spar circles.

The sustained-barrier board was visible from there.

My name had moved down.

Not bottom.

Middle.

Exactly where respectable belonged.

I ate.

A pair of Bronze fighters argued about whether low-force magic made sword sparring dishonest.

A healer nearby told them blood made it annoying either way.

Somewhere behind the freight sheds, a crowd cheered at something I could not see.

I liked the noise.

Not because anyone knew me.

Most did not.

I was one Silver among many.

One broad-plane score halfway down a board.

One decent precision result.

One crate caught without drama.

The afternoon spar clerk called names.

"Greg. Ring three. Sera Nin."

I looked toward ring three.

A woman about a foot shorter than me stepped under the rope carrying one curved practice blade and absolutely nothing else.

Bronze tab.

No shield.

No casting tool.

She rolled one shoulder.

Looked at me once.

Then at the walking leg.

Professional.

Fine.

I finished the meat bun.

Jorren had told me to go lose at something.

Very well.
