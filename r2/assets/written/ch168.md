# Chapter 168: The Brace

Kelm's first lesson on the Storm Road was that standing still could be a mistake.

We reached Span Twenty-One a little after second bell.

From fifty yards away it looked intact.

From twenty, it looked old.

From five, it looked like something I would have charged extra to stand on.

The road narrowed where it left the cliff and crossed a cut in the black stone. Not a huge gap. Maybe sixty feet. Enough that falling remained professionally undesirable.

Two main chains ran beneath the deck and rose into stone anchors on either side. Lighter lateral chains kept the deck from swinging too far under crosswind. Brass caps covered the old storm pins at four corners.

One lateral keeper was missing on the north side.

Kelm had installed a temporary chain from the deck rail to a lower anchor.

Temporary meant new iron beside forty-year-old bronze.

Temporary also meant the iron had already started polishing one edge of the old ring.

Kelm saw me looking.

"Four crossings," he said.

"Then replacement?"

"Then I stop calling it temporary and become embarrassed."

Reasonable maintenance standard.

Iria halted the convoy before the span.

Pavo moved the animals back from the cliff edge and had workers chock all four wagons.

Evin walked onto the empty deck alone.

The road moved under him.

Not dramatically.

Two inches sideways.

Maybe three.

Then back.

The movement traveled through the deck in a slow wave.

My first instinct was to stop it.

Kelm hit my forearm with two fingers.

I had not cast anything yet.

Rude anticipation.

"Don't."

"I didn't."

"You were about to."

Everyone had developed this ability around me.

Concerning.

Evin held one brass rod at shoulder height.

The rod hummed faintly.

"Wind channel is clean," he called. "Charge low. Crosswind twelve, gusting maybe seventeen."

Kelm stepped onto the deck.

I followed.

Field peg today.

The metal tip gave me honest feedback and worse comfort.

Good trade.

The deck rolled under my right foot.

My body corrected.

The peg landed half an inch farther out than expected.

No problem.

Then the whole span came back the other direction.

I corrected again.

Kelm noticed.

"Stop fighting it."

"I'm walking."

"You're walking like road owes you a floor."

"It is called road."

"Marketing."

He crouched at the temporary keeper.

The new chain was tight on the upswing and almost slack when the deck rolled south.

Kelm touched the old ring.

"This ring takes the lateral load late. Missing keeper used to catch earlier. We can cross wagons one at a time if we keep center and don't get a hard north gust."

I looked at the deck.

"I can hold north travel."

"No."

"A little."

"Still no."

"Why?"

He stood.

"Because the deck is supposed to give."

"Not indefinitely."

"Neither are knees. Doesn't mean you cast them straight."

Annoying analogy.

Effective.

He pointed at the south chain.

"Wind pushes north. Deck goes north. Main chains take angle. Lateral catches before rail meets rock. Then it comes back. If you pin the deck because movement offends you, where does the gust go?"

I looked at the anchors.

"Into stone."

"And chain. And boards. And every bolt between."

"So don't stop movement."

"Control the end of it."

I looked at him.

He smiled without friendliness.

"That sound familiar?"

Very.

I hated him less.

Worse development.

We tested with the empty maintenance wagon first.

Pavo drove.

Two workers walked each side with guide lines.

Sera led the first mule across on foot, because apparently the animal trusted her more than any of us.

Correct animal.

I stood at the north midpoint where the missing keeper mattered.

Kelm stood by the temporary chain.

Evin watched the wind.

Iria watched everything.

"Moving," Pavo called.

Wagon rolled onto the deck.

The bridge dipped slightly.

Not down.

Into load.

Chains tightened.

Deck shifted north.

My magic came easily now when the problem was small.

Not a wall.

One plane near the north rail.

Angled.

Loose.

I let the deck move into it.

Contact.

Pressure.

Then I moved the plane with the deck.

Not fixed.

Following.

The plane became less like a brace and more like a hand ready near someone's shoulder.

The next gust hit.

Evin called, "North seventeen."

Deck moved.

I moved with it.

The plane took just enough of the last inch that Kelm's temporary keeper did not snap tight.

Chain still loaded.

Just slower.

Kelm watched the ring.

"Again."

Pavo stopped the wagon halfway.

"I dislike that word on bridges," he called.

Nobody cared.

Evin waited for the next gust.

When it came, I let the span roll.

One inch.

Two.

Three.

Then I bled maybe a quarter of the remaining travel through the angled plane.

Not resistance.

Timing.

The temporary chain tightened without a jerk.

Kelm put two fingers on it while it loaded.

"There."

Iria said, "There what?"

Kelm pointed at me.

"Keep doing that."

High praise.

We crossed the maintenance wagon.

Then wagon one.

Medicine.

Pavo drove slower than I thought necessary.

Then I felt the difference.

Heavy forward load made the deck's return slower.

One rhythm out.

The bridge did not care about my previous successful solution.

Of course not.

I adjusted.

Wagon two carried lamp crystal and rode lighter.

Faster sway.

Different again.

Iria did not ask whether I was comfortable.

She asked, "Capacity?"

"Fine. One wagon at a time. Give me a breath between."

"One breath?"

"Two if you like me."

"One."

Cruel leadership.

Wagon three was the ugly one.

Merchant chests.

Machine bearings.

Weight low and rear-biased despite Pavo's corrections.

As the rear wheels reached the center, the deck kicked north harder than before.

My plane met it.

Too hard.

I felt the load spike through my wrist.

Wrong.

I released before the deck could transfer the mistake into chain.

The span swung another inch.

Kelm's temporary keeper snapped tight.

Iron rang against bronze.

Everyone froze.

Kelm looked at the ring.

I looked at my hand.

No pain.

Just feedback.

Pavo called, "Problem?"

Kelm inspected the ring.

"No damage."

Then to me, "You tried to correct early."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Heavier return."

"So?"

"I anticipated."

"Don't."

Sera, from the far side, shouted, "I have told him this."

Traitor.

Kelm said, "Bridge already knows how to be bridge. You don't have to beat it to the next movement."

Same lesson.

Different object.

Annoying pattern.

I waited.

Next gust.

Deck moved.

I watched what existed.

Not the motion I expected.

Actual rail.

Actual chain.

Actual rear wheel.

Plane appeared late enough to be useful.

The span settled into it.

I moved with the last part of the swing.

Kelm's keeper loaded cleanly.

"Good," he said.

This one I accepted.

Wagon three crossed.

Wagon four followed.

Then the remaining animals and workers.

Twenty people.

Four wagons.

No heroics.

No one died.

The road did what it had been built to do with one missing piece and a lot of supervision.

On the far side, Pavo checked every tie again.

Evin wrote wind readings on the route sheet.

Kelm removed one brass cap from the north storm pin and inspected the grease beneath.

Iria marked our crossing time.

I leaned against the stone wall and took the field peg off for three minutes.

Outer socket edge hot.

Not damaged.

I rubbed the skin dry.

Sera sat beside me.

"You look happy."

"I am sitting."

"No. The other thing."

"What other thing?"

"You got to do tiny annoying magic nobody else wanted to do."

I considered denying it.

"Yes."

She nodded.

"Thought so."

Kelm walked over holding something the size of a fingernail.

A thin blue-gray shard.

"Stormglass," he said.

"From?"

"Old pin cap. Flaked loose. Legal road scrap. Worth almost nothing this small."

He held it out.

I took it.

The shard was cold despite the sun.

Tiny lines inside caught light when I turned it.

"Why me?"

"You like junk."

Sera laughed.

I put the shard in my notebook pouch.

Legal salvage.

Still excellent.

Iria called movement.

We repacked.

The Storm Road turned south along the cliff and disappeared behind a wall of black stone.

Above us, one of the giant brass vanes rotated with a sound like a slow door opening.

The whole structure moved with the wind.

Not resisting.

Using it.

I put the field peg back on.

When we started walking, I let my body move a little more with the road.
