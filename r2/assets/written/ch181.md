# Chapter 181: The Visitor

I did not go home.

This required explanation at three ticket counters and none to myself.

The return coach from the western freight gate reached Halden late the next day.

Carrow connection left before dawn.

Northbank packet coach left after first bell.

I bought the north ticket.

The clerk looked at my west-road mud, field peg, sword, and repair pack.

"Wrong direction?"

"Temporary."

He shrugged.

Good clerk.

Northbank took most of a day.

I slept badly against the coach wall and woke every time my broad magic tried to appear in a dream as a door that opened too slowly.

This was not symbolic.

Probably.

The actual door at the Northbank station opened fine.

Mara's office did not.

Not for me.

A young clerk with square glasses blocked the desk entrance with one hand.

"Appointment?"

"No."

"Name?"

"Greg."

The clerk's expression changed slightly.

Ah.

Known problem.

"Greg Greg?"

"Unfortunately."

She looked toward the back office.

"Mara is in a captain review."

"I'll wait."

"Could be an hour."

"Still wait."

The clerk pointed at a bench.

I sat.

Northbank Vey office was narrower than Halden's and twice as busy.

Three service windows.

Route board covering one wall.

Blue string for river freight.

Red for road.

Yellow tags for ferries.

Somebody had invented a system where a single black clip meant delay and two meant cancellation.

Mara's desk sat behind a half wall near the back.

Not large.

Her own.

I could tell before I saw nameplate because the papers were stacked by function instead of size.

Also because one corner held the cheap clay cup I had mailed her from Carrow after she complained Northbank office cups tasted like soap.

The square-glasses clerk caught me staring.

"Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't."

"Mara said you might."

Slander traveled faster than packets.

I waited.

Twenty minutes.

Thirty.

A captain came out of back office looking angry in the way people looked when they had lost an argument to documentation.

Mara followed.

She wore dark work clothes, hair pinned badly, pencil behind one ear.

She saw me.

Stopped.

Then looked at square-glasses clerk.

"Bess."

Bess said, "He said no appointment."

"He doesn't understand appointments."

"I understand them."

Mara walked over.

"You came."

"You said when I can."

"You can now?"

"Apparently."

She kissed me once.

Public office.

Short.

Then she stepped back and looked down.

"Field peg?"

"Long road."

"You look terrible."

"Successful contract."

"Same thing, usually."

Bess looked between us.

"Can I go to lunch?"

Mara checked wall clock.

"Ten minutes. Torren covers west window."

"He is already at lunch."

Mara closed her eyes.

"Of course he is."

She turned toward the back.

"Greg, bench."

"Again?"

"I have a job."

There.

Good.

"Bench."

I sat.

Mara worked another forty minutes.

I watched without pretending not to.

Not because I needed to analyze her.

Because I had not seen this version.

People brought her problems.

She did not solve all of them herself.

One clerk asked whether to move a ferry handoff.

Mara asked three questions and told him to decide.

Another brought a damaged route seal.

She sent it to records.

A driver complained about a cutoff.

She pulled the tide sheet, checked one line, and said, "No. You missed it. Tomorrow's window is fifth bell."

He argued.

She did not become louder.

He left.

Bess returned with bread.

Torren returned with tea and an apology that sounded practiced.

Mara accepted tea, not apology.

Her desk.

Her decisions.

Not a story about me.

Good.

I allowed that one silently.

At last she closed the service ledger.

"Done enough."

"That sounds healthy."

"Don't ruin it."

She took my bag before I could object.

Then immediately gave it back.

"Heavy."

"Professional equipment."

"Rocks?"

"Stormglass."

"Rocks."

"Legal rocks."

"Worse."

Her apartment was six blocks uphill.

Third floor.

The bad-flute neighbor existed.

I heard him before we reached landing.

"You were not exaggerating."

"I never exaggerate."

The flute missed a note so violently it became a new one.

"You did not exaggerate enough."

Mara's place was small.

One main room.

Tiny kitchen.

Bedroom behind a curtain because Northbank builders apparently considered doors luxury.

Two windows facing river roofs.

One shelf crooked by maybe a quarter inch.

I noticed.

Mara noticed me noticing.

"No."

"I said nothing."

"You looked at shelf."

"Shelf looked at me first."

"Sit."

Oppressive relationship.

She had the bakery bun waiting.

The one she claimed could make Halden apologize.

Sweet bread with orange peel and dark sugar.

It was excellent.

I hated that immediately.

"Fine," I said.

Mara smiled.

"Fine?"

"Halden owes written statement."

"Thought so."

We ate by the window.

For a while she told me about Northbank.

Not summary.

People.

Bess hated exceptions but loved correct stamps.

Torren knew every driver by name and forgot his own lunch schedule.

Captain Orrin had tried to bully a cutoff twice and lost both times.

A ferry clerk named Mave had three children and could do tide conversions in her head faster than Mara's tables.

Mara had started giving Mave the ugly crossings.

Not punishment.

Trust.

"She wants them?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Good."

Mara looked at me.

I raised both hands.

"Normal good."

"Dangerous phrase."

Then she looked at my wrist.

Red band still visible where cuff had sat.

Her expression changed.

Not fear.

Recognition.

"You used it."

"Yes."

"The rental cuff."

"Yes."

"You said you didn't need it yet."

"I didn't when I wrote."

She waited.

I told her.

Not the whole road from first marker.

Enough.

Final span.

Storm refuge.

Iria's safe plan.

Cargo cradle.

The fact that everyone could have lived without my choice.

The cuff.

Three priming casts.

Crossing.

I did not make it noble.

Mara listened without interrupting until I said the broad shapes were slower now.

Then she asked, "How much?"

"Breath. Sometimes two."

"Gone?"

"No."

"Can retrain?"

"Probably some."

"Probably."

"Documentation was honest."

She rubbed one thumb over the edge of her cup.

"And the fast thing?"

I made a tiny plane beside the crooked shelf.

Instant.

Mara saw it appear.

"That fast?"

"Faster than before."

I moved it under shelf corner and lifted a fraction.

Shelf leveled.

Mara stared.

"I said no."

I dropped plane.

Shelf went crooked again.

"Respecting boundaries."

"Eventually."

She got up and pushed the window closed because evening air had cooled.

Then sat again.

"Would people have died if you didn't use it?"

"No."

"Would someone else have been hurt?"

"No reason to think so."

"You would have lost cargo."

"Maybe wagons too. Completion money. Merchant value. Time."

"And you wanted to cross."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly.

Not approval.

Not condemnation.

"Okay."

That was worse somehow.

"Okay?"

"You knew. You chose. It worked."

"Yes."

"I don't know what speech I'm supposed to give you."

"Preferably none."

"Good."

She almost smiled.

Then didn't.

"You keep borrowing from yourself."

There.

Simple enough to hurt.

I looked at her.

"I pay back."

"Sometimes."

"Often."

"Greg."

Fair.

Mara looked out window.

"Your leg. Reputation. Money. Magic. You keep finding a version of you later who can deal with what you spend now."

I had answers.

The later version usually could.

That was evidence.

Mara knew it too.

She touched my wrist once.

"I'm not saying don't."

"No?"

"I don't want that job."

"What job?"

"Being the person who tells you which pieces of yourself you're allowed to spend."

I sat with that.

Bad habit.

Sitting with things.

"I didn't ask permission."

"I know."

"Would you have told me no?"

"I don't know."

Honest.

Better.

She leaned back.

"Would you do it again?"

Faultglass question in different clothes.

I looked at crooked shelf.

Could fix it instantly with one family.

Could still fix it slowly with another.

Nothing ruined.

Nothing free.

"Depends," I said.

Mara laughed once.

Not happily.

Not unhappily either.

"Of course."

We did not solve me that night.

Good.

We went downstairs for noodles because Mara had no food except tea, two onions, and a jar of something labeled **WEST PICKLE** in Torren's handwriting.

The noodle place was loud.

Mara complained about ferry captains.

I complained about rental cleaning fees.

She said charging me for salt stains was reasonable.

I reconsidered relationship.

Later, the flute started again through wall.

Mara put a pillow over her head.

I laughed.

She threw second pillow at me.

My hand moved.

Tiny plane caught it in air.

Instant.

Too easy.

I dropped plane.

Pillow hit my face anyway.

Mara smiled.

"Better."

Maybe.
