# Chapter 30: The Workshop

Status: **CHARACTER REBUILD EXPERIMENT / BRANCH ONLY / NOT PUBLIC R2 AUTHORITY**

---

I took Jorren to Mill Street because I wanted him to see the filtration venture.

This was true.

It was also incomplete in a way Mara would have found irritating from Halden.

I wanted him to be impressed.

That part took longer to admit.

Not by the filters exactly.

By me.

Which was worse.

Jorren met me after midday carrying a new shoulder strap and half a loaf of bread.

"You eat?"

"Some."

He tore the loaf in half and gave me the smaller piece.

Friendship remained adversarial.

Mill Street smelled like wet wool, kiln smoke, and the particular mineral damp that came from businesses boiling things for money.

Jorren looked around while we walked.

"This your exciting secret?"

"No."

"Good."

"You have become demanding."

"Roads."

Apparently roads explained everything now.

Arlo's workshop door stood open.

Before we reached it, a familiar cart rolled out into the street.

Dena held the reins.

Two clean ceramic chambers were strapped upright behind her. One stained return sat in the rear cradle under oilcloth.

She saw me.

Then Jorren.

Her eyes moved to his pack strap.

"New leather."

Jorren looked at it.

"Yes."

"Oil it before rain."

"I know."

Dena nodded once.

Professional relationship established.

I lifted a hand.

"Hello, Dena."

"Greg."

She clicked her tongue at the mule.

The cart moved.

Jorren watched it go.

"Who is that?"

"Dena."

"I gathered."

"Service runs."

"For this?"

He pointed at the workshop.

"Yes."

There.

We had not even entered and the cart had already made my explanation look late.

Inside, Arlo was standing beside the kiln with both hands black to the wrists.

Not soot.

Slip.

A row of fresh chamber bodies sat on boards near the warm wall.

Ivena occupied the workbench with a ledger, three client tags, and an expression suggesting someone had recently asked for a discount.

Neither of them looked surprised to see me.

Jorren got more attention.

Arlo glanced at the sword first.

Then the boots.

Then his face.

"Road friend?"

I disliked how accurately people could identify categories I had struggled to name.

"Jorren."

Jorren held out his hand.

Arlo looked at his own black hands.

Jorren looked too.

"Later."

"Good."

Ivena closed the ledger.

"Does he have money?"

Jorren looked at me.

"This the woman?"

"Which woman?"

"The one who asks if you brought money."

Ivena's eyes moved to me.

"You talk about me?"

"Only under financial duress."

"Good."

She looked at Jorren again.

"Do you?"

"Some."

"Then welcome."

I had imagined this differently.

Not consciously.

That would have been embarrassing.

But somewhere between Lower Rook and Mill Street, I had built a version where I opened the door and showed Jorren the four ugly ceramic units that had become actual orders.

I would explain the first failure.

The bypass.

Ivena's wash.

Arlo's chambers.

The service model.

Maybe the road version of me would look slightly less like a man who spent his free time irritating posts with a sword.

Instead Dena had driven past before I could say anything and Ivena was checking whether Jorren qualified economically as a person.

Arlo wiped his hands on a rag.

"What did you bring him for?"

"To see the shop."

Arlo looked around.

"It's here."

Jorren laughed.

I hated everyone.

I picked up one of the finished chambers from the rack.

"This is the clean half of the service unit. The customer keeps two. One runs while the other comes back for cleaning."

Jorren took it from me.

Heavier than it looked.

He turned it once.

"Fragile?"

"Less than the first ones," Arlo said.

Jorren looked at the thickened collar.

Then toward the street where Dena had disappeared.

"How many break on the cart?"

Dena was no longer here to answer.

I knew approximately.

Arlo knew better.

"One so far," he said.

"Not cart," Ivena added. "Customer dropped it off the wash platform."

"They say that?"

"After Dena asked why the impact mark was on the top edge."

Jorren smiled.

"I like Dena."

Of course he did.

I took the chamber back.

"The ceramic isn't the important part."

Arlo looked at me.

I corrected.

"It is an important part."

"Good recovery."

Jorren had moved toward the service tags on the bench.

Three narrow wooden slips.

Purple mark.

Blue mark.

Black mark.

Client codes.

"What's this?"

Ivena answered.

Fixed service days.

East Mill on second and fifth day.

Lower bridge on third.

Dyehouse pickup whenever her own workers stopped pretending they could remember to send the dirty chamber back.

I looked at her.

"Fixed days?"

"Yes."

"Since when?"

"This week."

I had been on the West Cut.

Then asleep.

Then taking a day off.

Then finding Tavin in lamp oil and Jorren in onions.

Apparently commerce had continued being rude.

"Why?"

Ivena opened the ledger again.

Not because she needed it.

Because she liked having numbers present when explaining why someone else was wrong.

Dena had been making extra crossings because customers called the moment a running chamber slowed, even when the matched spare was already sitting clean beside it.

On-demand service had turned into three half-empty cart trips in one week.

Fixed days meant customers swapped to the spare, tagged the dirty one, and waited unless both units had a problem.

"And if both fail?" I asked.

"Runner."

"How fast?"

"They pay for fast."

That was Ivena.

I looked at the tags.

The future version in my head liked fixed routes.

Eventually.

Part of me still wanted to argue about whether three customers justified making schedule rules this early.

That part was not entirely wrong.

"What if the wool washer only needs service once that week?"

"Then Dena has one less chamber."

"Cart still goes."

"Because the soapworks still exists."

"Probably."

Ivena stared at me.

"Do you have information?"

"No."

"Then don't kill my customer."

Jorren looked down at the tags so I would not see him smiling.

I saw it anyway.

"You think this is funny?"

"Very."

"You have been here four minutes."

"Strong four minutes."

Arlo took the chamber from my hands and set it back on the rack.

"Dena wanted the fixed days."

I looked at him.

That mattered.

"Because?"

"Ask her tomorrow."

Also fair.

I hated fair answers when I was showing someone around.

Jorren moved toward the cart rack near the door.

Empty now except for two padded cradles and a spare tie strap.

He pressed one thumb into the padding.

"Wool?"

"Old blanket," Arlo said.

"Works."

"Dena cut it."

"Smart."

I stood in the middle of the workshop while my friend admired a blanket inside a cart cradle.

This was not the impression I had planned.

I had become a man with a business whose most exciting feature was apparently responsible packing.

Jorren looked back at me.

"Which part did you make?"

There it was.

Finally.

I opened my mouth.

Arlo answered first.

"Bad disk."

Ivena said, "Money."

I looked at both of them.

"I also had the filtration idea."

"Bad disk idea," Arlo said.

"It filtered."

"Until Ivena used it."

Ivena pointed at herself with the pen.

"I improved it by needing it to work."

Jorren nodded seriously.

"Important role."

"Thank you."

I crossed my arms.

Dena was not here, which deprived the room of the fourth accusation.

Then Arlo ruined the joke by looking at the ledger.

"Greg saw the first waste shimmer."

No smile.

Just fact.

"And he paid for enough clay that I could break three versions without closing the shop."

Ivena added, "He noticed the first hot-run failure was different before either of us wanted to admit we'd sold something unfinished."

I looked at her.

"You admitted it quickly."

"After I shouted at you."

"True."

Jorren looked from them to me.

This should have been the moment.

There.

Admiration available.

Instead he said, "You got yelled at into a business."

I stared.

Arlo sat down because laughing while standing had become inefficient.

Ivena made a noise I had never heard from her before and covered it by coughing into one hand.

I hated that sentence because it was not entirely wrong.

"I contributed more than that."

"I'm sure."

Worse.

Jorren picked up the spare tie strap.

"This buckle is backwards."

Arlo stopped laughing.

He looked.

The strap had been rethreaded through the brass loop from the wrong side after washing.

It would hold.

It would also jam under load if pulled tight enough.

Road problem.

Jorren flipped it around.

Threaded it correctly.

Set it back.

Nobody praised him.

He did not need it.

The workshop continued.

Ivena reopened the ledger.

Arlo went back to the wet chamber bodies.

A bell sounded somewhere near East Mill.

Jorren took the last bite of his bread.

I stood there with the stupid warm feeling I had expected to get when he admired my importance.

It had arrived sideways.

Arlo trusted me enough to insult the first design without protecting my ego.

Ivena had remembered exactly where I had been useful and exactly where I had been expensive.

Dena had changed service days without asking.

Jorren had fixed a strap within ten minutes of entering the building.

None of that was the speech I wanted.

Good.

No.

Fuck `good`.

I was still a little annoyed.

That was allowed.

Jorren nudged my boot with his.

"You buying lunch?"

"You ate half a loaf."

"That was before business."

"You did no business."

He pointed at the strap.

"Consulting."

Ivena looked up.

"How much?"

Jorren considered.

"Lunch."

"Greg pays."

"Agreed."

They shook hands.

I had brought Jorren here to show him something of mine.

I left owing him lunch to my own business partner...
