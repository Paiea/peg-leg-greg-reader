# Rehearsal A43: The Board

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The Guild board had not taken my absence personally.

This was rude.

I had missed it for Mara, then a chair, then Tavin, which sounded like a sequence invented by somebody trying to prove I possessed a social life.

By the time I actually crossed the Guild threshold, the morning crowd had already peeled away from the main board. Three chalk marks had been rubbed through contracts I might have taken. A fourth sheet hung crooked with one corner torn off.

Nobody had preserved opportunity in amber for me.

Tavin would have approved.

I stood in front of the board with my hands in my coat pockets and told myself I wanted a road.

Then I read every Carrow posting first.

That was not contradictory.

It was efficient.

Road jobs stayed on the right side. City work on the left. Skilled contracts in the middle where people could pretend the arrangement meant something about merit rather than paper size.

I read the left side twice.

A tannery wanted a night guard because someone had been stealing treated hides.

A cooper needed two bodies to roll empty barrels from East Market to the south warehouses.

A river factor wanted a Silver-ranked escort for three days upriver, which removed me neatly.

A ropehouse near the west quays wanted `WARD-CAPABLE LABOR / ONE DAY / POSSIBLE SECOND / RIVER RATE`.

That sounded suspicious.

I leaned closer.

The bottom line had been added in different ink.

`NO HEROICS. ASK KERREN.`

Better.

"You finally came back."

Sella stood at the desk behind me with a mug in one hand.

I turned.

"I have been here before."

"Not this week."

"Week is a hostile unit."

"Mara leave?"

The Guild knew too much too.

"Why does everyone know?"

Sella drank from the mug.

"You asked the river clerk what time the west boat left. Twice."

Institutional betrayal continued spreading.

"Yes. She left."

Sella nodded once.

No smile.

No question about whether I was devastated.

I appreciated her immediately and therefore became suspicious.

"Roads?" she asked.

I looked back at the board.

My eyes landed on the ropehouse posting again.

"Maybe."

"That's left."

"I can read."

"Debatable."

I pointed at `NO HEROICS`.

"What happened?"

"Nothing dramatic."

"Those words usually mean paperwork."

"Crate came off a hoist yesterday. Nobody under it. Kerren thinks one of the load wards is flickering when the river wind comes in. Owner thinks Kerren is trying to bill maintenance."

"Which is Kerren?"

"Rigger."

"Wardwright?"

"No."

"Then why ward-capable labor?"

"Because the wardwright looked at it yesterday and said the ward was fine."

"Useful."

Sella's eyebrow moved.

I heard the word after it left my mouth.

Old habit.

I rubbed one thumb against the edge of my purse through the coat.

Five copper less from the chair.

More than enough remaining.

Enough that work did not need to happen today.

That was probably why I wanted it.

"Rate?"

"Six copper plus midday meal. Eight if they keep you after second bell."

"River rate."

"River rate."

"Danger premium?"

"Smell premium."

"Excellent."

Sella reached under the desk and pulled out a wooden token with a number burned into it.

"Kerren. West ropehouse. Ask before touching anything suspended."

I took the token.

"That instruction feels targeted."

"It is."

"I have never worked for Kerren."

"No."

"Then how?"

Sella drank again.

"Your face."

I left before the Guild could become more accurate.

Carrow between the Guild and the west quays was awake in the loud way commercial streets preferred.

Carts occupied more road than they deserved. A fish seller had moved three baskets onto the walkway and seemed offended whenever feet encountered them. Two boys carried a pane of glass between them while arguing about a dog.

I crossed behind them.

Then crossed back because a dray horse decided the street belonged to its bladder.

Current life contained too much fluid.

The west quays smelled like wet rope, fish oil, old wood, river mud, and money.

Money had several smells.

None pleasant.

The ropehouse was not actually on the quay. It sat one building inland, long and low, with double doors open toward a paved loading yard. Coils of rope hung from wall pegs in sizes ranging from hand line to something I assumed could restrain architecture.

A timber hoist projected over the yard from the second level.

That was where everyone was looking.

A crate hung six feet above the paving.

Nobody stood beneath it.

Encouraging.

A thick man in a leather shoulder guard held one hand on the hoist line and stared upward.

"Stop feeding!"

The man at the crank stopped.

The crate swung once.

Twice.

A pale blue ward mark on the upper crossbeam brightened with each swing.

Then dimmed.

I stopped walking.

There.

Something.

Not enough to name.

Old Greg had used load wards on salvage rigs, siege lifts, ship cranes, cliff cages, and once a truly irresponsible suspended bath.

This mark was simpler.

Local.

Wrong in a way I could not immediately explain.

That last part annoyed me.

The thick man noticed me.

"Guild?"

I held up the token.

"Greg."

"Kerren."

He pointed at a chalk square painted beside the open door.

"Stand there."

I looked at the square.

"Why?"

"Because I said."

I stood there.

Growth.

Possibly.

Kerren went back to the hoist.

"Bring it down. Slow."

The crank turned.

Rope creaked through the upper wheel.

The crate descended.

The ward brightened again.

The river wind came over the roofs hard enough to push cold air through the yard.

The crate swung toward the building.

Blue mark flashed.

The rope snapped sideways six inches.

Not break.

Shift.

Kerren swore.

"Stop."

The crank stopped.

The crate hung three feet above the paving.

"Down now," Kerren said.

"You said stop."

"Now I said down."

The crank man resumed.

The crate touched stone.

Everyone exhaled at different times.

Kerren tied off the line himself.

Then walked to me.

"Ward-capable?"

"Yes."

"How capable?"

The truthful answer was complicated.

"Enough to know the mark is reacting to lateral load."

His face did not change.

"Yesterday's wardwright said wind doesn't matter."

"Wind doesn't. Swing does."

"Difference?"

I looked at the hoist.

"Ward is probably reading tension direction, not force alone. If the crate moves sideways, the line loads the top wheel differently."

"Probably."

There was a whole judgment in that word.

"I haven't touched it."

"Good."

Kerren pointed to the crate.

"Move that inside."

I stared.

"You hired ward-capable labor."

"Also labor."

Four men were already taking pry bars to the crate skids.

Fine.

I put my coat on a peg.

The crate contained dyed wool according to the side mark. Wool apparently became dense when merchants arranged enough of it to injure workers.

We got two short rollers beneath the skids, then pushed.

My shoulder complained before the crate moved.

Interesting.

Not injured.

Just nineteen and not as strong as Old Greg's body had been at fifty-nine, which continued to be personally inconvenient.

I adjusted my stance.

Pushed through my legs.

The crate rolled.

One of the other workers, a narrow woman with half her hair shaved close, said, "Don't lean your ribs into it."

I looked at her.

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

Apparently Sella had cousins.

We moved the crate inside.

Then another.

Then spent half an hour hauling coils from one wall rack to another so Kerren could clear the area beneath the hoist.

Actual rope work contained less romance than songs suggested.

It also contained fibers.

Everywhere.

By the time Kerren waved me back outside, my palms felt rough and my shirt had stuck between my shoulder blades.

He had lowered the hoist line until the hook hung at chest height.

"Look. Don't fix."

"You have developed an opinion of me quickly."

"Sella wrote `ASK FIRST` on your token."

I looked at the wood in my hand.

She had.

Tiny letters on the back.

Betrayal with excellent penmanship.

I stepped closer to the line.

The ward mark was carved into a bronze plate bolted to the upper beam. Four shallow channels ran from it toward the wheel bracket.

Basic load-sharing geometry.

Except the lower right channel had darkened around one edge.

"Salt?" I asked.

Kerren glanced toward the river.

"We're three hundred feet inland."

"River wind carries more than philosophy."

"Touch it?"

Permission.

I put two fingers against the lower channel.

Cold bronze.

Tiny magic response.

Then warmth.

Not from the ward.

From friction in the bracket beside it.

I leaned closer.

"Can you move the line by hand?"

Kerren did not answer.

He grabbed the hook and pulled sideways.

The upper wheel shifted.

Very slightly.

Blue line brightened.

There.

"Bracket moves."

"It shouldn't."

"Agreed."

"Wardwright said plate."

"Plate might be fine."

Kerren stared upward.

He looked angry in the direction of yesterday.

"Ladder."

The shaved-hair woman brought one.

Kerren climbed.

I held the base.

This was less prestigious than magic.

Also more useful at the moment.

He put one hand against the wheel bracket.

"Feed line."

The crank man moved it.

Kerren's shoulders tightened.

"Again."

The bracket clicked.

Small sound.

Bad sound.

"Bolt seat," he said.

"Loose?"

"Wallowed."

I knew that word from carts more than wards.

The timber around one bolt had compressed into a widened hole. The bracket could shift under lateral load. The ward was doing what it had been built to do, reacting to a load change, while the mechanical mount created the problem.

Kerren came down.

"Owner's going to love that."

"Because wood is cheaper than magic?"

"Because he paid a wardwright yesterday."

"Ah."

Kerren looked at me.

"Can ward stay while we brace?"

I looked upward again.

"Probably."

His expression returned.

"I can test it under a controlled side pull after you brace. I would not suspend another full crate until then."

"Better."

He shouted for timber.

The next two hours became carpentry, lifting, wedges, profanity, and one disagreement about whether a temporary sister block needed three bolts or four.

Kerren wanted four.

The owner, who arrived wearing a clean blue coat, wanted three.

"Three held twenty years," the owner said.

Kerren pointed at the widened bolt seat.

"No. Three held nineteen years and eleven months."

I liked him.

The owner noticed me.

"Who is this?"

"Guild."

"Wardwright?"

"No."

"Then why is he here?"

Kerren handed me a brace and said, "Lift."

I lifted.

Apparently this was my introduction.

We got the fourth bolt.

The owner complained until midday meal arrived, then complained while chewing sausage.

I ate two pieces because river rate included calories.

After meal, we tested the brace with an empty crate.

Then one quarter load.

Then half.

The ward remained steady until Kerren pulled a side line to force swing.

Blue light brightened.

No bracket shift.

"Again," he said.

We did.

Still steady.

The owner clapped once.

"Good. Full load."

Kerren said, "No."

The owner looked at him.

"We just tested."

"Half."

"And the ward held."

"Half."

I understood both arguments.

Unfortunately, understanding did not prevent me from saying, "Full would tell us if the timber compression changes under real load."

Kerren turned his head toward me.

There it was.

I had been hired for six copper and had already found a way to spend someone else's safety margin.

"Outside," he said.

I almost argued.

I had reasons.

Good ones.

A controlled full load with nobody beneath it would provide better evidence. Old Greg had tested worse systems under worse conditions. The brace looked sound. The ward response was clean.

I opened my mouth.

Then Kerren said, "You can hold the tag line outside the swing zone if you want to be useful. You don't decide my load."

I closed my mouth.

Not because he was completely right.

Because he owned the hoist.

I took the tag line.

We ran another half load.

Then another.

On the third, the new brace made a soft cracking sound.

Every muscle in my body tightened.

Kerren raised one fist.

The crank stopped.

The crate hung four feet up.

Nobody moved.

I could see the brace.

No split.

The sound came again.

Tick.

Not wood.

I looked at the line.

One strand near the hook had pulled loose.

"Rope," I said.

Kerren saw it.

"Down."

The crank man moved.

The loose strand peeled farther.

Too fast.

"Faster," Kerren said.

The crate dropped another foot.

The line snapped.

Not all of it.

One outer section.

The sudden imbalance kicked the hook sideways.

The crate swung toward the open doors.

A worker pushing a roller cart had stepped into the yard.

He froze.

I did not think.

This was important because I had spent most of the morning thinking.

I let go of the tag line, crossed two steps, grabbed the worker's shirt at the shoulder, and pulled.

He stumbled into me.

The crate hit the paving where he had been.

Wood corner exploded.

Dyed wool spilled out in a red compressed wall.

Something caught my left palm.

Rope.

Burning.

I yanked free.

Too late.

Heat ripped across the skin.

Kerren was shouting.

"Clear! Clear the fucking line!"

Everyone moved now.

The remaining rope hung slack.

Ward plate flashed bright blue once, then went dark because there was no load left to read.

The worker I had pulled stared at the crate.

Then at me.

"Fuck."

"Yes."

My palm hurt.

I looked at it.

Red line across the base of the fingers. Skin intact except one narrow blister forming near the thumb.

Not serious.

Painful enough to make the distinction theoretical.

Kerren reached us.

He looked at the worker first.

"Hit?"

"No."

Then me.

"Hand."

I showed him.

"Wash it."

"It's fine."

"Wash it."

I washed it.

The shaved-hair woman poured cool water over my palm from a bucket while I tried not to react like cool water hurt.

It did.

"Name?" I asked her.

"Ressa."

"You waited until injury."

"You didn't ask."

Fair.

Across the yard, Kerren had already cut the damaged rope section away and was examining it on the paving.

The owner stood beside him very quietly.

I dried my hand against my trousers and went over.

Kerren held the broken section out.

The inner fibers were dark.

Not fresh damage.

"Rot?" I asked.

"Oil," he said.

Ressa came up behind us.

"Stored coil?"

Kerren nodded.

The rope had been treated with something that looked protective from outside and softened the interior instead.

Mechanical problem beneath a magical one beneath another mechanical problem.

Old Greg had known enough to notice the ward.

Not enough to notice the rope before it failed.

My hand throbbed.

I did not turn that into philosophy.

Kerren did it for me anyway.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"What?"

"Possible second day. Posting said."

I looked at the broken crate.

"You still want me?"

"You pulled Renn."

The worker raised one hand from behind us.

Apparently Renn.

Kerren continued, "You also tried to tell me to full-load a bad rope."

"I did not know the rope was bad."

"Exactly."

Annoying man.

"Eight copper tomorrow," he said. "We inspect every line before anything goes up. You listen when I say where to stand."

I looked at my palm.

Then at the river beyond the roofline.

Roads were still on the right side of the Guild board.

"Fine."

Kerren nodded.

Ressa said, "Bring gloves."

"I own gloves."

"Then today was a choice."

I looked at my hand again.

Also fair.

I collected my coat after second bell.

The owner paid eight copper because we had stayed late and because arguing over the rate while a broken crate lay in the yard apparently exceeded even his appetite.

I walked back toward Lower Rook with my left hand held slightly away from my coat pocket.

I told myself it was because the blister hurt against the fabric.

Then I passed the Guild corner and looked toward the board entrance.

Road jobs were still inside.

I kept walking.

Not because I had changed my mind.

I had work tomorrow.

Different thing.

At Lower Rook, Noll was sitting in my second chair eating an apple.

He saw my hand.

"Road?"

"Rope."

He looked disappointed.

"Less good."

"I agree."

He bit the apple.

"Gloves."

I stared at him.

"Did Ressa send a packet ahead of me?"

"Who?"

"Nobody."

Noll pointed toward the field box under my bed.

My gloves were visible on top.

Of course they were.

I sat in the old chair.

He kept the new one.

My palm pulsed with each heartbeat.

Tomorrow I would go back to the ropehouse.

The field pack stayed beside the wall.

Two clean road socks inside.

I could see the top buckle from where I sat.

I looked at it long enough for Noll to notice.

He said nothing.

That was worse.
