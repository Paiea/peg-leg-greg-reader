# Rehearsal A44: Every Line

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

I wore gloves.

This was apparently enough to make Lower Rook suspicious.

Maud saw them when I came downstairs.

"Cold?"

"Work."

She looked at my left hand.

The blister sat beneath a strip of clean cloth, mostly because Noll had threatened to tell Vessa I was contaminating food-adjacent surfaces by existing.

"Rope?"

"Again."

Maud nodded.

"Don't bring rope home."

"Why would I?"

She had already turned away.

No faith.

At the west ropehouse, Kerren had every coil in the loading yard by the time I arrived.

Not some coils.

Every coil.

The yard looked like a nest built by a species with maritime anxiety.

Rope lay in labeled circles across the paving. Thick hoist line. Tag line. Dock line. Fine cordage. New stock. Old stock. Two coils marked with red chalk and separated from the others.

Ressa stood over one with a knife.

"Morning," I said.

"Gloves."

"Apparently I can learn."

"We'll see."

Kerren came through the doors carrying a ledger.

"You're late."

I looked at the light.

"No."

"I started earlier."

"That is not how lateness works."

"Touch nothing until I show you."

"Good morning to you too."

He shoved the ledger into my chest.

I caught it with my right hand because the left still objected to pressure.

"Supplier marks," he said. "Find every coil from Dath and Rell Cord. Match dates."

I opened the ledger.

Numbers.

Names.

Weights.

Delivery days.

I had been hired as ward-capable labor and promoted overnight to clerk.

Tavin would have been delighted.

"What are we looking for?"

Kerren pointed at the red-chalk coils.

"Both bad line came from same delivery. Different sizes. Same treatment smell inside."

"Oil?"

"Maybe."

Ressa sliced through a short damaged length and peeled the outer braid apart.

Dark inner fibers.

She held it under my nose.

Sharp smell.

Not lamp oil.

Something resinous.

"Waterproofing?"

Kerren said, "Supposed to be."

"New treatment?"

"Supplier says no."

"Do you believe them?"

"I believe people remember invoices better when money is leaving."

Fair.

I took the ledger into the yard.

The work became physical in a stupid way.

Find a mark.

Roll coil enough to expose the tied tag.

Read date.

Roll it back because leaving rope loose created murder geometry.

Write ledger line.

Move to the next.

My injured palm hurt through the glove each time I gripped too hard.

I began favoring the right hand.

Then my right forearm tired.

Bodies redistributed incompetence efficiently.

Ressa worked opposite me.

She knew the markings without checking the ledger.

"Dath uses square burn," she said.

"I see."

"You're reading every tag."

"I enjoy certainty."

"Slow way to get it."

"Fast certainty is often just confidence."

She looked up.

"That yours?"

"Probably."

"Sounds expensive."

I decided not to explain reincarnation.

By first bell we had isolated five coils from the same delivery batch.

Three looked fine.

Two smelled wrong after we opened sample ends.

Kerren cut six-foot lengths from all five and hung them from a test beam in the back yard.

The owner arrived while we were rigging weights.

Same clean blue coat.

Different expression.

"You're cutting paid line."

Kerren did not look at him.

"I'm preventing paid crates from becoming floor decorations."

"Dath says there is no treatment problem."

"Then these will hold."

The owner saw me attaching a weight hook.

"Guild boy."

I looked at him.

Nineteen-year-old body continued making `boy` technically defensible.

I disliked him anyway.

"Greg."

"Did you tell him the ward failed?"

Kerren said, "Ward didn't fail."

The owner ignored him.

He wanted me.

Not because he respected me.

Because I was external enough to become leverage.

"The ward reacted to the bracket moving," I said.

"So ward problem."

"No."

"It reacted."

"Correctly."

"Then why did crate fall?"

I looked at the test lines.

"Rope failed."

"Yesterday you said bracket."

"Also bracket."

His face tightened.

He wanted one thing.

One invoice.

One person to blame.

Understandable.

Wrong.

"So which is it?"

Kerren finally looked at him.

"Both."

The owner pointed at him.

"You told me bracket."

"Then we found rope."

"After you cut it."

Ressa made a noise that might have been a laugh swallowed into a cough.

I looked down because my face had become unsafe.

The owner turned back to me.

"Would full load have failed if bracket was fixed?"

There it was.

Yesterday's argument returning with better teeth.

I could answer technically.

Probably yes if that rope section had been in service under enough tension.

Maybe not on the first lift.

Maybe not on the tenth.

Failure timing depended on internal fiber damage I could not quantify by smelling resin.

I thought about telling Kerren to full-load before the rope snapped.

My palm pulsed beneath the glove as if memory had learned irony.

"I don't know," I said.

The owner stared.

"You're Guild."

"Yes."

"Ward-capable."

"Yes."

"And you don't know."

"Correct."

He looked personally cheated.

I almost enjoyed it.

Then realized I also hated saying it.

Not knowing felt like leaving a weapon on the table.

Old Greg had survived by knowing more than other people often enough that ignorance still felt like negligence.

Current Greg smelled bad rope and had a blistered hand.

The owner walked away to find somebody more useful to argue with.

Kerren said, "Better."

I looked at him.

"Do not grade me."

"Then stop taking tests."

Rude.

We loaded the first test line.

One hundred pounds.

Two.

Three.

The rope stretched normally.

Kerren marked extension with chalk.

Ressa watched the upper braid.

I stood beside the release lever.

At four hundred, the first line held.

At five, still held.

We stopped because nobody needed to prove rope could survive until it killed the test frame.

Second line.

Same delivery.

Same mark.

At three hundred, the inner fibers made a tiny dry popping sound.

Ressa heard it first.

"Stop."

Kerren raised his fist.

We lowered the weight.

The outer rope looked fine.

Inside, three strands had separated.

No magic.

No dramatic curse.

Bad material hidden beneath good braid.

Kerren sent a runner to Dath and Rell Cord with the sample and a note I suspected contained fewer polite words than necessary.

Then he looked at the remaining coils.

"Nothing from that batch goes up."

The owner came back.

"That is half my heavy line."

"Yes."

"We have freight today."

"Then freight waits."

"It cannot."

"Watch it."

The argument moved inside.

Ressa and I stayed with the rope.

She sat on an overturned bucket.

I leaned against the wall.

My right shoulder ached from rolling coils.

Left palm burned.

Road work had looked cleaner on the Guild board.

"You always work here?" I asked.

"Four years."

"Rigger?"

"Line hand. Learning rigging."

"Kerren teaching?"

"When he's not yelling."

"So rarely."

"He yells lessons."

That sounded plausible.

Ressa picked a loose fiber from her sleeve.

"You staying?"

"Today?"

"Carrow."

I looked at her.

Why did strangers keep asking questions shaped like decisions I had not made?

"I live here."

"People live places before leaving."

Maud had said something similar about the chair.

Conspiracy.

"Maybe road soon."

"Guild?"

"Maybe."

She nodded.

"Kerren will ask you back."

"He has known me one day."

"Enough."

"Why?"

"You do what you're told eventually."

I stared.

"That is terrible praise."

"Riggers value eventual."

Inside, something slammed.

The owner emerged first.

Face red.

He went into the street.

Kerren followed more slowly.

"Freight waits," Ressa said.

Kerren pointed at her.

"Do not sound happy."

"Not happy."

She was.

We spent the rest of morning replacing the old hoist line with a verified coil from another supplier.

That required lifting the new rope through the upper wheel, which required somebody on the second platform.

Kerren went up first.

Ressa followed.

I stayed below.

Again.

I wanted to go up.

Not because I had any reason.

Because height implied importance.

This was embarrassing enough that I tried to turn it into a safety concern.

The lower line needed control.

Fine.

I controlled it.

Kerren leaned over the platform rail.

"Feed."

I fed rope.

"Slow."

I slowed.

"Stop."

I stopped.

The injured hand made grip awkward, so I wrapped the line once around my hip the way Old Greg remembered from cliff hauling.

Kerren saw.

"Take that off your body."

I looked up.

"It's controlled."

"If Ressa drops that coil, you become part of the hoist."

I unwrapped it.

He was right.

I disliked that more because I had known the same thing in another life.

Old habit had transferred without the old body or current context.

Good memory could still produce bad behavior.

We got the line over the wheel.

Then the new hook.

Then spent an hour loading low-weight sacks one at a time.

By midday, the system worked.

Kerren did not celebrate.

He wrote numbers.

Ressa coiled the removed line for evidence.

I ate stew from a tin bowl sitting on the loading step with my gloves off and my left hand held above the heat.

The blister had flattened.

The skin around it looked angry but not infected.

Ressa sat beside me.

"You want road because city boring?"

I almost laughed.

Yesterday a crate had nearly crushed Renn.

Today hidden rope damage had stopped half the freight yard.

"No."

"Then why?"

I looked toward the strip of river visible between buildings.

"I wanted roads before I remember wanting them."

Ressa waited.

That sentence sounded insane without context.

"Old plan," I added.

"Then go."

Simple.

Annoying.

"I might."

"You say maybe a lot."

Apparently every relationship eventually discovered this.

"Maybe is accurate."

"Maybe is comfortable."

I looked at her.

"Do you rig philosophy too?"

"Only lunch."

She scraped the bowl clean.

After meal, Kerren tested the hoist at three-quarter load.

Then full.

His decision.

Not mine.

I held the tag line outside the swing zone.

The crate rose.

No bracket movement.

No rope pop.

Ward plate glowed steady blue.

At full height, Kerren had the crank stop.

We waited.

Wind crossed the yard.

The crate moved two inches sideways.

Ward brightened.

Line held.

I watched every connection.

My body wanted to step closer for a better view.

I stayed where the chalk line said.

Not because I had learned restraint in one day.

Because my palm hurt and I remembered Renn's shirt in my fist.

Kerren lowered the crate.

When it touched paving, everyone moved at once.

Freight resumed.

Workers rolled the next crate into position.

The owner reappeared and pretended the previous twelve hours had been his plan.

Kerren handed me eight copper.

"Tomorrow?"

There it was.

Ressa had predicted correctly.

"Posting was possible second."

"This is third."

"I can count."

"Can you?"

"Tavin would say no."

Kerren did not ask who Tavin was.

He pointed toward the warehouse.

"Two days next week. Inventory and lift. Same rate."

Regular work.

Not a road.

Useful.

Money.

People who already knew where to place me.

I hated how attractive that sounded.

"I don't know," I said.

Kerren nodded.

No persuasion.

That bothered me too.

"Tell Guild by fourth day."

I put the coins in my purse.

On the walk home I passed the Guild again.

This time I went inside.

Road side first.

Three postings.

Two required Silver.

One did not.

`DOSS / KELM / SIX TO TEN DAYS / THIRD BODY / CONTACT VELL`

I stared.

Vell.

Jorren's contact.

The posting might have nothing to do with Jorren.

Probably did.

My hand moved toward it.

Then stopped.

Not because ropehouse work existed.

Because Jorren was still away north-east on Doss.

If this was his route, maybe the paper was old.

If new, maybe someone else needed a third.

I could ask Sella.

I did not.

I copied Vell's name onto a scrap.

Put it in my pocket.

Then left.

At Lower Rook, Noll was not in my room.

Brass Spoon night.

The second chair sat empty.

I put the Vell scrap on the table beside Mara's note and the burned seven.

Then moved it beneath the burned seven because apparently paper had developed hierarchy.

I took off my gloves.

My left palm smelled faintly of rope oil.

I washed it twice.

The smell stayed.
