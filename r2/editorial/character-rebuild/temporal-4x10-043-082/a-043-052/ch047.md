# Rehearsal A47: West Spur

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Ossa was already angry when I reached East Gate.

This was reassuring because it meant I had not caused it.

Yet.

The sky was still dark enough that the gate lamps mattered. Mist sat low over the east road. Carts queued beneath the arch while a sleepy clerk checked loads with the moral enthusiasm of a man being paid before sunrise.

Ossa stood beside a narrow mill cart with one boot on the axle beam and both hands inside the left rear hub.

The mule watched her with the patient contempt of an animal that had seen this exact ritual before.

"Morning," I said.

"Hold this."

She handed me an iron pin without looking.

Warm from her hand. Greased black at one end.

I held it.

"Good morning to you too."

"If it falls, clean it."

"Professional intimacy."

"If you talk this much on the road, I charge Vell extra."

She pulled a retaining plate free and dropped it onto the dirt.

I crouched.

The hub smelled like grease, iron, and damp wood. A hairline crack ran across one edge of the plate.

"That the slipping?"

"Part."

"Safe?"

Ossa finally looked at me.

"You asking because you know carts?"

"No."

"Then yes until I say no."

Reasonable boundary delivered badly.

I liked it.

She replaced the plate with a spare from beneath the driver's board. I passed the pin when asked. Then the hammer. Then held the wheel while she drove the new pin through.

Physical usefulness without expertise.

That category suited current me better than I wanted.

The cart was mostly empty. Two wrapped millstones shaped like shallow disks sat tied near the front, outgoing for re-dressing. The return load would be iron pins and small hardware from a forge west of the Spur.

Not glamorous.

Road enough.

The gate clerk checked Ossa's paper.

Then my sword.

"Guard?"

"Second body," Ossa said.

I looked at her.

"Apparently my professional title."

"Better than tall man."

"Debatable."

The clerk stamped the paper and waved us through.

Carrow changed behind the gate faster than expected.

Stone street became packed dirt. Houses thinned into yard walls, then gardens, then open fields still gray under morning mist.

I walked beside the mule for the first mile because sitting immediately felt like fraud.

The road did not care.

My pack settled between shoulder blades. Sword shifted at hip. The healing rope burn on my left palm tightened around the lead rope when Ossa handed it to me while she adjusted a strap.

"Don't wrap around hand," she said.

"I know."

"Then why are you doing it?"

I looked down.

One loose turn had formed around my palm.

I unwrapped it.

"Habit."

"Bad one."

The ropehouse would agree.

We walked.

For a while nothing happened.

This was excellent.

Birds in wet grass. Cart wheel thump every few rotations where one rim section sat slightly high. Ossa humming badly under her breath. The mule stopping once to urinate with no concern for schedule.

I kept waiting for the road to become Road.

Capital letter.

Something worth wanting.

Instead it remained mud, hedges, morning cold, and a cart carrying two stones nobody would sing about.

I loved it anyway.

That realization embarrassed me enough that I did not examine it.

At second bell we reached the south ditch Vell had mentioned.

Ossa stopped before I understood why.

The road ahead narrowed between a low slope on the north and a drainage cut on the south. Rain had eaten away the outer shoulder, leaving a dark bite beneath the road edge.

Fresh collapse.

Cart tracks crossed it already.

One set.

Maybe two.

"Yesterday?" I asked.

"Worse today."

Ossa climbed down into the ditch without waiting.

I followed.

Mud reached my ankle.

The undercut extended farther beneath the road than it looked from above. Roots hung exposed. Water moved through the bottom in a shallow brown stream.

Ossa pushed the butt of her hammer into the soil beneath the wheel track.

It sank six inches.

"No."

"We can hug north."

She looked at the cart width.

"Maybe."

I climbed back up.

Old Greg remembered West Spur broader.

Later road, likely.

Current north edge pressed against a bank with brush and two embedded stones.

If we cut brush, maybe enough.

If the outer shoulder failed under the rear wheel, the cart could roll into the ditch.

Empty enough not to kill us automatically.

Heavy enough to ruin the day.

"Can we unload stones?"

Ossa came up behind me.

"Two hundred each."

"So technically."

"If you brought four more men."

"I did not."

"I noticed."

A farm cart approached from behind.

Older man driving. Two baskets of turnips. He slowed.

"Spur?"

Ossa nodded.

He pointed uphill with one finger.

"Field lane through Marr's."

"Open?"

"If he likes you."

"He doesn't."

"Then three copper."

Ossa swore.

The farmer smiled and continued, his light cart taking the damaged road without trouble.

I watched his rear wheels bounce over the narrow section.

"Could follow."

"His load is vegetables."

"Turnips are dangerous."

Ossa stared.

"Field lane."

We backed the cart forty yards to a split marked by nothing I would have recognized as a road.

Two wagon ruts climbed through wet grass toward a line of poplars.

The mule disliked the turn.

I took the lead while Ossa walked the rear wheel around by hand.

"Left."

I moved left.

"Not you. Mule."

"We are attached."

"Tell him."

I did.

The mule ignored my theory but followed pressure.

The field lane was worse underfoot and better structurally. No ditch. Grass hid stones. We moved slowly.

At the top stood a gate with a chain through it and a man waiting on the other side as if toll collection had summoned him through magic.

Marr, apparently.

Gray beard. Red cap. Holding a rake like it was legal authority.

"Ossa."

"Marr."

"Three copper."

"Two."

"Four for you."

"Then two for him."

Marr looked at me.

"Who?"

"Greg."

"Three."

Excellent negotiation environment.

"Road washed," Ossa said.

"Not my rain."

"City ditch."

"Not my city."

"You live inside it."

"Barely."

I enjoyed him immediately.

Ossa paid three copper from a small route purse and made him sign a scrap stating access.

Marr complained about literacy while signing perfectly.

The gate opened.

His field had been harvested. Stubble wet under wheels. We crossed behind a barn while two dogs barked from a fence.

One dog slipped through.

Black, low, furious.

It ran at the mule.

The mule kicked.

Ossa shouted.

I moved before thought.

Caught the dog's collar with my right hand as it passed.

Momentum spun me half around.

Dog snapped at my forearm.

Leather bracer took teeth.

I lifted enough to keep its front paws off ground.

Not elegant.

"Get him!" Ossa yelled.

Marr was already running from the gate, rake abandoned.

"Pike!"

Terrible dog name.

The dog twisted hard.

My shoulder protested.

I did not hit it.

Old reflex offered a short mana stun behind the jaw.

No.

The dog was frightened and not mine. No reason to add mana to the problem.

I kept physical hold only.

Marr reached us and grabbed the dog's scruff.

"Pike! You bastard."

The dog stopped fighting me to wag at him.

Betrayal.

I let go.

Marr inspected my bracer.

"Bite through?"

"No."

"Good leather."

"Dog is terrible."

"Good dog. Terrible judgment."

Ossa had the mule settled.

One rear hoof had scraped the cart pole but no damage.

She looked at me.

"You okay?"

"Yes."

"Dog?"

Marr said, "Fine."

Pike wagged.

Of course.

We continued.

The field lane rejoined West Spur beyond the collapsed ditch.

My forearm throbbed where teeth compressed leather.

No broken skin.

Ossa drove for another mile without speaking.

Then said, "You didn't use magic."

I looked at her.

"Why would I?"

"Sword man with magic usually uses magic when surprised."

"Usually?"

"Usually stupid."

Compliment maybe.

I accepted nothing.

By midday we ate Noll's dumplings cold beside a milestone that had lost its number.

Ossa ate the torn one.

I objected.

"Mine."

"You gave me half."

"Half of the perfect one."

"Road law."

"Invented."

She swallowed.

"Still law."

The West Spur mill appeared in late afternoon beside a narrow stream turning two wheels.

Not large.

Flour dust coated the loading shed beams white.

Workers rolled our two stones off using a timber ramp, three men, one woman, and language strong enough to move weight independently.

I helped with a pry bar under Ossa's direction.

The stone shifted one inch.

Then another.

My palm hurt.

I changed grip.

A mill worker named Kes noticed.

"Hand?"

"Fine."

"Then stop holding like idiot."

He moved my left hand farther down the bar.

Better leverage.

Current strangers remained efficient teachers.

We finished unloading at sunset.

The forge return pins would not be ready until morning.

One night became real.

The mill had a loft for drivers and laborers. Straw mattress. Shared wash basin. Dinner included thick oat stew, bread, and enough salt to make the road feel worth taking.

I sat on the loft steps afterward and removed my boots.

No blister.

Small victory unavailable to anyone else.

Ossa sat two steps below repairing a frayed strap with waxed thread.

"Worth fourteen?" she asked.

"Ask tomorrow."

"Road philosopher."

"Absolutely not."

Outside, water wheels turned in the dark.

My forearm had dog-shaped bruises.

My palm had ropehouse pain.

My room key sat in the inside pocket where I had checked it twice already.

I took it out.

Looked at it.

Put it back.

Ossa noticed.

"Lose something?"

"No."

"Then why count key?"

"Security."

"One key?"

"Very secure."

She shook her head and kept sewing.

I listened to the mill wheel until sleep won.
