# Rehearsal A48: The Shoulder

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The road tried to leave without us before breakfast.

Rain started sometime before dawn.

Not hard.

Steady.

The kind that made every surface reconsider whether it wanted to remain where people had built it.

I woke to the mill loft roof ticking above my head and Ossa already pulling on boots.

"Pins ready?"

"Forge opens at first bell."

"Then why awake?"

"Road."

She looked through the loft window.

I followed.

The stream had risen.

Not dramatically. Brown water higher against wheel housings, moving faster beneath the footbridge.

"West Spur?"

"Shoulder."

Right.

Yesterday's collapse.

Rain overnight.

We ate standing in the mill kitchen. Oat porridge, one egg each, bread hard enough to defend property.

The forge runner arrived before first bell carrying a wooden crate of iron pins wrapped in oiled cloth.

Forty-eight pounds according to his paper.

Ossa checked every bundle.

I asked why count individually if crate seal intact.

She said, "Because seal doesn't carry them home."

I stopped asking.

We loaded the crate near the axle, tied low.

The cart was still lighter than yesterday.

That should have made the washed road easier.

Should was an ambitious word.

The rain softened around second bell, but the lane behind Marr's field had become mud soup.

Ossa considered it from the Spur split.

"No."

"Marr would charge five in rain."

"Marr would charge for breathing if he could meter it."

"Can we use main?"

"Walk it."

We left the cart forty yards back and inspected the damaged section on foot.

Yesterday's undercut had widened.

A chunk of outer shoulder had fallen into the ditch, taking half a wheel rut with it.

The north side remained firm but narrow.

I climbed the bank and pulled brush aside.

One embedded stone could be moved.

Maybe.

Large enough to require lever.

Ossa looked.

"If we clear north six inches, cart fits."

"Six inches is a lot when wheel wants gravity."

"Yes."

No argument.

We cut brush with my knife and her small axe.

Then used a fence rail found twenty yards away as lever against the stone.

It moved once.

Stopped.

I repositioned.

Ossa put shoulder into rail with me.

"On three."

"Why three?"

"Because one is surprise."

"Two?"

"Preparation."

"You have thought about this."

"Three."

We pushed.

Stone rolled half a turn uphill and settled into wet grass.

The cleared edge gave perhaps eight inches.

Enough on paper.

Roads did not use paper.

Ossa returned for the cart.

I stayed at the narrow section to guide wheel placement.

Rain mist collected on my eyelashes.

The mule approached slowly.

Cart wheel thump.

Thump.

Thump.

Ossa walked beside the animal, one hand on bridle.

"North," I called.

She adjusted.

Front wheel passed.

Left rear reached the tightest point.

I crouched beside the road edge, watching distance between iron rim and collapse.

Four inches.

Three.

The road surface compressed under wheel.

I saw the crack before I heard it.

Thin line opening from ditch edge toward the rut.

"Stop."

Ossa stopped immediately.

Good.

The rear wheel sat half over supported road, half over soil that had just declared independence.

"Back?" she asked.

I looked.

Backing would load the same patch again.

Forward moved toward firmer north edge but the wheel needed another two feet.

The crack widened a finger.

"Nobody moves."

"Helpful."

I stood slowly.

The mule shifted one hoof.

Cart creaked.

"Easy," Ossa said.

I looked at the load.

Forty-eight pounds pins.

No millstones.

Driver board mostly empty.

We could unload the crate.

"Pins off."

Ossa nodded before I finished.

We untied from the safe north side.

The crate was heavy enough to make arms notice, not impossible.

I lifted one end.

Ossa the other.

We carried it ten feet forward and set on road.

Cart rose slightly on springs.

Crack stopped widening.

"Good," I said.

Then hated the evaluator fragment.

Ossa did not care about prose.

"Now what?"

I looked at road geometry.

Old Greg had moved wagons through worse.

Different wagons.

Different body.

Different soil.

Magic offered options.

I could brace the wheel upward with force.

Maybe.

My moving support practice was primitive here. A static support under an unmoving object was easier.

The cart was already stopped.

"I can take some weight off the wheel."

Ossa's face changed.

"Magic?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

Honest answer.

"Not enough to lift cart. Enough maybe to reduce pressure while you move two feet."

"Maybe?"

"Yes."

She looked at the crack.

Then at the mule.

"What happens if you fail?"

"Nothing extra. Weight returns."

"Does cart jump?"

"No."

"Mule feel?"

"Probably not if support is wheel-side."

Probably.

Bad word.

"I can test static first."

Ossa nodded.

Consent mattered more than pride.

I crouched beside the wheel from the north side and set my palm against the iron rim.

Opened mana sense.

Wheel, axle, load.

Simple shapes were never simple once force mattered.

I built a small plane beneath the lower rim, angled against the firm northward stone base rather than soft outer shoulder.

Not lift.

Support.

Pressure entered my forearm like holding a door closed.

The cart rose perhaps a quarter inch.

Ossa watched the spring.

"Again."

I released.

Cart settled.

No jump.

"Again longer."

I did.

Ten seconds.

Fifteen.

My shoulder began to burn.

"Enough," she said.

I released.

"Useful?"

"Maybe."

Fair revenge.

We rested two minutes.

Then Ossa took the bridle.

"You say stop, I stop."

"Yes."

"If magic feels wrong?"

"I say stop."

"If I say stop?"

"I stop."

Clear.

I set the plane again.

"Go."

The mule stepped.

Wheel rolled onto the support.

Moving support became harder immediately.

Static geometry wanted to stay where I put it. Wheel did not.

I shifted the plane forward in small increments.

Not smooth.

Pulse.

Move.

Pulse.

Move.

Cart advanced six inches.

My jaw clenched.

Another six.

The road edge cracked louder.

I overcorrected upward.

Wheel bumped.

Mule flinched.

"Easy!" Ossa said.

Not to me.

Maybe both.

I reduced force.

Cart moved.

One foot.

Two.

Then the outer soil dropped.

A whole slab broke free beneath where the wheel had been.

Brown mud collapsed into ditch with a wet roar.

The cart lurched north.

I lost the plane.

Wheel slammed down on firm road.

Ossa was pulled sideways by bridle.

The mule jumped forward.

"Brake!"

I reached the rear lever.

Wrong side.

Cart moving.

I ran two steps, grabbed the iron handle, and threw weight down.

Brake shoe caught.

Rear wheel skidded.

Cart stopped.

The mule kept trying for one step until harness tightened.

Then stood shaking.

I stood bent over the brake, lungs suddenly working too hard.

Ossa had one knee in mud.

"You hit?"

"No."

"Mule?"

She checked legs.

No cut.

No obvious strain.

The road behind us now had a gap large enough that return traffic would not use it safely.

We had crossed.

Not elegantly.

Crossed.

I released the brake slowly.

My hands shook.

Not much.

Enough.

Ossa saw.

She did not comment.

We carried the pin crate back and reloaded on firm ground.

Then spent another twenty minutes dragging brush across the damaged approach and tying a red cloth to a branch as warning.

"City?" I asked.

"West Spur post has runner."

"Will they close?"

"If they have sense."

"Dangerous assumption."

"You live there."

We moved on.

For the next hour I replayed the crossing.

Could have unloaded sooner.

Could have used field lane.

Could have avoided magic entirely.

Could have supported axle instead of wheel.

Old Greg supplied six better techniques and three stories of worse roads.

None changed that current me had held a crude moving plane for maybe four seconds under real load and nearly lost it when earth disappeared.

Ossa eventually said, "Stop thinking so loud."

"I'm not speaking."

"Your face is."

Mara's slander spreading.

"What did I do wrong?"

Ossa considered.

"Nothing that made me not hire you again."

That was not the answer I expected.

"That's a low standard."

"Road standard."

"Specifics."

She sighed.

"You saw crack. Good. We unloaded. Good. Magic worked until road failed. You grabbed brake. Good."

Too many evaluator words. Apparently contagious.

"And?"

"You wanted magic because it was yours."

There.

I looked at her.

"Maybe."

"Field lane cost three."

"And was mud."

"Still three."

"Marr hates you."

"Marr hates weather."

I laughed.

Then stopped.

She was right enough to hurt.

I had not pushed recklessly. But once a magical solution existed, I had wanted to test it.

Partly because it helped.

Partly because current Greg wanted evidence that the strange thing inside his head could move into his hands.

Both could be true.

We reached a road post near noon.

Ossa reported the collapse.

The post clerk sent two workers with a barrier cart.

No drama.

Just closure.

We ate in a shed while rain finally stopped.

My shoulders ached from the moving support.

The crate of iron pins sat under canvas beside us.

One worker asked what happened.

Ossa said, "Road fell."

He nodded.

Enough story for him.

On the way back toward Carrow the sky cleared violently, blue appearing between clouds as if nothing had happened.

We took a northern farm road that added two hours.

No complaint from me.

At sunset we camped beside a stand of alder because the detour made Carrow impossible before gate close.

Ossa built a small fire.

I gathered wet wood and discovered exactly how many pieces could look dry while remaining useless.

Dinner was beans, bread, and the last of Noll's dumplings warmed in a pan.

I gave Ossa the perfect one this time.

She looked suspicious.

"Guilt?"

"Diplomacy."

"Same thing sometimes."

We ate.

After dark my shoulder began to stiffen.

Not injury.

Mana fatigue layered over physical strain.

I lay on my bedroll looking at stars through alder branches.

Road.

I had wanted this.

Mud, dog teeth, collapsing shoulder, cold pins, beans.

I still wanted it.

That was inconveniently clarifying.

Then the mule farted close enough to ruin the moment.

Better.
