# Rehearsal B53: East Gate

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

I wanted a road until somebody put one under my boots.

Then I wanted breakfast.

These desires were not mutually exclusive, but the second had become urgent.

Jorren stood beside the east gate with a cup of something steaming in one hand and half a fried cake in the other.

He had purchased both after telling me we were leaving before sunrise.

This was treachery.

"Where did you get that?"

He looked at the cake.

"Food stall."

"There was no food stall."

"There was when I came through."

"You came through ten minutes ago."

"Yes."

I looked behind him.

A woman had opened a brazier cart beside the gate wall while I had been arguing with the strap on my bedroll.

Smoke rose around a stack of fried dough.

Fine.

The world had developed breakfast without consulting me.

I went to buy one.

"We're late," Jorren said.

I stopped.

"You are eating."

"I was early."

"So you used your advantage selfishly."

"Yes."

He took another bite.

I bought two cakes.

The second was planning.

When I returned, Beren had arrived with the cart.

I knew his name before I knew his face.

Jorren had mentioned him after Doss. Broad shoulders. Broken nose. Two good mules. Tended to whistle when reading because apparently literacy required accompaniment.

The man climbing down from the driver's bench had all of those except the whistle.

He was swearing instead.

"Left wheel again?" Jorren asked.

Beren kicked the rear wheel.

"Pin's holding. Hub sounds like a dying priest."

I looked at the wheel.

"Specific religion?"

Beren looked at me.

"You Greg?"

"Depends."

"Jorren says yes."

"Then probably."

He pointed at the wheel.

"You know carts?"

"No."

Beren sighed.

"Finally an honest man."

He crouched beside the hub.

I ate one fried cake while he and Jorren lifted the cart corner using a short jack and a block of wood.

The cart was a plain freight wagon with side rails, canvas bundle cover, a narrow driver's board, and enough patched timber to suggest several owners had believed replacement was a moral failure.

Our load was not glamorous.

Three sealed barrels of lamp oil.

Two crates of brass fittings.

A wrapped bundle of wool blankets.

One box of ceramic insulators going to a mill outside Kelm.

And six sacks of something Beren called road meal and I called flour until he showed me it contained oats, salt, dried onion, and the remains of hope.

The job had come through Jorren.

Not the Guild.

Not me.

A grain factor named Vell had introduced him to Beren on Doss. Beren needed a third body because his usual guard had taken a warehouse post after his wife threatened to sell his boots if he left again before winter.

Jorren had asked me after returning to Carrow.

Not immediately.

Two days later.

That detail still bothered me more than it deserved.

I had wanted to be the first person he thought of.

Apparently current friendship included discovering I was not always that person.

The route was Carrow to Doss Ferry, then north-east along the mill road to Kelm. Six days if nothing went badly. Eight if weather became involved. Ten if the world expressed itself creatively.

I had said yes before asking the rate.

Then asked the rate because I had standards.

Twenty-eight copper plus food and a share of return freight premium if we took one.

Good money.

Not owner money.

Road money.

I had packed that night.

Two socks finally gained a purpose.

Now, standing outside East Gate while Beren listened to a wheel, I tried not to feel triumphant about socks.

"Grease," Beren said.

Jorren handed him a small pot.

"You didn't grease it?"

"I greased it."

"Then why?"

"Because roads hate me."

"Personal?"

Beren looked at me again.

"You always talk like this?"

Jorren said, "Worse after rain."

We left Carrow fifteen minutes late.

The east road ran between market gardens first.

Low fences.

Wet fields.

Rows of late greens under pale morning light.

Carrow's walls shrank behind us slowly enough that I kept looking back without needing to admit I was looking back.

Lower Rook was invisible from here.

Mara farther west than the entire city.

Tavin south.

Noll probably asleep after Brass Spoon.

The second chair currently unoccupied unless he had developed lockpicking.

My room key was in my coat.

Maud had the spare.

I touched the key through the fabric.

Then immediately moved my hand because Jorren was walking beside me.

He saw anyway.

"Forget something?"

"No."

"Then why pocket?"

"Money."

"Other pocket."

I hated observant friends.

"Key."

Jorren nodded.

No joke.

That was suspiciously kind.

I ate the second fried cake to prevent conversation.

By full morning, Carrow had disappeared behind low hills.

The road changed from packed city gravel to brown dirt with a raised stone center where wagon wheels had failed to agree on a single track for several generations.

Beren drove.

Jorren walked left side.

I walked right.

The first hour felt excellent.

Cold air.

Open ground.

Sword at my hip.

Pack riding correctly after I tightened the shoulder strap.

No one asking me about chairs.

No paper.

No river clerk.

No room walls close enough to hear Noll swear through.

Road.

Young Greg had wanted this.

Old Greg had spent decades doing this.

Current Greg finally had it.

By the third hour my right heel hurt.

This felt insulting.

I ignored it.

The road climbed gradually through scrub pine and rough pasture.

A shepherd moved a flock across us near midday. We stopped while forty sheep demonstrated that collective intelligence had limits.

One stared at me from the ditch.

I stared back.

Old Greg had fought dragons.

The sheep remained unmoved.

"You're limping," Jorren said.

"No."

"Yes."

"Road is uneven."

"Same road under both feet."

Beren laughed from the bench.

I considered killing him.

Instead I adjusted my stride.

Worse.

Jorren stopped walking.

"Boot."

"We're moving."

"Boot."

"You have become Noll."

"That an insult?"

"Complicated."

Beren pulled the mules to a stop without being asked.

I resented everyone.

I took off the boot.

Blister.

Small.

Already rubbed raw at one edge because I had spent an hour proving my foot was dishonest.

Jorren looked at it.

"Good job."

"Thank you."

"Could've stopped earlier."

"Then we would have stopped earlier."

"Yes."

I understood the logic and disliked it.

Beren tossed me a strip of soft cloth.

"Wrap."

"I have cloth."

"Then use yours."

I used his.

This was how independence died.

We ate noon meal beside the road.

Bread.

Hard cheese.

Dried pear.

The mules ate better-looking grain and did not appreciate it.

Beren checked the left rear hub again.

Jorren lay flat on the grass with one forearm over his eyes.

I sat on my pack.

The sky was clear enough to make rain feel theoretical.

Old Greg remembered this route.

Not exactly.

Roads moved.

Settlements expanded.

Bridges replaced fords. Fords returned when bridges failed. Trees grew where fields had been. Fields appeared where woods had burned.

But the east line out of Carrow had existed in every version I knew.

Doss Ferry should be two days from here at this pace.

Kelm another day and a half beyond depending on the mill road.

There would be a stone bridge near the ridge tomorrow.

I remembered sleeping beneath it once at thirty-two because rain had washed out the lower camp.

Maybe.

The memory came with cold feet and somebody laughing.

No face.

No name.

Old Greg's life remained generous with infrastructure and stingy with people.

I looked at Jorren.

Current person.

Forearm over his face.

Mud dried on one boot from a puddle ten minutes earlier.

He had chosen Beren first on Doss.

No.

That was not fair.

He had chosen Beren because Beren owned the job.

Different thing.

I knew the difference.

My stomach did not care.

"Why'd you ask me?" I said.

Jorren moved his arm enough to look at me.

"For road?"

"Yes."

"Need third."

I waited.

He put his arm back.

"Excellent."

"You wanted poetry?"

"No."

I did.

Slightly.

Beren stood from the wheel.

"I asked him to find somebody who wouldn't rob me."

"High praise."

"He said you probably wouldn't."

Jorren laughed into his sleeve.

I threw a pear stem at him.

It landed nowhere near.

My shoulder had not adjusted to projectile friendship.

We moved again.

Afternoon became warmer.

The road narrowed between two low wooded slopes. Wagon ruts held old rainwater in black strips. In places, Beren drove one wheel onto the raised center to avoid the deepest mud.

My blister settled into a manageable burn.

Then my left shoulder began aching from the pack.

Current body had opinions about travel.

Old Greg's memories did not include most of them.

That seemed unfair too.

Around fourth bell, we reached a roadside shrine made from three stacked stones under an ash tree.

Blue thread had been tied around the upper stone.

Beren stopped the cart.

He climbed down.

Took a pinch of road meal from one sack.

Set it at the base of the stones.

I watched.

"What is that?"

"Food."

"I can see that."

"Then you're improving."

Jorren took a pinch too.

I looked at him.

"You did this before?"

"Doss road."

"Why?"

He shrugged.

"Beren does."

"That is not a reason."

Beren said, "Road takes wheels from people who don't feed it."

I looked at the road.

Then the wheel.

Then Beren.

He was serious.

Not completely.

Enough.

Old Greg remembered roadside offerings across five countries. Usually local spirit practice. Sometimes actual minor entities. Sometimes social habit surviving after the magic vanished.

This one meant nothing to me.

I could inspect the stones.

Sense for mana.

Ask what Beren believed.

Instead I took a pinch of road meal and put it beside the others.

Jorren smiled.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You are also bad at nothing."

"Noll says that?"

"No. I say it."

Right.

Current people could own sentences too.

We camped before sunset in a cut meadow beside a narrow stream.

Beren chose the site because the ground rose slightly and the mules could graze without reaching the road.

I would have chosen ten yards closer to the trees for wind cover.

I almost said so.

Then I noticed old hoof marks in the softer soil beneath the branches.

Many.

Large.

Not horse.

"Glasshorn?" I asked.

Beren nodded.

"They come through nights sometimes."

"This far south?"

"This road, yes."

Old Greg remembered their migration farther east.

Different century.

Different herds.

Different anything.

I looked at the trees again.

Beren saw me.

"You want shade, sleep there."

"I prefer life."

"Good preference."

We made camp in the open.

Physical travel continued after walking stopped.

Unhitch mules.

Water them.

Check wheel.

Gather deadfall.

Set bedrolls.

Cook road meal until it became a thick onion porridge with enough salt to preserve a corpse.

My hands smelled like mule leather and smoke.

The blister hurt when I removed my boot.

I cleaned it properly this time before anyone could look.

Jorren noticed anyway.

He said nothing.

I hated that more than mockery.

After dark, the three of us sat near the small fire.

Beren repaired a strap.

Jorren shuffled cards.

I watched sparks move upward.

No city sound.

No wall.

No footsteps above or below.

No Noll.

The silence was larger than Lower Rook silence.

Not empty.

Insects.

Stream.

Mule teeth tearing grass.

Wind pushing through the meadow.

I had wanted this.

I still wanted it.

I also wanted to know whether Noll had remembered his carter supper and whether Maud had rented the room across the hall and whether Tavin had received my letter and whether Mara had reached Halden without another Vey clerk miscounting boxes.

None of those wants canceled the road.

They occupied it.

I put my hand into my coat pocket.

The Lower Rook key clicked against a copper.

Jorren looked at the movement.

This time he said nothing.

I took my hand back out.

"Cards?" he asked.

"Your tell changed."

"Again?"

"Probably."

"Then lose money finding it."

Beren looked up from the strap.

"You two play for coin?"

"Unfortunately," I said.

Jorren dealt.

I lost three copper before the fire burned low.

This felt more like a road than any memory had promised.
