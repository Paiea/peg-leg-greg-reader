# Rehearsal B54: The Bridge

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The bridge existed.

This was disappointing.

I had spent most of the morning preparing for the possibility that Old Greg remembered a bridge which current reality had neglected to build.

Instead, after three hours of climbing road, we rounded a pine-covered shoulder and found gray stone spanning the cut exactly where memory placed it.

Four arches.

Low side walls.

Water flashing beneath the second span.

A small shrine built into the eastern parapet.

Familiar enough that my chest tightened before my mind found a reason.

"There," I said.

Beren looked over from the driver's bench.

"Yes."

"Bridge."

"Also yes."

Jorren walked beside the left mule.

"You sound surprised."

"I am observing."

"Badly."

I ignored him.

The blister on my heel had survived the night under clean wrap.

My back had not.

Sleeping on ground at nineteen should have been easier than sleeping on ground at fifty-nine.

Old Greg's memories were liars.

I had woken stiff between the shoulder blades, one hip numb, and a pine needle inside my shirt in a location no tree had permission to access.

Road life.

Excellent.

The bridge grew larger as we approached.

Then details arrived.

A timber rail had been built along one side.

Fresh pale wood against old stone.

The third arch bore a dark water mark six feet above the current stream.

One parapet section had been rebuilt with blocks that did not match.

And a painted board stood before the entrance.

`ONE WAGON AT A TIME.`

Below that:

`NO HEAVY CROSSING AFTER RAIN.`

Beren slowed the mules.

"Dry."

I looked at the road.

Mud still occupied the deeper ruts from older weather, but the last day had been clear.

"Should hold."

Beren glanced at me.

"You bridge man?"

"No."

"Good."

He pulled the cart to the shoulder before the stone.

"Then wait."

He climbed down.

Jorren went with him.

I stayed beside the mules because apparently road authority had been designed specifically to injure my pride.

Beren walked onto the bridge.

Not down the center.

He followed the right wheel track, stamping one heel occasionally, looking at mortar seams, leaning over the parapet at the third arch.

Jorren watched the opposite side.

I could have done this.

Probably.

Old Greg had assessed hundreds of crossings.

That thought arrived complete and convincing.

Then I remembered yesterday's roadside shrine meant nothing to me.

Fine.

I held a mule.

The left one tried to eat the sleeve of my coat.

"Stop."

It chewed harder.

I pushed its face away.

The mule looked offended.

"Your transport is attacking me," I called.

Beren did not turn.

"Means she likes you."

"Then she needs boundaries."

Jorren laughed from the bridge.

They returned after several minutes.

Beren had a chip of mortar in one hand.

"Crossing. Slow. Nobody rides."

"Problem?" I asked.

"Third arch repair's fresh."

"Bad?"

"Don't know."

"Then why cross?"

Beren looked at the sky.

"Because if we turn around every time I don't know something, Vell pays us in philosophy."

Good point.

He checked the load straps again.

Jorren moved the brass fitting crates slightly toward center.

I helped.

Weight distribution.

Actual useful thing.

We untied one side rail, shifted a barrel two inches, retied it.

My heel hurt when I climbed onto the wheel hub.

I ignored that less successfully than yesterday.

"Wrap holding?" Jorren asked.

"Yes."

"You checked?"

"This morning."

"Actually?"

"I can be responsible."

Beren said, "Write date down."

Everyone was hilarious on roads.

We began crossing.

Beren led the mules from the front.

Jorren walked beside the left rear wheel.

I took right rear.

The cart wheels struck stone with a harder sound than dirt.

Clack.

Clack.

Slow.

The first arch passed under us.

I felt vibration through my boots.

Normal.

Probably.

Second arch.

Water louder below.

The right mule hesitated.

Beren clicked his tongue.

She moved.

Third arch.

The repaired section.

I looked at the parapet.

New blocks.

Different mortar.

A thin line ran down one seam.

Crack?

No.

Water stain.

The wheel beside me hit a shallow depression.

The cart tilted.

Not much.

My body moved before thought.

One hand went to the side rail as if I could hold three thousand pounds upright with nineteen-year-old optimism.

Jorren saw.

"Don't."

"I wasn't."

"You were holding wagon."

"Steadying."

"With hand."

"Useful hand."

Beren did not turn.

"If wagon goes, let wagon go."

I removed my hand.

The cart rolled level.

We crossed the fourth arch.

Nothing collapsed.

No prophecy.

No revelation.

I was almost annoyed.

Then a bell rang behind us.

One sharp strike.

Beren stopped.

A man had come out of a small hut built into the western bank above the bridge.

I had not noticed the hut.

He wore a gray coat and held a hand bell.

"Toll!"

Beren swore.

"New?" Jorren asked.

"Since spring."

"You knew."

"I hoped he was asleep."

The toll keeper came down the slope carrying a ledger.

Of course.

"Freight weight?"

Beren gave him the load sheet.

The man read slowly.

Then looked at the wagon.

"Four copper."

"Three."

"Four."

"Road charter says three under standard axle."

"Bridge repair assessment."

Beren pointed at the sign.

"Sign doesn't say money."

"Says bridge."

"That is not accounting."

The toll keeper shrugged.

I liked Beren more every hour.

They argued.

Jorren sat on the parapet.

I looked at the hut.

Old Greg did not remember it.

No toll.

No gray-coated man.

Bridge had been free.

Maybe because future road authority absorbed maintenance.

Maybe because the hut burned.

Maybe because Old Greg had crossed illegally at night.

The memory contained rain, cold feet, laughter.

No money.

I wanted to ask the toll keeper when the assessment started.

Why repairs.

Whether floods had worsened.

Instead I listened.

"Three and one road chit," Beren said.

"Four."

"Three and meal token."

"I don't eat your food."

"Then you're making poor choices."

The toll keeper's mouth twitched.

"Three and half."

"You cut copper?"

"I have bits."

Dena's half-copper economy had infected the road too.

Beren paid three and half.

When the keeper left, I said, "You could have paid four five minutes ago."

"Yes."

"You saved half copper."

"Yes."

"We lost five minutes."

Beren climbed onto the driver's board.

"My five minutes."

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

Money meant different things depending on ownership.

Road lesson trying to become neat.

I refused it.

We walked.

The road after the bridge climbed more steeply.

Pine gave way to mixed oak and low scrub.

By midday, cloud had begun gathering over the northern ridge.

Beren looked at it three times.

"Rain?" I asked.

"Maybe evening."

"Bridge sign said after rain."

"We're past bridge."

"Return?"

"Different problem."

He thought in routes one direction at a time.

I thought in failure trees.

Neither felt obviously superior while my heel rubbed.

We stopped near a spring where a stone trough had been built beside the road.

The water was clear.

Cold enough to hurt teeth.

I refilled my bottle.

Jorren removed one boot.

I stared.

"What?"

"You. Boot."

"Pebble."

"You mocked me yesterday."

"Yes."

"Hypocrisy."

"Correct."

He shook the boot until a stone fell out.

No shame.

I envied that.

Beren fed the mules oats.

I sat on the trough edge and unwrapped my heel.

Skin looked better.

Still red.

The cloth from Beren had stuck slightly where the blister broke.

I peeled it off slowly.

Pain sharpened.

Jorren watched.

"Want help?"

"No."

"Good."

I looked at him.

"That sounded hostile."

"You say no before question finishes."

"I knew question."

"That's why."

I cleaned the heel.

Rewrapped it with my own cloth this time.

The road curved above us.

A wagon approached downhill carrying stacked cages.

Inside the cages were pale birds with long black beaks.

Dozens.

They made no sound.

I watched them pass.

"What are those?"

Beren said, "Rain crows."

"Those are not crows."

"Then tell them."

"Why cages?"

"Mine crews use them."

"For?"

"Bad air."

Interesting.

Old Greg knew canary-like practices with mana-sensitive birds in deep works.

These birds were unfamiliar.

"They magic-sensitive?"

Beren shrugged.

"They die."

"Most things do."

"Before miners."

Useful and horrible.

I looked back until the wagon disappeared below the curve.

Kelm had mill works, stone cuts, shallow mines farther north.

Current systems.

Current people.

My mind began assembling a future lecture on ventilation.

Then I noticed Beren had already moved.

I put my boot on and followed.

Afternoon rain arrived early.

First drops large enough to leave dark circles in dust.

Then steady.

We pulled canvas tighter over the load.

I put on my rain wrap.

Jorren did not have one.

"Where's yours?"

"Lost Doss."

"How?"

"Wind."

"You lost an entire wrap to wind?"

"It wanted road more."

Beren handed him a spare oilcloth from beneath the bench.

Jorren put it over his shoulders.

"Thanks."

No pride problem.

Again annoying.

The road changed within twenty minutes.

Dust became slick brown skin.

Wheel ruts deepened.

The mules slowed.

Beren got down to walk.

We all walked.

Rain found the gap at my neck and ran down my spine.

My socks stayed dry.

Victory.

The road descended into a narrow valley where water already ran along both edges.

Beren stopped before a fork.

Left road climbed immediately.

Right followed the valley floor.

"Which?" Jorren asked.

Beren looked right.

"Usual."

I looked left.

Old Greg remembered the valley route flooding after hard rain.

Not today specifically.

Not current drainage.

But the shape was obvious too. Low ground. Two side gullies feeding it.

"Left," I said.

Beren looked at me.

"Why?"

"Valley collects water."

"Left adds four miles."

"Right might add drowning."

"Rain's twenty minutes old."

"Upstream could have more."

Beren looked at the sky.

Then road.

Jorren waited.

No one automatically accepted my memory-shaped fear.

Good.

I disliked it.

Beren walked ten yards down the right fork.

Crouched.

Touched the flowing ditch.

Then looked toward the trees.

"Water brown already."

He came back.

"Left."

Relief arrived too strongly.

I wanted him to say I had been right.

He did not.

We took the climb.

It was awful.

Four extra miles sounded abstract until every one gained mud.

The mules strained.

We pushed behind the cart on the steepest sections.

My heel became fire.

My thighs burned.

Water ran off my nose.

At one point my boot slid and I landed one knee in mud deep enough to swallow dignity.

Jorren laughed.

I threw mud at him.

Missed.

Beren shouted, "Push, children!"

We pushed.

Near dusk the left road rejoined the main ridge route above the valley.

We could see the right road below.

Brown water covered part of it.

Not catastrophic.

Deep enough that wagon wheels would have been half-submerged.

Beren looked down.

Then at me.

I waited.

He said, "Four miles."

"Yes."

"Still hate you."

"Acceptable."

Jorren grinned.

We camped wet.

Everything took longer.

Fire required dry shavings from Beren's sealed box.

Bedroll ground had to be scraped of surface water.

Mules steamed in cold air.

My hands shook slightly when I unlaced my boot.

Not fear.

Fatigue.

The blister had reopened.

Blood mixed with rainwater in the cloth.

I stared.

Road.

Actual road.

I had chosen the safer fork and still hurt myself by walking on a damaged heel because apparently competence operated locally.

Jorren sat beside me.

He held out a small tin.

"Salve."

"I have."

He did not withdraw it.

I looked at my pack.

My salve was buried beneath the bedroll strap I had already tied under canvas.

I took his.

"Thank you."

"Write date."

"Fuck you."

He laughed.

I rubbed salve into the skin.

The rain softened after dark.

Beren cooked road meal.

I ate two bowls.

Then lay down with my foot outside the blanket until cold forced it in.

The bridge had existed.

The road I remembered had existed.

The flood risk had existed too.

Some memory had helped.

Some observation had helped.

Mostly Beren had walked ten yards and touched water.

I fell asleep before finding a satisfying hierarchy.
