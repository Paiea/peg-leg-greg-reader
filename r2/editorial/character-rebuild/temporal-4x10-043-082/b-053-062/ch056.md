# Rehearsal B56: Wren Post

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

My face looked worse than it felt.

This was useful because Beren remained annoyed with me and sympathy would have complicated things.

Morning at Wren Post began with a mirror polished enough to reveal three swollen moth bites across my cheek and one small red mark at my jaw where a wing edge had cut skin.

Jorren saw me inspecting it.

"Pretty."

"Your ear is still larger."

He touched the bite near his ear.

"Can't see."

"Trust me."

"No."

Fair.

Beren was in stable yard checking both mules before breakfast. His right palm had been wrapped again overnight. The rope burn ran across the center and into two fingers.

I hovered near the gate for perhaps five seconds before he looked up.

"If ask hand, fine."

"I was going to ask mule."

"Mules also fine."

"Good."

He stared.

"You say good too much."

Everyone had become an editor.

I moved closer.

The left mule, the one I had touched with calming mana, stood eating hay. No obvious distress. It flicked one ear when I approached.

I kept both hands visible and did not touch.

Beren noticed that too.

"You can pet mule."

"I know."

"Then why act like priest?"

"Professional respect."

"For mule?"

"For your mule."

That landed.

He nodded once.

I scratched the animal's neck.

It tolerated me.

Current peace treaty.

Breakfast was fried potatoes, eggs, and bread. Cloth merchant from our room had already left, taking his snoring with him and leaving one sock under bed. Wren Post owner held it up with tongs and announced a one-copper lost-property fee if he returned.

Road institutions remained creative.

We planned next leg over a stained map scratched into the dining table itself.

Beren traced with one finger.

"Low Perrin road bad. We stay north until White Fork, then down. Adds half day."

Jorren said, "Moths?"

"Moths plus ditch."

"Wren to White Fork how long?"

"Four hours if dry."

Outside rain began.

Beren looked through window.

"Five."

The road had opinions.

We left under oilcloth.

My blister was less raw but still present. I wrapped before boots without being told.

Jorren saw.

"Learning."

"Do not."

He smiled.

The north lane climbed through orchards and then into pine country. Moth traps disappeared. Road surface changed to gravel over clay, better drainage and worse footing for my heel.

I walked anyway.

Not to prove something.

Mostly.

Beren drove the first hour, then made Jorren take board while he rested hand.

That changed our movement.

Jorren knew carts enough to avoid disaster and not enough to stop asking Beren every third decision.

"Left rut?"

"Right."

"Why?"

"Left deep."

"Looks same."

"Then use eyes tomorrow."

I walked beside mule trying not to laugh.

At one incline, rear wheel spun in clay.

Jorren pushed rein wrong way.

Mule stepped sideways.

Beren swore, jumped down, and grabbed lead with injured hand before remembering hand was injured.

He hissed.

I caught cart rail.

"Brake?"

"Block."

I shoved a stone behind rear wheel.

Jorren climbed down.

We spent ten minutes placing branches in rut, then all three pushed while mule hauled.

Wheel climbed free.

Mud sprayed my trousers to hip.

Beren looked at me.

"Road."

"I am beginning to understand why you say it like disease."

"Good disease."

Interesting.

At White Fork we found the bridge closed.

Not broken.

Occupied.

A flock of long-necked black birds covered the span and both banks, hundreds of them. Some stood knee-deep in river. Others packed railings shoulder to shoulder.

No one crossed.

Three carts waited on our side.

A farmer sat on wheel smoking pipe.

"How long?" Beren asked.

"Since dawn."

"Why nobody move them?"

Farmer looked at him.

"You move them."

Beren did not.

Birds were taller than geese, narrower, with blue sheen on wings. Old Greg memory offered river stilts, migratory fish-eaters, territorial around nesting.

But this was not nesting season according to remembered calendar.

Maybe different species.

Maybe current migration shifted.

Maybe memory wrong.

"Do they bite?" Jorren asked.

Farmer said, "They stab."

Better.

A woman with a basket approached from bridge house carrying a brass pan.

She began striking it with wooden spoon.

Clang.

Clang.

Clang.

Birds closest to house lifted heads.

Then slowly walked off bridge.

Not flew.

Walked.

I stared.

"Why pan?"

Farmer said, "Lessa feeds them grain. Pan means food downstream."

Of course.

Current local knowledge defeated threat classification with kitchenware.

Birds followed woman along bank.

Bridge cleared in three minutes.

Beren started cart.

No one applauded.

We crossed.

I looked downstream.

Lessa dumped grain near reed patch. Birds crowded around her without aggression.

One nipped her sleeve.

She slapped beak.

Relationship apparently established.

At midday we stopped in a quarry village called Fenner. Gray stone dust covered roofs despite rain. Men carried baskets from cut road above village. Women worked splitting sheds beside stream.

Beren traded one broken harness buckle for two repaired ones at smith.

Not money.

Road exchange.

I bought salve because mine was running low.

The apothecary woman looked at my face.

"Moth."

"Yes."

"Gray orchard?"

"Yes."

"Don't scratch."

"I wasn't."

She looked at my hand on cheek.

I removed it.

Apparently bodies lied too.

We left Fenner with sky clearing.

The road descended toward Kelm basin.

First distant view arrived after ridge: patchwork fields, smoke, low river, dark line of town roofs perhaps half day away.

My chest lifted.

Not because Kelm mattered yet.

Because distance had become visible.

Road went somewhere.

Jorren watched me from driver board.

"Happy?"

"No."

He laughed.

Beren said, "He is."

Conspiracy.

We camped short of town near old stone shelter used by freight crews.

Three other groups already there.

A wool wagon, two salt carts, one pair of traveling laborers on foot.

Camp meant crowded fire, borrowed kettle, arguments about dry wood.

One salt driver recognized Beren.

"Moths?"

"Yes."

He looked at my face.

"Lost?"

"Only dignity."

"Cheap."

Reasonable.

After dinner I walked fifty yards from shelter to piss and saw movement in dark beyond road.

Low shapes.

Three maybe.

Not human.

I stopped.

Old Greg awareness sharpened automatically.

Hand to sword.

Shapes moved closer.

Eyes reflected firelight.

Jorren came behind me.

"What?"

"Animals."

Beren called from shelter, "Dogs."

"Wild?"

"Farm."

Three thin dogs emerged, tails low, attracted by food.

One had rope hanging from neck.

Not threat.

Maybe lost.

Jorren crouched with bread.

"Don't feed," Beren said.

Jorren stopped.

"Why?"

"Then stay all night."

He ate bread himself while dog watched.

Cruel but practical.

I sheathed sword.

The reflex embarrassed me more than it should.

Road was allowed to contain dogs without becoming combat.

Later, lying in shared stone shelter among snores and cart creaks, I thought about Gold parties Old Greg had known.

Not names.

Movement.

How nobody in a good team reached for sword because someone else saw a dog first. How threat assessment became distributed enough that fear did not need one brain.

I wanted that.

Not rank.

The shared noticing.

Then Beren threw a rolled sock at me.

"Stop staring ceiling. Sleep."

"You cannot hear staring."

"I can hear you think."

Everyone could apparently.

I slept.
