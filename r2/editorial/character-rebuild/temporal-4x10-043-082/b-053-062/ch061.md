# Rehearsal B61: The Ford

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The ford cost us six hours and one boot.

Not mine.

Brin's.

This made it funnier until we needed the boot.

Lower bridge south had been closed after upstream debris strike. Road warden redirected wagons to old ford two miles east.

Current maps showed it.

Old Greg memory did not.

Good.

We reached river near noon.

Wide shallow spread over stones, maybe knee deep in center, current stronger than appearance.

A ferryman? No, ford keeper stood on bank with painted pole marking depth.

She was woman named Esta, gray hair tied at neck, trousers rolled to thigh.

"One wagon at time," she said. "Unload top-heavy. Walk animals. No one downstream of wheel."

Hessa asked, "How deep?"

"Thirty-two at center."

"Inches?"

"What else?"

"Could be fingers."

Esta stared.

"Thirty-two fingers would be worse system."

I liked her.

Our cart low and light enough.

Salt wagon heavy but stable.

Wool bulky and high.

Tarl and Mave had to unload top bundles and carry separately on pack tarp.

Everyone helped.

Road delay became communal labor.

We crossed our cart first.

Beren drove mule by lead, not board. Jorren upstream side. I near rear upstream, exactly not downstream.

Water entered boots at ankle, then shin, then knee.

Current pushed sideways.

Cart wheels bumped stones.

At center mule stopped.

Simply refused.

Beren pulled gently.

No.

"Stone?" I asked.

Esta shouted from bank, "Hole ahead left! Bring right!"

Beren adjusted lead.

Mule stepped right.

Moved.

Local sightline from shore mattered more than our close view.

We crossed.

No drama.

Salt wagon next.

Hessa's horses entered well.

Rear wheel hit hidden rock and jumped.

One salt sack shifted but ropes held.

Crossed.

Then wool.

Tarl insisted wagon could go without removing second layer.

Esta said no.

Tarl argued weight was light.

Esta said height.

Tarl argued decades driving.

Mave started unloading while he argued.

Marriage solved safety.

We carried wool bundles across by hand.

Brin and I took one between us.

Water at center pushed hard against legs.

He stepped into hole Esta warned.

Dropped to thigh.

His right boot came off.

"Fuck!"

Bundle nearly went.

I caught his arm.

Jorren caught wool.

Boot vanished downstream.

Brin hopped on one leg swearing while everyone laughed except him.

Esta pointed.

"Eddy!"

Boot had caught near reeds thirty yards below.

I started after.

She shouted, "Bank!"

Right.

Do not chase downstream through current.

I climbed out, ran bank, retrieved boot with branch.

Filled with river mud.

Returned triumphant.

Brin said, "Keep it."

"Smells like you."

"Then definitely keep."

We washed it.

No dry sock survived his fall.

I had spare.

I gave one.

He looked at size.

My foot larger.

"Tent."

"Free sock."

He wore it folded at toe.

Road redistributed inventory.

By time wool wagon crossed, afternoon gone.

Then reloading.

This was the part adventure stories criminally omitted.

Wet people lifting same bundles back onto same cart.

Ropes retied.

Animals watered.

Esta collected two copper per wagon for ford maintenance.

Hessa objected because road authority already charged toll elsewhere.

Esta pointed at three workers clearing stones from channel.

"Then carry your wagon on tax receipt."

Hessa paid.

We camped just beyond ford because daylight insufficient to reach next post.

Everything wet.

Boots around fire.

Brin's borrowed sock steaming.

I sat rubbing feet and thought about how much current body had changed since Carrow.

Not stronger dramatically.

More used.

Callus positions. Shoulder soreness. Rope burn healing. Small bites. Familiarity with wet socks.

Old Greg body had been a weapon and ledger of damage.

Current body was becoming a life before weapon.

That distinction mattered.

I did not say it.

Hessa produced cards.

Jorren immediately cheated.

I caught him because his new tell was not finger.

It was breathing.

He held breath a fraction when bluffing.

Maybe.

I called.

He had good hand.

Wrong.

"You thought tell," he said.

"No."

"Face."

Everyone's favorite weapon.

We played until dark.

Then distant howl.

Not dog.

Longer.

Wolves maybe.

Mules reacted.

Beren stood.

This time no one panicked.

We moved animals closer, banked fire, checked weapons.

Hessa's nephew Brin had short bow.

Tarl carried old spear.

Jorren spear.

I sword.

Howls stayed across river.

No attack.

We remained alert twenty minutes.

Then sat.

Gold coordination thoughts returned.

Our accidental caravan was messy version.

People knew pieces.

Not seamless.

Still enough.

At midnight wolves howled again farther away.

Brin, half asleep, mumbled, "My boot scares them."

I laughed into blanket.
