# Rehearsal C63: Warning First

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Mara had asked for warning.

I gave her four days.

Then spent three of them wondering whether four days counted as enough warning.

This was a stupid problem because she could read numbers.

The packet I sent was simple.

**Mara,**

**I might come west on the fifth-day boat if that does not make your life worse. I can stay elsewhere. This is warning.**

**Greg**

I had almost added that I did not intend to prove anything.

Then remembered anyone who wrote `I am not trying to prove anything` was almost certainly constructing a proof.

I left it out.

Her answer came the next afternoon.

**Fifth-day is fine. Vey has me until second bell most days. Do not arrive expecting me to stop having a job. There is a decent room above the Green Finch on Salt Street. The blue sign, not the green one. Halden hates visitors.**

Then beneath it:

**This is also warning.**

I read that line four times.

Noll read it once over my shoulder and said, "She knows you."

"Why are you in my room?"

"Chair."

He was sitting in the second one.

Of course.

I folded the note.

"I'm going west."

"Yes."

"You knew?"

"Pack."

The field pack sat open on my bed.

Two road socks.

One clean shirt.

Spare cord.

Oil.

Whetstone.

Rain wrap.

Three days of food despite the river boat selling food because I had met commerce before.

"Maybe I was organizing."

Noll looked at the pack.

Then at me.

"You don't organize."

Insulting.

Accurate enough to require no response.

On fifth-day morning I left Lower Rook before sunrise with the pack on my shoulders and the room key in my coat.

Maud took one look at me in the courtyard.

"West?"

"Does everyone have a spy network?"

"Bag."

Apparently bags were public announcements.

"Halden."

She nodded.

"Don't bring back bugs."

"That feels avoidable."

"Visitors say that."

Then she went back to sweeping the front step.

No blessing.

No speech.

Good.

The west packet boat left Carrow at first bell.

I arrived before it.

Not early.

Prepared.

Mara was not there to dispute terminology.

River travel from Carrow to Halden took two days if the current behaved and three if it developed character.

The boat was broader than the one that had taken Mara west.

Cargo deck forward.

Passenger benches under a patched canvas roof aft.

Two shallow cabins beneath deck for people willing to pay six copper for privacy and the experience of sleeping beside cargo rats with social status.

I paid two copper for bench passage.

The river was enough.

At least that was what I told myself before the first rain.

It started three hours west of Carrow.

Not dramatic rain.

Persistent.

Fine enough to pass around the canvas edges and settle on everything through patience.

The woman beside me wrapped herself in oilcloth and slept.

A man across the aisle removed his boots, which introduced a new environmental hazard.

I moved forward.

The deck hand looked at me.

"Passengers stay aft."

"His feet don't."

She glanced under the canvas.

"Fair. Stay behind the yellow line."

A strip of faded paint crossed the deck before the cargo stacks.

I stood there with one hand on the rail.

The river widened as Carrow's outer farms disappeared.

Low banks.

Willow.

Wet grass.

A line of flat-bottomed freight boats moving east under dark canvas.

Old Greg remembered this river.

Not the exact surface.

Water never held still long enough for nostalgia.

But bends.

Distances.

The dark cut where a tributary entered from the north.

A ruined watchtower on the south bank.

Except the tower was not ruined.

It had a roof.

New timber platform.

Two men standing beneath a red awning.

I stared as we passed.

Old Greg remembered broken stone and thorn growing through the stair.

Current Halden road authority, or somebody with money, had repaired it.

I did not know why.

That felt good.

Then immediately bad because I wanted to know.

I spent five minutes constructing possibilities.

River toll expansion.

Flood watch.

Bandit suppression.

Lightfin migration observation.

Military post.

Then realized I had turned a roof into a thesis.

I went back under the canvas.

The man's boots remained off.

Halden did hate visitors.

It was starting early.

We stopped at Rell Ford near dusk.

Not a ford anymore.

A ferry crossing had become a small river landing with three inns, a cooper, a stable, and enough mud to qualify as urban planning.

Old Greg remembered a stone warehouse.

It was still there.

Now painted white.

I disliked that irrationally.

The boat stayed one hour to exchange packet sacks and take on two barrels of something that smelled like apples and vinegar.

I went ashore to walk.

My legs had not appreciated sitting all day.

At the landing, a boy sold skewers of grilled river fish.

I bought one.

Then another because the first vanished before I reached the end of the dock.

The town rose gently from the river.

Lanterns coming on.

Horses stamping near the stable.

A woman outside the white warehouse shouted numbers while three men rolled sacks toward a cart.

Nothing waited for my memory to approve it.

I walked as far as the old east road intersection.

There should have been a well there.

Instead there was a bakery.

Not even a good one from the smell.

The well might have moved.

The bakery might have been built over it.

Old Greg might simply be wrong.

That possibility remained intellectually familiar and emotionally irritating.

I bought a roll anyway.

It was bad.

Vindication came in strange forms.

Back on the boat, I slept badly on a bench.

The rain stopped after midnight.

Someone snored beneath the opposite canvas support with enough force to challenge river traffic.

I woke every time the boat bumped the bank or another passenger shifted.

At some point I dreamed about Mara's blue cup sitting on my second chair.

No Mara.

Just cup.

I woke annoyed at my own symbolism.

By second morning the river had narrowed between higher banks.

More traffic.

Timber rafts.

Two Vey freight boats under yellow marks.

A passenger ferry crowded with laborers.

Halden announced itself first through smoke.

Not black factory smoke.

Cooking smoke, kiln smoke, river mist, thousands of fires spread across a city climbing the western bank.

Then roofs.

Warehouses close to water.

Stone retaining walls.

Stairs.

Bridges over channels cut from the river.

Old Greg remembered Halden as larger.

Current Halden was already larger than Carrow.

Memory had no obligation to use scale honestly.

I stood at the rail while we approached the landing.

My stomach tightened.

Not danger.

Maybe.

Mara lived here.

Worked here.

Had coworkers here who knew what she ate at midday and how she looked when three ledgers disagreed and which street she took when rain made the lower stairs slick.

I knew she hated a blue cup her mother hated first.

No.

She loved the cup because her mother hated it.

Different.

I knew she got ink on the side of her hand.

I knew she kissed by grabbing my coat when she had decided the moment had gone on long enough without action.

I knew she left for work even when she wanted to stay.

That was current.

Enough to be dangerous.

The landing crew threw ropes.

The boat struck the dock harder than necessary.

Everyone stood at once because humanity preferred bottlenecks.

I stayed seated.

Growth again.

Or laziness.

When the aisle cleared, I shouldered the pack and stepped onto Halden stone.

The first thing I noticed was the smell.

Hot bread.

River mud.

Coal smoke.

Horse.

Something sharp and mineral from upstream.

The second was noise.

Carrow's quays were loud.

Halden's riverfront layered sound vertically.

Carts on the upper road.

Dockworkers below.

A pulley squealing above a warehouse door.

Bells from farther inland.

Someone shouting about pears.

Always pears somewhere.

I knew the route to Salt Street.

Probably.

Mara's note had said Green Finch, blue sign, not green.

I could go directly there.

Instead I looked toward the Vey office.

I knew approximately where it should be from Mara's letters and old memory.

Second bell had not rung yet.

She had told me she would be working.

I had told myself I would not appear at her office on arrival like a man whose warning had been camouflage for impatience.

I turned toward Salt Street.

Then walked two blocks toward Vey.

This was not my fault.

The streets curved.

Halden's river ward had been rebuilt more times than Old Greg's memory respected. A lane I expected to continue west ended at a new brick warehouse. The detour sent me north.

North happened to place the yellow Vey sign at the end of the next block.

Coincidence.

I stopped.

The office occupied the lower floor of a narrow stone building with warehouse doors behind it. Clerks moved through an open side entrance carrying boards and tied packets.

Mara was not visible.

Good.

I stood there anyway.

A man came out carrying two ledger boxes.

He looked at me.

I moved.

Salt Street was west and uphill.

I found it eventually after asking a fruit seller where the Green Finch was.

"Blue sign?" I added.

She stared at me.

"Finch is green."

"Mara said blue."

This did not help.

"There are two."

"Why?"

"Brothers."

"They hate visitors too?"

She pointed uphill.

I found the blue-sign Green Finch between a bookbinder and a shop selling brass lamps.

The sign depicted a green bird on a blue board.

Mara had been technically correct in the most annoying possible way.

The room above cost four copper a night.

Clean bed.

Narrow window.

Chair.

One chair.

I stared at it.

The innkeeper, a woman with gray hair braided down her back, said, "Problem?"

"No."

"You looked at chair like it insulted you."

"Personal history."

She held out the key.

I paid two nights.

Not because I knew I would leave after two.

Because buying more before seeing Mara felt like turning accommodation into legislation.

I put the pack beside the bed.

Washed my face.

Changed shirt.

Then changed back because the first clean shirt made me look like I had dressed specifically for Mara.

Which I had.

I changed again.

This was becoming expensive in dignity.

Second bell rang while I was lacing my boot.

I stood so quickly I hit my knee against the chair.

"Fuck."

The chair remained unrepentant.

I walked to Vey.

Directly this time.

Mostly.

The office was busier than before.

A clerk at the front desk looked up.

Young man.

Dark curls.

Ink on two fingers.

"Freight?"

"Person."

He waited.

"Mara."

His expression changed slightly.

Not recognition of me.

Recognition of the situation.

I immediately disliked him for knowing anything.

"Greg?"

Worse.

"Why?"

He smiled.

"She said you might come."

Reasonable.

I still disliked him.

"Pell?" I asked.

The smile widened.

"No."

Good.

Why good?

Irrelevant.

"Emmet."

"Greg."

"I know."

Halden was a hostile city.

Emmet pointed toward the side warehouse.

"She's counting straps."

"That sounds thrilling."

"She thinks so."

I followed the open passage.

The warehouse smelled like canvas, oil, wood dust, and river damp.

Stacks of freight occupied painted floor squares.

Four workers were fitting leather securing straps around long crates.

Mara stood on top of one crate.

Actually on it.

Boots planted wide.

Travel coat gone.

Sleeves rolled to elbows.

Ledger board in one hand.

She was arguing with a man below about whether six straps counted as six when one buckle had already cracked.

"It is physically still a strap," he said.

"It is emotionally a strap. It is not commercially a strap."

I stopped in the doorway.

Mara looked over.

Saw me.

Her entire face changed before she controlled it.

Not huge.

Enough.

Then she looked at the high window.

"Second bell."

"Exactly."

"How long have you been in Halden?"

Dangerous question.

"Some amount."

Mara closed her eyes.

The worker beside the crate looked between us.

"This him?"

I disliked Halden more every minute.

Mara pointed at me with the ledger board.

"Do not encourage him."

"I haven't done anything."

"You came to my workplace."

"After second bell."

"Technically."

"Technically is still a category."

She jumped down from the crate.

I reached one hand out automatically.

She did not need it.

She took it anyway for the last few inches.

That made the entire two-day boat ride become physical all at once.

Her palm.

Weight for half a second.

Current Mara.

Halden around her.

Then she let go.

"You found the Finch?"

"Blue sign."

"Good."

"One chair."

Mara stared.

Then laughed so hard she had to put the ledger board against her stomach.

The worker with the broken strap looked confused.

I smiled despite myself.

Mara wiped beneath one eye.

"You came all the way to Halden and you're complaining about furniture."

"I have standards now."

"Apparently."

She looked at me again.

Longer.

The laugh faded but not the warmth.

"Hi."

There.

Finally.

"Hi."

A voice behind us said, "Mara, seventh strap?"

She turned immediately.

Work.

Of course.

She looked back at me.

"Give me twenty minutes."

"I can wait."

"You can also carry that box."

She pointed at a stack near the wall.

I looked at it.

"Am I employed?"

"No."

"Then this is exploitation."

"You traveled two days to bother me. Earn it."

I put my hands under the box.

Heavy.

Of course.

"Where?"

Mara pointed across the warehouse.

I carried it.

Not because she needed me.

Because she had asked.

Different thing.

Probably.
