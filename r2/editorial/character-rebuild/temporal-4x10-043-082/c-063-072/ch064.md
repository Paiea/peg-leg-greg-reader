# Rehearsal C64: Twenty Minutes

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Mara's twenty minutes lasted fifty-three.

I knew because Halden had clocks.

Not good ones.

A brass face hung above the Vey warehouse office with one hand that jumped every few minutes instead of moving smoothly. I did not trust it completely, which meant I checked it constantly.

This was not waiting.

I had been given work.

Unpaid exploitation, but work.

The first box Mara pointed me toward contained ledger copies bundled in waxed cloth.

The second contained strap buckles.

The third contained actual straps and was heavier than the first two combined.

By the fourth, I had learned that `give me twenty minutes` at Vey meant `become furniture until my employer releases me`.

Mara moved through the warehouse without treating my arrival as the central event.

This was exactly what she had warned me about.

I still resented it.

Not much.

Enough.

She checked cargo marks, argued with a strap supplier, sent Emmet to copy two lines from a river sheet, then disappeared through the side office carrying three boards at once.

I stood beside the freight stack holding an empty crate frame.

The worker who had asked `this him?` earlier looked at me.

"You can put that down."

I looked at the frame.

I had forgotten I was holding it.

I put it down.

"Name?"

"Pell."

Not the Pell from Mara's letters?

I looked at him more carefully.

Late twenties maybe.

Round face.

Close-cropped beard.

Onion advocate.

"You bring her soup?"

Pell stared.

"What?"

"When she's sick. Onion soup."

His face changed.

Recognition.

Then suspicion.

"She tells you that?"

"Letter."

"She complained?"

"No."

"She said too much pepper?"

"She said you believe pepper hides bad meat and still eat onions at your desk."

Pell nodded slowly.

"That's fair."

Then he pointed at me.

"You're the memory one."

I stopped.

Mara had apparently distributed my medical history through freight.

"That phrasing is terrible."

"She said you got strange after something happened."

Better.

Not much.

"I was strange before."

"She said that too."

Excellent.

Pell went back to tightening buckles.

I wanted to ask what else Mara had said.

About me.

About Carrow.

About whether she had told coworkers I kissed her.

That thought arrived with enough heat that I became fascinated by a stack of ceramic insulators instead.

The warehouse had high windows and no privacy.

Vey workers moved between marked squares on the floor. Yellow paint for outbound river freight. White for local delivery. Red for holds.

Mara had described ledgers in letters.

She had not described the way she used her whole body to work.

She climbed crates instead of waiting for a step stool.

Held a pencil between her teeth when both hands were full.

Kicked one office door shut with her heel.

Touched people's sleeves when moving past in narrow space, not affectionate, just efficient.

Current habits.

None belonged to memory.

I noticed too many.

At thirty-eight minutes, Emmet came out holding a small cup.

He drank from it while reading a sheet.

Blue glazed clay.

Not Mara's cup.

My body reacted anyway.

Ridiculous.

He saw me looking.

"Want water?"

"No."

"You look thirsty."

"I am not looking at your cup."

That sentence guaranteed the opposite.

Emmet looked at the cup.

Then toward the office.

Understanding spread across his face with horrifying speed.

"Oh."

"Do not."

"Blue cup."

"You know about the cup?"

"Everyone knows about the cup."

I closed my eyes.

Mara had an entire life here and apparently the blue cup had achieved institutional status before me.

"Why?"

"She brings it to count days."

"Count days?"

"Month-end inventory. Says Vey tea tastes less insulting in it."

I had not known that.

Tiny fact.

Immediate jealousy.

Not of Emmet.

Of the fact itself.

He knew a current Mara ritual I did not.

I wanted to ask when she started bringing it.

Who washed it.

Whether Pell had broken one before.

Pointless ownership questions disguised as curiosity.

"Water," I said.

Emmet handed me a plain tin cup.

I drank.

At forty-six minutes, Mara came out with her sleeves rolled higher and a black mark across one forearm.

"Almost."

"That word has become hostile."

"You can leave."

There.

My mind immediately produced three interpretations.

She was tired of me.

She was giving me permission because she knew waiting was boring.

She expected me to understand work.

My mouth picked the stupidest.

"Do you want me to?"

Mara stopped.

Pell looked away too deliberately.

Emmet found a shelf fascinating.

Mara said, "Greg."

"I know. Bad question."

"I mean you can go put your bag down, eat, walk around. I have another problem."

"What problem?"

"You don't work here."

That landed harder than intended.

Maybe intended a little.

I looked toward the red freight square.

A narrow crate had been moved there while I waited. Vey seal broken. One side panel bowed.

Interesting.

Not mine.

"Fine."

I stood.

My body did not move toward the door.

Mara watched me remain standing.

"You are doing it again."

"What?"

"Standing like leaving is a philosophical position."

Pell coughed.

Definitely laughter.

I picked up my coat.

"Second bell was forty-six minutes ago."

"I know."

"You said twenty."

"I was wrong."

Simple.

No apology performance.

That annoyed me enough to become honest.

"I came two days."

Mara's face changed.

Small.

"I know."

"And I don't want to sit in an inn while you're fifty yards away."

"Then don't."

"You just told me to leave."

"I told you that you can leave."

"Different word."

"Yes."

I hated language.

Mara stepped closer.

Not romantic-close.

Warehouse-close.

Noise around us.

"I am happy you're here," she said. "I also have a broken seal, a supplier who swears we signed for twelve straps when we received eleven, and Sera is upriver with the west copy. I don't know how long this takes."

There.

No clean solution.

I wanted her to say she would stop.

She should not stop.

Both could be true without becoming wisdom.

"Who is Sera?"

Mara stared.

Of course that was the word I chose.

"West tally."

"You mentioned her?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because letters are not employee directories."

Pell laughed openly now.

I looked at him.

"Soup man."

He raised both hands.

Mara's mouth twitched.

The tension broke unevenly.

Not gone.

Enough.

"Go walk," she said. "Come back at third bell. If I'm still trapped, kidnap me."

"Legally?"

"No."

"Better."

I left.

That was the difficult part.

Halden outside Vey had continued existing during my warehouse occupation.

The riverfront climbed into narrow commercial streets. Salt Street smelled like lamp oil and bread. Two blocks north, brassworkers had open fronts where hammers rang against thin sheet. A woman sold dumplings from a copper pot on wheels.

I bought three.

Then walked with no destination.

This was harder than it sounded.

Old Greg had destinations everywhere.

A guild hall should be uphill and east.

A cheap bathhouse two streets past it.

A weapons seller where I had once bought a short spear after losing another weapon in a river.

Except I did not know when.

Maybe twenty years from now.

Maybe the bathhouse had not been built.

Current Halden kept forcing memory to declare its date.

I turned onto a lane I recognized.

Narrow.

Steep.

Stone steps down the center because carts could not use it.

There should be a painted red door halfway up.

I remembered knocking on that door bleeding from the eyebrow.

Someone had opened it.

Woman?

Man?

Old Greg memory gave me lamplight, warm cloth, and the smell of cloves.

No face.

My feet slowed.

Current lane had the red door.

Fresh paint.

A basket of laundry hanging above it.

A child sat on the step shelling peas.

I stopped across the lane.

The child looked at me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You staring."

"Door."

He looked behind him.

"It's door."

Good analysis.

Whoever lived there now had nothing to do with a future memory.

Probably.

I could knock.

Ask how long the family had lived there.

Turn an ordinary home into an archaeological site because my brain had found cloves.

I kept walking.

That was not restraint.

I simply did not want to discover the wrong person badly enough.

At the top of the lane, I realized I had been climbing for ten minutes.

My calves hurt.

Halden was built vertically out of spite.

From the upper street, the river looked smaller.

Warehouse roofs below.

Smoke drifting between them.

Vey's yellow sign just visible.

Mara was somewhere beneath it solving a broken seal.

I could see the building and still not access her.

This was probably healthy.

I disliked it.

I walked west instead.

A market occupied a broad square around a fountain carved like three fish biting each other's tails.

Old Greg remembered the fountain cracked.

Current water sprayed from all three mouths.

I bought an apple.

A knife sharpener had set up beside the fountain. His wheel turned by foot treadle while sparks jumped from a kitchen blade.

I stopped to watch.

He looked at my sword.

"Need edge?"

"No."

"Everyone says no until metal says yes."

I almost kept walking.

Then drew the sword two inches.

Edge had dulled slightly near the middle from normal use.

Not enough to require professional work.

Old Greg would have fixed it himself.

Current Greg could too.

I sat on the low wall anyway.

"How much?"

"Two copper."

"One."

"Two."

"One and you get to insult Carrow steel."

He looked at the blade.

"Carrow?"

"Yes."

"Two and I insult it free."

Halden economics were aggressive.

I paid two.

He worked while I ate the apple.

His name was Oren.

He hated Vey carts because they blocked the north lane at second bell.

He had never heard of the Green Finch with the blue sign despite living six streets from it.

His daughter worked in a dyehouse.

None of this mattered to plot.

It made Halden heavier.

By third bell, my sword was sharp and my feet had learned two routes I had not remembered.

I returned to Vey.

Mara was outside.

Sitting on a crate beside the warehouse door with her head tipped back against the stone.

Eyes closed.

For one second I thought something was wrong.

Then she opened one eye.

"You came back."

"Third bell."

"Exactly?"

"Approximately."

"Growth."

"Do not grade me."

Mara held out one hand.

I took it and pulled her to her feet.

She came closer than necessary.

Our shoulders touched.

Her forearm still had the black freight mark.

"Done?" I asked.

"No."

My face must have changed.

She smiled.

"Emmet has it."

"Can he?"

There.

Jealousy disguised as operational question.

Mara heard it.

"Yes."

I nodded.

"Good."

Too quickly.

She looked at me for another second.

Then hooked two fingers into my coat sleeve.

"Food."

We walked uphill.

Not hand in hand.

Her fingers stayed in my sleeve for half a block before letting go.

That was enough to occupy most of my attention.

"Where did you go?" she asked.

"Market. Fountain. Knife sharpener. Red door."

Mara stopped.

"Red door?"

I looked at her.

"Do you know it?"

"There are probably a thousand red doors."

Right.

Memory had made one special without permission.

"Never mind."

"No. Now I want to know."

"Future memory. Bad one. Not bad meaning violent. Bad meaning incomplete."

We resumed walking.

Mara waited.

I told her about the clove smell and warm cloth.

No face.

No date.

She listened without trying to solve it.

Then said, "Did you knock?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Kid with peas looked dangerous."

Mara laughed.

"Coward."

"Correct."

We reached a small restaurant built into the slope under a striped awning.

Inside smelled like roasted garlic and hot oil.

Mara chose a table near the wall.

Two chairs.

I noticed.

Said nothing.

She noticed me noticing.

Said nothing.

That was intimacy too, apparently.
