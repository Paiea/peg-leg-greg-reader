# Rehearsal C72: Visitor

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

My last full day in Halden contained no emergency.

This was harder to manage than expected.

I had booked return packet boat for next morning.

Not because the visit was over emotionally.

Because money, work, room, Carrow existed.

Mara had not asked me to stay longer.

I had not asked whether she wanted me to.

That omission became louder as the day progressed.

We agreed on no work together.

So we became tourists in her city, which irritated Mara because she was not a tourist.

"Where do visitors go?" I asked.

"Why would I know?"

"You live here."

"Exactly."

We started at the hill garden because Pell claimed the view was good.

The climb was terrible.

Stone stairs again.

Mara walked fast until I complained.

"You're six-three," she said.

"Height does not create lungs."

"Old Greg climbed mountains."

"Current Greg had fried noodles for breakfast."

She slowed.

At the top, the city opened below us.

Halden roof terraces, river bends, Vey yellow marks tiny, the burned warehouse a black gap near the quay, festival lantern strings still hanging.

I could identify places now.

Green Finch roof.

Lessa's street.

Guild hall.

The river stairs where Len came out.

Sera's office window, maybe.

The city had begun accumulating personal geography.

Dangerous.

Mara watched me looking.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Liar."

"I know things now."

"Congratulations."

"That wasn't a sentence."

"It was."

We sat on a low wall.

Children chased birds nearby.

An old man sold tea from a cart.

No one needed us.

I bought tea.

Mara stole mine after hers was too sweet.

"You ordered sugar."

"Mistake."

"Then live with the consequence."

She drank mine anyway.

We spent the morning walking neighborhoods she liked, not landmarks.

A narrow street with the cobbler who fixed her boots.

A public wash where women shouted at each other across basins.

A bookshop that let her keep an account because she always forgot coin.

Pell's machine shop, closed Sundays but with a window full of brass gears.

At one corner a woman upstairs shouted, "Mara!"

Mara looked up.

"Give back the line!"

The woman laughed.

The washing-line thief.

Real.

I pointed.

"Enemy."

"Nessa."

"I will remember."

"Do not start a feud."

"Too late."

Nessa threw a clothespin at us.

Missed.

I kept it.

Evidence.

Lunch with Sera and her husband became accidental because we met them at a noodle stall.

Her husband Orin was indeed around sixty, barrel-chested and gentle, carrying a bottle of beer Sera called criminal.

He offered me a taste.

It was terrible.

I tried a polite face.

Sera said, "Don't lie."

"It is... memorable."

Orin laughed.

"Bad then."

"Yes."

He seemed delighted.

My jealousy of Sera died completely and was replaced by pity for the beer.

Useful.

In the afternoon Mara took me to the old west wall where the freight road leaves the city.

Not the river route.

A road toward smaller towns, eventually Carrow by bends.

"You came by boat," she said.

"Yes."

"Next time, road?"

There.

Next time.

My chest reacted.

"Invitation?"

"Don't legislate."

"I remember the rule."

"Maybe road. Different view."

"I would like that."

Direct.

Mara smiled.

We walked outside the gate perhaps one mile.

No reason except the road.

Dust. Low vineyards. Stone drainage channels. Halden behind us.

I wanted to keep walking west.

Not toward anything specific.

My body responded to the road now.

Mara noticed.

"You really do want it."

"Yes."

"Not just Old Greg?"

"I don't think so."

"Good."

She caught herself.

I looked at her.

"Evaluator."

"Shut up."

We laughed.

On the return, a wagon wheel lost its iron tire near the gate.

No danger.

Just inconvenience.

The driver swore. People pushed the wagon aside. A blacksmith's apprentice came with a hammer.

We helped move one crate off the tilted side because it blocked the lane.

By evening we were tired from walking.

Dinner was at Mara's lodging instead of a restaurant.

Her room was smaller than the Finch but more lived in.

A desk under the window covered with papers.

Two cups, one blue.

Three books stacked beneath a wobbly table leg instead of read.

Work coat on a peg.

Hairpins in a bowl.

A sock drying near the stove that was not mine.

I stood in the doorway too long.

Mara said, "Do not inventory."

"I am appreciating."

"Same face."

She cooked eggs and potatoes badly.

I offered to help.

She refused.

Then burned the onions and accepted.

We ate from one plate because she had only one clean.

Domestic fantasy tried to inflate.

I punctured it by remembering I was leaving tomorrow.

Mara saw my mood shift.

"What?"

I almost said nothing.

"I don't want to leave."

There.

No manipulation.

No implied ask.

Just want.

Mara looked down at her fork.

"I don't want you to either."

My chest hurt.

"Should I stay?"

The question came anyway.

Mara thought.

Actually thought.

"No."

I hated the answer.

She continued.

"Not because I want you gone. Because you have Carrow. I have work. Another week becomes waiting around my shifts unless you build your own reason here."

"I found the Guild."

"Yes."

"Could work."

"Do you want to?"

Silence.

Not really.

I wanted Mara.

"No."

"Then go home. Come again."

Home.

Lower Rook entered the sentence from her mouth without being named.

That mattered.

I looked around her room.

"This is home?"

"Currently."

"Halden?"

"Currently."

Temporary could still be real.

I stayed late.

Later, half dressed and trying to move around her too-small room without knocking anything over, my knee hit the desk hard enough to send a stack of Vey papers across the floor.

Mara stared at the mess for one stunned second and then started laughing.

I tried to be offended while pulling my trousers back on enough to crawl under the chair.

That made her laugh harder.

We spent ten minutes collecting manifests from beneath the desk, the bed, and one impossible corner beside the stove.

Romance with paperwork.

One page had slid beneath the bed.

I reached for it.

Mara grabbed my wrist.

"Confidential."

"I can't read upside down."

"Still."

I handed it over without looking.

At midnight I walked back to the Finch alone because Mara worked first bell.

She did not escort me.

I knew the route now.

At the corner I looked back once.

Her window was lit.

Then it went dark before I reached the river.

The next morning she met me at the boat anyway.

Both of us early.

She brought two rolls.

I laughed.

"What?"

"Nothing. Carrow problem."

We ate on the river stairs.

When boarding was called, Mara kissed me, handed me my bag, and said, "Write when you arrive."

"I will."

"Not tax clerk."

"Tavin already has the trademark."

She smiled.

I boarded.

This time I watched from the deck while she stayed on the stairs.

The river pulled us apart physically.

Halden became smaller.

I stood until Mara was one dark figure among many.

Then went below because the boat breakfast smelled better than longing.
