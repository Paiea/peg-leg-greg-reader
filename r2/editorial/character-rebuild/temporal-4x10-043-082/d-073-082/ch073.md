# Rehearsal D73: Warm Stone

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The wall was warm.

Not hot.

Warm enough that I moved my hand away, then put it back because apparently curiosity required confirmation from skin.

Stone should not have been warm.

The service passage under Bell Tile was below street level, north-facing, damp at both ends, and miserable enough that the apprentice carrying our lamp had complained about his socks twice in ten minutes.

The wall beside me felt like it had spent the afternoon in sun.

There had been no sun here for perhaps sixty years.

"Problem?" Dena asked.

I looked over.

She stood thirty feet down the passage with one boot on a broken drainage lip, ledger tucked beneath her arm, and the expression of a woman who had agreed to one quick inspection and already knew the universe had lied.

Arlo crouched beside an old filtration return line farther in.

Ivena remained upstairs negotiating with Bell Tile's owner about why `one cracked service collar` had become `we found three and one smells wrong`.

I had come because Dena asked for another pair of hands.

Not because filtration needed rescuing.

The service run was theirs.

Fixed day.

Bell Tile happened to sit over one of Carrow's older drainage cuts, and access to the filter return line required using the same narrow corridor the tileworks used to inspect its old foundation wall.

I was carrying tools.

That was the job.

Then the wall became warm.

"This stone," I said.

Dena walked back.

Put her palm where mine had been.

Her face changed.

"Kiln?"

"Other side?"

"Other side is ground. Kiln's upstairs and south."

The apprentice raised the lamp.

His name was Pellin.

Sixteen maybe.

Thin wrists.

Hair that kept falling into his eyes.

He had been hired by Arlo three weeks ago according to Dena and had already asked why they needed their own kiln if Bell Tile's worked.

Dangerous young man.

"Maybe pipe," Pellin said.

Dena looked at the wall.

"No pipe on plan."

"Plan old?"

"Very."

"Then maybe pipe."

Dena gave him a look.

Not annoyed.

Considering.

Good.

I pressed my fingertips to the stone again.

Warmth spread over an area wider than my hand.

Dry too.

That was stranger.

The corridor floor glistened with thin moisture. Mortar seams along the opposite wall were dark. Our wall stayed pale and dusty.

Old Greg remembered dry-warm stone.

I waited for the memory to become useful.

It did not.

There were volcanic ruins far south where heat rose through mineral seams.

There were fungal colonies that produced warmth while digesting mana-rich wood.

There were warded storage vaults that kept moisture away by bleeding heat into masonry.

There were nesting drakes whose eggs could turn a cellar into a bathhouse.

This was Carrow.

Bell Tile.

Old drainage.

None fit.

That should have made me cautious.

Instead it made me interested.

Interest had killed smarter men.

"Greg," Arlo called.

I looked away from the wall.

"Tools."

Right.

I carried the wrench case down to him.

The filter return line ran waist-high along the passage, newer bronze and fired ceramic mounted against ancient stone.

One collar had cracked exactly as reported.

Two more showed hairline fractures near their lower clamps.

Arlo tapped one with a fingernail.

"Transport shock?"

Dena said, "Been installed six months."

"Then support."

Pellin raised the lamp.

The pipe sagged almost invisibly between mounts.

I crouched.

"Thermal?"

Arlo looked at me.

"Filter water isn't hot."

I looked back toward the warm wall.

He followed my eyes.

"What?"

Dena said, "Wall's warm."

Arlo stood.

We all went back to touch the wall because adults remained vulnerable to collective stupidity.

Arlo put the back of his fingers against it.

"Huh."

Pellin touched it too.

"Nice."

"Do not cuddle the infrastructure," Dena said.

He removed his hand.

Ivena's voice came from the stairwell.

"Why are four people petting a wall?"

She descended with Bell Tile's owner behind her.

The owner was a narrow man named Serit who wore a clay-stained apron over clothing expensive enough to make the apron decorative.

He saw us.

"That wall's always dry."

Everyone looked at him.

Dena said, "Always?"

Serit shrugged.

"As long as I've had the place."

"How long?"

"Nine years."

"Warm?"

"Sometimes."

I turned fully toward him.

"Sometimes when?"

He disliked the question immediately.

Not because he knew something.

Because it sounded expensive.

"Weather."

"What weather?"

"Wet."

The apprentice laughed once.

Serit looked at him.

Pellin became deeply interested in the lamp.

"Warmer when it rains?" I asked.

"Maybe."

"Does anything happen?"

"Wall dries."

"Besides that."

"No."

I looked at the service collars.

"Your pipe cracks."

Serit spread his hands.

"Your pipe."

Ivena stepped between that sentence and the next twenty minutes of owner argument.

"The pipe is ours. The wall isn't. We can fix our supports today. If the site is moving or heating, we need to know before we install replacement collars."

Serit frowned.

"Site isn't moving."

Pellin said, "Cracks."

Serit looked at him again.

I liked Pellin too.

This was becoming professionally dangerous.

Dena knelt and placed two fingers against the mortar seam at floor level.

"Dry six inches out."

Arlo checked the pipe support nearest the warm area.

"Bronze is warm too."

I touched it.

Slightly.

Enough to matter if ceramic expanded differently across repeated cycles.

Old Greg's mind supplied coefficients from materials I no longer had numbers for.

Current Greg had no measuring instrument beyond hands and the fact that three collars were cracking on one side of a corridor.

"Need temperature marks," I said.

Arlo nodded.

"Wax tabs."

Ivena said, "And somebody who knows this foundation."

Serit crossed his arms.

"My mason knows it."

"Get him."

"He's sixty-three."

"Excellent. Age often comes with memories."

I almost laughed.

Did not.

The sentence landed too close.

Serit sent Pellin upstairs to find a runner.

The apprentice handed me the lamp before leaving.

I held it.

Actual contribution restored.

We replaced the visibly cracked collar while waiting.

That required draining the line, catching residual water in two shallow pans, loosening clamps, and discovering one lower bolt had rusted into a personal commitment.

Arlo worked the wrench.

I held the pipe weight.

Dena braced the temporary support.

Ivena went upstairs to deal with customers because the tileworks did not stop selling tile when infrastructure became interesting.

My arms began shaking before the bolt released.

Not much.

Enough that Arlo noticed.

"Set block."

"I have it."

"Set block."

I held two seconds longer.

Why?

Because Old Greg could have held this pipe for an hour.

Because Arlo had asked me to carry tools and I had mentally upgraded myself.

Because my arms were shaking and I resented evidence.

Dena slid a timber block beneath the line herself.

The weight came off my hands.

"Idiot," she said.

"Accurate."

My forearms burned.

She looked at them.

"You keep doing that?"

"What?"

"Waiting until body tells you twice."

I flexed my fingers.

"Body is dramatic."

"Body is nineteen."

That was unfairly specific.

Arlo laughed without looking up.

We got the collar off.

The underside crack was wider than the visible top line.

A thin black mineral stain traced the ceramic edge.

I touched it.

Arlo slapped my hand away.

"Stop touching unknown black shit."

"It is probably iron."

"Probably can be on your own finger."

I wiped it on a rag.

Dena leaned close.

"From water?"

"Could be."

Arlo smelled the rag.

Then made a face.

"Sulfur."

I smelled it.

Faint.

Egg and stone.

Warm wall.

Dry mortar.

Black stain.

Rain.

Something beneath Bell Tile moved from interesting to structurally annoying.

The mason arrived after midday.

His name was Harl.

Sixty-three according to Serit, though he looked older because masonry had apparently negotiated directly with his spine.

He came down the stairs slowly with a cane he did not need until the last step, then used it to point at all of us.

"Who's digging?"

"Nobody," Dena said.

"Good."

His eyes went to me.

"You look like digging."

I did not know how to answer that.

"We're looking at the warm wall," I said.

"Don't dig it."

"Why?"

Harl walked to the wall.

Touched it.

No surprise.

"Because old men told me not to."

"Why did they say?"

"Because older men told them."

This was not ideal documentation.

"Anything behind it?"

"Stone."

"Beyond stone."

"More Carrow."

"Helpful."

Harl looked at me.

"You want a story or a wall?"

"Both."

"Greedy."

He tapped the mortar with his cane.

"Drain cut used to run deeper. Before my father's father. Lower channels sealed after the sink."

"Sink?"

Serit said, "Foundation collapse."

Harl shook his head.

"Not this building. Street. Two houses. Mule."

"Killed?"

"Mule."

He considered.

"One man broke leg."

Dena asked, "Where were the lower channels?"

Harl pointed through the warm wall.

"There."

That was not what I wanted him to say.

"Sealed with what?"

"Stone. Lime. Whatever they had."

"Any ward?"

Harl shrugged.

"Blue marks."

My attention sharpened.

"Load wards?"

"Blue marks."

Could mean anything.

Old Greg knew hundreds of blue marks.

People loved blue for protective magic because people loved making future scholars miserable.

"Did the wall get warm then?"

Harl looked at me like I had asked whether rain was wet.

"Wall's warm."

"Always?"

"Sometimes."

Serit crossed his arms harder.

"I've never had a problem."

The building gave a tiny sound.

Tick.

Everyone stopped.

Not loud.

Ceramic maybe.

Arlo turned toward the exposed line.

The replacement collar sat on the floor beside him.

The remaining mounted collar nearest the wall had developed a fresh line down one side.

We watched it extend.

Slow.

Almost delicate.

Tick.

Another half inch.

Nobody touched anything.

Harl said, "Rain coming."

I looked toward the stairwell.

The light from above had gone gray.

A drop struck the courtyard beyond the door.

Then another.

Dena swore.

"Drain line off?"

Arlo said, "Filter line drained."

"Tile drainage?"

Serit frowned.

"Roof drains east."

Rain began hitting the upper yard hard enough to become audible.

The wall under my hand grew warmer.

Not imagination.

I was not touching it anymore.

I stepped closer and put my palm flat.

Heat.

Definite.

Harl slapped the stone with his cane.

"Don't dig."

"I wasn't going to."

He looked at my face.

"You were thinking."

Apparently thinking now had posture.

Dena said, "We stop service until temperature settles."

Serit immediately said, "No."

Ivena's voice came from above.

"Yes."

She had returned at exactly the right moment, which was probably why businesses needed multiple owners.

Serit went upstairs to argue with her.

Dena started packing tools.

Arlo capped the open line.

Harl stood beside the warm wall like a guard who had forgotten what he guarded.

I stayed.

Rain hammered above us.

The corridor floor near the opposite wall became visibly wetter as seepage worked through old mortar.

Our wall stayed dry.

Then something moved beneath my palm.

Not stone.

A vibration.

Three soft pulses.

Pause.

Two.

I jerked my hand away.

Harl saw.

"What?"

"Did you feel that?"

He put his palm against the wall.

Waited.

Nothing.

I put mine beside his.

Warmth.

Rain.

Distant voices upstairs.

Then one pulse.

Harl's eyes narrowed.

Two more.

He removed his hand.

"Water."

"Maybe."

"Water."

Old Greg remembered underground creatures that knocked through stone.

Mana pumps that pulsed in cycles.

Heat vents.

Wards transferring force into bedrock.

Buried engines from civilizations with no business surviving in Carrow.

My mind became very helpful very quickly.

Too quickly.

I wanted to open the wall.

That desire arrived before evidence.

Strong.

Physical.

I could almost feel the pry bar in my hands.

Harl watched me.

"Don't."

"I know."

"No, you know words."

Rude old man.

Dena came back.

"We're out."

"Wall pulses."

"Of course it does."

She looked at Harl.

He nodded once.

That made her expression change.

"City drainage?"

"Tomorrow," Harl said. "When rain stops."

I said, "If pressure is building, tomorrow could be late."

There.

The part of me that could see ten disasters from three clues had entered the room.

Dena looked at me.

"Building moving?"

"No."

"Water entering here?"

"No."

"Wall cracking?"

"No."

"Then we tell city now and leave until somebody with drainage keys comes."

"If it's a sealed channel under pressure, leaving doesn't reduce pressure."

"Opening wall might."

"Exactly."

Dena stared at me.

I heard myself.

Harl raised his cane and pointed it at my chest.

"Digging face."

Arlo laughed from behind us.

I did not.

Because part of me still wanted to.

Not recklessly.

Carefully.

Controlled opening.

Small bore near the upper seam.

Measure heat, air, pressure.

Useful information.

Current information.

Better than waiting.

The wall pulsed again beneath no one's hand.

This time I saw dust tremble along one mortar seam.

My body stepped toward it.

Dena caught the back of my coat.

Not hard.

Enough.

"Out."

I turned.

"I wasn't touching it."

"You were approaching with personality."

Arlo picked up the wrench case.

Harl started toward the stairs.

Pellin had returned and stood halfway down holding two wax temperature tabs.

"Got them."

Everyone looked at him.

He looked at the wall.

"Bad time?"

Dena took the tabs.

Pressed one to the warm stone.

Then another six feet away.

"Now out."

We went.

I was last up the stairs.

Rain hit the courtyard in silver ropes.

Bell Tile workers were moving finished stacks beneath awnings. Ivena stood under the main overhang arguing with Serit while writing something on the back of his own invoice.

The world remained commercial.

I looked back at the dark stair opening.

Warm stone below.

Unknown.

Old Greg had no answer.

I should have liked that.

I did.

I also wanted a hammer.
