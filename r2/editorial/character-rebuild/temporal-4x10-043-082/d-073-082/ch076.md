# Rehearsal D76: Old Salt

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The old salt store had become seventeen apartments and one illegal bakery.

This complicated drainage access.

Vaska stood in the street with an old municipal plan in one hand and the expression of a woman personally offended by urban history.

"Here," she said.

The paper showed a rectangular warehouse labeled OLD SALT STORE with a service well at the rear.

Reality showed a four-story brick tenement, laundry over the courtyard, three children chasing a hoop, one butcher sharpening a knife, and bread smell coming from a basement window that definitely did not have a bakery sign.

"Same footprint?" I asked.

"Mostly."

"Mostly is dangerous underground."

"Yes."

Ilan carried rope. Mett carried two lanterns and a short pry bar. Dena had come from Bell Tile because the warm-wall pulse still affected their service pipe. Pellin followed with a notebook until Ivena appeared behind him and took it.

"You're staying upstairs," she said.

Pellin stared.

"Why?"

"Because if Greg falls in a hole, we already own part of the problem. We don't need an apprentice too."

"I didn't fall in a hole."

My bruised knee objected to wording.

Vaska knocked on the rear door.

A woman opened it with flour on both forearms.

Bakery.

"City drainage," Vaska said.

Woman looked at paper, then at us.

"No."

"We need cellar access."

"No."

"There is a municipal service well under your rear room."

"There is flour under my rear room."

Dena leaned toward me.

"I like her."

Vaska did not.

The baker's name was Ema. She rented basement and first-floor rear. Her oven occupied the exact wall where Vaska's plan wanted a stairwell.

Current city had overwritten old infrastructure with bread.

"When was oven built?" Vaska asked.

"Before me."

"Landlord?"

"Upstairs."

Landlord was a man named Corl who took seven minutes to descend and another five to admit he owned building.

He denied knowledge of service well.

Vaska showed deed notation.

He denied understanding notation.

Ivena smiled in a way that meant money was about to become a weapon.

"If city opens wall," Corl said, "city pays repair."

"If you've built over access illegally, you pay," Vaska said.

"I didn't build."

"Then whoever did."

"Dead."

"Convenient."

Argument became property law.

I wanted underground.

The creature had struck gate all night according to Vaska's morning inspector. Three impacts. Pause. Two. Again.

Bell Tile's wall warmed once before dawn.

Something below was changing.

I kept looking at basement door.

Dena noticed.

"Don't."

"I'm standing."

"Your face is crawling."

Face slander again.

Eventually Corl signed emergency access with compensation deferred.

Ema made us move twelve flour sacks before she would let anyone touch wall.

This was the most physically honest part of municipal investigation.

Each sack perhaps fifty pounds.

We carried from rear room into front storage while oven made basement hot enough to sweat through shirts.

Ilan took two at once until Ema told him not to show off near bread.

He obeyed.

Mett and I carried one between us when my bruised knee complained on stairs.

Dena stacked.

Vaska documented everything because apparently flour location could become court evidence.

Behind the last stack, old brick differed from surrounding wall.

Narrow vertical seam.

Service doorway bricked over.

Ema stared.

"That's been there?"

"Yes," Vaska said.

"I thought repair."

"It is. Badly."

Ilan tapped mortar.

Hollow beyond.

We did not immediately break it.

Good restraint without applause.

Vaska checked plan, measured from courtyard, then had Corl sign second line authorizing opening.

Ivena photographed? No cameras. She copied witness names instead.

Then pry bars.

Mortar old enough to crumble around edges. We removed bricks one by one, stacking because Ema insisted they not contaminate flour.

After twelve bricks, cold damp air came through.

Wrong temperature.

Bell Tile wall had been warm.

Opening widened.

Lantern showed narrow stair descending behind wall.

Not well.

Stone steps.

"Plan wrong," Vaska said.

"Or building changed," Dena said.

I looked at blue mineral line in mortar beside stair.

Same material as drain ward.

Faint.

"Active."

Vaska glanced.

"How much?"

"Less than lower gate."

"Touch?"

"No."

We had established culture.

Ilan rigged a safety line anyway because steps disappeared into dark and old stone liked betrayal.

Vaska first.

Mett second.

Me third.

Dena stayed above with Ema and Ivena.

The stair descended one floor below basement, turned, then ended at iron door half submerged in water.

Not in plan.

Water lapped lower step.

Sulfur smell stronger.

Warmth too.

I crouched.

Water here was warm.

"This connects west channel?"

Vaska looked at old door.

"Should."

"Should."

"Yes, Greg. I know."

Three impacts sounded through door.

Metal vibration traveled step.

Pause.

Two.

Closer than yesterday.

Mett swore softly.

"That's large."

"Unknown," Vaska said.

I wanted to open door.

Every part of me leaned forward before thought.

Ilan's rope line behind my harness tightened slightly.

He had noticed.

"Nobody opens," he said.

"I wasn't."

"Body voted."

I hated him.

Vaska examined hinges.

Water line had risen several inches recently. Door bowed outward toward us, meaning pressure behind.

Opening could flood stair.

"Back," she said.

We backed.

Then floor moved.

Not earthquake.

A pulse under feet.

Warm water surged through gap beneath door.

Blue mineral lines brightened.

Three.

Pause.

Two.

The iron door jumped in frame.

One hinge bolt cracked stone.

"Up," Vaska said.

No hesitation.

We climbed.

Halfway, another impact.

Door below tore partly free.

Water roared.

I looked back.

Bad instinct.

Warm brown water hit lower stair and climbed fast.

"Move!"

Ilan shouted from above.

We ran.

Current became ankle, then shin before turn.

Mett slipped.

I caught harness strap, she caught rail.

No fall.

We reached basement opening as first wave hit stair top.

Dena saw water.

"Flour!"

Of course.

Emergency priority expanded.

Warm water spilled through bricked doorway into bakery rear room.

Not torrent enough to drown.

Enough to destroy business.

Ema screamed at us and started hauling sacks herself.

Everyone moved.

Ivena dragged wooden pallets. Pellin disobeyed earlier order and came down because water was now in bakery, not drain.

"Take front sacks!" Dena shouted.

He did.

We moved flour.

Physical absurdity after underground mystery.

Water spread across floor toward oven.

That mattered.

Hot oven base plus water maybe cracking stone, extinguishing fire unpredictably.

Ema ran to damper.

Ilan and Mett pulled remaining bricks to widen flow? No, worse. Instead they built temporary barrier with flour boards and empty sacks at doorway, redirecting water toward basement drain on opposite side.

I helped shovel sand from courtyard bucket into gaps.

My hands became paste.

Vaska ran upstairs to street and shouted for drain crew.

Corl appeared, saw water, said, "City pays."

Ivena nearly killed him with eyes.

Then another pulse came through floor.

Three.

Pause.

Two.

Water temperature rose noticeably against boots.

I opened mana sense.

Blue line under wall lit like thread.

Not explosion.

Flow.

Something carrying charge with water.

"Dena! Pipes?"

"What?"

"Bell Tile service line may be same network. Shut filtration feed now."

She understood and ran.

No proof.

Cheap precaution.

Ten minutes later city drainage crew arrived with portable pump and hoses.

They took over water management.

Vaska closed basement access.

Ema stood in front room surrounded by saved flour sacks, soaked to knees.

She looked at us.

"Bread today?"

No one answered.

She started laughing.

Not because funny.

Stress finding exit.

Then I laughed too.

Soon half room did.

Corl did not.

Ivena said, "You are paying her lost batch until city determines source."

"Why me?"

"Because I need somebody to invoice before lunch."

Ema liked her.

Outside, warm water ran from temporary hose into street gutter and steamed faintly in cold air.

The mystery was no longer under Bell Tile.

It had entered someone's bakery.

That changed stakes more effectively than any lore answer.
