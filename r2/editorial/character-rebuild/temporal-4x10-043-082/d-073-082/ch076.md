# Rehearsal D76: Old Salt

Status: **TEMPORAL 4×10 REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The old salt store had become seventeen apartments and one illegal bakery.

This complicated drainage access.

Vaska stood in the street with an old municipal plan in one hand and the expression of a woman personally offended by urban history.

"Here," she said.

The paper showed a rectangular warehouse labeled OLD SALT STORE with a service well at the rear.

Reality showed a four-story brick tenement, laundry over the courtyard, three children chasing a hoop, one butcher sharpening a knife, and bread smell coming from a basement window that definitely did not have a bakery sign.

"Same footprint?" I asked.

"Mostly."

"Mostly is dangerous underground."

"Yes."

Ilan carried rope. Mett carried two lanterns and a short pry bar. Dena had come from Bell Tile because the warm-wall pulse still affected their service pipe. Pellin followed with a notebook until Ivena appeared behind him and took it.

"You're staying upstairs," she said.

Pellin stared.

"Why?"

"Because if Greg falls in a hole, we already own part of the problem. We don't need an apprentice too."

"I didn't fall in a hole."

My bruised knee objected to the wording.

Vaska knocked on the rear door.

A woman opened it with flour on both forearms.

Bakery.

"City drainage," Vaska said.

The woman looked at the paper, then at us.

"No."

"We need cellar access."

"No."

"There is a municipal service well under your rear room."

"There is flour under my rear room."

Dena leaned toward me.

"I like her."

Vaska did not.

The baker's name was Ema. She rented the basement and first-floor rear. Her oven occupied the exact wall where Vaska's plan wanted a stairwell.

The current city had overwritten old infrastructure with bread.

"When was the oven built?" Vaska asked.

"Before me."

"Landlord?"

"Upstairs."

The landlord was a man named Corl who took seven minutes to descend and another five to admit he owned the building.

He denied knowledge of the service well.

Vaska showed the deed notation.

He denied understanding the notation.

Ivena smiled in a way that meant money was about to become a weapon.

"If the city opens the wall," Corl said, "the city pays repair."

"If you've built over access illegally, you pay," Vaska said.

"I didn't build it."

"Then whoever did."

"Dead."

"Convenient."

The argument became property law.

I wanted underground.

The creature had struck the gate all night according to Vaska's morning inspector. Three impacts. Pause. Two. Again.

Bell Tile's wall had warmed once before dawn.

Something below was changing.

I kept looking at the basement door.

Dena noticed.

"Don't."

"I'm standing."

"Your face is crawling."

Face slander again.

Eventually Corl signed emergency access with compensation deferred.

Ema made us move twelve flour sacks before she would let anyone touch the wall.

This was the most physically honest part of the municipal investigation.

Each sack was perhaps fifty pounds.

We carried them from the rear room into front storage while the oven made the basement hot enough to sweat through shirts.

Ilan took two at once until Ema told him not to show off near bread.

He obeyed.

Mett and I carried one between us when my bruised knee complained on the stairs.

Dena stacked.

Vaska documented everything because apparently flour location could become court evidence.

Behind the last stack, the old brick differed from the surrounding wall.

A narrow vertical seam.

A service doorway bricked over.

Ema stared.

"That's been there?"

"Yes," Vaska said.

"I thought it was a repair."

"It is. Badly."

Ilan tapped the mortar.

Hollow beyond.

We did not immediately break it.

Vaska checked the plan, measured from the courtyard, then had Corl sign a second line authorizing the opening.

Ivena copied the witness names onto the access sheet and made Ema initial the flour count.

Then came the pry bars.

The mortar was old enough to crumble around the edges. We removed the bricks one by one, stacking them because Ema insisted they not contaminate the flour.

After twelve bricks, cold damp air came through.

Wrong temperature.

Bell Tile's wall had been warm.

The opening widened.

Lantern light showed a narrow stair descending behind the wall.

Not a well.

Stone steps.

"Plan wrong," Vaska said.

"Or the building changed," Dena said.

I looked at a blue mineral line in the mortar beside the stair.

Same material as the drain ward.

Faint.

"Active."

Vaska glanced at it.

"How much?"

"Less than the lower gate."

"Touch?"

"No."

We had established a culture.

Ilan rigged a safety line anyway because the steps disappeared into darkness and old stone liked betrayal.

Vaska first.

Mett second.

Me third.

Dena stayed above with Ema and Ivena.

The stair descended one floor below the basement, turned, then ended at an iron door half submerged in water.

Not on the plan.

Water lapped against the lower step.

The sulfur smell was stronger.

Warmth too.

I crouched.

The water here was warm.

"This connects to the west channel?"

Vaska looked at the old door.

"Should."

"Should."

"Yes, Greg. I know."

Three impacts sounded through the door.

Metal vibration traveled through the step.

Pause.

Two.

Closer than yesterday.

Mett swore softly.

"That's large."

"Unknown," Vaska said.

I wanted to open the door.

Every part of me leaned forward before thought.

Ilan's rope line behind my harness tightened slightly.

He had noticed.

"Nobody opens it," he said.

"I wasn't."

"Body voted."

I hated him.

Vaska examined the hinges.

The water line had risen several inches recently. The door bowed outward toward us, meaning pressure behind it.

Opening it could flood the stair.

"Back," she said.

We backed away.

Then the floor moved.

Not an earthquake.

A pulse under our feet.

Warm water surged through the gap beneath the door.

The blue mineral lines brightened.

Three.

Pause.

Two.

The iron door jumped in its frame.

One hinge bolt cracked the stone.

"Up," Vaska said.

No hesitation.

We climbed.

Halfway, another impact.

The door below tore partly free.

Water roared.

I looked back.

Bad instinct.

Warm brown water hit the lower stair and climbed fast.

"Move!"

Ilan shouted from above.

We ran.

The current reached ankle height, then shin before the turn.

Mett slipped.

I caught her harness strap. She caught the rail.

No fall.

We reached the basement opening as the first wave hit the top of the stair.

Dena saw the water.

"Flour!"

Of course.

Emergency priority expanded.

Warm water spilled through the bricked doorway into the bakery's rear room.

Not a torrent enough to drown anyone.

Enough to destroy a business.

Ema screamed at us and started hauling sacks herself.

Everyone moved.

Ivena dragged wooden pallets. Pellin disobeyed the earlier order and came down because the water was now in the bakery, not the drain.

"Take the front sacks!" Dena shouted.

He did.

We moved flour.

Physical absurdity after underground mystery.

Water spread across the floor toward the oven.

That mattered.

Hot oven base plus water could crack stone or kill the fire unpredictably.

Ema ran to the damper.

Ilan and Mett built a temporary barrier from flour boards and empty sacks at the doorway, redirecting the water toward a basement drain on the opposite side.

I helped shovel sand from a courtyard bucket into the gaps.

My hands became paste.

Vaska ran upstairs to the street and shouted for the drain crew.

Corl appeared, saw the water, and said, "City pays."

Ivena nearly killed him with her eyes.

Then another pulse came through the floor.

Three.

Pause.

Two.

The water temperature rose noticeably against my boots.

I opened mana sense.

The blue line under the wall lit like thread.

Not an explosion.

Flow.

Something carrying charge with the water.

"Dena! Pipes?"

"What?"

"Bell Tile's service line may be the same network. Shut the filtration feed now."

She understood and ran.

No proof.

Cheap precaution.

Ten minutes later the city drainage crew arrived with a portable pump and hoses.

They took over water management.

Vaska closed the basement access.

Ema stood in the front room surrounded by saved flour sacks, soaked to the knees.

She looked at us.

"Bread today?"

No one answered.

She started laughing.

Not because it was funny.

Stress finding an exit.

Then I laughed too.

Soon half the room did.

Corl did not.

Ivena said, "You are paying her lost batch until the city determines the source."

"Why me?"

"Because I need somebody to invoice before lunch."

Ema liked her.

Outside, warm water ran from a temporary hose into the street gutter and steamed faintly in the cold air.

The mystery was no longer under Bell Tile.

It had entered someone's bakery.

That changed the stakes more effectively than any lore answer.
