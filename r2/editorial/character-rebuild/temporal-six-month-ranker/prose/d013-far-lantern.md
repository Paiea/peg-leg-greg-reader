# D013: Far Lantern

Status: **SIX-MONTH TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Carrow closed the east river road for one woman.

I found out because my cart stopped.

Not my cart.

Guild cart.

Important financial distinction.

The driver swore at a watchman holding red rope across Pell Road.

"Ranker passage," the watchman said.

The driver stopped swearing.

That got my attention.

Gold parties delayed traffic all the time through ordinary incompetence, large equipment, dead monsters, and people believing four Gold badges improved wagon turning radius.

A titled Ranker was different.

I leaned sideways to see past the canvas.

"Who?"

The watchman looked at me as if this should have been obvious.

"Far Lantern."

My stomach went cold.

Not fear.

Recognition without ownership.

Merel Dane.

S-class Ranker.

**Far Lantern.**

The title existed in my memory long before the woman did.

Kestrel Pass.

Seven nights of winter light.

Two thousand evacuees moved through a battlefield under a support lattice nobody after her had reproduced at full scale.

Dead at forty-three.

Or forty-four.

Old Greg had argued about the number once with someone drunk enough to care.

I had never met her.

Not really.

Maybe seen her.

A Guild hall somewhere when I was young. A blue coat crossing a balcony. People moving differently around somebody important.

Memory offered nothing I trusted.

Songs did.

Records did.

Technique manuals written twenty years after her death did.

Half of those disagreed.

"Why is she here?" I asked.

The watchman shrugged.

Excellent intelligence network.

Then the river changed direction.

Not actually.

For one breath it looked like it did.

A freight barge had lost its stern line upstream.

I heard shouting first.

Then wood struck stone.

The barge swung broadside into current.

Forty feet of loaded deck.

Maybe more.

Two draft horses on the tow path panicked as the loose line snapped across water.

The bow rope held.

Badly.

The whole barge rotated toward the lower bridge.

People ran.

The watchman dropped the red rope.

I got one foot down from the cart.

Then light appeared over the river.

Twelve lines.

No.

More.

I counted automatically and lost count at twenty because the geometry nested.

Pale gold planes crossed from quay wall to water, not touching the barge at first.

Then the moving hull met them.

The first plane bent.

Actually bent.

Force surfaces were not supposed to do that unless you wanted to spend mana proving a point.

The second took angle.

Third shifted load toward a fourth I had not noticed.

The barge slowed.

Not stopped.

Slowed.

Momentum moved sideways into the quay through a fan of light wide enough to cover half the river road.

No impact.

No giant flash.

The stern came around until the barge lay almost parallel to current again.

A final plane caught the loose end three feet from stone.

The horses stopped trying to become airborne.

Silence lasted maybe one second.

Then everybody started shouting again because humans disliked empty sound.

I remained half out of the cart.

My hands had gone cold.

Not from magic.

Professional offense.

I could make one feathered plane cleaner than I could six months ago.

Merel Dane had just redistributed a loaded river barge through at least twenty moving surfaces while walking.

Walking.

I found her fifty yards upstream.

Blue coat.

Gray hair braided once down her back.

No staff.

No dramatic stance.

She was talking to a harbor clerk.

One hand remained raised because three of the planes were still holding the barge away from quay while workers reset rope.

Three.

Of twenty-something.

Casually.

A man beside her wore Platinum.

Not Gold.

Platinum.

His badge looked almost decorative.

He waited for her to finish talking before speaking.

There.

One kind of ceiling.

Metal still mattered.

Bronze. Silver. Gold. Platinum.

It told the Guild what kind of contracts your current body, magic, judgment, and team could be trusted to carry.

A title was not the next metal.

A title meant something you had done had become important enough that people stopped describing only your badge and started using a name.

No title, no Ranker.

Then the tiny titled registry argued about letters.

C. B. A. S.

Not a clean power ladder either.

A Platinum could be stronger in a fight than a C-class Ranker and remain completely untitled.

A lower-metal adventurer could do one impossible, consequential thing and enter the registry at C while still being lower-metal for ordinary contracts.

The letters tracked the weight and repeatability of results attached to the title.

They could climb.

Most people never entered the list at all.

Merel had both kinds of authority.

Platinum by operating class.

S-class among titled Rankers.

Far Lantern because Kestrel Pass had made the name stick.

Old Greg had eventually become S-class too.

Current Greg could barely hold a wagon axle without thinking about socket pressure.

Both facts fit.

I hated how much I liked that.

"You getting down?" the cart driver asked.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Bad judgment."

I took my pack.

My contract could wait twelve minutes.

Maybe fifteen.

Reputation mattered.

So did not being stupid about it.

I told the driver where I was going.

"If I'm not back before the road opens, leave me."

He nodded.

No heroic disappearing from assigned transport.

Progress.

I walked upstream.

Not toward Merel directly.

Toward the barge.

There were twenty people closer to her with better reasons to speak.

Harbor workers threw new stern line.

The last three planes vanished one at a time.

No collapse in posture.

No visible drain.

Merel rubbed one thumb against the side of her forefinger.

That was all.

A Gold woman from a local road crew approached her.

"Ranker Dane."

Merel looked over.

"Don't call me that unless you're writing an invoice."

The Gold woman blinked.

"Far Lantern?"

"Worse. Merel works."

I almost laughed.

Didn't.

The Platinum man did.

Merel looked past them.

At me.

Not because destiny.

Because I was staring.

Fair.

Her eyes dropped once.

Walking leg.

Then my hands.

Then back to my face.

"You need something?"

Twenty possible answers arrived.

Old Greg would have chosen the useful one.

Current Greg remembered Faultglass.

"No," I said.

Merel waited.

Apparently that answer was unusual.

"Then why are you here?"

"I wanted to see if the stories were lying."

The Platinum man made a sound somewhere between cough and warning.

Merel smiled.

Small.

"Were they?"

I looked at the river.

"Wrong direction."

"Meaning?"

"They make it sound larger."

The smile disappeared.

Interesting.

"And?"

"That was cleaner."

The Platinum man looked at me now.

Not friendly.

Merel's attention sharpened.

"What was cleaner?"

I should have stopped.

I didn't.

"You didn't stop the barge. You gave the momentum somewhere else to go."

Her thumb stopped rubbing her finger.

"Name."

"Greg."

"Rank."

"Silver."

The Platinum man actually laughed.

Merel didn't.

"Work?"

"Load support. Ward interaction. Known routes. Prepared sites."

She looked at the wooden left foot again.

"Recently?"

"Yes."

"Magic before leg?"

"Yes."

"Different after?"

"Also yes."

She nodded once.

Then turned back to the harbor clerk.

Conversation over.

I stood there one second too long.

The Platinum man said, "Silver."

Not insulting.

Amused.

I looked at his badge.

"Platinum."

He smiled.

"Excellent observation."

"Working on it."

I walked back to the cart.

The driver had not left.

Road still closed.

"Worth it?" he asked.

I climbed in.

My left socket pinched slightly at the outer edge.

I adjusted before sitting.

Across the river, Merel Dane lifted one hand again.

A single pale plane formed under the barge stern while workers shifted cargo.

One plane.

Enough.

"Yes," I said.

The red rope dropped five minutes later.

We went to work.
