# D183: Field Attachment

Status: **SIX-WEEK TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Renn left southeast at first bell.

I left northwest before second.

Carrow managed to contain both events.

Disappointing city.

I saw his wagon once from the upper road.

Dark shirt from the wedding folded under his coat because apparently clean clothing had a lifespan of three days.

He saw me too.

Raised one hand.

I raised mine.

No running across roads.

No final warning about Dunbarrow.

No speech.

Good.

Merel Dane watched from the lead wagon.

"That him?"

"Yes."

"You going to stare until his wagon disappears?"

"Probably."

"Then do it from the right side. Teamster needs left mirror."

I moved.

Excellent mentorship.

Not mentorship.

Field attachment.

She had corrected that enough times I could hear the phrase without liking it.

Orris Hale sat across from me.

Platinum badge on his coat.

No title plate.

The route packet listed him as:

**ORRIS HALE — PLATINUM — FIELD VANGUARD / IMPACT CONTROL**

Merel's line read:

**MEREL DANE — PLATINUM — S-CLASS RANKER — FAR LANTERN**

Same metal.

Different second line.

I looked between them.

Orris noticed.

"Ask."

"You don't have a title."

"Excellent start to six weeks."

"I mean the packet."

"I know what you mean."

Merel did not turn around.

"He has been waiting three years for somebody to notice."

Orris leaned back against the wagon rail.

"I am devastated daily."

"You're Platinum."

"Correct."

"Stronger than some titled people."

"Many."

"And not a Ranker."

"Also correct."

I hated how simple he made it sound.

"Why?"

Orris thought.

"Never did anything history got attached to."

Merel looked back now.

"That's not quite fair."

"Never did anything history got attached to that survived committee."

"Closer."

I looked at Merel's title line again.

Far Lantern.

Kestrel Pass.

Seven nights.

Two thousand evacuees.

The name did not describe her mana.

It described what people remembered she had done with it.

Orris tapped his Platinum badge.

"This gets me contracts."

Then he tapped the blank space where title would have been.

"That would get me songs."

"You want one?"

"No."

Immediate.

Interesting.

"Why not?"

"Because people start expecting the story every time you enter a room."

Merel said, "He says that while traveling with me."

"Educational environment."

"Coward."

"Platinum coward."

The teamster snorted.

We left Carrow behind.

Summer road.

Dry enough for dust to get inside everything.

My walking socket had been adjusted nine days earlier and still hated long seated stretches more than actual walking.

I changed angle twice before noon.

Merel noticed once and did not ask.

Also educational.

Our first day contained no monsters.

No collapsing bridge.

No magical ruin.

We passed two farms, one broken cart, three children throwing dirt at a fence, and a toll clerk who made Merel show paperwork because titles apparently did not defeat municipal stamps.

Good institution.

At camp I expected Merel to begin.

Whatever beginning meant.

Assessment.

Lecture.

Training.

She ate stew.

Orris repaired a strap.

I waited long enough to become annoyed at myself.

Then asked.

"What am I supposed to be doing?"

Merel looked into bowl.

"Tonight? Eat."

"For six weeks."

"Work."

"Specific."

"You signed field support observer."

"Yes."

"Observe field support. Support field work when contracted and safe."

"That's it?"

"What did you expect?"

Too much.

I put spoon down.

"I don't know."

Merel looked at me.

Not kindly.

Not unkindly either.

Waiting.

I had spent three days refusing to turn identity into categories.

Now an S-class support Ranker sat across a fire asking a category-shaped question.

Rude.

"I want to understand why I became support," I said.

Orris stopped working strap.

Merel's face changed slightly.

"Old you?"

"Yes."

"You don't know?"

"I know what I did. I know I was good at it. I know people wanted me there."

"Not same thing."

"No."

Fire cracked.

Somewhere beyond camp a horse sneezed hard enough to sound offended.

Merel said, "Six weeks won't answer that."

"I know."

"Good."

I almost told her to stop using that word.

Renn had ruined it.

Merel continued.

"What do you want from me?"

Easy answer.

Techniques.

Distributed surfaces.

Moving planes.

How she bent force without wasting edge load.

How she held multiple remote anchors hundreds of yards apart.

All true.

Not enough.

"I want to know what you look at."

That came out before analysis.

Merel stared.

Orris smiled at strap.

"Better," she said.

I pointed spoon at her.

"You are becoming a problem."

"Field attachment."

"Worse branding."

"Still free to leave."

I looked down road we had come.

Carrow gone behind dark trees.

Renn heading opposite direction toward a town I remembered broken.

Mara farther northwest in Northbank, living a life I had not selected.

Noll owning part of a restaurant.

Daro willing to put my name on work again.

And me in a wagon with a dead historical woman who was inconveniently alive.

Time had become cheap.

Apparently that made choices more expensive.

"I'm staying," I said.

Merel nodded.

"Then sleep. We leave before first bell."

"For what?"

"Flood camp."

"Dangerous?"

"Wet."

Orris said, "He hates wet."

"How do you know?"

"Everyone hates wet."

Fair.

I finished stew.

No lesson.

No technique.

Day one cost me dust, socket heat, and a bowl I had to wash myself.

Field attachment.
