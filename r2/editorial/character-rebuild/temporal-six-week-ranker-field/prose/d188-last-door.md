# D188: Last Door

Status: **SIX-WEEK TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

Tessa Brin was shorter than me by almost a foot.

This was not difficult.

More relevant: Orris could probably throw her over the flood wall one-handed.

She was also the first C-class Ranker I met on purpose.

**Last Door.**

Gold badge.

C plate under it.

Title stitched in small gray thread because apparently people with permanent public names still had opinions about embroidery.

The flood camp at Reedbank used an old schoolhouse, two grain sheds, and every dry table in the village.

Tessa stood on one of those tables arguing with a cooper.

"No, barrels go upstairs."

"Floor won't take it."

"Then half upstairs. Half church. Blankets stay schoolhouse."

"Church leaks."

"Only north aisle."

"How do you know?"

"Because it leaked last flood. Move."

The cooper moved.

No glowing magic.

No title announcement.

No one saluted.

People just listened.

Merel waited until Tessa finished.

That got my attention more than title plate.

"Far Lantern," Tessa said.

"Last Door."

Both women made faces.

Orris whispered, "Titles are dignity poison."

I almost laughed.

Tessa looked at me.

"Who's tall Silver?"

"Greg," Merel said.

"Useful?"

"Occasionally."

"Good enough. Greg, upstairs floor. Tell me what it can carry before somebody stores a winter in it."

I went.

No introduction ceremony.

Professional paradise.

The schoolhouse had been built by someone who trusted oak more than arithmetic.

Good beams.

Bad repairs.

Two joists had water rot where the chimney leaked.

I marked a safe storage strip with chalk.

Tessa came up, looked once, and changed her barrel plan.

"Enough for twenty?"

"If they're not full."

"They're full."

"Then twelve."

"Church gets eight."

Done.

The flood itself was ordinary by Ranker standards.

River high.

Three farm lanes already underwater.

One retaining bank softening.

Nobody expected the village to disappear.

They expected wet beds, dead chickens, ruined grain, and six weeks of arguments about whose ditch caused what.

Expensive enough.

At lunch I sat beside Tessa under school porch.

She ate cold potatoes with salt.

Orris ate twice as much without visible shame.

I kept looking at her plate.

Not the food.

The title strip.

Tessa noticed.

"Ask before I charge you."

"Last Door."

"Regrettably."

"What happened?"

Orris stopped chewing.

Apparently I had asked acceptable question.

Tessa pointed potato at Merel.

"She didn't tell you?"

"Field attachment. She thinks ignorance builds character."

"It does. Bad character."

Merel drank tea.

Tessa said, "Briar Mine. Six years ago. Lower service run collapsed after gas fire. Main shaft gone. Secondary lift jammed. Eighty-six people below."

I knew the story.

Not well.

Old Greg remembered a line in rescue doctrine.

**BRIAR ACCESS: LAST DOOR PRINCIPLE.**

I had never connected it to a woman eating potatoes.

Tessa continued.

"Service tunnel had one timber throat left. I could reinforce enough of it to keep passage open."

"For how long?"

"Forty-one minutes."

Orris said, "Forty-three."

Tessa looked at him.

"Forty-one useful. Last two were empty tunnel and me being stubborn."

"Records say forty-three."

"Records like round lies."

"Forty-three isn't round."

"Then they like ugly lies."

I asked, "All eighty-six?"

Tessa shook head.

"Eighty-four. Foreman died before we reached him. One breaker went back for brother and both got caught past turn."

Not clean.

Songs probably made it clean.

"And that got the title?"

"That got people calling me Last Door. Registry took four months to admit everybody had already decided."

"C-class?"

"Entry C. Still C."

"You're Gold."

"Also still Gold."

I looked at Orris.

Tessa followed.

"He could beat me bloody in a yard."

Orris said, "Wouldn't."

"Because scared."

"Terrified."

Tessa bit potato.

"Metal says what kind of work Guild trusts your current capability to carry. Title says something happened that people kept needing your name for afterward. Letter says what kind of result history attached itself to that name. Different paperwork."

Merel said, "Better than the handbook."

"Handbook written by people who enjoy columns."

I thought of old Greg.

S-class.

A title I had not let myself remember aloud yet.

Not because memory failed.

Because saying it felt like putting on a dead man's coat.

Tessa looked at me again.

"You want one?"

"A title?"

"Everybody staring at the plate eventually asks themselves."

I thought about Faultglass.

Daro.

Harrow.

Sella refusing Renn's private file.

People in Carrow deciding whether my word cost more or less than last month.

"I want the reputation," I said.

Tessa laughed.

Not cruel.

"That's worse."

"Why?"

"Title is reputation after everybody else gets involved. You don't control the shape."

Merel looked at me over cup.

Of course she did.

I hated this field attachment.

That afternoon we moved blankets.

Hundreds.

Wet village children carried smaller bundles and lied about how many they could lift.

Orris spent an hour shifting full grain barrels with a strength that made Gold laborers look offended.

Tessa never touched one.

She knew which family had six people in one room, which widow would refuse church lodging unless somebody brought her cat, and which lane flooded first when west culvert backed.

Merel used three remote planes to reinforce the soft retaining bank while local crews packed stone.

I checked schoolhouse load twice.

At dusk rain started again.

Tessa stood in doorway counting beds.

No one asked her to hold a mine open.

Nobody needed the story.

They needed Last Door to know where Mrs. Pell's cat had gone.

She did.

Under a wagon.

Of course.

I went with a nine-year-old to retrieve it.

Cat hated me.

No title acquired.
