# D000: The Date

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

I arrived at the river stairs early.

Not very early.

Enough early that Mara was not there yet and I had time to resent myself.

The first barge crew had already started loading the forward deck.

Crates went down the ramp in pairs. One man called numbers. Another man repeated them. A third man appeared to have been hired specifically to disagree with both.

River work had a reassuring amount of yelling in it.

I stood beside a post with my hands in my coat pockets and tried not to look toward Vey's office every ten seconds.

I managed twelve.

Progress.

The sky over Carrow was gray at the edges and pale above the river. Morning mist sat low over the water, thin enough that the opposite bank existed but thick enough to make everyone over there look employed by mystery.

A cart wheel hit a gap in the quay stones.

The driver swore.

One of the barge hands swore back, apparently on principle.

The city was awake.

Mara appeared at the top of the stairs carrying the same travel bag she had carried into my room two nights earlier.

Blue cup inside.

I heard it knock against something when she took the first step down.

She saw me.

Stopped.

Looked up at the sky as if requesting patience from a higher authority.

Then continued.

"You are early."

"Good morning."

"How early?"

"Unclear."

"Greg."

"Seven minutes."

"That's early."

"By river standards?"

"By the terms I gave you last night."

Fair.

I took her bag when she reached me.

She let me.

That felt different from winning.

"Heavy," I said.

"Books."

"Why?"

"Because apparently Carrow knows things Halden doesn't."

"Dangerous precedent."

"Emmet found three old route books after you left."

"He has betrayed the city."

"He labeled them."

"Execution."

Mara smiled.

Tired still.

She had slept, but not enough.

There was a shallow crease beside one eye from whatever pillow Vey considered adequate for temporary staff. Her hair was tied properly this morning, which made the escaped strands of the last two days feel privately ours.

I disliked that thought immediately because it was sentimental and accurate.

The barge clerk shouted for Halden freight.

Mara looked toward the ramp.

"That's me."

"You're freight?"

"Today."

"Expensive packaging."

"Fragile too."

"Unproven."

She took the bag back from me.

I kept one hand on the handle for half a second too long.

Mara noticed.

Of course.

"They're not leaving yet," she said.

"I know."

"You looked like they were stealing me."

"Vey has paperwork. That makes it legal."

"Exactly."

She set the bag beside the post.

For a moment we just stood there.

Two people on wet stone beside a working river.

No room.

No ledger desk.

No excuse to be busy.

I had discovered in the last two days that Mara was easier when something else needed doing.

So was I.

This was inconvenient.

She looked at my coat.

"You wore the good one."

"This is not the good one."

"You only have two."

"Then statistically."

"Greg."

"Fine."

She reached up and flattened the collar where it had folded under itself.

A small movement.

My body treated it as an event.

"There," she said.

"Thank you."

"Don't make it strange."

"Too late."

Mara's fingers stayed at my collar for one second.

Then dropped.

The barge crew rolled a cask down the ramp badly.

Everyone nearby shouted at once.

The cask survived.

No one apologized.

Mara watched until it was secured.

"I got the appointment," she said.

I looked at her.

"What appointment?"

Her mouth tightened.

Not angry.

Preparing.

"Halden east desk."

"I thought you already worked there."

"Trial placement."

Ah.

Six weeks.

Travel, packet work, dates, warehouses, blue cup.

I had assumed the job was simply a job because most jobs I knew did not reveal a second job hiding underneath them.

"And now?"

"Two-year term."

The words landed cleanly.

Mara watched me receive them.

"Better pay," she said.

"Good."

She raised one eyebrow.

I heard myself.

"No. Sorry. That's not an approval."

"You lasted almost three days."

"Record."

"Possibly."

I put both hands into my pockets before they found another collar to fix.

"Do you want it?"

Mara looked toward the river.

"Yes."

Simple.

I waited.

She looked back.

"That's the part where you say something sensible."

"I'm trying to stop doing that when nobody asked."

"Terrifying growth."

"I hate it."

She laughed.

"I want it," she said again. "The east desk handles mixed river and road accounts. Half the problems are because the two sides record the same movement differently."

"Sounds miserable."

"It is."

"Congratulations."

"Thank you."

That one worked.

She nudged the toe of her boot against mine.

"There's a housing allowance after third month."

"Good housing?"

"Vey housing."

"So a room with walls."

"Most of them."

"Luxury."

"And if I finish the term clean, I can apply for route accounts or senior desk."

There it was.

Not a girl leaving Carrow.

Not someone I had forgotten.

A woman building something while I stood on the wrong side of a river schedule.

I liked it.

I hated parts of liking it.

Both seemed fair.

"When does it start?"

"Today."

"Of course."

"Vey enjoys paperwork that becomes true before you finish reading it."

"When does it end?"

Mara looked at me differently.

"Two years."

"Exact?"

"Very."

"Why?"

"Because Vey would measure a marriage in ledger quarters if allowed."

"That didn't answer it."

"First bell, two years from today. Halden office closes my term. I sign out my account keys, somebody counts everything I was responsible for, and then I either sign another posting or I don't."

"You have to be there?"

"Unless I'm dead."

I frowned.

"That is an aggressive exception."

"Vey policy."

"Do not test it."

"Approval voice."

"That one was a request."

"Accepted."

The barge clerk shouted another load number.

Mara checked the river clock mounted above the warehouse door.

Not yet.

Two years.

The number was absurd enough to become solid.

A year had once felt like a unit adults used to discuss harvests and rent.

Then I had lived sixty years.

Then I had woken nineteen again and six weeks had become long enough for people to leave, return, kiss me, correct my assumptions, and leave again.

Two years was not long.

Two years was enormous.

"Will you stay in Halden the whole time?" I asked.

"Probably not every day. Route audits travel. Vey sends people where the books stop agreeing."

"So I could come to Halden and miss you."

"Yes."

"Poor city design."

"It existed before your convenience."

"Allegedly."

Mara's smile softened.

"You don't have to come."

The sentence hit somewhere stupid.

I looked at her.

She did not take it back.

"I know."

"I mean it."

"I know."

"You said you'd come before."

There it was.

Young Greg.

Carrow getting small.

Halden eventually.

A promise made by a boy whose memories I had inherited selectively enough to be offensive.

"And I forgot."

"Yes."

No cruelty.

Worse.

Fact.

The river moved behind her.

One of the ropes creaked against a bollard.

A gull landed on a crate marked DRY GOODS and immediately began proving the label optimistic.

I looked at Mara's travel bag.

Blue cup.

Route books.

Two-year appointment papers, probably.

A whole life I was not responsible for carrying.

"When will you definitely be in Halden?"

Mara narrowed her eyes.

"Why?"

"Answer first."

"Dangerous phrasing."

"Please."

She considered.

"Last morning."

"The term close."

"Yes."

"First bell?"

"Before first bell. They take the keys at first bell."

"Where?"

Mara stared at me now.

She knew.

I had not finished knowing yet.

"Halden river office," she said. "Stone steps on the east side."

"Good."

"Greg."

"Two years from today."

"Don't."

I stopped.

Not because I changed my mind.

Because she had asked.

Mara rubbed her forehead once.

"You don't need to make another promise because you feel bad about the first one."

That was irritatingly possible.

I examined it.

Shame.

Two forgotten kisses.

Tavin.

The unopened letter.

Young Greg saying eventually.

Current Greg arriving seven minutes early because seven minutes apparently counted as penance.

Some of it was guilt.

Not all.

"I'm not trying to repair the old one."

"Then what are you doing?"

I looked toward the barge.

Men moved crates.

A woman checked passenger names against a slate.

Someone had already started arguing about a goat.

Ordinary departure.

No destiny visible.

"Making one I can remember."

Mara went still.

Too much sentence.

True anyway.

I continued before I could improve it into something worse.

"You said two years from today. Halden river office. Stone steps. Before first bell."

"I did."

"I'll meet you there."

She looked at me for a long time.

"Why?"

Fair question.

I had asked her the same thing without words when she sat outside my room six weeks ago.

Why are you here?

Why do you remember me?

Why should I matter if I can't remember enough of you?

Now she got to ask.

"Because you'll be deciding what happens next."

"That's my decision."

"Yes."

"You don't get a vote."

"I know."

"You're very convincing when you say that now."

"Practice."

Her mouth twitched.

I took one breath.

"I don't want a vote. I want to be there."

Mara looked away.

That was when I knew the sentence had landed.

Not because she cried.

She didn't.

Mara watched a deckhand drag a coil of rope out of the path of the goat.

The goat immediately moved into the path of a different rope.

Competent animal.

"Two years is stupid," she said.

"Yes."

"You don't know what you'll be doing."

"No."

"I don't know what I'll be doing."

"You know where you'll be that morning."

"Assuming Vey still exists."

"If Vey collapses, send a letter."

"To where?"

"Excellent question."

She looked back at me.

"You might hate me by then."

"Possible."

Her eyebrows rose.

"You could at least pretend."

"You might hate me too."

"Currently plausible."

"Then we can meet efficiently."

"For what?"

"To confirm."

Mara laughed.

Actually laughed.

Head down for a second.

When she looked up, her eyes were wet enough that I chose not to notice.

I am occasionally capable of professional restraint.

Rarely in useful professions.

"All right," she said.

The words changed my breathing.

"All right?"

"Stone steps. Halden east office. Two years."

"Before first bell."

"Not too early."

"Define too early."

"Greg."

"Fine."

She reached into her coat.

Pulled out a folded Vey slip.

I recognized the form from the upstairs office. Duplicate transfer record. Blank on one side except for a printed border.

Mara borrowed the pencil from behind my ear.

I had forgotten it was there.

She wrote on the back.

Not a speech.

Four lines.

**HALDEN EAST OFFICE**

**STONE STEPS**

**TERM CLOSE**

**FIRST BELL**

Then beneath it:

**TWO YEARS. DO NOT BE EARLY.**

She handed me the slip.

I read it twice.

"This is legally binding now."

"Only if your court system is extremely bad."

"Carrow."

"Fair."

I folded the slip carefully.

Too carefully.

Mara saw.

"If you lose that, I'm not writing another."

"Cruel."

"Memory training."

I put it into the inside pocket of my coat.

The barge clerk shouted, "Passengers west!"

There it was.

Mara picked up her bag.

This time I did not take it.

She noticed that too.

We walked down the last section of stairs together.

The river barge sat low against the quay under crates, passengers, two covered carts, and one goat who had apparently won every negotiation.

At the ramp, Mara turned.

No dramatic wind.

No final speech.

A porter waited behind her with a sack of flour and the expression of a man prepared to become impolite.

Mara hugged me.

Longer than yesterday.

My hands settled at her back without delay this time.

"Write," she said against my coat.

"Yes."

"Open mine."

"Hostile clarification."

"Necessary."

"Yes."

She stepped back.

"And don't spend two years planning the trip."

"I would never."

Mara looked at me.

"Fine. I would."

"Live somewhere in between."

That one hurt more than I expected.

Probably because she did not say with me.

She didn't need to.

"You too," I said.

Mara nodded.

Then she boarded.

The flour porter passed me immediately and muttered something about lovers blocking commerce.

Reasonable institution.

I moved aside.

Mara found a place near the middle rail.

Not romantic bow position.

Less spray there.

Of course.

The lines came off.

The barge pushed away from Carrow stone slowly enough that leaving looked ridiculous for the first thirty feet.

Mara lifted one hand.

I lifted mine.

Then the current caught the hull.

Distance became real.

I stayed until the barge passed the warehouse corner and disappeared behind morning mist.

Only then did I take the folded Vey slip out again.

Halden.

Stone steps.

Two years.

First bell.

Do not be early.

I had remembered wars for forty years.

This seemed easier.

I put the slip back inside my coat and went to find breakfast.
