# D341: Brell Again

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

The Brell bridge had acquired rules.

This was progress.

A board stood at the south approach with six lines painted in black.

**ONE LOADED WAGON AT A TIME**

**KEEP CENTER**

**NO CROSSING DURING ICE RELEASE**

**RIDERS WALK HORSES**

**REPORT RAIL DAMAGE AT BRELL CROSSING**

**DO NOT REMOVE THIS BOARD**

The last rule suggested history.

Jorren read it beside me.

"Board's new."

"Bridge isn't."

"Still here."

"Excellent inspection."

He kicked my boot lightly with his.

The same river ran beneath us.

Not the same water.

Old sentence.

Probably true enough to survive philosophy.

Eleven months earlier I had crossed this bridge with a caravan after two days of rain. A wagon wheel broke through the soft south approach. Jorren climbed onto a leaning cart. Meral shouted orders across brown water. I had caught a haul rope without knowing what I was going to do with it.

The bridge had survived.

So had we.

Now winter had stripped the trees along both banks.

Thin ice clung to shaded stones near shore.

The river itself ran dark and fast through the middle channel, carrying small plates of broken ice that turned slowly as they passed beneath the wooden deck.

The south approach was stone-faced now.

Someone had repaired the washout properly.

I liked that more than nostalgia.

Kessa did not look at the board first.

She went under the bridge.

Not directly under.

Professional adjacency.

A narrow maintenance path followed the south bank to the first stone footing. Kessa walked it with a hammer, a sounding rod, and a rope around her waist because the bank was frozen hard enough to punish confidence.

I stayed above.

Silver had not improved my qualifications for crawling beside winter rivers.

Our contract was a five-wagon mill movement from Carrow to Rellin Cross.

Not glamorous.

Expensive.

One wagon carried a replacement shaft for the lower grain mill, wrapped in oiled cloth and braced inside a timber cradle. Another carried brass collars, bearings, and two crates of machined parts that had taken three months to arrive from east of Carrow.

The mill factor had paid for two guards, one rigger, and one Silver support hand because replacing any single piece would cost more than hiring all of us.

I was the Silver support hand.

That sentence still felt stolen.

Edda guarded the front wagon.

Jorren had taken second guard because Vell requested him by name.

Kessa owned rigging and crossing judgment.

Vell owned his wagons until Kessa told him otherwise, which had happened three times before breakfast.

I owned whatever shaped load Kessa or Vell asked me to own.

No one had said party.

We had simply worked together often enough that Vell stopped checking which one of us had brought rope.

Jorren always had rope.

Kessa always hated his knots until she needed one.

Edda always carried dried apples and denied it when asked.

I had learned all three facts by being bored near them.

The road had become social memory.

Kessa returned from beneath the bridge after fifteen minutes.

Her knees were muddy.

Her expression was not worried.

Important distinction.

"South footing sounds clean," she said. "Deck repairs from autumn are holding. Third rail post on east side is loose. Doesn't matter for load."

Vell looked at the river.

"Ice?"

Kessa looked upstream.

A bell tower at Brell Crossing was visible through bare branches on the north bank.

The bridge keeper had told us the upper river had released twice in the last week when warmer afternoons broke shelf ice loose from the bends.

Not a flood.

A surge of water and broken ice lasting ten or fifteen minutes.

Enough that the board had acquired rule three.

"Keeper says no release since yesterday afternoon," Kessa said. "Water's steady."

"Cross now?"

"Light wagons first. Mill shaft last."

Vell frowned.

"Why last?"

"If anything moves under the first four, we don't put the expensive one on it."

"And if first four weaken it?"

Kessa looked at him.

"Then I'm wrong and we stop."

No defensiveness.

Vell nodded.

That was why people paid Kessa.

We unhitched the rear pair from wagon one before crossing.

Not necessary by weight.

Necessary because the board said riders walked horses and Vell believed rules were cheaper than explanations.

Edda walked the first team across.

Jorren watched the rear wheels.

Kessa watched the bridge.

I watched Kessa.

Also the bridge.

Mostly Kessa.

Not romantically.

Professionally.

Important clarification no one had requested.

Wagon one crossed.

The deck complained in normal wooden language.

No sharp knocks.

No visible deflection beyond what Kessa expected.

Wagon two followed.

Then three.

At the north side, Brell Crossing workers guided each wagon into a waiting area off the road so the bridge never held more than one loaded vehicle.

Competence was mostly people being willing to do slower things.

Wagon four carried the brass.

Heavier than the first three.

Kessa stopped it before the approach.

"Crates off."

Vell sighed.

"All?"

"Two."

We unloaded both bearing crates.

Forty minutes of labor to remove weight from a crossing that would take less than one minute.

Adventure.

Jorren and I carried one crate between us using a pole through the rope handles.

My shoulders barely complained now.

That was new enough that I noticed.

At the north side, we set the crates on dry planks near the wagon queue.

A boy from Brell Crossing stared at the Silver tab on my coat.

Then at my sword.

Then at the crate.

He looked disappointed.

Good education.

Wagon four crossed without incident.

Kessa walked the deck afterward.

Stopped near the middle.

Crouched.

Touched one plank.

I felt my chest tighten.

She stood.

"Ice underneath."

"Problem?" Vell asked.

"No. Piece lodged against crossbeam. Small."

Jorren looked upstream.

"More coming."

Thin plates.

Nothing large.

Kessa waited anyway.

Five minutes.

Ten.

The river stayed steady.

The mill shaft wagon waited on the south approach.

Long timber cradle centered over both axles.

Load heavy but balanced.

We had checked it twice that morning because the shaft had shifted one finger-width on the first downhill grade.

Kessa had added a second cross strap.

I had complained that one finger-width did not justify forty minutes.

She had asked how much a new shaft cost.

I stopped complaining.

"Take the forward collars off," Kessa said.

Vell stared at her.

"They're sixty pounds."

"Then you'll enjoy putting them back."

We unloaded them too.

Another twenty minutes.

No one hurried us.

This mattered later.

Not because anyone knew later existed.

Because the whole job had already been boring enough to prove we had tried.

The shaft wagon moved onto the bridge after midday.

Vell walked at the lead team's left shoulder.

Jorren walked right.

Edda stayed north with the other wagons and clear road.

Kessa stood at midspan against the west rail where she could see both wheel lines.

I stood three paces south of her.

Why me on the bridge?

Because Kessa had asked.

If the cradle shifted, my plane could guide the timber enough to keep it centered while the driver stopped.

Not hold the wagon.

Not hold the bridge.

Guide one piece long enough for ordinary brakes and ropes to matter.

Silver scope.

Specific.

I liked that.

The front wheels reached midspan.

Deck flexed.

Normal.

Kessa watched the east beam.

I watched the cradle.

One strap moved.

Not loose.

Settling.

I put my hand near the timber anyway.

No shape.

Ready.

The rear wheels came onto the central span.

A deep sound came from upstream.

Not loud.

Wrong.

Kessa's head turned.

So did mine.

Around the bend, perhaps two hundred yards upriver, the surface changed.

White line.

Broken ice.

Not enormous.

Enough.

"Release!" Kessa shouted.

The bridge keeper on the north bank heard her and rang the warning bell.

Three fast strikes.

Everyone moved.

Vell did not whip the horses.

Good.

He led faster.

Jorren took the right bridle.

The wagon was already more than halfway across.

Turning back would keep it on the bridge longer.

Kessa pointed north.

"Keep moving!"

We did.

The first ice reached the south footing below us.

Small pieces.

Then larger.

Water rose around the stone by inches, not feet.

The bridge shuddered once.

Deck vibration under my boots.

Not failure.

Impact.

The shaft wagon moved.

Front wheels reached north span.

Good.

Rear wheels crossed the center seam.

Almost.

A slab of ice the size of a table struck the south footing.

Broke.

Another hit behind it.

The sound traveled through the bridge as a dull knock.

Kessa swore.

"Go!"

We went.

The rear wheel crossed the seam.

North approach ten yards away.

Then the bridge moved under me.

Not down.

Sideways.

Half an inch.

Maybe less.

Enough that every old instinct arrived at once.

I shaped toward the wagon cradle.

Wrong target.

Stopped myself.

The cradle had not moved.

Bridge.

Kessa was already looking south.

Her face changed.

"Off!"

One word.

Complete.

I dropped the unused shape.

Ran north.

No argument.

Jorren and Vell pulled the horses onto the stone approach.

The wagon's front axle cleared the bridge.

Rear axle still on wood.

Another impact came from below.

Harder.

The east rail jumped.

Kessa was behind me.

No.

I looked back.

She had stopped at the center seam.

Not frozen.

Looking down through a gap in the deck.

"Kessa!"

"Footing moved!"

South footing.

Hidden from where we stood.

The bridge leaned another fraction.

She ran.

Good.

We reached the north bank as the rear wheels came off the deck.

Everyone clear.

The entire shaft wagon on stone.

No injury.

No lost cargo.

The bridge remained standing.

For the moment.

Kessa turned immediately.

"Close it."

The keeper was already moving the barrier across the north approach.

Edda ran to the south-side signal rope that crossed above the river and hauled the red closure flag up where approaching traffic could see it.

No one celebrated.

The ice surge passed under us for another nine minutes.

We watched from shore.

Broken plates struck the footings.

Most split.

Some rolled.

The bridge moved twice more.

Small.

Visible now because we knew where to look.

When the water settled, Kessa did not go underneath again.

"Not until river drops," she said.

The keeper nodded.

Vell looked at the five wagons safely north.

"We got lucky."

Kessa looked at him.

"We got off."

Different statement.

I liked hers better.

Brell Crossing sent two workers downstream to watch for debris and one rider south to stop road traffic before wagons reached the bridge.

The keeper wrote the closure time in his book.

Then looked at us.

"You saw movement?"

Kessa nodded.

"South footing or bed under it. Can't tell which from deck."

"It was checked autumn."

"I know."

No accusation.

No easy fault.

The river had spent winter working where inspections could not see.

The mill shaft continued north after lunch.

That surprised me.

Part of me expected the whole contract to stop because something nearly happened.

Commerce disagreed.

We reloaded the brass collars.

Repacked the bearing crates.

Checked the shaft straps again.

Then kept moving.

The bridge stayed behind us with red flags on both approaches and two men waiting for the water to drop enough to inspect the footing.

Jorren walked beside me for the first mile.

"You shaped?"

"Almost."

"At what?"

"Wrong thing."

"Useful."

"Bad word."

"Silver word now."

"Leave."

My hands were cold despite better gloves.

Not because I needed it on every road.

Because moving rooms, coats, and bags had taught me that important paper survived better when it had one place.
