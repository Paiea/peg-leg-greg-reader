# D350: Below the Knee

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

My foot died before I did.

Tovan checked three times.

I appreciated the thoroughness and hated every repetition.

First with his fingers.

Then with a thin metal disk he warmed over a lamp and pressed against the skin below my ankle.

Then with a healer named Ressa who closed her eyes with both hands around my calf and listened to something I could not hear.

When she opened them, she looked at Tovan before she looked at me.

That was enough.

"Say it," I said.

Tovan sat on the stool beside the bed.

"The foot isn't viable."

Professional sentence.

Small words.

I looked at the blanket.

My left leg made a shape beneath it.

Knee.

Shin.

Foot.

Still there.

Current fact fighting future fact.

"How high?"

Tovan's face changed slightly.

Maybe he had expected a different question.

"If the tissue stays good where it is now, below the knee."

"How far below?"

He touched the blanket perhaps a handspan beneath my kneecap.

"We need enough healthy coverage to close. I won't promise the exact line until we're in it."

"Knee survives."

"That is the plan."

Plan.

Not promise.

Good.

I had seen amputations in my first life.

Battlefield ones.

Bad ones.

Late ones.

Men who died because the wound was filthy before anyone touched a knife.

People who survived because somebody had done the ugly thing quickly.

I had never been the leg.

That distinction turned out to matter.

"How long can I wait?"

"Not long."

"Hours. You said."

"Yes."

"How many?"

Tovan looked toward the window.

Late afternoon light had gone blue.

"I would operate before midnight. Earlier is better."

"If I refuse?"

Ressa answered.

"The dead tissue poisons what we're trying to save. Infection risk climbs. So does bleeding when damaged vessels fail again."

No threat.

No persuasion voice.

Information.

I hated both of them less for that.

Kessa sat in the second chair by the wall.

Not my second chair.

Brell owned one too.

Inferior model.

She had been there since someone brought my coat from the bridge.

Mud dried on her trousers.

One sleeve had a tear near the elbow where the brace had knocked her against the platform rail.

I had not noticed until now.

"You hurt?" I asked.

Kessa looked at me.

"Bruise."

"Show."

"No."

"Assessment voice."

"You're in bed."

"Temporary authority."

"Greg."

Fine.

I looked at Tovan again.

"What do you use?"

He misunderstood.

"For the operation?"

"For pain."

That was the question current Greg needed.

Tovan nodded.

"Sleep draught. Ressa keeps you under and controls bleeding with me. You should not wake during it."

Should.

Excellent word.

"After?"

"Pain medicine. Healing work for closure and infection risk. Not enough to erase the wound."

"Good."

Bad word.

No one corrected me.

"Walking?"

Tovan did not answer immediately.

"Eventually."

"With what?"

"Crutches first. If the stump heals well, fitted leg later."

There.

Fitted leg.

The phrase was almost ordinary.

I knew wooden prosthetics.

Old ones.

Peg legs, socket legs, straps, terrible pressure points, people who could walk better than strangers expected and worse than stories claimed.

Later Carrow had craftsmen who made articulated feet for rich merchants and officers.

Later.

Not now.

Current first.

"Who fits them?"

"Depends where you live."

"Carrow."

"Good city for it."

I almost laughed.

Of course.

Carrow had specialists for doors nobody owned and wards nobody understood. It could probably produce a man whose entire profession was arguing with knees.

"How soon?"

"Months before a final fit. Temporary earlier if healing is clean."

Months.

The word hit harder than two years had.

I looked toward Kessa.

"My room has stairs."

She closed her eyes.

Not grief.

I had apparently offended her with practicality.

"Yes," she said.

"Three floors."

"I know."

"Maud will charge extra if people carry me."

Kessa laughed.

One sharp sound she tried to stop and failed.

Good.

Tovan looked confused.

"Landlord," I said.

"Ah."

He did not understand.

No one understood Maud quickly.

I looked at the blanket again.

"Do it."

The room changed.

Not dramatically.

Tovan leaned forward.

"I need you to understand what you're agreeing to."

"Remove the lower left leg below the knee because the foot and damaged tissue will not recover and leaving it puts the rest of me at risk."

He watched me.

"Yes."

"Do it."

"You can have a witness."

I pointed at Kessa without looking.

"Unfortunately."

"I'm charging," she said.

"Put it on bridge invoice."

Another laugh.

Smaller.

Then silence returned.

Tovan asked me to repeat my name.

My age.

Nineteen.

That one felt particularly insulting.

He asked whether there was anyone else who should be told before the operation.

I thought of Noll.

Jorren.

Mara.

Three different problems.

"Noll Rook," I said.

Kessa interrupted.

"Noll doesn't have Rook as a surname."

"I know. Lower Rook. Noll."

"I know Noll."

"Then why interrupt?"

"You sound concussed."

"Existing condition."

Tovan waited.

"Jorren," I said. "He's with Vell on the south detour. He'll reach Carrow tomorrow if the road holds."

Then stopped.

Mara.

Halden.

382 days.

No.

381 after midnight.

Still counting at the wrong time.

Kessa watched me.

She knew the name waiting.

Did not say it.

That helped.

"Mara," I said.

"Halden?" Kessa asked.

"Vey east office."

"What do I tell her?"

I looked at her.

That was suddenly impossible.

Not because words did not exist.

Too many did.

Bridge accident.

Crushed leg.

Amputation.

Alive.

Sorry.

Don't come.

Please come.

I had no right to choose what she did with the information.

That was familiar.

"Facts."

Kessa waited.

"Bridge repair accident. Left leg crushed. They have to take it below the knee. I'm alive."

"Anything else?"

I almost said no.

Then:

"Tell her I still intend to be there."

Kessa's face tightened.

There.

Too dramatic.

I heard it after speaking.

"No."

She raised an eyebrow.

"Don't send that."

"Why?"

"Because I just lost an argument with my own leg and apparently became a poet."

Kessa looked at Tovan.

"Can you remove his mouth too?"

"Different specialist."

Good surgeon.

I thought again.

"Just facts. Let me write after."

Kessa nodded.

Better.

No promise weaponized from a bed.

Mara would get the same thing Noll got.

What happened.

What was happening.

What had not happened yet.

The rest belonged later.

Tovan left to prepare.

Ressa stayed.

She gave me water in small amounts.

Then medicine that made the room warmer at the edges without making the leg less present.

Kessa sat beside the wall.

For ten minutes neither of us spoke.

This was unusual enough to become uncomfortable.

"Was it the brace?" I asked.

Kessa looked up.

"What?"

"Our brace. Wrong?"

She answered immediately.

"No."

Too immediately.

I stared.

She swore.

"Not no forever. No based on what I know. New anchor held. Footing shoe held. Bank failed under and west of the anchor."

"Because we thawed it?"

There.

The question she had probably been asking herself for hours.

Kessa looked at her hands.

"Maybe partly."

No dodge.

"Charcoal trench was shallow. Scour channel was deeper. Thaw could have sped the roof giving way. Water could have done it tonight anyway. Packing the void could have redirected seep. Pulling the old brace could have changed pressure. All of it could matter."

"So we don't know."

"No."

"Will we?"

"Maybe."

I made Jorren's noise weakly.

Kessa smiled without looking up.

Then her face changed.

"I put you there."

There it was.

Not technical.

I closed my eyes for one second.

Opened them.

"You put me on a platform for nine breaths."

"Then kept you on the crew."

"I signed the contract."

"I asked you to stay."

"Because I can do the work."

"Could."

She flinched after saying it.

Not because she meant harm.

Because grammar had arrived before mercy.

Could.

Past tense.

My chest tightened.

There was the first thing I actually hated.

Not Kessa.

The tense.

"Can," I said.

She looked at me.

"Greg."

"Some of it."

"You don't know that yet."

"Neither do you."

She looked away.

Fair hit.

Not good one.

I regretted it and did not apologize immediately.

Current Greg remained available.

After a while Kessa said, "Can."

Quiet.

Not promise.

Correction.

That was enough.

Tovan returned after dark.

Two assistants with him.

Ressa stood.

The sleep draught came in a small ceramic cup.

Smelled like bitter cherries and metal.

I looked at it.

"How long?"

"Operation?"

"Sleep."

"Several hours."

"Wake tonight?"

"Probably morning."

Probably.

Fine.

I drank.

Terrible.

"This is worse than Noll's first stew," I said.

Kessa leaned close enough that I could see the tear in her sleeve again.

"I'll tell him."

"Don't. He'll compete."

The room softened.

Not visually.

Edges stopped mattering.

Ressa put one hand over my wrist.

Warm.

Tovan asked me something.

I answered.

Maybe.

The last thing I felt clearly was my left foot.

Cold.

Not pain.

Cold toes under a blanket.

That was absurd because the foot had no blood worth naming.

Still cold.

Still mine.

Then nothing.

When I woke, morning light was on the wall.

My mouth tasted like old copper.

Someone had put a different blanket over me.

My left foot was cold.

I tried to pull it under the other leg.

Nothing moved.

The sensation remained.

Cold toes.

Tucked slightly inward.

I opened my eyes fully.

For several seconds I did not remember.

Then I did.

I looked down.

Right leg made the familiar shape beneath the blanket.

Left stopped below the knee.

Not flat.

Bandaged rise.

Then nothing.

My foot was gone.

My foot still felt cold.

Both things were true.

I stared until the door opened.

Tovan came in carrying a cup of water.

He saw where I was looking.

Did not say anything profound.

Good man.

"How do you feel?" he asked.

I considered the question.

Pain.

Heavy and hot beneath the bandage.

Head thick.

Mouth dry.

Toes cold where no toes existed.

Alive.

"Thirsty."

Tovan handed me the cup.

That was the first problem.

I drank.
