# D365: One Year

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

One year after Mara left, I dropped a spoon.

It landed under the bed.

This became the morning.

I stared at it from the chair.

The spoon stared back with the confidence of an object beyond immediate consequences.

My crutches leaned against the wall beside me.

Too far.

Not actually too far.

Six inches beyond comfortable reach.

I could stand.

Take two steps.

Turn.

Get them.

Then retrieve the spoon.

Or I could bend sideways from the chair and possibly fall into the bed.

The second option had efficiency.

I tried it.

Badly.

My right hand caught the mattress.

The chair shifted.

My left knee lifted instinctively to balance a foot that no longer participated.

Nothing happened below the bandage except pain.

I stopped.

Sat upright.

"Fine."

The spoon remained unconvinced.

I reached for the crutch.

Fingertips.

Missed.

Again.

Caught the leather wrap near the top and dragged it toward me until the second crutch fell against the wall.

Wood on plaster.

Loud.

Noll called from the cookroom.

"Dead?"

"Spoon."

Pause.

"Worse."

Good household.

I stood properly this time.

Crutches under arms.

Weight through hands.

Right foot.

No left foot.

My body knew more than it had a week ago and less than I wanted it to.

I turned.

Reached down using one crutch as support.

The spoon sat two inches beyond my fingers.

Of course.

I considered kicking it closer.

Current inventory objected.

"Noll."

He appeared in the doorway holding a knife and half an onion.

"What?"

I pointed.

He looked under the bed.

Then at me.

"You want spoon?"

"No. I was showing you where I keep it."

Noll crouched.

Picked it up.

Put it on the table.

No ceremony.

"Thanks."

"Bad storage."

"Temporary system."

He left.

I sat again.

The ground-floor room smelled faintly of onions now even with the door closed.

This had been an advantage for the first three days.

It was becoming architecture.

My upstairs room still existed.

Maud had extended the double-rent arrangement through the end of the month after I negotiated her down by exactly half a copper.

Victory.

Most of my things remained upstairs because I had discovered possession and access were different categories.

Jorren fetched clothes.

Noll fetched books.

Maud fetched nothing unless paid.

I had gone upstairs once.

Yesterday.

Tovan had not approved.

He had also not been present.

Important jurisdictional fact.

Jorren and Noll were.

They put one man above me and one below while I learned that stairs on crutches were a different species than flat ground.

Up with the good leg.

Crutches follow.

Again.

Slow.

The fourth stair squeaked beneath my right boot.

I stopped there long enough that Jorren asked whether I was dizzy.

"No."

"Then move."

"History."

"Stair."

Noll, behind me, said, "Bad stair."

No one appreciated anything correctly.

I reached the third floor sweating hard enough to need a shirt change.

Then stood in my own doorway and realized the room had become smaller.

Not physically.

Paths.

Chair near window.

Bed against wall.

Table.

Field bag.

All the little spaces I used to cross without noticing now had widths.

Turning circles.

Places a crutch could catch.

The good sword hung on its wall hooks.

I touched the sheath.

Did not take it down.

Going upstairs had been enough ambition for one visit.

Back in the ground room this morning, the spoon had beaten me anyway.

Reasonable correction.

Tovan arrived after breakfast.

He had started coming every second day because Brell had transferred my follow-up to a Carrow healer's room but Tovan wanted to see the wound himself while he was still in the city for bridge testimony.

The bridge investigation had become paperwork.

Large paperwork.

City engineer.

Brell keeper.

Quarry-style ground specialist whose title I had forgotten because he used six words where one should exist.

Kessa had given evidence twice.

Pell three times.

I had given mine once from bed and declined to improve it afterward.

Current finding remained irritatingly distributed.

The hidden scour had extended farther under the bank than anyone knew.

The winter ice release had accelerated it.

The shallow first brace was inadequate but had not failed.

The replacement brace angle was sound.

The thaw trench may have weakened part of the upper crust but did not reach the deepest channel.

Packing rubble changed seep paths shortly before the collapse.

No one could prove which change caused the final roof of the hidden channel to give way.

The bridge had been closed before the repair.

Workers had cleared the lower platform when movement appeared.

Then the bank failed outside the expected collapse line.

Not nobody's fault.

Not one person's fault.

A list of decisions, conditions, limits, and missing ground.

Mara would appreciate that.

Probably.

Tovan put his bag on the table.

"Bandage."

I rolled the trouser leg higher.

Current trousers had one full leg and one shortened left side tied loosely above the bandage.

Noll had cut the first pair.

Badly.

Then Vessa had apparently seen them, sworn at him, and sent a woman from the laundry room upstairs with a needle.

Now the edge was hemmed.

Social medicine.

Tovan unwrapped the stump.

There was the word again.

Less offensive now.

Maybe repetition killed drama.

The incision curved across the front below my knee, dark thread still in place. Swelling had come down enough that the shape beneath it looked less like injury and more like part of a leg that ended.

Not attractive.

Not horrifying.

Mine.

Tovan touched the skin above the closure.

"Pain?"

"Yes."

"More specific."

"Rude."

"Greg."

"Deep ache most of the time. Sharp at the outer side when I stand too long. Burning here."

I pointed.

He checked.

"Better than three days ago?"

"Yes."

"Drainage?"

"Less."

"Fever?"

"No."

"Sleep?"

I looked at him.

"Different profession."

"Answer."

"Bad."

"Pain wakes you?"

"Sometimes. Foot wakes me more."

"Phantom?"

"Heel hurts now. Toes stopped itching."

"Good."

I did not correct him.

He checked the closure for several minutes.

No magic fireworks.

Ressa had used healing during surgery and the first days to help tissue close and reduce infection risk. Tovan had made it very clear that more was not always better because forcing surface closure over damaged deeper tissue could hide problems rather than solve them.

I liked medicine less every time it resembled ward maintenance.

"Stitches in four days if this stays clean," he said.

"Then?"

"Then it stays clean without stitches."

"Walking."

"Crutches."

"Fitted leg."

Tovan sighed.

"You ask every visit."

"Has the answer improved?"

"No."

"Then your profession lacks urgency."

He smiled slightly.

"Temporary training socket maybe in three to five weeks if swelling settles and the incision tolerates pressure. Proper fit after the shape stabilizes. Could be several months."

"Three weeks."

"I said three to five."

"I heard the useful number."

"You'll hear five when you split the wound being clever."

Hostile medicine.

He rewrapped the limb.

Then looked at the crutch handles.

"Hands?"

I showed him.

Two blisters.

One already hardening.

"Gloves."

"I have gloves."

"Wear them inside."

"Absurd."

"Blisters don't care about architecture."

Everyone had phrases now.

After Tovan left, I went to the Guild.

Not for work.

Sella had sent a note asking for me.

That distinction mattered enough that I repeated it to Noll twice.

He did not ask.

The Guild was six streets away.

Before Brell, I would have called that close.

Now I divided streets by places to sit.

The baker had a bench outside.

Copper Street had a low wall.

Guild lane had nothing because apparently adventurers were expected to arrive with legs as standard equipment.

I made it without sitting.

Barely.

My hands hurt.

Right calf shook by the last corner.

People looked.

Some at the crutches.

Some at the missing lower leg.

Some at my face after realizing I had noticed.

One man moved aside so aggressively he almost stepped into a cart.

I appreciated the space and resented the performance.

Both.

At the Guild door, Sella came around the counter when she saw me.

I disliked that immediately.

"Don't."

She stopped.

"I was opening the door."

The door was already open.

We looked at it.

Sella looked back at me.

"Fine."

Better.

I crossed the hall.

The Silver tab was still on my coat.

I had considered removing it.

Not because rank vanished with bone.

Because I did not know what it meant yet.

That uncertainty annoyed me enough that I left it there.

Sella put three papers on the counter.

"First is your Brell contract pay. Full repair rate through injury day."

"Good."

"Second is Guild field injury support."

I looked at her.

"We have that?"

"You have paid into it on every contract since registration."

I thought about all the tiny deductions I had complained about.

"How much?"

She told me.

Not generous.

Not insulting.

Enough to cover part of Tovan, wagon home, and several weeks of reduced work.

"Useful institution," I said.

Sella looked offended by the compliment.

"Third is temporary scope hold."

There it was.

I read.

No road combat.

No open-water structure work.

No active load support requiring unsupported standing.

No field response until healer clearance and Guild reassessment.

My jaw tightened.

"Silver?"

"Still Silver."

"Then why reassessment?"

"Because your body changed."

Simple.

I hated her.

"My judgment didn't."

"No."

"My magic didn't."

"Probably not."

"Sword skill."

"Body changed."

Same answer.

I looked at the paper.

"What can I do?"

Sella pulled a fourth paper from beneath the counter.

Prepared.

Malicious.

"Bench ward work. Inspection records. Static shaping where you're seated or braced. Training support. Some warehouse work once Tovan clears pressure and fatigue."

"Clerical."

"Some."

"You know I hate records."

"Mara likes them."

I stared.

Sella almost smiled.

Everyone had routes.

"Temporary," she said.

"How temporary?"

"Ask Tovan."

"He has no useful numbers."

"Then become patient."

"Different specialist."

That got her.

Small laugh.

Then she became Sella again.

"You don't need to decide today."

I looked at the work board.

Silver contracts above eye level.

Road escort.

Warehouse response.

North Quarry follow-up.

A three-day river movement.

Things I could have signed eleven days ago.

Could.

There was that tense again.

I looked away.

"Bench work pay?"

Sella told me.

Lower.

Not devastatingly.

Enough to live if I stopped paying for two rooms like an idiot.

"I'll take two shifts next week."

"Tovan cleared?"

"For sitting?"

"Greg."

"I'll ask."

"Ask first."

"Hostile bureaucracy."

"Alive bureaucracy."

I signed nothing.

Progress.

Outside the Guild, I sat on the low step despite having mocked it for existing the first time I joined.

My arms needed rest before the six streets home.

A Bronze adventurer came out behind me and hesitated because I blocked half the stair.

I shifted my crutches.

"Other side."

"Thanks."

He passed.

Looked at the Silver tab.

Then the bandaged leg.

Then kept walking.

No question.

Good man.

I rested five minutes.

Maybe seven.

No need to count everything.

At Lower Rook, a letter waited.

Not Mara.

Kessa.

Same city.

No packet seal.

Someone had carried it three streets because apparently she could not simply knock on my door.

I opened it in the ground room.

One page.

**Bridge report meeting tomorrow. I am not going. Pell can explain soil and the city engineer can explain inspection intervals. I have explained the brace three times and it remains the same brace.**

Good.

Then:

**I have a warehouse roof job next week. Flat floor. No river. You are not invited. I am writing this because I almost invited you and then got angry at myself for almost inviting you. I don't know what your work is now and I am trying not to decide for you. This is difficult because deciding for people is most of rigging.**

I laughed.

Then stopped at the final line.

**When you know what you can do, tell me. Not what you used to do.**

I put the letter on the table.

Not could.

Can.

Different direction.

I found paper.

Before answering Kessa, I noticed the Vey slip under Mara's latest letter.

One year old today.

I had not remembered the date until then.

No bell.

No anniversary visit.

No river stairs.

The paper had softened at the folds from being moved through too many rooms and coats.

The writing remained.

HALDEN EAST OFFICE.

STONE STEPS.

TERM CLOSE.

FIRST BELL.

TWO YEARS. DO NOT BE EARLY.

I looked at the empty space below my left knee.

Then at the crutches.

Then at Kessa's letter.

One year remained.

I put the Vey slip back under Mara's letter.

Then wrote Kessa first.

**I can sit extremely well. Start there.**
