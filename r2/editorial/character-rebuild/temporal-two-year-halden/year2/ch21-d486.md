# D486: Prepared Site

Status: **TEMPORAL REHEARSAL / ZERO AUTHORITY**

---

My first field job back had three exits, two benches, one healer, and no river.

Sella had negotiated like she was sending a child to market.

I signed anyway.

The old dyehouse sat south of Carrow behind a collapsed brick wall and enough thorn growth to prove nobody had wanted the property recently.

The city planned to turn the site into storage.

First problem: cellar wards still held charge in three places.

Second problem: one interior support wall had cracked during winter frost.

Third problem: nobody knew whether the old drain tunnel beneath the building remained open.

Prepared site.

Known surface exits.

No pursuit requirement.

No open water.

Job lead accepted my clearance.

Everything written correctly.

I still hated that Sella had attached my restriction sheet to the contract in red thread.

"It's visible," I said.

Kessa looked at the thread.

"That's why red exists."

"Humiliating color."

"You want gray?"

"Yes."

"Then nobody reads it."

Fine.

The team was small.

Kessa on structure.

Meren on wards.

Edda on security because old industrial sites occasionally contained animals, thieves, or people who claimed ownership after hearing someone else was repairing them.

Me on load and static support.

One city laborer named Pellin who had no relation to Pell and was tired of explaining it.

Excellent team identity.

I wore the third training leg with a new lower shaft.

Same simple foot.

Different socket.

Bera had widened the rear edge because the old one pinched after long standing.

I carried the short staff clipped to my pack instead of in hand.

Good sword at my right hip.

No crutches.

That still felt like theft.

Not freedom.

Something stolen back.

The dyehouse floor was terrible.

Broken tile.

Old dye pits covered with warped boards.

Brick dust.

One patch of dried blue powder that Meren told everyone not to touch until she decided whether it was merely poisonous or magically poisonous.

Current industry had standards.

Kessa had placed two benches before I arrived.

I pretended not to appreciate this.

She pretended not to notice.

We began at the west support wall.

Crack ran from floor to shoulder height beside an old timber post.

The post still carried load.

Brick around it had shifted.

Kessa wanted to remove the loose outer course and see whether the timber behind had rotted.

"I can brace the post while Pellin clears brick," I said.

Kessa looked at my leg.

There.

Quick.

Professional.

Still a look.

"Seated," she said.

"Better angle standing."

"Clearance says static shaping may be standing if braced."

She knew my restriction sheet better than I did.

Annoying.

"Wall brace counts."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the wall is the thing we're testing."

Right.

I hated correct people.

We moved the bench.

I sat with the left side angled toward the post and planted the wooden foot flat enough to stabilize my hip without taking full load.

Plane formed between both hands.

Six inches wide.

Vertical.

Pressed against timber.

Not holding the building.

Holding enough change that Kessa could see what happened when Pellin removed the outer brick.

One brick.

Two.

Mortar dust.

Nothing moved.

Pellin took a third.

The timber surface behind was dark.

Kessa scraped it.

Sound wood beneath.

"Good," she said.

I felt the plane flex.

Not much.

"Wait."

Everyone stopped.

There.

No debate.

That felt good.

Too good.

I lowered pressure slightly.

The timber moved toward me half a finger.

Kessa saw.

"Load changed."

"Not from the bricks. Something below."

She crouched.

Looked at the floor line.

Pellin said, "Drain?"

Maybe.

The old drain tunnel ran under the south half of the building according to city plans.

Plans were copied from a twenty-eight-year-old survey that used the phrase **approximately centered** twice.

Criminal document.

I shifted the plane lower.

The pressure changed again.

Not wall load.

Upward movement.

Something beneath the floor was lifting the post base very slightly.

Meren stopped her ward inspection across the room.

"Magic?"

"Maybe."

She came over.

Knelt.

Put two fingers against the tile.

Closed her eyes.

"Charge."

Edda's spear came off her shoulder.

"From the ward?"

Meren shook her head.

"Moving."

There.

Different problem.

Pellin stood.

"Animal?"

Edda said, "Everyone off the center floor."

No panic.

Kessa pointed.

"West door for Pellin. Greg stays bench."

I looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because if you have to move fast, I want to know before we need it."

Correct.

Still irritating.

I stayed.

Meren followed the charge with one hand above the floor.

South.

Then west.

Then directly beneath one of the covered dye pits.

The warped boards over it lifted once.

Dropped.

Edda moved between us and the pit.

"Back."

Kessa took two steps.

I did not.

Bench already against wall.

Exit behind me.

Good position.

The boards lifted again.

One snapped.

Something gray and wet pushed through.

Not monster in the heroic sense.

Large tunnel lizard.

Blind white eyes.

Broad head.

Copper-blue streaks under the skin where old ward residue had probably been leaking into the drain water for years.

It was the size of a pig and deeply offended by architecture.

Edda said, "Don't kill it unless it commits."

Good.

The lizard committed immediately.

It came through the boards sideways, landed badly, and launched toward the brightest open space.

Unfortunately that was the west door.

Unfortunately I was beside it.

Edda moved.

Too far.

Kessa shouted my name.

I did not stand.

Important improvement.

Old reflex wanted feet.

Current body had bench, wall, sword, magic.

Use current inventory.

The lizard hit six feet away.

I formed a plane at floor angle.

Not shield.

Ramp.

Its front feet struck the invisible surface and slid left.

Body followed.

Momentum turned.

Edda's spear shaft caught behind the foreleg and redirected the rest.

The animal slammed into an empty dye trough instead of me.

Metal rang.

It scrambled.

Edda backed off instead of stabbing.

Meren opened the south door.

Pellin, outside, yelled something unhelpful.

The lizard found the larger opening and ran into the weeds.

Gone.

Silence.

I still held the plane.

Wrong angle now.

No purpose.

I dropped it.

Kessa looked at me.

Then the bench.

"You didn't stand."

"I noticed."

Edda smiled.

"Good ramp."

"Plane."

"Ramp."

"Fine."

My heart hammered.

The wooden leg had not moved.

The sword remained sheathed.

No heroic comeback.

No running.

No proving I could do what I used to.

I had sat down and changed where a pig-lizard's feet went.

It felt fucking excellent.

Then Meren said, "We still have moving charge under the floor."

Right.

Work.

The lizard had not been the source.

It had been carrying residue.

We traced the leak to an old ward drain plate beneath the dye pit.

The plate had cracked and was feeding low charge into the runoff channel.

Meren isolated it.

Kessa found the post base had shifted because the drain bricks beneath one side had degraded.

My earlier load change had been real.

Different cause than expected.

By late afternoon, we had the post temporarily braced, the ward plate dead, and a city order posted over the cellar entrance.

Prepared site had become useful mess.

Sella arrived before we left.

Of course she had heard about the lizard.

"Anyone hurt?"

"No," Edda said.

"Greg move?"

I stared.

Edda laughed.

"No. That's the good part."

Sella looked at me.

I knew what she saw.

Wooden leg.

Bench.

Silver tab.

Restriction sheet still tied red to the contract board.

"Prepared-site clearance stands," she said.

"Obviously."

"Greg."

"Thank you."

The words hurt more than the socket.

Sella smiled.

Hostile institution.

On the walk back to Carrow, Kessa matched my pace without pretending she wasn't.

I noticed after half a mile.

"You can walk faster."

"Yes."

"Then do."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because we're talking."

There was no operational answer to that.

We kept walking.

After another hundred yards she said, "You liked it."

"The job?"

"When everyone stopped because you said wait."

I looked at her.

Too perceptive.

"Yes."

Kessa nodded.

"Thought so."

"Problem?"

"Not yet."

I disliked those words.

Mostly because they sounded exactly like something I would say.
