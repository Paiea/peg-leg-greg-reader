# D000: First Bell

The river stairs were wet.

Not rain.

Carrow had managed one clear morning and the river had objected by putting mist everywhere people needed to stand.

I arrived early.

Obviously.

Mara had specifically told me not to.

So I spent twelve minutes pretending I had arrived for the river.

It was a river.

Brown water. Two packet barges. One fisherman working hard enough to make breakfast feel accusatory.

Excellent river.

I leaned against the rail and watched a Vey porter swear at a trunk.

The trunk won.

First bell had not rung when Mara came down the street with her coat buttoned wrong.

I noticed the coat first.

Then the small case in her left hand.

Then the Vey document tube under her arm.

Then Mara noticed me.

She stopped.

Looked at the bell tower.

Looked back at me.

"You were early."

"Unproven."

"Greg."

"I have witnesses who will lie."

The porter looked over from the trunk.

"No."

Traitor.

Mara came down the last three steps.

Her hair was pinned properly this morning. No ink on her fingers that I could see.

She looked tired anyway.

"Did you sleep?"

"Some."

"That means no."

"It means some."

"Office language."

"Useful language."

I took the small case from her.

She let me.

That felt more intimate than it should have.

The case was heavier than expected.

"Books?"

"Ledgers."

"Worse."

"Much."

We walked toward the Vey landing.

The river stairs were crowded at first bell. Porters. Clerks. Two families saying goodbye loudly enough to make private affection seem inefficient. A woman with three baskets of green onions and no visible plan for defending them from everybody else's elbows.

Mara moved closer when a handcart came through.

Her shoulder touched mine.

Stayed there two steps longer than necessary.

The Vey packet boat was already tied below.

Blue stripe along the hull.

Ugly little chimney.

I had watched boats like it become larger, faster, and more expensive over forty years.

This one looked proud of managing smoke.

Mara stopped at the top of the landing.

"There's something I didn't tell you last night."

Good sentence.

Never caused trouble.

"Married?"

"No."

"Wanted in Halden?"

"Only by payroll."

"Worse."

Mara smiled, then looked at the boat.

"Vey offered me the east-route term."

I waited.

Apparently my face asked the question.

"Two years," she said.

Ah.

The bell above the quay gave one warning strike.

Not first bell yet.

Just the city making sure everybody knew time intended to happen.

"In Halden?"

"Based there. Carrow, Dell, East Quay, maybe Orin twice a year. Mostly Halden."

"You said yes."

It wasn't a question.

Mara looked at me.

"Yesterday afternoon."

I adjusted the case in my hand.

Heavy with ledgers.

Two years sounded different when attached to a person.

Forty years was history.

Two years was somebody not being at dinner.

"Good job?"

"Better pay."

"That wasn't the question."

"Then yes."

She thought about it.

"Probably."

"Strong endorsement."

"I get route accounts instead of one office. Pell thinks I can take over east reconciliation by winter if I stop arguing with him about how he files exceptions."

"Will you?"

"Stop arguing?"

"Yes."

"No."

Good.

The boatman below called for Vey baggage.

I handed Mara the case.

She did not take it immediately.

Her fingers closed around the handle beside mine.

"Two years," I said.

"It isn't exile."

"I know."

"There are boats."

"Also roads."

"You hate boats."

"I dislike trusting floors that move."

"Roads move."

"Slower."

Mara's mouth shifted.

She had something else.

I waited this time.

"The term closes first day of autumn, two years from now," she said. "Pell wants my answer on renewal that morning."

"Of course he does."

"He says if I wait until afternoon he'll assume I'm leaving."

"Healthy workplace."

"Very."

The warning bell rang again.

Two years from now.

First day of autumn.

Halden.

A Vey office I had never seen in this life.

Mara looked past me toward Carrow.

"I don't know what I'll do."

"You don't have to."

"I know."

She said it with enough edge that I believed her.

Good.

I had no useful advice anyway.

My expertise in long-term planning had recently produced three copper, a wet coat, and an existential crisis before breakfast.

Improving, admittedly.

Below us, someone untied the stern line.

The boatman shouted at them to tie it again.

Apparently departure remained theoretical.

Mara looked back at me.

"Come to Halden before then."

"I will."

Her expression changed.

Tiny.

Enough.

Another promise.

I heard it after saying it.

Mara did too.

I almost qualified it.

Probably.

If work allows.

If money.

If roads.

If the world behaves.

All very reasonable.

I left them in my mouth.

"Actually," I said, "two years."

"What?"

"When Pell asks you. First day of autumn."

Mara watched me.

I could feel my own confidence trying to become theatrical.

I cut it off before it embarrassed both of us.

"I'll be there."

"Greg."

"Halden. That morning."

"You don't even know where the office is."

"River."

"There are eleven Vey buildings on the river."

"That's excessive."

"Very."

"Which one?"

She studied me another second.

Then pointed east, which was useless at this distance.

"Stone steps. Blue doors. Old customs house."

"Before first bell?"

"Pell asks at first bell."

"Then before."

Mara's fingers tightened around the case handle.

"I'm not asking you to wait two years for me."

"Good."

"I'm serious."

"So am I."

"You could be somewhere else."

"Probably."

"I could be someone else."

That one stayed.

Not because it hurt.

Because it was true.

"Probably," I said.

Mara laughed once through her nose.

"Terrible answer."

"Accurate."

"And if you hate me by then?"

"I'll complain in person."

"If I hate you?"

"Same system."

She shook her head.

The boatman called final boarding.

Mara took the case fully.

Then kissed me.

Not the short office kiss.

Not last night's kiss either.

This one happened on wet river steps with a porter dragging a trunk six feet away and somebody's onions rolling downhill.

One onion reached my boot.

I did not move.

Priorities.

Mara pulled back first.

"Two years is a long time."

"Yes."

I had lived forty after this once.

That did not make two small.

She started down toward the boat.

Halfway she turned.

"Don't make it weird!"

Half the quay heard her.

I looked around.

"Too late."

The porter laughed.

Mara boarded.

I stayed until the packet boat pushed away from the landing and its ridiculous chimney began coughing properly.

She stood at the rail until the bend took her.

Then there was just river.

I picked up the escaped onion.

The woman with the baskets had recovered the other two.

I handed it back.

"Thank you."

"Apparently I'm useful."

She looked at me like I had made her morning harder.

Fair.

By second bell I was at the Guild.

The board had changed overnight.

The bad south-road job was gone.

Someone with lower standards had taken it.

Three new slips hung in its place.

Warehouse ward inspection.

Two-day escort north-west.

Drain clearing under Weaver Street.

I read the pay twice.

The drain paid best.

Of course it did.

I stood in front of the board with Halden two years away and sewage available immediately.

The future had excellent timing.

I took the drain slip.
