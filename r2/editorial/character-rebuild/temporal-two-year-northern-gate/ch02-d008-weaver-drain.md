# D008: Weaver Drain

The drain beneath Weaver Street was tall enough to stand in if I disliked my skull.

I did.

Not enough to test it.

"Duck," Noll said behind me.

"Excellent advice after the beam."

"I said it before the second one."

There was a second one.

I ducked.

Progress.

The city had paid six Bronze contractors and one licensed mason to find out why three Weaver Street cellars smelled like the river at low tide.

The answer was water.

This had not satisfied the city clerk.

He wanted to know why the water was there.

This was how employment happened.

We followed the mason through brick dark enough to drink the lantern light.

His name was Iven Marr.

I knew because he had introduced himself twice and then made me repeat it after I called him Iver.

"Marr," he said.

"That's what I said."

"No."

"It was close."

"Names aren't horseshoes."

I liked him immediately.

He was perhaps thirty-five, square through the shoulders, with a white line of old mortar permanently living under one thumbnail. His city badge hung from a cord at his neck beside a wooden measuring wedge worn smooth at the corners.

He carried no sword.

Iven distrusted adventurers on principle.

"You people see a dark hole and start looking for teeth."

"Usually because the teeth are in the dark hole."

"This is a drain."

"Teeth can travel."

Noll said, "There were tunnel biters under Dye Street last winter."

Iven stopped.

Looked back at us.

"Were you there?"

"No."

"Then shut up."

Fair procedure.

We reached the blockage ninety feet in.

The drain narrowed where old brick met newer stone.

A section of the left wall had bowed inward.

Mud pressed through the joints in slow brown fingers.

Water moved around our boots toward the river outlet.

Iven set down his lantern.

His irritation disappeared.

That got my attention.

He touched three bricks.

Not the bowed ones.

The row above.

Pressed his thumb into a joint.

The mortar came away wet.

"Nobody touch the wall."

Six Bronze adventurers developed excellent manners.

Iven crouched.

Water reached his knees.

He checked the floor with the measuring wedge, then looked toward the dark behind us.

"Street load above?"

The city clerk had stayed outside, which suddenly felt strategic.

One of the other contractors said, "Market lane."

"Weaver Street," Noll said.

"I know the street. What's above this exact section?"

Silence.

I pictured the approach.

Left turn after the second access shaft. Thirty paces. Maybe thirty-five.

"Dyer's yard," I said.

Iven looked at me.

"Which?"

"The one with the blue awning. Two carts parked outside this morning. Stone weights stacked against the rear wall."

"How many?"

I hadn't counted.

Memory offered six.

Present memory, not the useful dangerous kind.

"At least four. Maybe six."

Iven swore.

"Back."

Nobody argued.

We made twenty feet before something behind us clicked.

Small sound.

Brick on brick.

Iven froze.

"Keep moving."

Everybody kept moving.

Another click.

Then a low grinding sound began behind us.

One of the Bronzes turned around.

"Don't," I said.

He looked at me instead.

Better.

The wall didn't explode.

Buildings rarely respected drama that much.

It slumped.

Wet brick shifted into the channel, three courses folding inward, mud following through the opening.

The water jumped around our calves.

Iven swore again.

"Access shaft. Now."

We moved.

Noll's boot caught under something.

He stumbled.

I grabbed his coat and nearly went down with him.

The lantern in Iven's hand swung hard enough to throw the whole tunnel sideways.

Water thickened around our boots.

Not rising much.

Moving faster.

That was worse.

The access shaft was twelve feet ahead.

Between us and it, one of the overhead crosspieces had started to sag.

Not much.

An inch.

Maybe two.

The young woman in front reached it first.

She ducked under.

The man after her went through.

Iven looked up.

"One at a time. Don't touch it."

Noll went.

I was next.

The crosspiece made a noise I knew.

Not from drains.

From siege works.

Load finding somewhere new to go.

I stopped.

Iven was behind me.

"Move."

"Wait."

"Move."

The timber dipped another fraction.

There were two people behind Iven.

If it came down while we were directly under it, the problem stopped being sewage.

I reached for mana.

The response was still insulting.

A thin pressure behind my eyes.

A little warmth in my palm.

Enough.

Maybe.

I put a plane under the left end of the crosspiece.

Not across the tunnel.

Not holding the roof.

Just a hand where a hand could not reach.

The plane formed crooked.

I corrected.

The timber settled onto it.

Pain sharpened behind my eyes immediately.

Iven stared at the empty air beneath the beam.

"Go," I said.

He went.

The next man went.

The last woman hesitated.

"Now."

She went.

I stepped under last.

The pressure in my head became a spike.

My plane slipped.

The timber dropped perhaps half an inch.

I was clear before it found the place I had been.

Then I let the magic go.

The crosspiece came down.

Not the whole roof.

Just the timber and one ugly shelf of brick.

Water slapped mud up the back of my coat.

We climbed the access shaft and came out behind a cooper's shop smelling like the part of civilization nobody wrote songs about.

The city clerk was waiting.

"Find the blockage?"

Iven sat on an overturned bucket.

He looked at the clerk for a long time.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"Your street."

The clerk blinked.

Iven pointed at me.

"He gets the hazard supplement."

I was wiping drain water off my face.

"There's a supplement?"

"Three copper."

"I love municipal work."

"Don't."

Noll laughed hard enough to cough.

The city closed half of Weaver Street by noon.

The dyer complained about the carts.

Iven made him move the stone weights anyway.

I stayed because the hazard supplement apparently covered standing around while professionals argued.

That was when I learned Iven Marr had been trying to qualify as a city master mason for six years.

Not because he volunteered the information.

The clerk said, "If you'd passed the north-wall examination, you'd have your own crew for this."

Iven's face changed.

Only slightly.

"If the examination measured masonry instead of handwriting, I might."

The clerk sighed.

Old argument.

Useful information.

Iven looked at the bowed street above the drain.

"I'll pass next time."

The clerk said nothing.

Neither did I.

By evening, temporary braces filled the damaged section.

I got nine copper instead of six.

Three disappeared immediately to food, lamp oil, and replacing one sock whose relationship with the drain had become irreparable.

At Lower Rook, Maud stopped me in the hall.

She sniffed once.

"No."

"I live here."

"Not like that."

"This is class discrimination."

"Wash outside."

I washed outside.

Two years to Halden.

Seven hundred twenty-two days, if I were the kind of person who counted.

I was not.

I knew because I had checked once.

For scheduling.
