# D034: The Angle

Iven Marr hired me without the Guild.

That sounded illegal when he said it.

It wasn't.

Apparently a licensed mason could hire day labor directly for non-monster work, provided nobody used the phrase `adventuring services` on the invoice.

"What phrase do we use?" I asked.

"Temporary structural assistance."

"That's worse."

"Pays twelve copper."

"I have always believed in structural assistance."

We were rebuilding the Weaver drain.

Properly this time.

The city had removed the stone weights from the dyer's yard, dug half the street open, discovered two old drainage branches nobody had drawn on any current map, and spent three weeks deciding which office had failed to draw them.

The answer appeared to be all of them.

Iven's crew had six permanent workers.

I knew five names now.

Pela mixed mortar and sang badly.

Torr had three missing fingertips and used that hand to point at unsafe behavior.

Senn was seventeen, broad, quiet, and could carry two dressed stones at once because somebody had apparently built him to annoy me.

Mira kept the measurements and refused to let Iven borrow her pencil because he chewed them.

The sixth was a man named Ors who did not speak before second bell unless someone was actively dying.

Nobody had tested the exception.

My job was less impressive.

Hold things where Iven wanted them for a few seconds at a time.

The first morning I wasted half my mana trying to support a lintel from underneath.

Flat plane.

Obvious.

Bad.

The force came straight down through the shape and into me as pressure behind the eyes.

I managed six seconds before my vision started sparkling.

Iven watched me sit on a stack of brick.

"Useful."

"Thank you."

"For six seconds."

"Praise feels less sincere now."

He ignored me.

The lintel went in with timber supports like civilization intended.

At lunch I stared at it.

I had made barriers across battle lines.

I remembered surfaces curved into overlapping shells, force spreading sideways, anchors shifting under impact, three layers taking different loads.

I remembered teaching other people to do it.

My current mana reserve could barely support an argument.

Still.

The geometry had not become illegal.

After lunch I asked Iven for a loose stone.

"How loose?"

"Heavy enough to be rude."

Senn brought me one.

Of course he did.

I set it on two timber blocks.

Made a plane beneath the center.

Lifted.

Pressure immediately.

I dropped it.

Then made two smaller planes, angled outward like shallow wedges.

One left.

One right.

The stone rose perhaps a finger.

The pressure came differently.

Still there.

Spread.

Iven crouched beside it.

"Again."

I did.

He put two fingers beneath one edge.

"Don't."

"I'm not stupid."

"You've met fingers?"

He showed me both hands.

All present.

"Professionally attached."

Fair.

I held the stone for eleven seconds.

Then sixteen.

On the fourth try I stopped at twenty because stopping voluntarily was a skill my younger self had apparently considered cowardice.

Iven looked at the stone.

Then at me.

"Can you push sideways?"

"Some."

"How much?"

"Define much."

"A handspan."

"Probably."

"Bad word."

"Excellent word."

He made me prove it with the loose stone before putting the idea anywhere near his wall.

That afternoon, instead of two men leaning against a temporary brace while Mira set a pin, I held one angled plane against the brace for nine seconds.

Nine seconds was enough.

Torr drove the pin.

I let go.

Nobody died.

Municipal triumph.

Iven paid me twelve copper.

Then another two because we finished before fourth bell.

I stared at the coins.

"There are bonuses everywhere."

"Only if you stop looking at the Guild board."

That was dangerous information.

At Lower Rook, a letter waited beneath my door.

Halden paper.

Mara.

I put my fourteen copper on the table and opened the letter before taking off my boots.

She had written four pages.

One page was mostly complaints about Pell.

One described a warehouse clerk named Sivi who had corrected Mara's arithmetic in front of six people, been right, and then bought her tea because apparently Halden professional warfare had rules.

Half a page described her room.

Small window.

River noise.

Neighbor with a baby who screamed at second bell every night as if employed by the clock tower.

At the bottom of the third page Mara had drawn a terrible map.

A blue square labeled `VEY`.

Three wavy lines labeled `RIVER, OBVIOUSLY`.

A set of steps.

An arrow.

**In case your legendary navigation fails.**

I laughed alone in my room.

Then stopped.

Read the line again.

Legendary.

She knew enough of my claims now to make fun of them.

Not enough to understand what they meant.

Neither did I, some mornings.

The last page was shorter.

She had been offered a trip to Dell next month.

Three weeks.

She wanted it.

She also hated boats.

I took unreasonable comfort in that.

At the bottom:

**Pell asked if I plan to stay after the term. I told him to ask me in twenty-three months like the paper says. He hated that. Thought you'd approve.**

I did.

I found my own paper.

Started with the drain.

Deleted the part where I described the smell.

Put it back because Mara deserved accuracy.

Told her about the angled planes.

Told her I had made fourteen copper in one day.

Did not tell her how impressive that currently felt.

She knew arithmetic.

She could work it out.

At the end I wrote:

**Your map is unusable. I will arrive at the wrong Vey office out of principle.**

Then I counted my money.

Fourteen new copper.

Seven already in the box.

Rent due in five days.

Boot heel separating on the right.

Sword edge needed work.

Lamp oil low.

I moved four copper into the rent pile.

Two into food.

One for oil.

Stared at the rest.

A man could become locally wealthy if he stopped requiring objects.

I put three aside for the cobbler.

The remaining four went into the small tin beneath the bed.

Halden was not a savings goal yet.

Two years was too far away to become a jar.

Still, when I closed the tin, I thought of stone steps and blue doors.

Then I went downstairs because Maud had started yelling about the stair rail again.

It had finally come loose.
