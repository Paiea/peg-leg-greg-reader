# D109: North Wall

The Northern Gate was smaller than I remembered.

That was the first problem.

The second was that I could not prove I remembered it at all.

"Greg."

I looked down.

Iven Marr stood twelve feet below me with both hands on his hips.

"If you fall off my scaffold while staring at masonry, I will tell everyone it was romantic."

"Cruel."

"Measure the joint."

I measured the joint.

Three fingers of old mortar between two blocks, dark with spring water but firm under the pick.

"Wet. Sound."

"Mark it."

I chalked a short white line beside the seam.

The Northern Gate had two square towers joined by an arch broad enough for three wagons if all three drivers were willing to lie about how wide their wagons were.

The outer stone was older than most of Carrow's north district.

The inside was not.

Repairs nested inside repairs.

New lintels under old arches. Iron straps at the west hinge. A blue-gray reinforcement band around the inner tower where somebody twenty years ago had apparently decided subtlety was less important than keeping stone above people's heads.

Good choice.

Below us, traffic passed through the gate in both directions.

Farm carts coming in.

Timber wagons going out.

A Guild team wearing clean boots and optimism.

Two children running alongside a mule until their mother discovered them and repaired the situation verbally.

Normal gate.

I had spent the morning waiting for memory to scream.

It had not.

Instead it whispered stupid things.

This angle looked familiar.

That stone might have been in a painting.

The west tower had once been shorter.

Or taller.

Or perhaps I had attended an anniversary ceremony forty years later where somebody sold little sugar biscuits shaped like the gate.

History contaminated memory efficiently.

Iven climbed the ladder.

He had passed the written half of his master mason examination three weeks earlier.

Barely.

He had brought the notice to Lower Rook and made me read the score before allowing himself to look pleased.

Seventy-one.

Passing was seventy.

Mira had bought him a new pencil.

He still chewed it.

Now the practical exam required him to submit two city inspection reports under a master mason's seal.

Northern Gate was one.

I was temporary structural assistance again.

Better title every time.

Iven crouched beside the wet joint.

Scraped it.

"Fine."

"Fine good or fine we're writing fine?"

"Fine good."

He moved to the next seam.

I looked through the arch.

Northern Gate collapsed.

The thought arrived whole.

No image with it.

No screaming.

No date.

Just the sentence.

People had known it later.

There had been an anniversary.

Or a memorial.

Or an argument about reconstruction money.

Maybe all three.

I had written the uncertainty down on my first morning because even then I had not trusted it.

Good instinct.

Annoying now.

"How old is the foundation?" I asked.

Iven did not look up.

"Older than the gate."

"Helpful."

"Nobody knows."

That got me.

"Nobody?"

"The north road used to come through a timber gate. Stone towers went up later. East tower was rebuilt after the fire. West footing is probably original wall foundation."

"Probably?"

He looked at me.

"Welcome to masonry."

I hated his profession.

"Any settlement?"

"Everything settles."

"Recent."

"Three lines we're watching. None grew over winter."

"Water?"

He pointed with the pencil.

"Drain channels there, there, and under the west shoulder. City cleared all three before thaw."

"Undermining?"

Now he stared.

"Why?"

There it was.

Present proving things.

Or refusing to.

I could tell him I knew the gate collapsed.

Then explain that I knew because in another life, decades from now, people remembered an event I could not place in a year.

Excellent way to become employed elsewhere.

"Weaver drain," I said instead. "Water made me suspicious of everything that stands up."

Iven accepted that.

Because Weaver drain had earned suspicion honestly.

"No sign of scour," he said. "North ditch was relined six years ago. West channel's ugly, but it moves water."

"Can we see it?"

"After lunch."

We did.

The west channel ran beneath a stone shoulder outside the gate and emptied into a ditch twenty yards downhill.

Ugly was accurate.

Roots in one wall.

Silt at the bend.

A section of newer brick where the old lining had failed.

Water moved through it anyway.

Iven crawled halfway in with a lantern.

Came back muddy.

"Clear enough."

I checked the outlet.

No sinkhole.

No soft ground.

No mysterious hum.

No divine message explaining why history had said `collapsed.`

Good.

I was relieved.

Also disappointed.

That was embarrassing.

We ate lunch on the outer wall.

Iven had cheese.

I had bread and a sausage whose contents had chosen privacy.

Below us, a Vey wagon rolled through the gate.

Blue stripe.

For half a second I thought of Mara.

Then I thought of Halden.

Then I checked the gate again because apparently romance had become structural assessment.

Iven followed my eyes.

"You're doing it again."

"What?"

"Looking at the whole thing like it insulted your mother."

"I dislike arches."

"You live in a city."

"Constant struggle."

He bit the cheese.

"It's fine."

I looked at him.

"The gate."

"I know."

"You don't believe me."

"I do."

Mostly.

That was the irritating part.

Every present fact I had seen said Northern Gate was an old structure being inspected by people who knew what they were doing.

History said it collapsed.

History had not supplied a date.

Today was not obligated to become interesting just because I knew tomorrow eventually did.

I finished the sausage.

"Can I get a copy of your report?"

Iven laughed.

"Why?"

"I enjoy municipal documents."

"No one enjoys municipal documents."

"Then I'm expanding."

He narrowed his eyes.

"You're weird about this gate."

"Probably."

"Bad word."

"Excellent word."

He shook his head.

The final report marked six wet joints, one cracked facing stone for replacement, two rusting hinge straps, and no immediate structural concern.

Iven's supervising mason signed it.

The city accepted it.

I read the copy twice.

Then folded it into my room box beside Mara's letters.

Not because I expected the gate to fall.

Because I didn't know why it would.

Those were different problems.

That evening Nera sent a runner asking if I wanted a two-day marsh contract.

The pay was good.

The northern wall remained standing.

I went to the marsh.
