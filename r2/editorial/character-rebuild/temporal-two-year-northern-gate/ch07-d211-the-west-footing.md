# D211: The West Footing

The crack had grown.

Not enough to see from the street.

Enough that Iven Marr stopped chewing his pencil.

That was worse.

We stood inside the west tower of Northern Gate with the old inspection report open on a crate between us.

D109.

Six wet joints.

One facing stone.

Rusting hinge straps.

No immediate structural concern.

The chalk mark beside joint four was still visible.

The mortar beside it had opened the width of my smallest fingernail.

"How much?" I asked.

Mira knelt with a brass gauge.

She had moved to Iven's North District crew when he got the post, taking her pencils with her.

"Two lines since last month."

Iven looked at the floor.

"Other side?"

"One line."

"Tower?"

"West face hasn't moved at the upper marks."

That bothered him.

It bothered me too, although I needed a second to understand why.

If the whole tower were settling, the upper marks should have told the same story.

They didn't.

Something lower was changing shape.

Iven walked to the interior wall and put his palm against the stone.

As if the gate might confess.

Outside, wagon traffic passed through the east half of the arch.

Iven had closed the west lane that morning.

Nobody had argued after he put two city guards in front of it.

Progress in municipal authority.

"Drain," he said.

We went down.

The west drain was flowing.

Too well.

I knew that before Iven said anything.

On D109 the channel had carried a shallow brown stream.

Today the same channel ran clear and fast despite three dry weeks.

I crouched at the outlet.

Cold water.

Iven swore quietly.

"Source?"

"Not street runoff."

"Pipe?"

"No city line under here."

"That you know of."

He gave me a look.

Weaver Street had ruined certainty for both of us.

Mira arrived with the north-district drainage map.

She unfolded it on the grass.

Three blue lines.

None where the water was coming from.

Iven stared at them.

"I hate old cities."

"You became a mason voluntarily."

"Youth."

"Four months ago?"

"I was younger."

Mira ignored us.

"Could be spring below the footing."

"New spring?"

"New path."

That made sense.

Water found old mistakes eventually.

I looked at the west tower.

Northern Gate collapsed.

The sentence returned.

Still no picture.

Still no date.

But now the present had handed me water where no water belonged and a crack moving below a tower that wasn't settling evenly.

That was enough to stop treating the memory as trivia.

Not enough to know the answer.

"Close the whole gate," I said.

Iven looked at me.

Mira did too.

There were three reasons not to.

Northern Gate carried nearly half the north-road freight.

The west lane was already closed.

We had no proof the east lane was unsafe.

Iven asked the useful question.

"Why?"

I pointed at the outlet.

"Because we don't know where that water is moving underground."

"That's not enough."

"No."

He waited.

I could tell him.

I had reached the point where not telling him had started to feel dishonest.

Not about another life.

Not all of it.

But the lead.

"I remember hearing about this gate collapsing," I said.

Mira stared.

Iven did not.

He knew I had strange historical opinions.

Not their source.

"When?"

"I don't know."

"Why?"

"Don't know."

"How many died?"

"Don't know."

Now he stared.

Fair.

"Excellent memory."

"I've been trying to explain that."

Mira said, "From where?"

I looked at the water.

"Later records."

Technically.

Horribly technically.

Iven rubbed one hand over his face.

"You knew this in spring?"

"I knew the sentence. We inspected the gate. It was sound."

"And you didn't mention it."

"Would you have closed the gate because I remembered a future anniversary without a date?"

He opened his mouth.

Closed it.

"No."

"Now we have movement and unexplained water."

Mira had stopped looking at me strangely and returned to the map.

Professionals were wonderful that way.

"If the west footing is being washed," she said, "east lane load could still transfer through the arch."

Iven's face hardened.

There.

Present evidence.

He stood.

"Close it."

The city did not collapse because one master mason said close.

It complained first.

The North Market office sent a clerk.

Then a freight representative.

Then another master mason named Beret Halm, old enough that Iven became respectful against his will.

Beret inspected the crack.

Inspected the water.

Asked six questions nobody had answered yet.

Then told the guards to keep the gate closed.

No villain.

No idiot insisting profit mattered more than stone.

Just a city suddenly discovering that one of its main roads had become a wall.

By afternoon carts backed up six streets.

By evening temporary signs rerouted traffic through East Gate.

Merchants complained.

The city hired diggers.

Iven got three new gray hairs or I learned where the old ones were.

I stayed because he asked me to.

At fourth bell Beret brought us inside the tower.

"You make force planes?"

I looked at Iven.

He had told her.

"Small ones."

"Can you feel load through them?"

That question woke up something old.

I had forgotten the sensation until she named it.

Late in my first life, yes.

A barrier was not just a wall.

Force moved through it.

Direction. Rhythm. Impact. Weight.

You could read it if the shape stayed clean enough.

Current me could barely maintain three planes.

Still.

"Maybe."

Iven groaned.

"Bad word."

Beret looked between us.

"Show me."

They had already installed a timber brace across one interior arch.

I put a palm-sized plane between brace and wall.

Thin.

Angled.

Let the timber settle against it.

Pressure came behind my eyes.

I breathed.

Did not resist it.

Listened.

That was the remembered word.

Wrong sense.

Right idea.

The load was not steady.

It pulsed.

Tiny changes.

Not magic.

Wagons were gone.

Wind too weak.

I moved the plane lower.

The rhythm changed.

Again.

Strongest near the floor.

"Something's moving," I said.

Iven crouched beside me.

"Now?"

"Slow."

"How slow?"

"I don't know."

He almost objected.

Then stopped.

Beret said, "Useful anyway."

I released the plane after twelve seconds.

My vision flashed white at the edges.

I sat down.

S-class memory.

Twelve-second body.

Both facts again.

Beret sent for another brace.

Then another.

At sunset they found the void.

Not under the wall where the map said the old drain ran.

Six feet north of it.

A forgotten stone channel, probably older than the gate, had opened beneath the west approach.

Water had been carrying soil through it for weeks.

Maybe months.

No one knew how far the hollow reached.

Iven stood beside the excavation looking sick.

"We inspected it."

"You inspected what existed on the map," Beret said.

He looked at her.

"That isn't better."

"No."

She did not make him feel better.

I respected her for it.

The city posted a night watch.

Iven stayed.

Mira stayed because Iven stayed.

I stayed because fourteen copper had quietly become twenty and because the sentence in my memory had stopped being historical trivia.

At midnight, a Vey packet runner found me sitting on a coil of rope outside the closed gate.

Letter from Mara.

I laughed when I saw the blue seal.

Iven looked over.

"Good news?"

"Unknown."

"Open it."

"Privacy."

"You're sitting on my rope."

Fair.

I opened it.

Mara had reached Orin for the first time.

She hated the hotel.

Loved the river market.

Pell had finally given her the route-reconciliation title without changing her pay.

She had written **I KNOW** beside that sentence hard enough to dent the page.

At the bottom:

**Nineteen months. Stone steps. I'm keeping the terrible map on file.**

I looked up at Northern Gate.

Closed.

Braced.

Still standing.

For now that was enough.
