# D289: Reopened

Northern Gate reopened with no ceremony.

Iven wanted one.

Not a parade.

He wanted a city clerk to stand in the road and say, publicly, that North District had finished the work on schedule.

The city clerk declined to participate in Iven's emotional development.

So at second bell on D289, two guards moved the barriers aside and the first wagon rolled through.

That was it.

I stood inside the west tower with one hand against a new timber-and-iron brace and a force plane thin enough to feel ridiculous.

Stable.

I moved the plane six inches.

Stable.

Again.

Still stable.

Beret Halm watched me from the doorway.

"You're smiling."

"No."

"You are."

I let the plane go.

"Must be structural fatigue."

She snorted.

The repair had taken eleven weeks.

The forgotten channel beneath the west approach turned out to be one branch of an older drainage system cut before the stone gate existed. The city had opened thirty feet of road, removed fill that looked like wet cake, packed the void with graded stone, relined the active channel, and driven new support piers beneath the most affected footing.

I knew these things because Iven had explained them to me until masonry became an involuntary part of my personality.

The west tower had been unloaded, braced, measured, remeasured, and watched through three hard rains.

No new movement.

The interior crack stopped growing.

The unexplained water returned to a reasonable trickle.

Beret signed the reopening order.

Iven signed below her.

Then the city imposed a freight limit anyway.

Competent paranoia.

I approved.

Outside, the second wagon came through.

Then the third.

Drivers had been waiting nearly three months to complain directly at the gate.

They did excellent work.

Iven came in carrying a rolled inspection sheet.

"South merchant says East Gate added two days to his route."

"Did you apologize?"

"I told him to leave earlier."

"Master mason behavior."

"Exactly."

He slapped the sheet onto the crate.

All settlement marks unchanged.

I looked at them.

The sentence in my memory remained.

Northern Gate collapsed.

The present remained stubbornly repaired.

Good.

That was what I wanted.

I had spent months waiting for the gate to prove my memory right.

That was backward.

The repair had found a real problem and fixed it.

If the gate stood another century, I would celebrate being wrong.

Probably.

Iven caught the thought on my face somehow.

"Still waiting for it to fall?"

"No."

"Liar."

"I'm waiting to stop waiting."

He considered that.

"Worse."

Fair.

We walked outside.

Rell and Vessa were waiting near the north market arch.

Rell had grown approximately six feet since I met her, by which I meant she had new boots and wanted everyone to notice.

She ran to Iven.

"Is it open?"

He looked at the wagons moving through.

"No."

She stared at him.

"Papa."

"Yes."

"Can we walk through?"

"People have been walking through for forty years."

"After you fixed it."

There it was.

Ceremony.

Iven glanced at me.

I looked away before he could become sentimental and ruin his reputation.

The four of us walked through Northern Gate.

Vessa took Iven's arm.

Rell jumped over every drainage seam because children understood infrastructure primarily as obstacle design.

Halfway beneath the arch she pointed upward.

"Did Papa fix all of that?"

"No," I said.

Iven frowned.

"Helpful."

"He fixed the part trying to become underground."

Rell nodded seriously.

"Good."

Iven looked satisfied.

That afternoon I took a Silver road contract with Nera.

Real Silver pay.

Thirty-eight copper a day for three days escorting a surveyor north toward Cress Hill.

My new token had already changed what appeared on the board.

It had not changed the price of food.

At the market I bought dried meat for the trip and discovered somebody had raised the price by two copper.

I complained.

The seller recognized my Silver token and charged me the same amount anyway.

Humbling.

Before leaving Carrow, I passed Northern Gate again.

The guard waved me through.

"Marr's little mage."

I stopped.

"I have a name."

"Marr's little Silver mage."

"Worse."

He laughed.

Local renown had begun badly.

I walked north.

Behind me, Northern Gate stayed exactly where masonry belonged.
