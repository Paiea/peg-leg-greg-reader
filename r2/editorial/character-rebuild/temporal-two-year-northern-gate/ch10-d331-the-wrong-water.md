# D331: The Wrong Water

It rained for four days.

Carrow survived this with the dignity of a wet dog.

By the fifth morning, the river stairs had disappeared under brown water, three market roofs leaked, and Lower Rook smelled faintly of everybody else's socks.

I was eating breakfast when Iven Marr's runner found me.

Not a city runner.

Rell.

She came through the Brass Spoon door soaked from hood to boots and breathing hard.

I stood before she said anything.

"Papa says gate."

That was enough.

I left half my eggs.

This later bothered me for reasons unrelated to breakfast.

Outside, the rain had weakened to mist.

Rell kept up with me for one block before I noticed she was trying.

"Go home."

"Papa said bring you."

"You brought me."

"He said make sure."

"You've made sure."

She glared.

Iven's daughter had inherited his professional respect for instruction and his personal relationship with argument.

"Rell. Home."

Something in my voice worked.

She stopped.

Not happy.

"Tell him I did it."

"I will."

"Exactly."

She turned and ran south.

I kept north.

The gate was still open when I arrived.

That scared me more than seeing it closed would have.

Two lanes of traffic moved beneath the arch.

Slow because of mud.

Normal because normal things were dangerous that way.

Iven stood outside the west tower with Beret Halm and three city workers.

Mira held a measuring rod against the drainage outlet.

No water came out.

I stared at it.

Four days of rain.

The channel should have been full.

"Where is it?"

Iven pointed east.

The answer was running out of a crack between paving stones thirty feet inside the gate.

Not much.

Clear water bubbling where no drain existed.

Wrong water.

I crouched.

Touched it.

Cold.

Beret said, "West channel stopped sometime after second bell last night. East seep appeared before dawn."

"Settlement marks?"

"West unchanged."

"East?"

Iven handed me the sheet.

One upper mark had moved less than a line.

Almost nothing.

The lower interior arch had moved three.

My stomach tightened.

"Close it."

Beret was already turning.

"Guard!"

No debate this time.

The city had learned the price of uncertainty months ago.

Guards stepped into traffic.

Wagons complained.

Drivers complained louder.

Nobody cared.

The last northbound freight wagon cleared the arch.

A southbound vegetable cart had to reverse in mud and nearly lost a wheel.

Iven put both hands on the axle and helped shove it back.

Then the barriers went across.

Northern Gate closed again.

"Plane," Beret said.

Inside the east tower this time.

That mattered.

The original problem had been west.

We had spent months repairing west.

I set a palm-sized force plane between the lower brace and stone.

Listened.

Pressure.

Steady at first.

Then a small pulse.

Another.

Not impact.

Movement.

I shifted the plane higher.

Less.

Lower.

More.

Toward the gate arch.

Much more.

I pulled my hand away.

My head hurt.

"The load is walking inward."

Iven frowned.

"Meaning?"

I searched for current words.

"Something below is giving unevenly. The arch is carrying weight it wasn't yesterday."

Beret looked toward the outer wall.

"East footing?"

"Maybe."

Iven made the bad-word face.

No time.

A city warder arrived at a run.

Older woman, shaved head, blue cord around one wrist.

I had seen her twice during the repair and forgotten her name both times.

She touched the wardstone set into the interior arch.

Its carved line glowed weak blue beneath her fingers.

"North line's loaded," she said.

Beret swore.

I remembered her name.

Sella.

Fear helped nouns.

"From what?" I asked.

Sella looked at me.

"Rain wards are compensating along the wall. Gate anchor's taking the transfer."

Of course.

The city wall was not just stone.

The north ward line ran through the gate because gates were where defensive geometry became complicated.

Later systems separated structural anchors from ward anchors.

This one was old enough to think sharing was efficient.

My memory handed me a battlefield forty years away.

A broken wall.

A wardline snapping under redirected load.

Three people injured because everyone had stared at the breach instead of the anchor behind them.

I looked at Sella.

"Can you isolate the gate from the north line?"

She blinked.

"Yes."

"How fast?"

"Why?"

"If the footing moves and the wardstone stays loaded, where does that force go?"

Her face changed.

Good.

Not because I liked frightening people.

Because she knew enough to answer herself.

"Into the nearest live anchor."

"Which is?"

She pointed up.

West tower cap.

Across the arch from the failing east load.

Worst possible direction.

Iven looked between us.

"Words for masons."

Sella said, "If the gate shifts while the ward is compensating, it could pull the line across the arch."

"Pull how?"

"Hard."

Excellent professional terminology.

Beret said, "Isolate it."

Sella hesitated.

"That drops the north-wall weather skin and warning lattice."

"For how long?"

"Until I reroute through East Post. Half an hour if nothing fights me."

Beret looked at the sky.

Rain.

No monsters visible.

A city full of roofs.

"Do it."

Again, competence.

Again, no argument for drama.

Sella went to work.

Iven sent Mira to clear the two buildings closest to the east tower.

Beret sent guards farther.

I stood in the arch and looked north.

Market stalls lined the approach outside the wall.

Inside, North Street ran downhill toward the Guild district.

Hundreds of people used this space on a normal morning.

Not now.

Not if we did this correctly.

"Clear the whole north market," I said.

Beret looked at me.

"We're already clearing the gate frontage."

"More."

"Why?"

I looked up at the east tower.

No visible lean.

No falling stone.

No proof large enough for the sentence in my head.

Northern Gate collapsed.

Present evidence had finally caught up enough that ignoring the memory became stupid.

"Because if I'm wrong, merchants lose an hour."

"And if you're right?"

"Don't know."

I hated the answer.

Beret hated it too.

She turned to the guard captain.

"Clear North Market to Tanner Lane."

He stared.

"That's six blocks."

"Then start now."

The captain ran.

Bells began three minutes later.

Not alarm bells yet.

Market-clear bells.

Two short, one long.

People complained.

Stall owners covered goods.

Porters dragged handcarts south.

A butcher stood in his doorway arguing until a guard physically took the cleaver from his hand and pointed downhill.

Iven watched all of it.

"If nothing happens, they will hate us."

"Probably."

He sighed.

"Bad word."

The gate made a sound.

Not loud.

Stone rubbing stone somewhere deep enough that direction stopped mattering.

Everyone under the arch went quiet.

A line of dust slipped from the east tower wall.

Sella said, "Isolation in ten."

I looked at the tower.

Ten minutes felt enormous.

The second sound was louder.

Iven grabbed my arm.

"Out."

We moved.

Behind us, a stone the size of my fist fell from the interior arch and broke on the paving.

Small thing.

Almost embarrassing.

Then the alarm bell started.
