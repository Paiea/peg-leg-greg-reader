By D360 I had forgotten what clean socks felt like.

Not literally.

Probably.

Seven days since Nine Teeth had reduced Field Support Three to a collection of repairs wearing people.

Kell carried an expedition spare spear with a head two inches shorter than his old one and hated it personally.

Bera's shield rim had been hammered straight enough that it no longer caught her coat, though one corner still folded inward where the rimewing hit.

Leth's ward frame wore a translucent patch cut from rimewing membrane over the cracked hinge. He had spent three nights testing whether the material actually resisted cold charge or merely looked expensive.

It resisted enough to keep.

Miri had sewn the torn finger of her glove with red thread because no black remained.

Senn had developed a cough he insisted was dry air and everyone else insisted was age.

He kept the useless bell.

I approved.

My Blackglass Anchor survived Nine Teeth with one primary eye bent five degrees out of true and the dampener sleeve missing its forward screw.

We could not replace the eye safely in field.

Maren and Leth de-rated it instead.

Two primary eyes for full load.

Bent third only for sensing or light balance.

Splitter still limited to its stupid two-hundred differential.

The brass tag remained attached.

**TWO HUNDRED. NOT HEROIC.**

I had stopped finding it insulting.

Mostly.

The seven days themselves had been ugly.

D354 we reversed down Nine Teeth staging road and slept four miles east of where we had started that morning.

Negative progress.

D355 engineering repaired the warped test-cart axle and redistributed the dead shelter wagon's remaining canvas again because apparently one wagon could continue dying for weeks.

D356 Low Venn Corridor gave us sleet instead of snow and a road narrow enough that two wagons had to back half a mile after meeting a northbound ore train.

D357 one cook broke her wrist falling off a completely stationary wagon.

D358 we crossed a frozen river in three shifts while engineers cut every load by hand.

D359 a pack mare went lame and her cargo became everyone else's problem.

No monsters.

No magical revelation.

Just expedition slowly eating itself into a more efficient shape.

The twenty-eight wagons that left Carrow were now twenty-five full wagons, one repair cart converted from damaged frame, and enough redistributed cargo that every axle mattered more.

We had lost no people westward permanently except injury returns before Harth.

That felt like success.

It also felt nothing like victory.

D360 began with the Black Stair five days away by my paper and somewhere between four and seven days away by road.

Those were different clocks.

We reached **Rook's Mouth** after first bell.

Not related to Lower Rook, despite my brain trying to force sentiment.

Two mountain ridges pinched together around a black rock outcrop shaped vaguely like an open beak if you had poor standards for birds.

Beyond it the road split.

Left: the modern freight switchback, wider, maintained, snow-marked, six days to Black Stair under current conditions according route captain.

Right: the old **Load Road**, cut nearly straight through the western shelf, four days in summer, maybe five now.

The old route was why Westreach still carried archival maps.

It had not been used by a full expedition in eleven years.

One bridge was uncertain.

Two blackglass retaining fields were listed `intermittent` in the last survey.

The safe road put us at D366 if nothing went wrong.

One day late.

The old road could put us at D364 or D365.

Or kill a wagon in a gorge.

There it was.

My sentence on paper finally had a vote available.

Command did not ask me first.

Good.

The route council included road captain, expedition lead, Gold section leads, engineering chief, animal lead, healer lead, quartermaster, and two scouts who had actually walked parts of both routes this winter.

Field Support Three waited fifty yards away because specialists did not automatically become generals when emotionally invested.

I liked the system less today.
