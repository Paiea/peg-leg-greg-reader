# B HARD — Dinner

Status: **HIGH-FIDELITY REPLACEABLE FUTURE / NOT NUMBERED / NOT REBUILD AUTHORITY**

Fresh re-performance required before any A promotion.

---

Mara arrived in Carrow one day late and already angry at three ledgers.

I knew this before she saw me.

The Vey east desk occupied two rooms above a packet office near the river, with a narrow stair that forced everyone carrying boxes to discover cooperation or murder.

I had been there twice that day.

Not waiting.

Checking.

Different activity.

The first time, the clerk said the Halden packet had not arrived.

The second time, the clerk looked at me and said, "Still no."

This was unfair because I had not asked yet.

By sixth bell I had successfully stayed away for three hours.

Then a Vey wagon stopped outside while I was buying roasted chestnuts across the street.

Coincidence had become extremely generous.

Two clerks climbed down.

A driver.

Three document boxes.

Then Mara.

Travel coat dark with old rain at the hem.

Hair tied back badly enough that several strands had escaped near one ear.

One hand held a leather ledger case.

The other held the blue cup.

I stared.

She had brought the cup.

Of everything she owned in Halden, she had apparently chosen evidence.

Mara stepped onto the street and immediately turned toward a clerk behind her.

"That box says east handoff."

The clerk looked at the box he was carrying.

"It is east handoff."

"Then why is the river tally tied to it?"

"Pell packed them."

"Pell eats onions at the desk. This is now his second crime."

There she was.

Not letter-Mara.

Current Mara, irritated in public.

I smiled before she saw me.

Then she did.

Her face changed.

Not dramatically.

The anger left one part of it.

Stayed in another.

"Greg."

I crossed the street.

"Mara."

Excellent opening.

Forty years of life had prepared me for naming people.

She looked at me once from boots to face.

Not shy about it.

I felt the inspection physically.

"You have a better coat."

Not what I wanted.

"Sword too."

Her eyes dropped to it.

"Expensive?"

"Unfortunately."

"Then you really have changed."

There.

I should have been satisfied.

I was not.

"What did you expect?"

Mara shifted the ledger case higher.

"Honestly?"

Dangerous word.

"Yes."

"More hair."

I stared.

She laughed.

Not graceful.

Travel-tired laugh.

I had missed that sound without remembering it properly.

Annoying trick.

I took the ledger case from her hand before asking.

She let me.

That felt more intimate than expected.

"Room upstairs?"

"Vey paid above the office."

"Bedbugs?"

Mara stopped.

"Why would you say that?"

Historical contamination.

Wrong chronology.

Excellent.

"Bad inn experience."

"Not mine."

"Good."

She looked at me suspiciously.

I changed subject before future quarry became prophecy.

"Dinner?"

Mara looked toward the document boxes.

Then at the office stairs.

"I have to get those upstairs."

Right.

Work.

Dinner had not suspended it because I had arrived attractively near chestnuts.

I carried one box.

Mara carried another despite my offer to take two.

The clerk carried the third and looked relieved that Pell's crimes had acquired labor.

Upstairs, the Vey rooms smelled like ink, damp paper, and somebody's lunch from hours earlier.

A long counter filled most of the front room.

The back held shelves, three chairs, and a desk with one leg propped on folded cardboard.

Mara saw me notice.

"This one tilts too."

"Vey policy?"

"Apparently."

She set the box down.

Opened it.

Checked the first bundle inside.

Closed her eyes.

"Wrong."

The clerk winced.

"Pell?"

"No. Carrow."

Local guilt.

She pulled out two ledgers, flipped through the first, then stopped with one finger on a line.

I moved closer automatically.

Numbers.

Dates.

Packet marks.

I could probably understand enough to become annoying within ten minutes.

Mara closed the book.

On my fingers.

Not hard.

Enough.

"Dinner."

I looked at her.

"You were checking."

"I was. You were starting."

Fair.

"Where?"

"You promised real food."

"I did not promise quality."

"Then I can still leave."

"Brass Spoon."

Mara's eyebrows rose.

"You can afford Brass Spoon?"

"Occasionally."

"You really have changed."

That one landed better.

We left the office with the clerk still sorting boxes.

Mara carried the blue cup.

I finally pointed at it.

"Why?"

She looked down.

"Evidence."

"Your mother is not here."

"You are."

"I already agreed."

"People become weak under pressure."

"You packed ceramic drinkware across two cities to preserve litigation."

"Correct."

The cup went into her travel bag.

Current Mara remained dangerous.

The walk to East Market took fifteen minutes because Carrow had put several carts between us and food.

We talked around larger things.

Her trip.

One wheel stuck outside Rell Ford.

A Vey driver named Corren sang the same verse for six hours until the other driver threatened to abandon him.

Halden had turned colder.

Pell had stopped eating onions for exactly one day after Mara complained and then returned with pickled onions, apparently as escalation.

I told her Noll had a kitchen trial.

She stopped walking.

"Noll next door?"

I looked at her.

"You know Noll?"

Mara looked offended.

"Of course I know Noll."

There were two increasingly common categories in my life.

People I did not remember.

People who remembered each other without permission.

"How?"

"He lived next to you."

"I know that part."

"He used to throw apple cores at Tavin from the landing."

I stared.

"Why?"

"Tavin threw them back."

"That does not answer anything."

"It answered them."

She kept walking.

"Did you know he steals soap?"

Mara laughed.

"He still does that?"

Betrayal expanded.

"Apparently everyone knew except me."

"You knew."

Past tense remained rude.

She heard it too.

The laugh faded slightly.

Not gone.

"Sorry."

"Don't."

Mara looked at me.

I meant it.

Mostly.

"I don't want every ordinary thing treated like broken glass."

That came out sharper than intended.

Mara's mouth tightened.

"I wasn't."

"You apologized."

"Because it landed badly."

"That is allowed to happen without someone being wrong."

She stopped at the corner.

People moved around us.

A man carrying two baskets swore because we had become architecture.

We stepped aside.

Mara looked at me for a second.

"You got better at that."

"At what?"

"Saying the irritating part before I have to drag it out."

I wanted to feel proud.

Then remembered the card game with Jorren.

Mixed evidence.

"Sometimes."

"Better answer."

We reached The Brass Spoon.

The dining room was fuller than my last visit.

Vessa saw me from the register and then saw Mara.

Her eyes sharpened.

Not jealousy.

Customer mathematics.

Two people.

One table.

Profit.

"Rear wall," she said.

No greeting.

Comforting.

Mara looked at me as we sat.

"Friend?"

"Management."

"Terrifying?"

"Deeply."

The kitchen hatch opened behind her.

Noll appeared carrying two plates.

Stopped.

Actually stopped.

The plates remained level.

Growth.

His eyes went to Mara.

"Mara?"

She turned.

For one second she stared at him the way I had stared at Jorren in the onion market.

Then smiled.

"Noll."

He looked down at his apron.

Then back at her.

"You left."

"Four years ago."

"Still left."

Mara laughed.

"You got taller."

"Everyone did."

"Greg didn't."

I looked between them.

"I am sitting here."

Neither cared.

Noll's eyes moved to me.

"Blue cup?"

I stared at him.

Mara put both hands on the table.

"You remember the cup?"

"Your mother hated it."

"She said Halden had cups."

"Does it?"

"Yes. Wrong cups."

Noll nodded as if this settled a dispute he had been carrying for years.

Then Vessa shouted his name from the kitchen.

He vanished.

Mara looked at me.

"You didn't know we knew each other."

"I know many things."

"Not that one."

"Correct."

She smiled.

I did not entirely mind being the least informed person at the table.

Only mostly.

We ordered.

Mara chose fish because apparently travel had not damaged judgment.

I chose stew because the universe deserved a chance to rehabilitate the word.

Noll sent dumplings we had not ordered.

Vessa charged us.

I checked the bill later.

Institutional values remained stable.

The first half of dinner was easy.

Not empty.

Easy.

Mara told me Halden's east office used three different ink colors to track delays and nobody agreed what yellow meant.

I told her about Dena charging me one copper for four minutes of letter-posting time.

"Good," Mara said.

"You don't know the facts."

"I know you cost someone four minutes and argued."

"I paid."

"Eventually."

That word had now spread beyond quarantine.

"Jorren is responsible for this."

"Jorren's back?"

"Was."

Mara's fork stopped.

"Was?"

There.

The road.

I had not planned when to mention it.

Apparently now had applied without permission.

"He came back. Got another offer. Left tomorrow."

"You going?"

"No."

Mara looked at me.

Not pleased.

That surprised me.

"Why not?"

"You."

Wrong word.

I knew immediately.

Her face changed.

"Greg."

"Dinner," I corrected. "I said I'd be here."

"That's not much better."

I put the spoon down.

"Why?"

Mara folded one hand around her cup.

Not the blue one.

Restaurant cup.

"Because I did not ask you to stop your life."

"I didn't."

"You just said no to a road because I was coming."

"I said yes to dinner before the road existed."

She looked at me.

I continued because the sentence mattered.

"Then he asked. I wanted the road. I still said no."

Mara's thumb moved once along the cup rim.

"And now?"

"Now I'm here."

"Are you angry?"

"A little."

She looked down.

There it was.

The thing I had been half-hoping she would like had become weight on the table.

I disliked that.

Also disliked that some part of me still wanted credit.

Mara looked up again.

"At me?"

"No."

Fast answer.

I checked it.

"Not really."

"Greg."

"I'm angry the road existed at the wrong time. I'm angry Jorren asked after I had already said yes to you. I'm angry he can take Beren instead."

Mara blinked.

"Who is Beren?"

"Exactly."

That got a laugh out of her.

Small.

Enough to loosen something.

"I don't want to owe you Doss Ferry," she said.

"You don't."

"Then don't make tonight proof you made the right choice."

That landed hard because I had already been arranging the evening exactly that way somewhere behind my ribs.

Good dinner.

Mara here.

See?

Worth it.

Transaction complete.

I hated being predictable to a woman who had known me before I knew myself.

"Fine."

Mara raised one eyebrow.

"Terrible answer."

"Jorren said that too."

"Smart man."

"Everyone is forming alliances."

"Maybe you're the problem."

"Impossible."

She ate another dumpling.

The awkwardness did not disappear.

It changed shape.

That was enough to keep eating through.

Later, Mara asked about the West Cut.

Not the system.

What the nights were like.

Whether I was scared.

If the sword came out.

How bad the rain got.

I told her about glasshorns under moonlight.

She asked what they sounded like.

I had to think.

Not because the sound was complicated.

Because I had spent the original night noticing route behavior, animal spacing, distance, and whether the caravan would move.

The actual sound had been beneath those things.

"Low," I said. "Like somebody dragging a wet bow across a large string."

Mara made a face.

"That sounds awful."

"It was beautiful."

"Those can coexist."

Apparently.

I asked about Halden.

Not Vey.

Halden.

Her room.

Food.

Whether she had friends besides Pell and his onion crimes.

Mara looked at me over the cup.

"Two."

"Only two?"

"Two I would tell you about without context."

"That sounds worse."

"One is a woman named Sera who works the west tally and cheats at darts."

"Useful."

"She is not useful. She is very good at darts."

"Different category."

"The other is Pell."

"Onions disqualify him."

"He brought me soup when I was sick."

There.

Pell became harder to dislike abstractly.

Annoying man.

"What kind?"

"Onion."

Of course.

By the time the plates cleared, the room had thinned.

Noll came out once, looked at our empty dumpling plate, and nodded at himself.

"Don't."

He kept nodding.

Mara laughed.

Vessa brought the bill.

Mara reached first.

I reached too.

Her hand landed on mine.

Small contact.

Immediate heat.

Not magical.

Worse.

I looked at her fingers on the back of my hand.

Ink still marked the side of her thumb.

Current detail.

She saw where I was looking.

Did not move her hand yet.

"Half," she said.

I almost argued.

The letter had specifically warned me.

"Half."

She smiled.

Then removed her hand.

Loss registered faster than dignity.

We paid.

Outside, Carrow had gone cold.

Mara pulled her coat closed.

I wanted to touch her again.

That thought no longer needed historical justification.

We walked toward the river office.

Halfway there, Mara slowed at the Lower Rook turn.

She looked down the street.

"That's you?"

"Three streets."

"You still describe it badly."

"You have seen it."

"Before the fire."

"Ah."

She looked at me.

"Show me."

My body heard the sentence first.

Unhelpful institution.

"Now?"

Mara's mouth twitched.

"No. Next year."

"Work at first bell."

"I know."

"Your room is the other direction."

"I know where streets go."

Apparently everyone did.

She turned toward Lower Rook.

I followed.

This was not the road I had said no to.

I decided not to compare them again tonight...
