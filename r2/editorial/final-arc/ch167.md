# Chapter 167: The Passenger

Station bread deserved its reputation.

I learned this at dawn outside Carrow while chewing something that had once been flour and now existed mainly to test commitment.

Sera watched me from the opposite bench of the west coach.

"Iria warned you."

"I brought food."

"Then why are you eating that?"

"Comparison."

"To what?"

I looked at the bread.

"Failure."

The coach jolted before she could answer.

My walking foot slid half an inch on the floorboard.

I caught the seat rail.

Sera did not.

She had wedged both boots under the opposite bench and was asleep before Carrow's west wall disappeared behind us.

Professional traveler.

I spent the first hour trying to read.

The road disagreed.

I spent the second hour watching freight carts and pretending that counted as work.

It almost did.

Load distribution showed itself in ugly little ways when people stopped caring about elegance.

One cart carried clay roof tile stacked too high on the left.

Driver compensated by riding right.

Another had a rear axle that flexed every time the wheel struck a rut.

The woman driving it had tied a bell to the frame, not for charm, but because the pitch changed when the bend got worse.

Good system.

Cheap sensor.

I wrote it down.

Sera woke enough to see.

"You lasted two hours."

"Doing what?"

"Not turning the road into homework."

"I am enjoying myself."

"That is the problem."

We reached Halden late afternoon.

The city still smelled like river mud, hot metal, bread, and too many carts trying to own the same street.

For half a second I expected Mara to come around the Vey office corner.

Wrong city version.

She had left.

Halden had not.

That was how places worked when you weren't arrogant enough to confuse your departure with theirs.

The freight yard sat west of the river district behind a wall tall enough to discourage theft and low enough to admit anyone with a ladder.

Four wagons waited under long sheds.

Not pretty wagons.

Freight wagons.

Broad wheels.

Double pins on the rear assemblies.

Brake shoes thick enough to smell when they got hot.

The maintenance wagon had two spare wheel rims lashed underneath and a rack of iron bars along one side.

I liked it immediately.

A man was standing under the lead wagon with his legs sticking out.

"Pavo?" Iria called.

The legs stopped moving.

"No," the man said from under the wagon.

Iria looked at me.

"Pavo."

He rolled out.

Late thirties maybe.

Round face.

Short beard.

One sleeve rolled higher than the other because grease had claimed the lower half.

"You bring the Carrow people?"

"I brought the contractors."

Pavo looked at Sera.

"You again."

"Still alive."

"Shame."

Then at me.

His eyes dropped to the walking foot.

"You have the other leg?"

Kelm had asked the same thing.

"Packed."

"Good. West road eats nice shoes."

"This is not a nice shoe."

"Road doesn't care."

He went back under the wagon.

Iria said, "Cargo check at sixth bell. Sleep here or find an inn. We leave before first."

Evin was standing near the western gate with both brass rods extended horizontally.

The air was still.

Mostly.

One rod trembled at the tip.

I walked over.

"Wind?"

"Not here."

"Then what?"

"Charge."

He rotated one rod.

A tiny blue spark jumped between the forked end and his thumbnail.

"Still residue in the western air. Not dangerous in town. Means the road got hit harder than the survey notes sound."

"Kelm surveyed it."

"Kelm describes a bridge that's missing half its deck as 'needs boards.'"

"Efficient."

"Infuriating."

Kelm appeared behind us carrying a coil of chain.

"Needs boards means needs boards."

Evin did not turn.

"You see?"

The freight yard cook sold stew by bowl and bread by apology.

I skipped the bread.

At sixth bell, Iria opened every sealed cargo list with the merchant factors present.

Not the cargo.

The lists.

Medicine in wagon one.

Lamp crystal in two.

Bearings and two merchant chests in three.

Dyed cloth, copper wire, and one crate of glass instruments in four.

Pavo cared about weight, not value.

"Chest three is too far rear," he said.

One merchant factor objected immediately.

"That compartment is locked."

"Move the compartment."

"It is built into the bed."

Pavo looked at Iria.

Iria looked at the factor.

"Then we move the lighter crate ahead of it."

"The seals—"

"Remain sealed."

"My packing order—"

"Does not own the axle."

Conversation ended.

Good lead.

We spent an hour shifting cargo without opening anything expensive.

The maintenance wagon gained two coils of wire because wagon four had too much rear sway.

Wagon two lost one water barrel to wagon one.

Pavo made three workers walk beside each wagon while he rolled them twenty yards over the yard stones.

He listened.

Not to voices.

To wheels.

At wagon three he stopped us.

"Again."

We rolled back.

"Greg."

I looked at Iria.

"What?"

"You wanted to know who owns load. Watch Pavo."

I did.

Pavo put one hand on the rear bed rail while the wagon moved.

Not enough to influence it.

Just feel.

The bed gave a tiny side kick when the right rear wheel climbed a stone.

Then another half a second later.

Cargo returning late.

I felt it too once I knew where to put my hand.

"Upper tie," I said.

Pavo nodded.

"Loose by a finger."

Worker climbed up.

He was right.

I had seen the movement.

Pavo had known what it meant before I finished naming it.

Good.

I almost said the word out loud.

Caught myself.

Growth.

Maybe.

We slept in the freight loft above the wagons.

Sera claimed the wall.

Kelm claimed the darkest corner.

Evin slept near the window and complained that everyone else breathed too warmly.

Iria said nothing and was asleep within minutes.

I lay on a thin mattress listening to twenty people settle.

Horses below.

Mules shifting chains.

Someone coughing.

Pavo swearing softly at a worker who had forgotten to cover the spare grease.

Ordinary travel noises.

Before sleep, I checked my socket.

Red at the outer edge.

Not bad.

Coach day.

I changed liner.

Rubbed the skin dry.

Put the walking leg beside the mattress and set the field peg within reach for morning.

Sera's voice came from the dark.

"You always do that before bed?"

"Take off my leg?"

"Check it like an invoice."

"Skin is equipment."

"That's a deeply weird sentence."

"Skin failure is expensive."

"Worse."

She rolled over.

I lay back.

Outside the loft window, Halden's western sky flashed once.

No thunder.

Too far.

Evin sat up immediately.

"That," he said, "was not in the morning estimate."

Iria's voice came from the other side of the loft.

Awake already.

"How bad?"

Evin waited.

Another faint flash.

Then nothing.

"Could be shelf discharge," he said. "Could be the western band throwing ahead. I'll know at roadhead."

Iria grunted.

Nobody panicked.

Nobody declared destiny.

Pavo shouted from below that if the specialists were going to discuss weather all night they could come help cover the feed.

Sera laughed into her blanket.

I put the field peg closer.

Before first bell, we rolled west.

Halden thinned into mills.

Mills into farms.

Farms into rough ground.

By midday, the sea appeared as a strip of gray beyond the hills.

By third bell, the road started climbing.

By fifth, I understood why Kelm had laughed when I asked how much the spans moved.

The Storm Road appeared across the next valley in pieces.

Stone ledges cut directly into black cliff.

Brass vanes taller than trees.

Chain-supported roadways hanging over open air.

Shelter towers built into the rock like square teeth.

And beyond all of it, the western sea was throwing white lines against cliffs hundreds of feet below.

One suspended span moved in the distance.

Not much.

Enough.

The whole road breathed under the wind.

Kelm stood beside me at the overlook.

"Ruler?" he asked.

"Packed."

"Throw it away now. Save time."

I looked at the moving span.

"How much?"

"Enough," he said again.

I hated him slightly.

Promising.
