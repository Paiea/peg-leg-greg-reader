# Chapter 170: The Camper

Shelter Twenty-Four had a roof, three walls, half a fourth wall, and soup that made station bread seem ambitious.

We arrived before sixth bell with the wind pushing salt through every gap in the stone.

The shelter sat inside a notch cut into the cliff, deep enough that the road disappeared from sight once the wagons rolled behind the outer wall.

Good protection from direct wind.

Less good from sound.

The sea spent all evening trying to remove the cliff below us.

It had time.

Pavo got the animals under cover first.

Iria assigned cargo watches.

Kelm went straight to the shelter's storm hardware and started swearing at a drain.

Evin climbed the outer stair to the vane platform before anyone finished unloading.

Sera found the kitchen.

I found a bench.

Priorities differed by specialty.

The walking leg came off immediately.

Outer socket edge red again.

Not broken skin.

Close enough that pretending would be expensive.

I cleaned it.

Dried it.

Changed liner.

Then sat with one trouser leg empty while everyone else worked around me.

This had become ordinary enough that nobody performed concern.

Pavo stepped over the prosthetic without looking down.

"Need the bench?" I asked.

"Need the bucket under it."

I moved the leg.

He took the bucket.

Excellent social integration.

The shelter keeper was a woman named Lette who had one cloudy eye and no apparent respect for contractors.

Her soup contained barley, onion, two kinds of root vegetable, and something Sera claimed had once been meat.

Lette heard her.

"Still is."

Sera poked it with a spoon.

"What kind?"

"Road kind."

Kelm laughed hard enough to cough.

I ate two bowls.

Taste was secondary to warmth.

Afterward, I found Evin on the outer platform with both brass rods mounted into a waist-high socket.

The storm vane above us was enormous up close.

Three bronze fins around a central spine.

Each fin longer than I was tall.

The whole assembly rotated slowly while smaller rings beneath it shifted independently.

Not decorative.

The rings tracked different wind layers.

"What do the small rings do?" I asked.

Evin did not turn.

"Upper, road, and cliff-return flow."

"Cliff-return?"

"Wind hits face, drops, curls back. That's what throws wagons outward when everyone thinks wind is pushing inland."

Good.

I leaned on the stone rail.

One of his brass rods vibrated.

"Charge?"

"Yes."

"How much?"

"Enough that you stop asking me for exact numbers unless you want me to invent them."

Kelm influence spreading.

Evin adjusted one rod by a finger width.

The vibration changed pitch.

I watched the rod.

Then the vane.

Then the road below.

"Could you use the rod as a moving reference?"

He looked at me.

"For what?"

"My planes."

"No."

Fast answer.

"You don't know what I'm asking."

"You want to tie your little corrections to my wind read so you can anticipate when the wagon shifts."

I paused.

"Mostly."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because these rods are telling me what the air is doing. If I start moving them to make your geometry cleaner, I corrupt the read."

"Not moving them. Referencing them."

"Then when I rotate one you will have to decide whether the reference changed because wind changed or because I changed instrument angle."

"I could learn the difference."

"In a yard."

"Probably."

"Not on this road."

He went back to the rod.

"Storm is not the place you discover a new professional identity for me."

There it was.

Sharper than needed.

Also fair.

"I wasn't trying to change your identity."

"You were trying to make me part of your reference system."

"Temporary."

"Still no."

I looked at the road.

"Understood."

Evin glanced sideways.

Maybe expecting argument.

I had some.

Kept it.

Growth.

Annoying.

We stood quietly for a minute.

The vane rotated.

One lower ring turned the other direction.

Evin said, "You were late during the wirewings."

"Yes."

"I noticed."

"Thank you."

"You aren't slow."

"Slower than the problem."

"Sometimes."

He tapped his rod.

"Everybody is slower than weather. That's why we predict."

"Prediction caused problems at the bridge."

"Bad prediction."

"Helpful distinction."

"You're welcome."

He dismissed me by pretending I had already left.

I went back downstairs.

Pavo was checking harness leather under a hanging lamp.

One mule stood beside him chewing with the blank moral superiority of animals that had never signed contracts.

I sat on the low wall and watched.

Pavo pulled one strap through his fingers.

Stopped.

Bent it.

Tiny crack opened near a punched hole.

He set it aside.

"Replace?" I asked.

"Tomorrow."

"Would hold."

"Probably."

"Then why replace?"

He looked at me.

"Because tomorrow has cliffs."

Good answer.

I almost said it.

Did not.

Pavo picked up another strap.

"You keep staring at wagons."

"Professional interest."

"You stare like you're waiting for them to confess."

"They move differently than buildings."

"Sharp."

"Buildings usually have better manners."

"Wagons tell you everything. You just keep asking road."

I frowned.

"What?"

He put the strap down.

"During the wirewings, you watched wheels against road."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Road is reference."

"For road."

He pointed at the harness.

"Team doesn't care where road is for first half-second. Team cares where wagon is. Wagon doesn't care where cliff is until later."

I looked at him.

Pavo continued because apparently my face invited lectures now.

"When lead mule jumps right, tongue moves before rear wheel does. Harness tells you. Bed rail tells you. Cargo line tells you. You're waiting for consequence down at tire."

I stood.

"Show me."

"Now?"

"You are already here."

Pavo sighed like I had assigned him homework.

We went to wagon two.

Chocked.

Empty animal traces.

He had me put one hand on the wagon tongue.

Then he shoved the front corner of the bed sideways.

Tongue moved first.

Tiny.

Obvious through my palm.

"Again."

He shoved.

I cast a small plane against the tongue.

Too rigid.

Pavo felt it immediately.

"Don't stop."

Everyone knew this lesson now.

"I know."

"Then why stop?"

"Habit."

"Bad one."

Again.

This time I let the tongue move.

Plane followed beside it.

Not touching until the last inch.

Pavo pushed harder.

The wagon bed shifted.

My plane caught late travel.

"Better."

"You can say good."

"Didn't feel like it."

We repeated with the side rail.

Then a cargo tie.

Then he had one worker harness a calm mule and walk two steps sideways on command.

I watched the harness ring instead of the wheel.

Movement announced itself earlier.

Not magic.

Information.

That was almost offensive.

"You could have told me this during the attack."

Pavo looked at me.

"I was busy with mule."

Fair.

Night settled fully outside.

Lette closed the kitchen.

Kelm finished bullying the drain into working.

Sera came in carrying three blue-gray shards of stormglass from the waste basket beside the old vane maintenance box.

"Legal?" I asked.

"Lette says take it or she sweeps it over the cliff."

"Excellent."

She gave me one.

Bigger than Kelm's piece.

Still worthless enough to be mine.

I held it up to the lamp.

Thin lines inside glowed faintly when Evin's rods hummed upstairs.

Not power.

Residue.

I put it with the first shard.

Two pieces of junk.

Collection now.

Before bed, Iria gathered us around the map.

"Weather update."

Evin pointed at marker twenty-seven.

"Western band accelerated. We still have margin, but less. Maybe half a day."

"Meaning?"

"If nothing changes, we reach the far gate before the bad wind. If we lose a full day, we shelter and probably wait two more."

Merchant factor asked, "Completion premium?"

Iria said, "Secondary to getting there."

He did not like that.

Professional hierarchy remained healthy.

I lay down with my field peg beside the mattress.

Socket skin cooler.

No damage.

Pavo's lesson sat in my head.

Harness before wheel.

Tongue before wagon.

Movement before consequence.

Across the shelter, Evin was writing weather numbers by lamp.

I did not ask to borrow his rods again.

This felt less like restraint than basic listening.

Still counted.
