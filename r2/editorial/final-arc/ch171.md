# Chapter 171: The Counterweight

Windrams looked less ridiculous when thirty of them were trying to cross the road at once.

From a distance they looked like mountain sheep somebody had designed after hearing descriptions from a drunk sailor.

Wide bodies.

Short legs.

Thick gray coats.

Horns that curled sideways instead of back, broad and flat enough to catch wind.

The horn edges held a faint blue shine where storm charge had collected in the ridges.

Evin saw the herd first.

"Stop."

Iria raised one hand.

Entire convoy stopped.

Good system.

The herd stood above us on a steep shelf two hundred yards ahead.

Not blocking road yet.

Watching.

Pavo swore softly.

"They're coming down."

"Why?" I asked.

"Salt."

Sera looked at the sea.

"Plenty down there."

Pavo pointed at the road surface.

"Road crew salts the drain cuts. They lick stone after storms."

Of course.

Every dangerous animal eventually wanted something boring.

Iria said, "Can we wait?"

Pavo shook his head.

"Maybe hour. Maybe six. Herd decides."

Evin raised one brass rod.

It hummed immediately.

"Horn charge is high. Do not scare them into the vanes."

Kelm looked offended by the possibility.

"I wasn't planning to scare livestock into road hardware."

Sera said, "You scare people into road hardware."

"People deserve it more."

Iria walked forward far enough to see the next bend.

Road narrowed there.

One wagon could pass comfortably.

Two sheep could not, apparently.

"Options?" she asked.

Pavo said, "Let them cross before bend. Keep teams calm. No shouting."

Sera nodded.

"I can hold the lead edge if one gets stupid."

Evin said, "No wind shaping near horns unless needed. Charge will jump."

I waited.

Nobody looked at me.

Healthy.

Then Pavo did.

"You can help wagon three."

"Why three?"

"Rear load. If team jumps sideways, bed follows ugly."

Good.

We chocked the wagons.

Workers moved off the road edge and stood against the cliff wall.

Sera took position near the lead team with no sword drawn.

Important detail.

The first windram came down.

Big male.

Horns blue at the edges.

He stepped onto the road, sniffed a drain cut, and licked stone with a tongue too pink for the rest of him.

Sera looked at me.

I shrugged.

Majestic.

More followed.

Five.

Ten.

Young ones in the middle.

One ram bumped another and a blue spark jumped horn to horn.

Evin whispered, "Don't touch metal."

Everyone suddenly became aware of every buckle they owned.

The herd moved past wagon one.

Then two.

The mules hated them.

One flattened its ears.

Another pulled backward against trace.

Pavo murmured names.

Not commands.

Names again.

At wagon three, the lead mule took one step left.

Harness ring moved.

There.

Before wheel.

Before tongue even moved much.

I put a small plane beside the forward harness bar.

Not touching.

Moving reference.

Pavo saw it.

"Wait."

I waited.

Mule took another half-step.

Plane followed.

Then the wagon bed started to yaw.

I let it.

Last inch only.

Small contact.

Enough to slow the yaw while Pavo corrected the team.

No jerk.

No animal reacting to invisible resistance it didn't understand.

Pavo nodded once.

Not praise.

Confirmation.

The herd kept moving.

Then one young ram stopped directly beside wagon three and stared at the merchant chest bed as if considering a career change.

Sera whispered, "Don't."

Ram ignored her.

It lowered its head.

Not charge.

Scratch.

Horn edge scraped the wagon rail.

Blue light snapped across the iron fitting.

The mule team exploded sideways.

Not metaphorically.

All four animals moved at once.

Pavo shouted.

Workers grabbed lead lines.

I watched harness.

Forward bar shot right.

Plane moved with it.

No road reference.

No wheel.

Harness.

I let the first movement happen, then caught the end.

The wagon bed swung.

Rear load lagged.

I put a second plane near the left rear rail.

Too much.

Could not hold both cleanly.

Counterweight.

That was what the cargo wanted.

Not two stops.

One relationship.

I dropped the front plane and shifted the rear one lower, angled against the lagging bed.

The wagon's own mass pulled the tongue back toward alignment.

Pavo got the team straight.

Sera stepped between mule and windram.

No blade.

She slapped the ram's horn with the flat of a wooden feed paddle somebody had handed her.

The animal jumped like it had been insulted by God.

Charge flashed blue.

Evin swore.

Sera swore louder.

The ram bolted across the road and rejoined the herd.

"Wood," Sera said.

"Correct," Evin said.

"Still shocked me."

"Indirect induction."

"I don't care what it's called."

"I do."

"Of course you do."

The mules settled slowly.

Pavo checked harness.

No break.

Wagon three remained on road.

Merchant chests remained inside.

I had not touched the road once.

Interesting.

Pavo came around the wagon.

"Better."

"You can say good."

"Still don't feel like it."

Consistent man.

The herd took another twenty minutes to clear.

One worker collected three bits of horn scale from the road afterward.

Evin stopped him from touching them barehanded.

We wrapped the pieces in dry cloth.

Road salvage rules allowed shed horn.

Not valuable.

Charge-holding material useful for cheap weather charms.

The merchant factor wanted to claim it as convoy incidental salvage.

Iria said no.

Worker found it.

Worker kept it.

Correct civilization.

We moved again.

By midday, the road climbed into white mist.

Not cloud exactly.

Sea spray pushed high enough by wind to become weather.

Everything tasted faintly of salt.

My lips cracked.

Walking foot slipped once on wet stone.

Field peg went back on.

At marker twenty-five we passed a dead storm vane lying beside the road.

Not ancient.

Broken last season.

One bronze fin twisted like soft paper.

Kelm stopped long enough to point at the base.

"See the scoring?"

Four deep lines circled the pivot housing.

"Overheld," he said.

I knew what he meant now.

Someone had tried to keep the vane facing one direction under load.

Instead of letting it travel.

The wind had won.

It always did eventually.

"Magic?" I asked.

"Setter crew."

Evin heard from ahead.

"Bad setter crew."

Kelm shrugged.

"Same result."

We camped that night at an open road station instead of a stone shelter.

Three low buildings around a yard.

Repair shed.

Stable.

Kitchen.

And behind them, a forest of spare storm-vane fins standing upright in racks.

Metal leaves.

Some taller than the roofs.

A woman was on a ladder painting numbers onto one.

Kelm called up.

"Maelin."

She looked down.

"You're late."

"We're early."

"Then you're late for tomorrow."

Kelm nodded like this made sense.

Maybe road people shared a disease.

Maelin climbed down.

Thin woman.

Gray braid.

Left wrist wrapped in dark leather under a brass cuff.

I knew that shape.

Not exactly.

Same family.

Three narrow bands.

Inset glass nodes.

Setter's cuff.

The dead-inventory vendor in Halden had wanted too much for one.

I had bought socks.

Maelin caught me looking.

"You know what this is?"

"I've priced one."

"Did you buy it?"

"No."

"Smart."

She flexed her hand.

The cuff did not glow.

Nothing dramatic happened.

Kelm said, "Maelin sets vanes."

"I gathered."

She pointed at the huge fin behind her.

"Want to see something useless?"

"Usually."

Maelin raised one hand.

A tiny force shape appeared at the fin's pivot.

Not a plane.

A curved pressure track maybe two inches long.

Fast.

So fast I almost missed the forming step.

She moved her fingers.

The shape rotated around the pivot with mechanical precision.

Then vanished.

"Again," I said.

She did.

Instant.

Not powerful.

Exact.

"How long?" I asked.

"Cuff?"

"Yes."

"First one twenty-six years ago. This one's newer."

"You still need it?"

Maelin smiled.

"Need is rude."

"Fair."

She took the cuff off.

Raised her hand again.

Same curved shape appeared.

Slightly slower.

Still very fast.

"It stuck," I said.

"Some of it."

"Cost?"

She looked toward the yard.

Then formed a broad flat barrier between two rack posts.

It came.

Slowly.

Not weak.

Slow.

Edges uneven for almost a full breath before settling.

Maelin dropped it.

"Used to do that better."

"How much better?"

"Good enough to take general ward jobs."

"Now?"

"Now I set vanes."

She did not sound sad.

That mattered more than the answer.

"Regret it?"

"No."

"Ever?"

"Sometimes when my roof leaks and I know exactly enough broad work to be annoyed."

She put the cuff back on.

"But I make twice what I made as a general ward hand, and nobody else on this coast can set a return ring as fast as me."

There.

A life.

Not curse.

Not warning.

Trade.

Kelm walked toward the repair shed.

"Don't sell him one."

Maelin called after him, "Why?"

"He likes bad ideas if they have documentation."

She looked at me.

"That true?"

"Documentation helps."

Maelin laughed.

I looked at the cuff again.

The road behind us had already given me half a heartbeat worth caring about.

The road ahead had seven more miles of damaged ward cover before the next full shelter.

I asked the question I had asked in Halden months ago.

"How much?"

Maelin's smile got wider.

Kelm swore from inside the shed.

Not an answer.

Still useful.
