# Candidate Chapter 119: Five Days

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Second bell turned out to mean breakfast.

This was less obvious than Mara believed.

She found me on the same stone steps where she had left me, except I had moved down one because a delivery boy needed the upper landing for two crates of paper and did not care about promises completed before sunrise.

Mara stopped at the office door.

"You stayed."

"You said breakfast."

"I thought you'd walk around."

"I did."

She looked at the distance between the office door and the bottom of the steps.

"Greg."

"Very efficiently."

Mara laughed and came down to me.

She had three folders under one arm now.

I knew what they were before she said anything.

Carrow.

Halden.

Northbank.

Her future had apparently become stationery.

The top folder had a red smear across one corner.

I pointed at it.

"That one is compromised."

Mara looked down.

"Jam."

"Exactly."

"It's Northbank."

"Excellent. Eliminate it."

"No."

"Contaminated offer."

"Breakfast first."

She started walking before I could improve the argument.

I followed.

Halden after second bell was louder than before first.

Carts had taken the river street. Two packet clerks were arguing beside a post board about whether a sealed tube had arrived yesterday or only been recorded yesterday. A woman in a blue apron carried six empty bottles in one hand and shouted through a bakery window with enough confidence to suggest she owned at least one side of the dispute.

Mara knew where she was going.

I had spent three days in Halden and already understood the important landmarks.

The Vey office.

The inn.

The prosthetic fitter.

One decent noodle stall.

One terrible noodle stall pretending to be the decent one.

Mara led me past both.

"Where?"

"Breakfast."

"Specific."

"You survived two years without me."

"Poorly."

"Clearly."

She turned into a narrow place beside a metalworker's yard.

The front room held six small tables, one long bench against the wall, and a kitchen opening where a man was peeling potatoes into a bucket with the concentration of a temple scribe.

Heat from the yard next door came through the brick wall in soft pulses.

The room smelled like onions, bread, hot iron, and tea.

A server looked up.

"Mara."

"Morning, Tali."

The server looked at me.

Then at Mara.

Then back at me.

"Greg?"

I stopped.

Mara kept walking.

"This is getting worse."

Tali smiled. "Two years."

"That explains nothing."

"It explains everything."

Mara chose a table by the wall.

I sat across from her and worked the walking foot under the table.

The bench had been built for two equal legs and people who did not need to think about where a wooden ankle wanted to rest.

I turned the left knee out slightly.

Better.

Not comfortable.

Fine.

Tali brought tea without asking Mara.

She looked at me.

"Same?"

"I don't know what same is."

"Tea."

"That seems safe."

"He's difficult," Mara said.

"I noticed."

Tali left.

I looked at Mara.

"You've been telling people."

"I work with them."

"You've been telling coworkers."

"You have been writing me for two years."

"Private correspondence."

"You once sent me three pages about a cracked bridge pin."

"It was interesting."

"It was not."

"Then why did you keep the letter?"

Mara opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Good.

Victory remained possible.

Tali returned with tea and two rolls.

One had apricot jam.

Mara took that one.

Good woman.

I tore mine open.

Steam escaped.

Fresh enough.

Across the room, two dockworkers were discussing a crate that had gone into the river.

One blamed the rope.

The other blamed the rain.

Within thirty seconds they had also blamed city stonework and a dead uncle named Parris.

I approved of the widening inquiry.

Mara put the three folders on the table beside her plate.

The red smear belonged to Northbank.

The gray folder was Halden.

Carrow was darker brown, thickest of the three, with a seal pressed too deeply into the corner.

I looked at them.

Mara caught me.

"Five days."

"I remember."

"You have the face."

"This is my face."

"The planning face."

Apparently I had categories now.

Bad development.

Tali brought a small plate of eggs and asked, "Anything else?"

Mara said, "No, thank you."

I said, "Do you know how accurate the clock is?"

Mara closed her eyes.

Tali looked at the clock above the kitchen opening.

"Loses about four minutes an hour."

I looked at Mara.

Mara looked at me.

Tali left before becoming collateral.

"See?" I said.

"You've been watching it."

"Only intermittently."

"How long?"

I checked the clock.

Then decided honesty had already harmed me enough today.

"Not long."

"Greg."

"There was also potato activity."

Mara glanced toward the kitchen opening.

The man was still peeling.

"How were the potatoes?"

"Consistent."

She drank tea.

I had missed this.

Not potatoes.

Not exactly breakfast.

The useless space around another person.

For two years almost every visit had carried a clock.

A departure.

A job.

A road window.

A promise with an exact end.

Even letters had dates because packets required them.

Now the largest deadline in my life had rung once and disappeared.

Mara was here.

I was here.

Nobody required us to accomplish anything before the tea cooled.

My head disliked the arrangement immediately.

Mara opened the Halden folder.

I looked away.

She noticed.

"You can look."

"You said five days."

"I said I have five days to choose. I did not say the paper becomes invisible until then."

"That distinction was not included."

"Nothing is real to you until someone writes terms."

"Unfair."

"Mostly fair."

She read.

I ate.

The dockworkers moved from Parris's failures as an uncle to his failures as a fisherman.

Apparently the man had been dead nine years and remained available for operational review.

Mara turned a page.

I tried not to read upside down.

Failed.

Salary line.

Travel allowance.

Temporary housing support.

There.

Halden housing was better than she had guessed in her last letter.

That changed the comparison.

Not enough by itself.

Northbank still paid more.

Carrow paid less but reduced travel between us to almost nothing, assuming I stayed in Carrow, which was not actually guaranteed anymore because my own work had become...

I stopped.

Mara was staring at me.

"What?"

"You stopped chewing."

"I was chewing."

"You were calculating."

"No."

Her eyes moved to my bag.

My notebook was inside.

"Did you make a table?"

Silence remained a valid tactical choice.

Mara put down the Halden offer.

"Greg."

"A small one."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

"You made a comparison table yesterday."

"Yes."

"Before first bell."

Technically.

"Yes."

"Before I finished the term."

"Yes."

She stared at me.

I drank tea.

Across the room, dead Parris had become responsible for municipal drainage.

Mara leaned back.

"How many columns?"

"That feels accusatory."

"How many?"

"Eight."

Her mouth opened.

"Eight?"

"Salary. Housing. Travel. Contract length. Advancement. Route access. Staff authority. Effective distance."

"Effective distance?"

"Six road days and six river days are not the same thing."

"Nine columns."

I frowned.

"No."

"You just added transportation type."

"Contained within effective distance."

"Nine."

"Methodological disagreement."

She covered her face with one hand.

I waited.

Her shoulders moved once.

Laughing.

Good.

Probably.

When she looked at me again, her eyes were wet at the corners.

"You are ridiculous."

"I did not make a recommendation column."

"That is not the defense you think it is."

"It demonstrates restraint."

"It demonstrates you considered a recommendation column."

Also true.

I tore the rest of the roll in half.

Mara pointed at me with two fingers.

"I do not want the table."

"Understood."

"And I do not want you pretending you don't care."

Less easy.

"I care."

"I know."

"Carrow is what I want."

"I know."

I looked at the three folders.

"Northbank may be professionally better."

"Maybe."

"Halden has the best eastern route access and the people here already know you."

"Maybe."

"Carrow puts you near your mother."

"And you."

"Yes."

Mara waited.

I disliked waiting when I knew what the other person wanted.

That was usually when I filled silence with useful information.

I tried something else.

"What do you want?"

She looked down at the folders.

Not dramatic.

No sudden answer.

"I want my own work," she said.

I nodded.

"Not someone's overflow. Not a year of proving I can count someone else's route sheets. I want to decide something and have it stay decided unless I'm wrong."

That removed two assumptions from my table immediately.

I did not reach for the notebook.

Growth.

Possibly.

Mara touched Northbank's red-smeared corner.

"They're offering a route desk. Small staff. Western handoffs. I would own the weekly adjustments."

"Actual authority?"

"Some."

"How much?"

She gave me a look.

"Question, not table."

"Same mouth."

"Different crime."

Mara laughed.

Then she tapped Halden.

"Here I know everyone. Better pay than Carrow. Bigger office. But I'd still be under Sera's desk for another year."

"Do you like Sera?"

"Yes."

"Does that matter?"

"Yes."

Carrow next.

"Carrow is home," Mara said. "Mother. Kessa when she's there. You, sometimes."

"Rude."

"Accurate."

"And the work?"

She made a face.

There it was.

Not terrible.

Not enough.

I looked at Northbank again.

The jam had dried darker at the edge.

Five days suddenly felt unnecessary.

I knew what I thought.

That was not the same as what she would choose.

Dangerous distinction.

Mara watched my face change.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Planning face."

"No."

"Greg."

"I think I understand the table better."

She groaned.

I held up a hand.

"Not showing you."

"Good."

"No new columns."

Her eyes narrowed.

I added, "Probably."

She kicked me under the table.

Right shin.

Correct target selection.

I looked offended.

Mara looked satisfied.

Tali arrived with the bill.

I reached for it.

Mara reached first.

"I have a job," she said.

"For five more days."

"I have three job offers."

"Potential money."

"You owe Harrow two more payments because you stole a magical lens."

I released the bill.

"Strong argument."

"Thank you."

Tali took Mara's coins.

She did not ask who Harrow was.

Halden professionalism.

Outside, the river street had become fully awake.

A freight wagon waited while two men changed a wheel pin.

Someone farther uphill was selling blue-glazed cups from a blanket.

A child ran past holding a paper bird that fluttered even when his hand stopped.

Minor charm.

Cheap.

Excellent.

Mara shifted the folders under one arm.

"What are you doing today?"

"Leg adjustment."

"All day?"

"No."

"Then?"

I had answers available.

Check the Guild board.

Find temporary work.

Price the Harrow payment.

Walk the market.

Inspect the western bridge because the approach looked old.

Write Noll.

Write Jorren.

Maybe send Pell the sketch he asked for.

All useful.

None urgent.

"I don't know."

Mara stopped walking.

"You don't know."

"Correct."

"Are you ill?"

"Probably terminal."

She smiled.

I looked uphill.

Halden was still mostly unknown to me.

That should have bothered me.

Instead it felt almost wasteful to convert the whole city into routes and rates before lunch.

"Maybe walk," I said.

"Just walk?"

"Don't make it strange."

"It is strange."

"Hostile city."

"Terrible place."

She stepped closer and kissed my cheek.

Not long.

Public street.

Ordinary.

"Come by after work?"

"After which bell?"

Mara sighed.

"Sixth."

"Actual sixth or office sixth?"

"Greg."

"Sixth."

She walked away toward Vey.

I watched until she crossed the street.

Then I took out the notebook.

Not the table.

Different page.

Harrow payment due in eight days.

My current Halden cash below that by enough to matter.

I wrote:

**Work.**

Then stopped.

The obvious next line was a list.

Guild board.

Vey freight.

Warehouse district.

Day labor.

Survey crews.

I knew how to solve this.

That was not the problem.

I closed the notebook.

Pera first.

Then maybe the market.

Then work.

Probably.

I started uphill.

My walking foot landed slightly hard on the first rise.

Not pain.

A tiny outer-edge pressure I had noticed yesterday and ignored because arriving before first bell had seemed more important.

Pera was going to be insulting about that.

Good.

A normal problem.

I could use one...
