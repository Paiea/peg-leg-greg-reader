# Candidate Chapter 133: The Tourist

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

Mara took a day off.

This was suspicious enough that I asked whether she had been fired.

She threw a folded scarf at me.

"Put that in the bag."

I caught it.

"What bag?"

She held up a small canvas satchel.

"This bag."

"Why?"

"Because we're leaving."

"Northbank is in five days."

"Not that leaving."

Worse.

Unscheduled leaving.

I was at her room before first bell because I had been told to arrive before first bell and had learned enough to recognize command language when Mara used it.

No work today.

That had been explicit.

I had checked the Guild board anyway.

Nothing urgent with my name on it.

Not cheating.

Probably.

Mara slung the satchel over one shoulder.

"Ready?"

"For what?"

"Halden."

"We are in Halden."

"Exactly."

She walked out.

I followed because relationships sometimes required accepting poor briefing.

The morning was cool enough that my walking leg felt stiff for the first block.

Pera's new socket edge had settled well after the fire. No skin break. No swelling. The field peg stayed at the inn because Mara had said, specifically, "Wear the city leg."

That should have warned me.

She led us downhill toward the river.

Not Vey.

Not breakfast.

Not the Guild.

The ferry steps.

I stopped.

"Why?"

"Boat."

"North bank?"

"No."

"South bank?"

"Also no."

"Then the boat has limited ambition."

Mara bought two loop tickets.

I stared at them.

"A sightseeing ferry."

"Yes."

"People pay to ride in a circle."

"You have paid to inspect bridges."

"Different."

"Obviously."

The ferry was already half full.

Families.

Two older women carrying shopping baskets.

A group of apprentices with matching blue cuffs who were pretending not to be hungover.

One man with a little dog wearing a red coat.

The dog had better tailoring than I did.

Mara chose the open upper deck.

I looked at the stairs.

Narrow.

Wet at one edge.

Rail both sides.

Fine.

She waited without offering help.

Better.

Right foot.

Walking foot.

Rail.

Up.

At the top we found a bench facing the river instead of forward.

"Terrible navigation position," I said.

"Sit."

I sat.

The ferry pushed away from the dock.

Halden changed immediately.

Not actually.

Perspective did.

From the street, the city was stone and slopes and people obstructing useful routes.

From the river, the glass district caught morning light across a hundred high windows.

Blue.

Green.

Clear.

Amber where furnaces had stained older panes.

Three bridges crossed ahead of us.

The lowest carried carts.

The middle one carried foot traffic and ward lines.

The highest was newer, slender enough that I distrusted it on principle.

I leaned slightly to see the pier spacing.

Mara put one hand over my eyes.

"No."

"What?"

"You are not inspecting the bridge."

"I was looking."

"At the piers."

"They are visible."

"Look at something useless."

She removed her hand.

I considered the river.

Boats.

Warehouses.

Bridge.

Bridge remained best.

Then the little dog barked at a gull and nearly dragged its owner into a bench.

Useful enough.

I watched the dog.

Mara smiled.

"Better."

"This is coercive leisure."

"Yes."

The ferry passed under Copper Bridge.

Someone had hung hundreds of thin glass pieces beneath the pedestrian rail.

I had seen them from shore.

From below they made sense.

Each piece caught wind independently.

Small taps.

Different pitches.

Not music exactly.

Too irregular.

A glass rain that never landed.

Children on the lower deck shouted when the sound started.

The man with the dog lifted it so the animal could see upward.

I looked too.

No load analysis.

Mostly.

The ferry continued west, turned at the old customs tower, then came back along the opposite bank.

Forty-three minutes.

I knew because I checked once.

Only once.

Mara did not catch me.

Excellent day.

At the south market she bought fried dough with river salt and something sweet poured over it.

"What is that?"

"Honey."

"Why is it purple?"

"Glassberry."

"That explains color, not judgment."

She handed me one.

I ate it.

Good.

I refused to say so immediately.

Mara watched my face.

"Well?"

"Concerning."

"You like it."

"Evidence incomplete."

I ate the rest.

Evidence improved.

We wandered.

Actual wandering.

Mara had no route.

I asked twice.

The second time she pointed at a narrow street and said, "That way because there's a yellow awning."

This offended several decades of field experience.

The yellow awning belonged to a game stall.

Of course.

Six little brass rings.

Five floating glass fish moving slowly around a shallow basin under a cheap current charm.

Ring a fish by the neck and win whatever number was painted underneath.

Children's robbery.

I watched one boy miss all six.

The stall keeper rang a bell.

"Try?"

"No," I said.

Mara bought twelve rings.

"Six each."

"Waste."

"You stole an artifact."

"Unrelated scale."

"Throw."

I studied the fish.

Current speed.

Ring weight.

Distance.

One fish moved slower near the far corner where the charm basin had a weak return.

I threw.

Missed.

Mara laughed.

"Wind."

"Inside market?"

"Localized."

Second throw.

Missed again.

The stall keeper looked professionally neutral.

I disliked him.

Third.

Ring hit the glass fish's nose and bounced out.

Mara had started crying from laughter.

"You are enjoying failure too much."

"Again."

I threw the fourth without calculating.

Missed worse.

Good.

Maybe.

Fifth caught a tail and spun away.

Sixth landed clean around the slow fish.

Victory.

I raised both hands.

Mara clapped with insulting enthusiasm.

The stall keeper turned the fish over.

Painted number: three.

Prize three was a tiny wooden bird with one blue glass wing and one green.

I stared at it.

"This is worth less than the rings."

"Yes," the stall keeper said.

"You admit it?"

"You paid for hope."

Criminal economy.

I took the bird.

Mara went next.

First ring.

Miss.

Second.

Miss.

Third caught the fastest fish.

No analysis.

No respect for probability.

I objected.

She won a paper charm that made written ink smell faintly like mint.

"Useless," I said.

"Perfect."

We kept both prizes.

Lunch became noodles because Halden had apparently decided every important day required them.

Not our usual place.

A stall near the east steps with three stools and broth spicy enough that Mara's eyes watered.

She refused to admit this was a flaw.

After lunch we visited a public glass gallery where nobody sold anything.

That confused me more than the tourist ferry.

Panels from old guild houses.

Failed experiments.

Memorial pieces.

A window made from battlefield salvage that still carried two melted arrowheads inside the glass.

A child's panel full of tiny handprints pressed into different colors.

Mara liked that one.

I liked a plain clear pane from two hundred years ago because the maker had managed almost no distortion without modern heat wards.

"Of course," Mara said.

"It's excellent work."

"It is transparent."

"Exactly."

She took my hand.

We stayed longer than necessary.

There was no necessary.

That was still difficult.

By late afternoon my socket had started warming at the outer edge.

Not damage.

Information.

I sat on a low river wall while Mara bought two cups of cold tea.

She came back and handed me the blue one.

"Wrong cup," I said.

"Temporary custody."

Veya had apparently infected the city.

Mara sat beside me.

The river below moved brown-green under the bridges.

Five days.

I did not say it.

"This was nice."

"Expensive."

"Greg."

"Also nice."

"Thank you."

"Not turning today into preparation."

Bad idea.

"I inspected one bridge."

Mara sat up.

"You what?"

"Visually."

"Greg."

"Barely."

"Idiot."

"Violent tourist."

"You did that on purpose."

"Maybe."

Definitely.

Green wing.

"You won it."

"Exactly why I'm trying to get rid of it."
