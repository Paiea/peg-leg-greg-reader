# Candidate Chapter 135: The Hunter

Status: **BOOK RE-PERFORMANCE CANDIDATE / NOT SELECTED AUTHORITY**

The marsh did not care what I had become good at.

This was refreshing until my field peg sank six inches into mud.

Then I hated the marsh.

"Stop fighting it," Suri Vale said.

I looked at my leg.

Then at her.

"I am standing."

"Aggressively."

"Standing has limited emotional range."

Suri pointed at the peg.

"You plant every step like the ground owes you an answer. Mud doesn't. Put it down flatter."

The field peg did not have much flatter available.

Still, I tried.

Less vertical commitment.

More slide before load.

The next step sank only three inches.

I disliked her immediately.

Useful people were often terrible this way.

Low Marsh started east of Halden where the river stopped respecting maps.

Reeds.

Shallow channels.

Mud islands that looked solid until informed otherwise.

Stunted willow.

Water reflecting sky in patches too small to navigate by.

Insects large enough to deserve Guild classification.

I wore the field peg because the walking foot would have collected half the marsh by noon.

Pera's crawler-tendon washer sat above the ferrule in its waxed sleeve.

Completely wasted.

Mud damped impact better than expensive dead monster.

Research result.

Probably not the one Pera wanted.

Suri led.

No Guild tab.

No rank marker.

Short bow across her back, two knives, marsh boots nearly to the knee, hair tied under a faded green cloth.

She had looked at my Silver clasp at the Guild office and said, "Can you follow instructions?"

I had said, "Depends."

She had almost hired someone else.

The other two members of the bounty crew were Bo Tern, a hide worker who knew how to preserve proof, and Cerin, a Bronze Guild blade who had spent the first mile trying to flirt with Suri and the second mile discovering silence.

Suri had done that without magic.

Impressive.

The bounty sheet called our targets **low marsh grazers**.

This was misleading.

They did graze.

They also ate fish, eggs, carrion, river dogs, young goats, and recently one farmer's forearm.

Two animals had begun crossing warning stakes near the east reed farms after flood season.

Verified predation.

Two heads authorized.

Not all grazers.

Not a clearing operation.

Suri had repeated this twice before we left the road.

"You kill the marked pair," she said. "If we see six others, we leave six others."

Cerin asked, "How do we know the pair?"

Suri stopped.

"One male has a split left jaw plate. Female has white scar behind second foreleg. Both travel west of black willow line."

Cerin looked at the miles of reeds.

"Black willow line."

"Yes."

"Which willow?"

Suri pointed.

There were approximately four thousand willows.

One line leaned slightly darker where soil rose beneath them.

Maybe.

I saw it after she pointed.

I would not have before.

Good.

We kept walking.

I had spent a lot of the last week around people who knew one thing well enough to make me feel broad and shallow by comparison.

Apparently Halden had more available.

Suri stopped beside a puddle.

Not a pool.

Not a track.

Just brown water with three reeds bent across it.

She crouched.

Touched the surface with two fingers.

Smelled them.

"Male."

Bo crouched beside her.

"Fresh?"

"Hour. Maybe less."

I stared at the water.

Nothing.

"How?"

Suri rubbed her fingers together.

"Oil."

"Skin oil?"

"Jaw plate rub. He roots here. Water films different."

I looked harder.

Still mostly water.

Cerin said, "I see it."

Suri looked at him.

"No, you don't."

Cerin closed his mouth.

I liked her more.

We followed nothing I could identify for another mile.

Suri changed direction because two marsh birds flew low instead of high.

She stopped once because frogs had stopped calling on one side of a channel.

She found a scrape on a willow root that looked older than my current body and said it had happened that morning.

I asked fewer questions after that.

Not because I stopped wanting answers.

Because the answers were not improving fast enough to justify the noise.

At noon, Suri raised one hand.

We froze.

Cerin saw the grazer first this time.

I saw reeds.

Then the reeds moved against wind.

Broad gray back.

Low.

Long.

Six legs moving underneath the body in a rhythm that made it look like the marsh itself was crawling.

Split left jaw plate.

Male.

Maybe eight hundred pounds.

Maybe more.

The head was wider than my chest.

Suri crouched and drew four lines in mud with one finger.

"Bo here. Cerin right bank. Greg left. I push from north."

I looked at my line.

"Push where?"

"Toward Cerin first. If it turns, you make left look worse."

"Magic?"

"Whatever works. Don't try to stop it."

There.

I understood enough to dislike the possibility.

"Why not?"

Suri looked at the grazer.

Then my field peg.

Then the mud.

Question answered.

I moved left.

No wall.

No stable footing.

No structure worth reading.

The marsh absorbed load without returning useful information.

My current specialty had become almost decorative.

Good.

I drew the sword.

Cerin caught my eye from the opposite bank and nodded once.

At least swords remained culturally portable.

Suri disappeared into reeds.

Actually disappeared.

I knew where she had gone and lost her anyway.

Thirty breaths.

Forty.

A bird called twice.

The grazer lifted its head.

Suri's arrow struck behind the right foreleg.

The animal screamed.

High sound.

Wrong for the body.

It spun toward Cerin.

He stepped out, blade ready.

Suri shouted, "Not yet!"

Cerin obeyed.

The grazer charged three strides, saw him, then turned away exactly as Suri had wanted.

Left.

Toward me.

Excellent.

My peg sank when I shifted.

No fixed point.

No time to complain.

I formed a small plane near the grazer's left eye.

Not in front.

Beside.

It clipped the invisible edge with the jaw plate and jerked its head right.

Barely.

Enough to make open reeds on my right look easier than my body on its left.

It chose the opening.

Suri's second arrow hit flank.

Cerin moved now.

Fast.

Better footwork in mud than mine.

He cut across the rear right leg and got clear before the grazer kicked.

Good blade.

Very good.

The animal stumbled.

Did not fall.

Turned on him.

I was closer.

No elegant answer.

I stepped in and cut the damaged rear leg again.

Sword dragged through hide.

Blood hot across my hand.

The grazer dropped one side.

Suri came from somewhere impossible and put an arrow under the split jaw plate.

Then another.

Animal shuddered.

Stopped.

Silence came back slowly.

First frogs.

Then insects.

Then Cerin breathing hard enough to count as weather.

I wiped the sword on marsh grass.

Suri looked at my cut.

"Fine."

"High praise."

"Don't make it needy."

Bo arrived carrying tags and a short bone saw.

He examined the jaw plate.

"Good split. Bounty mark clean."

I stepped back because his work had started.

He knew exactly where to cut, how much tissue to leave, how to mark the plate so Guild could verify species and age.

I knew none of this.

Again.

Suri drank water.

Cerin looked at me.

"Silver?"

"Yes."

"Thought you'd do more magic."

"So did I."

He laughed.

No offense.

The second grazer took four hours.

Not because it was harder to kill.

Because hunting contained an unreasonable amount of not killing anything.

We waited beside a reed channel while sun moved west.

Cerin stopped trying to impress Suri and became tolerable.

Bo told us about a tanner who had tried to preserve a river wyrm hide with salt meant for pork and ruined enough money to buy a house.

Suri slept for eleven minutes sitting upright against a willow.

I checked the time twice.

No one cared.

Eventually she opened her eyes and said, "Female moved."

I heard nothing.

Of course.

We followed.

The white scar gave her away when she crossed shallow water.

Smaller than the male.

Faster.

She did not accept Suri's first approach.

Circled downwind.

Then came at Bo.

Not us.

The person carrying salt and rope.

Reasonable monster.

Bo dropped everything and ran.

Three steps.

His boot caught his own rope.

He went down.

Grazer lowered its head.

I had no wall.

No good footing.

No time for better terrain.

"Stay down!"

Bo stayed down.

I threw a plane over his back.

Angled.

Small enough to cast fast.

The grazer hit it with the front of the jaw plate.

The plane broke instantly.

Expected.

The break still sent force somewhere.

Up.

Not much.

Enough to lift the animal's head and one front leg.

Its belly passed over Bo instead of through him.

Cerin hit from right.

Suri's arrow struck behind the scarred foreleg.

I got there last.

Fine.

Sword under jaw when she turned.

Bad angle.

Second cut better.

The grazer fell into reeds and thrashed until Suri finished it.

Then nobody moved for a few breaths.

Bo rolled onto his back.

"Dead," he said.

"You?" I asked.

"Emotionally."

"Guild doesn't pay that claim."

"Then alive."

Cerin laughed.

Suri checked Bo herself before allowing the joke to stay funny.

No injury beyond mud and one scraped palm.

Good.

Bo stood and looked at the grazer.

"You broke magic on its face."

"Temporary use."

"Expensive?"

"Less than your chest."

"Strong pricing."

We tagged the second jaw plate.

Suri took one strip of tendon from behind the rear leg and handed it to me without explanation.

I looked at it.

"Why?"

"Bo says your leg has monster in it."

I looked at Bo.

He shrugged.

"Guild people talk."

Terrible profession.

"Different animal," I said.

Suri adjusted her bow.

"Then compare."

There it was.

Not legendary loot.

Gray tendon wrapped in cloth.

Probably useful.

Maybe trash.

Pera would complain either way.

We walked back after sunset with two jaw plates, one tendon sample, and no transformation of my professional identity.

Suri remained the reason we had found anything.

Bo remained the reason the bounty proof would not rot before morning.

Cerin had been a better mud fighter than I was.

I had turned one animal and kept another from opening Bo.

Enough.

At the road, Suri held out her hand.

I shook it.

"You staying Halden?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Good."

I stared.

"Why does everyone like that answer?"

"Means I don't need to schedule you."

Finally.

A practical reason.

She left toward the reed farms.

Cerin went toward the south barracks.

Bo carried the plates to the Guild weigh house.

Nobody asked when we would all work together again.

I started back toward Halden alone.

Mud dried on my trousers.

The field peg clicked once when the road became stone again.

Pera's washer resumed having a job.

Nice of the world to provide one eventually.
