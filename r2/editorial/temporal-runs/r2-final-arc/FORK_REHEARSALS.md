# R2 Final Arc Fork Rehearsals

Status: **SPECULATIVE / ZERO AUTHORITY**

These are decision rehearsals, not chapter drafts.

Current challenger: `The Storm Road`.

## Shared setup

A western storm-road convoy must cross an exposed cliff route during a short weather window.

The convoy carries a mix of high-value cargo and ordinary necessities. Losing cargo matters, but this is not a save-the-world shipment.

The crew is mixed by availability and rates rather than Greg's ideal composition.

Provisional role ownership:

- **convoy lead** owns go / stop / cargo abandonment decisions;
- **wind specialist** owns storm reading and charged-air behavior;
- **road / span specialist** owns the old suspended structures;
- **fighter / animal handler** owns immediate creature and pack-animal control;
- **Greg** owns tiny force routing, moving fixed points, short failure-path corrections, and support geometry.

At least one person is known to Greg, but the crew is not a reunion special.

The job repeatedly rewards Greg's narrow lane.

At the final exposed span, charged wind, cargo motion, animal panic, and damaged road hardware create too many simultaneous micro-failures for Greg's current reaction speed.

The convoy lead has a real safe option:

- cut loose / abandon a valuable rear cargo assembly;
- retreat behind the last stone shelter;
- wait for the weather window to pass;
- accept contract loss, salvage loss, and delayed delivery;
- keep people alive.

No one is forcing Greg to take the uglier option.

A setter's cuff or equivalent professional reinforcement tool is available because Greg encountered and deliberately acquired / rented / borrowed it earlier in the arc after learning the road's actual demands.

Its known tradeoff is not mysterious:

- repeated use makes one structural family faster and more reflexive;
- less-used families become harder to reach cleanly;
- the effect may persist after removal;
- exact permanence is uncertain, but the direction of cost is understood.

Greg is not tricked.

---

# Fork A — REFUSE / TAKE THE LOSS

## Decision

Greg looks at the cuff.

He does the arithmetic correctly.

The cargo can be abandoned.

The people do not need his body or magic to become the missing piece.

He tells the convoy lead to cut the rear assembly.

They do.

## Immediate result

The maneuver works.

A wagon / cargo cradle goes over the side or is deliberately left on the exposed span.

The crew retreats to shelter.

Nobody dies because Greg chose restraint.

The convoy loses meaningful money and possibly misses part of its delivery terms.

Greg's reputation does not collapse. Some people think he made the professional call.

## What this says about R2

Greg learned restraint.

He no longer spends himself simply because he can.

This is emotionally healthy.

It is also a weaker resolution to **R2 specifically**.

The story has spent too much time proving Greg's adaptability for the final movement to resolve by simply declining the strongest temptation.

This fork could work in 3L if the causal architecture earns a more mature endpoint.

In R2 it risks feeling like the author stepped in to correct Greg before his dangerous belief had fully paid off.

## Verdict

**REJECT AS PRIMARY R2 ENDING.**

Keep the safe option real in the final scene so Greg's later choice has moral / practical weight.

# Fork B — USE / MAKE IT WORK

## Decision

The convoy lead orders preparation to cut the rear cargo assembly.

Greg agrees that this is the safe answer.

Then he recognizes that the failures are not individually beyond him.

There are simply too many of them arriving too quickly.

That distinction matters to him.

He knows what the cuff does.

He knows the Bell Room history.

He knows Faultglass taught him that permanent cost can still leave useful capability behind.

He knows his broad barrier work is already merely respectable compared with true specialists.

He has increasingly built his professional value around one narrow family anyway.

The trade becomes legible:

> spend breadth he is not currently winning with in order to become faster at the thing that is saving people now.

Greg chooses the cuff.

Not because there is no other way.

Because he believes this way is worth the price.

## Immediate result

It works.

Not instantly and not cleanly.

The cuff does not create new power.

It collapses decision latency around one family of movements / anchors / transfers.

Greg stops having to consciously build each tiny correction from scratch.

He becomes fast enough to:

- hold one moving span point while the span specialist resets hardware;
- bleed cargo swing into a safer direction;
- keep an animal's panic from becoming a wagon rollover;
- give the wind specialist the fixed geometry needed for one strong intervention;
- keep the fighter from having to choose between monster control and catching falling cargo;
- route the final coupled failure somewhere the road can survive.

Other people still own the major actions.

Greg makes those actions available in time.

The convoy crosses.

The cargo mostly survives.

People get paid.

The employer is thrilled enough to make the choice socially rewarding too.

## Clean win requirement

Give Greg one moment after the crossing where the crew is simply impressed.

No immediate ominous nosebleed.

No whispered curse.

No instant punishment.

He did something difficult and valuable.

Let it feel good.

## Durable consequence

The cuff comes off.

Greg is not broken.

His narrow lane remains faster.

That is the problem.

Later, during a trivial broad task he could previously perform competently, he reaches for the old shape and it takes longer to arrive.

Not impossible.

Farther away.

He tests again.

Same result.

The specialization stuck more deeply than he expected.

His immediate reaction should not be horror.

It should be something closer to:

> Annoying.
>
> Manageable.

That is the R2 ending pressure.

He can rebuild around this too.

Of course he can.

## Social / professional consequence

The successful crossing may also change how institutions price Greg.

Instead of broad structural work, he begins receiving offers specifically for dangerous moving-load / failure-routing situations.

His own choice narrows the work the world wants from him at the same time his magic narrows.

Again: useful, lucrative, survivable.

Also a life shape.

## Mara consequence

Mara should hear the choice after the fact, not be placed on the road to approve or forbid it.

Her reaction should not be a morality speech.

She can understand why he chose it and still notice the pattern:

Greg increasingly treats future versions of himself as resources current Greg is allowed to spend.

She does not need to phrase it that neatly.

## Landing image possibilities

Strong candidates:

- Greg attempts a broad old shape, feels the delay, then uses the new narrow movement effortlessly to fix something tiny and mundane.
- Greg gets a highly specific lucrative contract offer and realizes the employer now knows exactly what kind of problem he is.
- Greg sees another dangerous object / difficult opportunity, prices it automatically, and catches himself not asking whether he wants the resulting life.
- Greg repairs / adjusts the prosthetic while a new job offer sits unopened nearby.

The final image should not answer whether he will make a different choice next time.

## Verdict

**STRONG SURVIVOR.**

This is the clearest current R2 ending because the choice is:

- informed;
- voluntary;
- useful;
- successful;
- costly;
- survivable;
- and therefore dangerous to Greg's worldview in exactly the way R2 has earned.

# Route A viability result

`The Storm Road` survives the fork test.

The setter's cuff works only if the arc first proves three things in scenes:

1. Greg's current reaction speed, not raw power, is the actual constraint;
2. the safe cargo-abandonment / retreat option is genuinely available;
3. Greg has enough direct evidence about the cuff's tradeoff that using it is an informed decision rather than artifact roulette.

If full prose fails to establish any of those, abandon the cuff ending rather than forcing it.
