# Candidate Chapter 119: Five Days

Mara came back after second bell carrying three folders and half a roll.

The roll had jam on it.

One folder did too.

I pointed.

"Institutional collapse."

She looked down at the red smear on the corner.

"That one is Northbank."

"Excellent. Eliminate it."

"No."

"Contaminated offer."

"Sit down, Greg."

I was already sitting.

The breakfast place had benches built for people with equal legs and strong opinions about personal space.

My walking foot fit under the table if I turned the knee slightly outward. Fine for ten minutes. Annoying after thirty.

We had been there forty-two.

I knew because the clock above the kitchen door lost almost a minute every quarter hour and I had spent enough time watching it to calculate the error.

Mara dropped into the seat across from me.

"You watched the clock the whole time?"

"No."

"Greg."

"There was also a man peeling potatoes."

"How were the potatoes?"

"Consistent."

She put the folders beside her plate.

Carrow.

Northbank.

Halden.

No labels on the covers.

I knew anyway.

She caught me looking.

"Five days."

"I remember."

"You have the face."

"This is my face."

"The planning face."

I had apparently accumulated categories.

Dangerous.

The server brought tea and another roll.

Mara tore it in half and gave me the side without jam.

"You don't like apricot," she said.

"I don't trust orange food."

"Carrots."

"Vegetable propaganda."

She smiled into her tea.

I had missed that.

Not her smile specifically.

Well.

That too.

But this.

Sitting across from someone while nothing required immediate competence.

No route clock.

No promise hanging two years ahead.

No hospital ceiling.

No artifact under a floorboard.

I should have enjoyed it more easily.

Instead my head kept trying to find the next fixed date.

Mara opened the Halden folder.

I looked away.

She noticed.

"You can look."

"You said five days."

"I said I had five days. I did not say you had to pretend the folders are invisible."

"That distinction was not clear."

"Nothing is clear to you unless someone writes terms."

Unfair.

Mostly.

She read for a while.

I ate.

Two tables over, a dockworker told a story about dropping a crate into the river and blamed the rope, the rain, municipal stone, and a dead uncle in that order.

The kitchen smelled like onions and hot iron.

Mara turned a page.

I looked at the Northbank folder again.

Three routes from Carrow.

Fastest six days if the river cooperated.

Eight to nine by road depending on season.

Winter worse.

Halden was easier from here, obviously. Carrow easier from home.

Northbank had the best salary based on what she had said last month.

Unless the housing allowance changed the real comparison.

I reached for my notebook.

Stopped.

Mara looked up.

"Were you about to make a table?"

"No."

"Your hand is on the notebook."

"Coincidence."

"Greg."

I moved my hand to the tea.

"I am respecting the process."

"You are vibrating at the process."

That seemed medically unlikely.

"Five days," I said.

"Five days."

She went back to reading.

I made it four minutes.

"Does Northbank still include housing?"

Mara lowered the folder slowly.

"You made the table already."

Fuck.

"A small table."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

"Before first bell?"

Silence had uses.

Mara stared.

"You made a comparison table before I finished the term."

"It was not persuasive."

"What does that mean?"

"No recommendation column."

She covered her face with one hand.

I drank tea.

The dockworker's dead uncle remained under review two tables away.

Finally Mara said, "Do you still have it?"

"Yes."

"Of course."

"Would deleting it improve the ethics?"

"No."

"Then resource conservation."

Her hand dropped.

She was trying not to laugh.

Good.

Maybe.

"I don't want the table," she said.

"Understood."

"And I don't want you pretending you don't care."

Less understood.

I looked at the folders.

"I care."

"I know."

"Carrow is what I want."

"I know."

"Northbank sounds professionally better."

"Maybe."

"Halden has strong glass work, better eastern routes, and apparently food with dangerous colors."

"Also maybe."

"You want me to stop."

"A little."

I nodded.

She reached across and tapped the notebook with one finger.

"You can keep the table."

"Generous."

"You cannot show it to me until I choose."

"Fine."

"And no new columns."

I hesitated.

Mara's eyes narrowed.

"Greg."

"What counts as new?"

She kicked my right shin under the table.

Not hard.

Important side selection.

I looked offended anyway.

The server returned with the bill.

I reached for it.

Mara reached faster.

"I have a job," she said.

"For five more days."

"Three offers."

"Potential money."

"You owe a warehouse collector three payments because you stole a magical lens."

I released the bill.

"Compelling argument."

"Thank you."

Outside, Halden had become properly morning.

Carts on the river street. Office shutters open. Ward lamps fading as daylight took over.

Mara tucked the folders under one arm.

"What are you doing today?"

I had no answer prepared.

That was rare enough that she noticed.

"Nothing?"

"I need the leg checked."

"That takes all day?"

"Depends how insulting the fitter becomes."

"After?"

I looked toward the uphill market streets.

No job.

No deadline.

No fixed route.

"Walk," I said.

Mara smiled.

"Terrifying."

She kissed my cheek and headed toward Vey.

I watched until she turned the corner.

Then I took out the notebook.

Not the table.

Different page.

**Harrow payment: 8 days.**

Below it:

**Work.**

There.

A perfectly normal problem.

Much better.

# Candidate Chapter 120: Dead Inventory

The fitter's name was Pera.

I had forgotten this during the first adjustment and called her Bera twice.

She had charged me extra.

I respected the system.

"Walk," she said.

I walked.

Six steps from the bench to the door.

Turn.

Six back.

"Again."

"You enjoy authority."

"Again."

I walked.

Pera crouched beside the left socket when I returned.

No ceremony.

Two fingers on the outer edge while I shifted weight.

"You're rolling out."

"Wet stone."

"Your stone isn't here."

"Habit."

"Bad habit."

She stood.

The shop smelled like leather, boiled glue, and whatever oil people used when they wanted wood to survive human sweat.

Three finished legs hung from pegs behind the counter.

A child-sized brace sat half-laced on the worktable.

I looked away from it before curiosity became staring.

Pera pulled my trouser cuff higher.

"Liner's holding. Socket edge isn't. I can widen the lateral cup."

"Cost?"

She named it.

"Robbery."

"You came back."

"Extortion through customer retention."

"Exactly."

She marked the leather with chalk.

"Two hours."

"What am I supposed to do for two hours?"

Pera looked toward the street.

"Halden exists."

Suspicious claim.

I left with the field peg on because she had my walking leg in pieces.

The peg made different noise on city stone.

Less heel.

More declaration.

People noticed sometimes.

Most did not.

Halden had river crews, glass workers, caravan guards, dock injuries, furnace burns, and enough veterans that one missing leg did not qualify as civic entertainment.

Good city.

I followed the slope toward the market.

Not because I needed anything.

This became false before the second block.

The Lantern Rows sat beneath a roof of old iron trusses strung with ward lamps even during the day.

Most were off.

A few blue-white globes remained lit over stalls selling delicate work.

Glass tubes.

Ward needles.

Copper inscription strips.

Monster teeth bundled by size.

A crate of black feathers labeled **NOT WYRM** in letters large enough to suggest recurring dispute.

I stopped there.

The vendor looked up.

"They're not."

"I didn't ask."

"You looked."

"What are they?"

"Marsh drake."

"Then why not write marsh drake?"

"People pay more while arguing."

Sound business.

I kept walking.

A table of military leftovers held buckles, cracked ward plates, three short blades without grips, and a brass cuff with six narrow bands nested inside each other.

I stopped.

Not because it was beautiful.

It wasn't.

Ugly little thing.

Dark brass rubbed bright on the inner edge. Two bands bent slightly out of round. Old maker's stamp near the hinge.

I knew the stamp.

Later.

Much later.

I had seen cuffs like this on structural corpsmen who could throw the same brace twenty times faster than anyone else and struggled when you asked them for anything imaginative.

Setter's cuffs.

Professional shortcut.

Professional trap.

I had hated them.

"How much?" I asked.

The vendor looked at the cuff.

Then me.

"You know what it is?"

"How much?"

He named a number.

I laughed.

Actually laughed.

"You can keep it."

"Rare piece."

"Obsolete piece."

"Still rare."

"So is plague."

He shrugged.

I picked up the cuff.

Heavy.

Heavier than it looked.

The nested bands shifted when I turned my wrist, each one carrying a shallow groove along the inside.

Mana guides.

Repeated pattern reinforcement.

I remembered a man named Corven holding a bridge throat against artillery for eleven minutes because his cuff had trained one load plane so deeply he could cast it while bleeding through both ears.

I also remembered Corven six years later unable to make a simple curved ward without tracing the geometry on paper first.

I put the cuff down.

"Does it work?"

The vendor's attention sharpened.

"You know what it is."

"I know what people said they did."

"Guild tested it last winter. Takes mana. Bands still answer."

"Documentation?"

"Costs extra."

"Of course it does."

He reached under the table and brought out a folded inspection card.

I did not touch it.

"Half your price."

"No."

"Then enjoy owning history."

I started walking.

"Three quarters."

I kept walking.

"Inspection included."

I stopped.

Fuck.

Not because I was buying it.

Because that was a better price.

Different problem.

I turned back.

"Two thirds."

"Three quarters."

"Two thirds and you stop pretending dead inventory is heritage."

The vendor smiled.

"You buying?"

I looked at the cuff.

Old Greg would have left already.

Not negotiated.

Not asked whether it still took mana.

Not looked at the grooves and wondered whether feathered planes counted as one family or several.

He had spent forty years keeping flexibility because flexibility had kept him alive.

Reasonable.

Very reasonable.

I had one leg in a repair shop and a second one under me made mostly of shaped wood.

Flexibility had acquired context.

"No," I said.

The vendor's smile disappeared.

"Then why waste my time?"

"Price discovery."

"Asshole."

Common law again.

I walked away.

Bought socks.

Actual socks.

Pera had complained about friction and good socks were cheaper than new skin.

On the way back I passed the cuff stall again.

Did not stop.

Looked.

Different.

Still counts.

Pera had my walking leg ready when I returned.

"Try."

I changed legs behind the screen.

Stood.

The widened cup caught lateral roll earlier.

Not dramatic.

Better.

I turned twice.

Pera watched.

"Well?"

"Acceptable."

"Highest praise?"

"Expensive praise."

I paid her.

Harrow in eight days.

Now less money.

A dead military cuff I was not buying.

A walking leg that turned better on wet stone.

Two pairs of socks.

Progress had poor branding.

# Candidate Chapter 121: Six Millimeters

The warehouse wall had moved six millimeters since spring.

The owner told me this as if six millimeters were personally insulting.

"It isn't supposed to move," he said.

"Everything moves."

"Walls shouldn't."

"Slowly is usually allowed."

He stared.

I looked at the crack running from the upper window corner toward the loading arch.

"This is not slowly enough."

Better.

The owner folded his arms.

We were getting somewhere.

Halden Guild had sent me because their structural desk had three larger jobs and apparently my file now included the useful phrase **municipal restriction removed**.

The owner had read Silver and expected robes.

I had arrived with chalk, short staff, a field notebook, and bread in my pocket.

Disappointment had shapes too.

"How long?" he asked.

"Inspection first."

"How long is inspection?"

"Depends what's wrong."

"How long to know what's wrong?"

I looked at him.

"Longer if you keep asking me how long."

He left.

Excellent client management.

The warehouse stored river glass in padded crates.

Heavy enough to matter.

Fragile enough that everyone walked carefully without admitting it.

I traced the wall from inside.

Crack at the window.

Mortar dust beneath the south pier.

Floor slightly low near the loading arch.

No obvious ward failure.

I crouched and pressed two fingers to the floor seam.

Nothing magical.

Cold stone.

Then I stood and looked at how the crates were stacked.

Three rows high along the inner wall.

New shelves bolted into old masonry.

There.

"Who installed those?"

The foreman looked up from a ledger.

"Shelves?"

"Yes."

"Winter."

"Who?"

"Brosan's crew. Why?"

I walked closer.

New iron anchors.

Old wall.

Heavy glass.

The shelves were not merely resting against the masonry. They were dragging it inward every time the floor settled under load.

Tiny movement.

Repeated.

Six millimeters eventually.

I formed a feathered plane beneath the first shelf run.

Low mana.

Wider at center.

Thin at edges.

The load changed.

Not much.

Enough.

The wall made a sound.

Small.

Stone rubbing stone.

Every worker in the room stopped.

I did too.

The owner reappeared immediately, summoned by fear at professional speed.

"What was that?"

"Useful."

"Useful sounds bad."

"Most useful sounds are terrible."

I moved to the second shelf.

Another feathered plane.

Then the third.

The trick from Faultglass was not strength.

It was refusing hard edges where load wanted transition.

Center carried.

Boundary surrendered gradually.

Daro's note had said the same thing with fewer stolen artifacts involved.

I adjusted the third plane.

The wall settled.

Dust fell from the crack.

Then stopped.

The owner looked horrified.

The foreman looked interested.

Better audience.

"You're pulling the shelves off the wall," he said.

"Temporarily."

"They'll fall."

"Not today."

"Comforting."

I liked him.

We spent the next three hours unloading the top crate rows, bracing the shelves physically, and moving anchor points into the floor where they belonged.

Actual work.

Sweat.

My socket started rubbing after the second hour because warehouse stone had a shallow slope I kept correcting against.

I ignored it for twenty minutes.

Then stopped because twenty-year-old pride had already cost me enough body parts.

I took the walking leg off in the foreman's office and checked the skin.

Red.

Not broken.

Good.

The foreman knocked once and entered before I answered.

He saw the leg on the chair.

Saw the stump.

Looked at my face.

"Need anything?"

"Five minutes."

"Owner thinks you're dying."

"Tell him six."

The foreman smiled.

"Cruel."

"Accurate."

He left.

I changed liner, put the leg back on, and finished the job.

By late afternoon the shelves carried through new floor braces.

I removed the feathered planes one at a time.

Nothing shifted.

No crack sound.

No drama.

The wall remained a wall.

The owner stared at it.

"That's it?"

"Would you prefer fire?"

"No."

"Then yes."

He paid the Guild rate plus materials.

My share came in a small envelope.

I counted twice outside.

Harrow payment covered.

Socket widening covered.

Socks recovered through heroic financial performance.

Money left.

I stood on the warehouse steps while evening traffic moved toward the river.

A Guild runner found me before I reached the corner.

Young boy.

Out of breath.

"Greg?"

"Depends."

He checked the slip.

"Greg. Silver structural. Carrow file."

"Unfortunately."

"West quarry wants a specialist."

"Tomorrow?"

"Now."

I looked back at the warehouse.

Of course.

"What happened?"

The runner swallowed.

"They opened something."

"What?"

"Don't know. Hollow under the limestone. Three men missing. Guild says old structure."

Interesting.

Bad word.

I looked toward Pera's shop.

Field peg at the inn.

Sword there too.

Mara probably still at Vey.

Harrow payment in my pocket.

Perfectly normal problem had lasted almost seven hours.

Respectable.

"Tell them I need forty minutes," I said.

"They said twenty."

"Then tell them thirty and look frightened."

He did already.

Useful.

# Candidate Chapter 122: Quarry Bell

The quarry foreman had blood on his sleeve that was not his.

I noticed this before the hole.

Old habit.

People first when danger had already happened.

The hole came second.

Large enough for a wagon if the wagon had terrible judgment.

Fresh limestone break around the edge. Dust still hanging low where evening air moved through the cut.

Lanterns had been set in a half-circle twenty feet back.

Nobody stood closer.

Good sign.

Or very bad one.

The foreman pointed.

"Three went in."

"Why?"

His jaw tightened.

Wrong question emotionally.

Correct question professionally.

"Heard shouting after the blast. Thought someone was trapped."

"Were they?"

"No."

"What were they shouting at?"

"Nothing when we got there."

That answer improved nothing.

A Halden Guild woman stood beside a folding field table covered in maps.

Gray coat. Bronze clasp. Ink on both hands.

"Tessa Mar," she said. "Works desk."

"Greg."

"I know."

Concerning trend.

She pushed a quarry plan toward me.

The blasted face should have been solid limestone for another forty feet.

It was not.

"Old survey?" I asked.

"Three surveys. Same answer."

"Magic?"

"Ward reader says yes. Then says no. Then asked for more money."

"Experienced."

Tessa almost smiled.

"We have a deeper team coming from Red Quay. Gold lead. Until then I need to know whether this face stays up."

"And the missing men?"

"Search team won't enter until Red Quay arrives."

Good.

Someone had learned patience before I got here.

I traded the walking leg for the field peg behind a supply wagon.

Less elegant.

Better grip in broken stone.

Sword belt on.

Short staff in hand.

No heroism.

Preparation.

The foreman watched the change.

"You going in?"

"To the threshold."

"Guild said nobody in."

Tessa answered before I did.

"Threshold is not in."

I liked her immediately.

The blast cut sloped toward the opening.

Loose chips shifted under the peg.

Right boot.

Peg.

Staff.

Three points when needed.

The air coming from the hole was colder than evening.

Wet too.

Not cave wet.

Stone-room wet.

I stopped six feet from the break.

Old masonry behind the limestone.

Not natural.

Blocks dark with age. Narrow vertical seams. A curved rib disappearing upward into the unbroken rock.

Built first.

Buried later.

Interesting remained a bad word.

I formed a thin plane beneath the nearest cracked lip.

Feathered.

Low load.

The structure answered strangely.

Not resistance.

Echo.

Mana moved through my plane and came back with a second pressure half a breath later.

I dropped the cast.

Tessa called from behind me.

"Problem?"

"Maybe."

"Useful maybe or expensive maybe?"

"Those are usually the same."

I tried again from a different angle.

Plane under stone.

Pressure.

Echo.

Same delay.

Something inside the buried structure was carrying load farther than the visible wall.

A connected frame.

Large.

Very large.

I stepped left.

The field peg found a shallow groove in the stone.

Not quarry work.

Old floor.

The blast had exposed an entrance.

Behind me, someone whispered.

"Bell."

I heard nothing.

Then the stone under my staff vibrated.

Once.

Low enough that sound arrived through bone before air.

The quarry went silent.

A second vibration followed.

Not loud.

Deep.

Somewhere inside the hill, something rang.

Tessa said, very calmly, "That happened after the blast too."

"How many times?"

"One."

"This makes three?"

"Two."

I looked back.

"You said after the blast."

"That was the first. This is the second."

Right.

My head had counted the two pulses as separate events.

Good correction.

Present first.

I lowered the staff.

"Face will hold tonight if nobody blasts again."

The foreman exhaled.

"And tomorrow?"

"Ask tomorrow."

He hated that.

Reality did too.

Lanterns appeared on the quarry road above us.

Five riders.

No banners.

No dramatic formation.

They came down at working pace, dismounted before the loose grade, and started unloading gear before introductions.

Gold-caliber party.

You could tell because nobody looked at the hole first.

One checked the injured workers.

One checked the quarry map.

One checked the sky.

One walked the perimeter without crossing Tessa's lantern line.

The last person came toward me.

Woman in a dark padded coat with a shield strapped flat across her back.

Gray at the temples.

Not old.

Experienced enough that the distinction mattered.

Her eyes went to my field peg.

Then staff.

Then the plane marks I had chalked beside the entrance.

"Structural?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Guild?"

"Silver."

She looked at the hole.

"Good. We need one."

Not **we could use one**.

Need.

I should have asked the rate first.

Instead I looked into the dark where the bell had moved through stone.

First-life Greg had spent decades learning which holes not to enter.

Useful knowledge.

Very useful.

I said, "What's the contract?"

Close enough.
