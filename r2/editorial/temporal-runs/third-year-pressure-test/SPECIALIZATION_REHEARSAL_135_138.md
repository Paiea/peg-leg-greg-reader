# Candidate Chapter 135: Northbound

Mara's boat left at third bell.

We arrived at first.

This was my fault.

"We could have slept," she said.

"Boats leave early."

"This one leaves at third bell."

"Schedules lie."

"The boat is physically tied to the dock."

"For now."

Mara stared at me over the top of one trunk.

The dock porter tried not to smile.

Failed.

I disliked him immediately.

Halden's north quay was already awake anyway.

Fish carts.

River barges.

Two passenger boats taking on crates beneath painted route boards.

Mara's went east first to Mill Junction, then she would transfer north by road.

Six days normal.

Five if everything behaved.

Seven if the world remembered itself.

I had checked.

Obviously.

The trunks were tagged correctly despite Mara's packing philosophy.

One large.

One medium.

One small crate of books that had been arranged by size under protest.

Her desk papers rode in a waxed case she had packed herself after refusing my proposed indexing system.

The mug remained inside the boot.

I had lost that war.

We stood beside the boarding line with nothing left to fix.

Terrible condition.

Mara looked at my walking leg.

"You sure the field peg isn't better for docks?"

"No."

"Pera said wet stone."

"Pera says many hostile things."

"You're rolling less."

"Yes."

She noticed.

Good.

Bad.

Both.

A porter took the book crate.

I watched him carry it wrong.

Mara caught my sleeve.

"No."

"He's tilting it."

"Books survive angles."

"Some don't."

"Greg."

I let the man destroy literature in peace.

Second bell.

Still too early.

We bought coffee from a dock cart.

Mara's had sugar.

Mine did not because dock coffee needed consequences, not disguise.

We sat on a low wall facing the river.

My knee fit badly against the stone.

I moved once.

Then again.

Mara said nothing.

Good.

She had stopped treating adjustments like emergencies months ago.

The river carried morning light in pieces.

"So," she said.

Bad word.

"So."

"You're going back Carrow?"

"Eventually."

"That means no."

"It means eventually."

"Greg."

I looked at the boat.

Red Quay had another contract in three days.

Veya had lost the mill job to another pair after I said no.

I had one emergency fire payment, two quarry payments, one canal payment, and enough money that Harrow no longer occupied every meal.

Halden had work.

Carrow had home.

Northbank had Mara.

"I don't know," I said.

Again.

Still ugly.

Mara drank coffee.

"Good."

"Everyone says that when I hate the answer."

"Maybe you need more answers you hate."

"Unkind before travel."

"Efficient."

Third bell approached.

Boarding started.

People became motion.

Families hugged.

Porters shouted numbers.

A child cried because his wooden horse had been packed into the wrong trunk and civilization nearly ended.

Mara stood.

I stood slower.

No drama.

Body mechanics remained body mechanics even when someone important left.

She looked at me.

"I'm not asking you to promise a date."

There.

The old version of us would have built one.

Probably down to bell.

"I know."

"I'm serious."

"I know."

"Write."

"Obviously."

"Visit when you can."

"Yes."

"Not when the route math becomes optimal."

I frowned.

"Those can overlap."

"Greg."

"Fine."

She smiled.

Then she hugged me hard enough that the walking foot slid half an inch on damp stone.

I corrected.

She felt it.

Did not release.

Neither did I.

For a few breaths I could smell her hair, coffee, river, wool coat.

Normal things.

Then she stepped back.

Kissed me.

"Don't steal anything."

"Broad restriction."

"Goodbye, Greg."

"Goodbye, Mara."

She boarded.

I did not follow her to the rail like a tragic painting.

I moved out of the porters' way because one had a crate and no patience.

Mara found a place near the stern.

Waved once.

I waved back.

Rope came off the bollard.

The boat pushed away.

No bell from under a hill.

No monster.

No deadline completed.

Just distance beginning.

I watched until the boat became another shape in river traffic.

Then I stood there another minute because leaving immediately felt performative.

Afterward I bought breakfast.

Two eggs.

Bread.

Too much money for riverfront food.

I ate alone.

That hurt less than expected.

Then more.

Then differently.

By fifth bell I was at the Guild.

Not because work solved departure.

Because fifth bell still existed.

At the board, three contracts had been pinned since yesterday.

Warehouse escort.

River pest bounty.

Training-yard temporary instructor.

I read all three.

Did not take any.

Someone behind me said, "You busy tomorrow?"

Korr.

Of course.

He held a paper with a red bounty stamp.

"Depends."

He handed it over.

**LOW MARSH GRAZER KILL / VERIFIED PREDATION / TWO HEAD MINIMUM / LOCAL HUNTER LEAD REQUIRED.**

I looked at him.

"Grazer?"

"Big."

"That's not taxonomy."

"Has teeth."

"Worse."

"Renn doesn't want it. Says mud isn't a building."

"Strong argument."

"Veya said no fixed points. Mett said marsh sounds disgusting. Asha asked what bites do."

"And?"

"I said bite."

"Helpful."

Korr grinned.

"Hunter says four people. I hit. You sword. Hunter hunts. Fourth carries proof."

"Why me?"

"You don't panic."

Not specialization.

Not structural.

Not clever.

Just a person asking another person.

I looked at the bounty again.

Mara's boat was already hours downriver.

Tomorrow existed.

"Fine," I said.

Korr punched my shoulder lightly.

"Party."

"Temporary labor arrangement."

"Party."

"Bounty crew."

"Party."

I walked away.

He followed.

"Party."

I regretted everything.

# Candidate Chapter 136: Mud

The marsh did not care about geometry.

I found this refreshing for almost ten minutes.

Then I hated it.

Low Marsh began two miles east of Halden where river floodplain widened into reeds, mud islands, shallow water, and insects with criminal ambition.

The local hunter was **Suri Pell**.

No relation to anyone useful.

She wore knee-high marsh boots, a short recurved bow, three knives, and the expression of a person who had already decided city adventurers were expensive children.

Korr introduced himself.

She looked at his hammer.

"No."

He blinked.

"No what?"

"Full hit. Ground soft. You bury feet, spook herd, maybe lose hammer."

Korr looked personally attacked by soil.

I liked Suri immediately.

The fourth member was a tanner named Bo who carried rope, salt sacks, and proof tags because the bounty paid extra for intact jaw plates.

He had no rank.

He also knew more about grazer anatomy than the three of us combined.

Again.

Specialists everywhere once I stopped expecting tabs.

The animals were called mud grazers despite eating fish, roots, carrion, small livestock, and apparently one fisherman who had ignored warning stakes.

Six-legged.

Low bodies.

Broad heads with backward-facing jaw plates used to root through mud.

Largest maybe eight hundred pounds.

Not monsters in the legendary sense.

Monsters in the livestock-insurance sense.

Two had begun hunting near farms after flood season.

Our job was kill those two, not empty the marsh because we had weapons.

Suri made that clear.

"You shoot?" she asked me.

"Badly."

"Magic?"

"Force. Mostly structure and position."

She looked around at miles of wet reeds with no walls, beams, arches, gates, or convenient masonry.

"So sword."

"Apparently."

This felt insulting.

Good.

We walked single file.

Field peg sank less than the walking foot would have but more than I wanted.

Monster washer irrelevant.

Mud absorbed impact better than expensive dead crawler.

Pera would enjoy that.

Suri stopped twice because birds changed direction.

I noticed birds only after she stopped.

Third time she crouched and touched water.

"Fresh."

I looked.

Water.

Mud.

Something smelled terrible.

"What?"

"Oil."

She rubbed fingers together.

"Grazer skin. Big male. Twenty minutes maybe."

Could Old Greg have known that?

Maybe with campaign training.

Current Greg did not.

I accepted ignorance without attempting to become a marsh hunter through concentration.

Growth.

We found the first grazer at noon.

Not because I saw it.

Suri did.

She pointed to reeds thirty yards ahead.

Nothing.

Then the reeds moved against wind.

There.

Broad gray back.

Korr whispered, "I can hit."

Suri whispered, "You can wait."

He waited.

She placed Bo on a dry hummock with rope.

Placed me ten yards left along a shallow bank.

"If it runs your way, turn it."

"How?"

"Sword. Shout. Magic. Be ugly."

Technical.

"Don't stop it?"

"No."

There.

Important.

Eight hundred pounds on mud.

Stopping would be stupid.

Turning I understood.

Maybe the marsh cared about geometry a little.

Suri moved right alone.

No magic.

No dramatic stealth.

Just placing feet where water did not complain.

She disappeared behind reeds.

Thirty breaths later an arrow struck the grazer behind the right foreleg.

It screamed.

Not animal sound I expected.

Higher.

Ugly.

It charged left.

Toward me.

Good.

Bad.

Both.

I planted field peg and immediately reconsidered the concept of planting anything in mud.

The peg sank two inches.

No fixed point.

No good wall.

No stone to redirect through.

Just animal.

Me.

Open ground.

I formed a plane near its left eye.

Small.

Not to stop.

Annoy.

The grazer hit invisible resistance with one side of its face and veered.

Only a little.

Enough.

I shouted and stepped right, sword out.

It chose open left.

Toward Korr.

Korr had waited exactly as instructed.

Now he moved.

One hammer strike.

Not full.

Side of skull behind the jaw plate.

The sound traveled through wet ground badly.

Grazer stumbled but stayed up.

Korr's feet sank to his ankles.

Suri had been right.

He swore.

Grazer turned on him.

My specialization had no elegant answer.

So I ran badly through mud and used a sword.

Current Greg could still do that.

I came in behind shoulder.

Cut hamstring on rear left leg.

Not heroic.

Messy.

Blade dragged through hide and muscle.

Grazer collapsed one side.

Korr got clear.

Suri put second arrow into the soft mouth as it turned.

Then third under jaw plate.

Animal stopped.

Four people breathing.

Mud everywhere.

No clever architecture.

Suri walked over and looked at my sword.

"Good cut."

"Ugly."

"Dead."

Fair.

Korr pulled one boot out of the marsh with a wet sound.

His sock remained in mud.

We all stared.

He looked down.

"Problem."

Bo laughed hard enough to sit.

I did too.

This was the first monster kill in weeks where my most valuable contribution might have been a hamstring and emotional support around lost footwear.

It felt good.

Not everything needed my new thing.

The second grazer was worse.

Female.

Smaller.

Smarter maybe.

She circled us through reeds and refused Suri's first bait line.

We waited two hours.

Actual hunting.

Nothing happened.

Korr became unbearable after forty minutes.

Suri threatened to use him as bait.

He became quiet for six minutes.

At last the grazer rushed Bo instead of any armed person because nature respected economics.

Bo dropped the proof sack and ran exactly three steps before tripping over rope.

I did not have structure.

I had people.

"Bo, stay down!"

Blunt command.

Correct this time.

He stayed down.

I put one plane above his back, angled.

The grazer's jaw plate struck it.

Plane shattered immediately.

But the animal lifted half a foot.

Enough to pass over Bo instead of through him.

Suri's arrow hit flank.

Korr charged from side and shoulder-checked rather than hammering because his feet still hated mud.

The grazer rolled.

I cut throat when opening appeared.

Then everything became blood and reeds.

Asha would have complained.

Bo lay in mud staring at sky.

"Alive?" I asked.

"No."

"Bad answer."

"Then yes."

Good.

We sat afterward while Suri and Bo handled the jaw plates because I knew nothing useful about preserving bounty proof.

Korr found his sock.

Nobody celebrated.

It was worse now.

At sunset we carried two tagged plates back toward road.

Suri walked beside me.

"You said structure."

"Yes."

"Didn't see much."

"Marsh rude."

She nodded.

"Still useful."

No Gold praise.

No revelation.

Just enough.

"You work Halden often?" I asked.

"Season. Then south reed farms."

"Bounties?"

"Whatever pays and doesn't make me sit indoors."

"Korr will ask you again."

"No."

Korr, ahead of us, shouted, "Yes!"

Suri sighed.

I smiled.

Not party.

A hunter.

A hammer idiot.

A tanner.

Me, mostly with sword because mud had no respect for professional identity.

Still worked.

Maybe that was part of it too.

Specialization did not mean demanding the world provide your favorite problem.

Sometimes you were just the second-best blade in a marsh.

And somebody else knew where the monster was.

# Candidate Chapter 137: Pins

Veya practiced with doors.

This explained several things about her personality.

Two days after the marsh bounty, I found her behind a rented warehouse near Red Quay's lodging with six old doors propped against a wall.

Not good doors.

Training doors.

Already scarred by dozens of straight white cuts.

She had sent a note to my inn.

Three words:

**Bring your planes.**

No rate.

Suspicious.

"This is work?" I asked.

"No."

"Then why am I here?"

"You want better."

Annoying because true.

She set two copper rods into opposite edges of the first door.

"Fixed point from canal. Do again."

"On door?"

"Door moves."

"That's the problem."

"Yes."

There.

Training.

Not artifact.

Not monster loot.

A thing I was bad at becoming less bad through repetition.

I put one small plane around the upper rod.

Held.

Veya pushed the door with her boot.

My plane moved with the surface badly.

Rod shifted.

"Dead," she said.

"You didn't cut."

"Would have cut wrong. Dead imaginary person."

"Helpful."

Again.

Smaller plane.

Instead of holding the rod in space, I held relationship between rod and the door edge.

Different thought.

The door moved.

Plane moved with it.

Better.

Veya kicked harder.

Rod twisted.

"Dead."

Again.

By the twelfth attempt my shoulders hurt from tiny casts more than they had from the warehouse fire.

Humiliating.

The problem was not power.

Every instinct wanted more power because more power felt like certainty.

But more power made the plane stiff.

Stiff broke when the door moved.

Feathering helped.

Not feathering around the rod exactly.

Feathering **permission**.

Hold this direction.

Allow that one.

Stop rotation.

Allow translation.

The words were bad.

The feeling improved.

On attempt nineteen Veya kicked the door hard enough to make the lower hinge jump.

Upper rod stayed true.

She cut.

White line crossed the wood.

Perfect.

Door fell into two pieces.

I stared.

"Wasteful."

"Training door."

"Still door."

"You sixty?"

I looked at her.

"What?"

"You complain like old landlord."

Dangerous coincidence.

"Professional respect for lumber."

"Again."

We moved to second door.

This one hung from rope instead of hinges.

Worse.

Veya set rods.

"Why not practice your own fixed points?" I asked.

"I do."

"Without me."

"Yes."

"Then this is for me."

"Mostly."

There.

People teaching without becoming mentors.

I liked that more than I wanted.

"Why?"

Veya shrugged.

"You make my technique better. I want you less annoying."

Clear incentive.

Beautiful.

We trained until sunset.

No level.

No breakthrough light.

No ancient artifact noticing my destiny.

I held moving points seventeen times correctly out of forty-three.

Veya called that bad.

I agreed.

Then she showed me her own failure.

She placed rods on a curved barrel hoop.

Tried to establish line.

It warped immediately.

Again.

Warped.

Third time the white line snapped sideways and cut a shallow groove in the dirt.

"Curves," she said.

Pure hatred.

"You can't?"

"Can fake with many short lines. Slow. Expensive. Ugly."

"So don't."

She looked at me.

"Exactly."

That landed.

Not overcome every weakness.

Build around some.

Old Greg had known that professionally and forgotten it personally more than once.

We packed the rods.

I counted twelve.

Then checked my bag.

Two more.

"Fourteen."

Veya held out her hand.

I gave her one.

She closed fingers.

Waited.

"Keep other."

"Why?"

"You're useful with it."

Not sentimental.

Maybe better.

I put the rod back in my bag.

A small piece of somebody else's specialization.

Not mine.

That distinction mattered.

At the alley mouth Korr waited with three skewers of grilled eel.

"Training done?"

"Yes."

He handed us food.

"Suri says no."

"To what?" Veya asked.

"Another hunt."

"Good."

"She means maybe."

"She said no?"

"Yes."

"Then no."

Korr looked betrayed by language.

I ate eel.

Veya did too.

For five minutes nobody discussed contracts.

That may have been the most advanced team exercise of the week.
