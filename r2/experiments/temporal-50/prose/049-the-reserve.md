# Chapter 49: The Reserve

Status: **TEMPORAL 50 NOW CANDIDATE / NOT ACCEPTED STORY AUTHORITY**

The service reserve had been more pleasant when it was imaginary.

Ivena had called it prudent.

Arlo had called it money we were not using.

I had voted for it because both descriptions were true and one of them sounded adult.

Now a cracked chamber sat on the bench between us.

The reserve had become expensive clay.

"Again," I said.

Dena leaned against the wash trough with both sleeves rolled above the elbow.

"North dye house sent the boy before breakfast. Their outside line stopped. I took the clean matched spare, swapped it, checked flow, brought this one back."

She pointed at the chamber.

"They're running."

There.

Customer problem already solved.

Nobody had waited for the third owner to arrive and become important.

Good business.

I picked up the cracked chamber.

Not heavy.

Heavier than nine copper had felt when the first distribution reached my hand.

The split began beneath the brass collar and ran down one side perhaps four inches.

Clean fracture at the top.

Rougher lower down.

White mineral line inside the crack.

I looked at Arlo.

"Bell Tile?"

He looked offended.

"Yes."

That answer pleased nobody.

The borrowed kiln had produced cleaner chambers than his own.

Apparently better did not mean immortal.

Useful correction.

Ivena had a page.

Of course.

"Installed twelve days," she said. "First swap after six. Cleaned once. No prior leak."

"Outside line?"

Dena nodded.

"Wash shed. Roof, three walls, open south side."

Deepwinter.

Excellent architecture.

"They drain overnight?"

Dena made a face.

Not yes.

Not no.

Customer evidence.

"Foreman says they do."

"You believe him?"

"I believe he thinks they do."

Better.

Arlo turned the chamber in my hands.

He had already marked two places with chalk.

One at the collar.

One lower.

"See here?"

I did.

Tiny dark line around the inside wall.

Not a crack.

Residue.

"Water sat below that."

"Maybe," I said.

"Water sat below that."

More confident.

Ivena looked at him.

"Can you prove it was full when it froze?"

"No."

"Can you prove the collar didn't stress the body?"

Arlo's mouth tightened.

"I fitted it."

"That was not my question."

Ownership remained healthy.

I ran one thumb along the brass collar.

No visible distortion.

The fit looked clean.

Later Greg knew what frost could do to ceramic bodies carrying trapped water.

Later Greg also knew what over-tight collars could do.

Later Greg knew three different seal compounds that would make this entire fitting method look unnecessarily personal.

Current chamber remained cracked.

Current customer remained running because Dena had already done her job.

That simplified the important question.

Not cause.

Money.

"What did we promise them?" I asked.

Ivena looked at Dena.

Dena looked at Arlo.

Arlo looked at me.

Excellent documentation culture.

"Matched spare on service," Arlo said.

"Meaning?"

"If theirs comes out, clean one goes in."

"Dirty or broken?"

He hesitated.

There.

"We didn't separate it."

Ivena folded both arms.

"We do now."

Reasonable.

I put the chamber down.

"What did the dye house hear?"

Dena answered.

"If the chamber stops working, send word. We swap it."

"Who said that?"

"Me."

Not defensive.

Just ownership.

"Because that's what we do."

Also true.

Ivena looked at the cracked chamber again.

"A clean spare is not a free replacement body."

"Customer doesn't know that," Dena said.

Irena's eyes narrowed.

I corrected the name in my head before it escaped.

Ivena.

Current people deserved current spelling.

"They will," Ivena said.

Arlo tapped the crack.

"If they froze it full, why are we buying their mistake?"

Good question.

The old Greg answer arrived immediately.

Warranty exclusions.

Freeze damage.

Improper drainage.

Customer-caused failure.

Service terms.

The sort of language businesses learned after losing money in exactly this shape.

I could write it now.

Probably better than current people would.

That did not mean we had sold it yesterday.

"Because we didn't tell them it was their mistake," I said.

Arlo frowned.

"We didn't know they could make this mistake."

"Exactly."

Ivena looked at me.

Not agreement yet.

"You want the reserve to pay."

I looked at the page beside her.

Reserve amount.

Replacement bodies.

Bell Tile shelf cost.

Cleaning supplies.

Dena's route pay.

Numbers that had been much friendlier when they lived in separate columns.

"For this one," I said.

Arlo exhaled.

Not happy.

"Even if they froze it."

"Even if they froze it."

"Why?"

"Because Dena told them we swap it when it stops working. We did not say, unless you failed a drainage procedure we never gave you."

Dena looked at the ceiling.

Possibly avoiding responsibility.

Possibly hiding a smile.

"That sounds expensive," Arlo said.

"It is cheaper than promising something and discovering afterward what we meant."

Ivena's expression changed a fraction.

There.

Not future knowledge.

Current obligation.

She reached for a second page.

Of course there was a second page.

"Replacement body, collar inspection, firing shelf share, Dena's extra run."

She named the total.

Not ruinous.

Large enough that the first nine-copper distribution stopped feeling representative of ownership.

"That's from reserve?" I asked.

"That is what reserve means."

I disliked definition through consequence.

"And after this?"

"Reserve still exists. Smaller."

Arlo pointed at the chamber.

"I want to cut it open."

I stared at the existing four-inch crack.

"It has begun helping."

"Full section. Collar seat too. If the freeze started inside, fracture should tell us something."

Ivena nodded.

"Do that before we blame anything."

Good.

Not customer.

Not firing.

Not fitting.

Evidence first.

Dena pushed away from the trough.

"What do I tell them?"

This was the part businesses eventually hid inside printed cards.

Current business still had Dena.

Better in some ways.

Worse for Dena.

"Tell them this replacement is ours," I said.

Ivena lifted one finger.

"The body. Service swap was already theirs."

"Tell them the body is ours."

Dena nodded.

"Then?"

I looked at the broken chamber.

"Then ask them how they drain the outside line. Actually watch it next service."

Arlo added, "And tell them not to leave water in the chamber overnight below hard freeze."

Dena looked at him.

"How much water?"

He opened his mouth.

Stopped.

Good question.

"None," he said finally.

"Easy instruction."

Ivena was already writing.

"All outside installs get winter handling. Drain before night freeze. If full drain is impossible, bring chamber into heated storage during hard freeze."

Arlo made a face.

"Some are plumbed fixed."

"Then we need an instruction that works."

There.

Not solved.

The page stopped.

Good page.

Dena rubbed one wet hand against her apron.

"North dye house can pull theirs. Collar clamp is hand release."

"So tell them that one," Ivena said. "Do not tell every customer until we know what every install can actually do."

I smiled.

She looked at me.

"What?"

"You are resisting universal policy."

"I resist paying twice for stupidity."

Still Ivena.

Not all of them.

Enough.

Tannery units lived mostly indoors.

A small brewer had a service chamber beside an unheated cistern room that became the second concern.

Ivena checked the ledger anyway.

Apparently a filtration business eventually became plumbing through insult.

No single instruction fit all three.

Good.

Drain point below chamber.

Vent above.

Isolate feed.

Open return.

Current collar did not.

I could describe it.

Maybe Arlo could make it.

Dangerous thought.

One problem.

Current evidence.

Not suspicious.

Then stopped.

"I am not handing out ceramic gifts because winter exists."

"Good."

"You helping cut it?"

Then at the saw.

"Owner role unclear."

"Hold it."

Apparently clear enough.

Messy work.

Ceramic dust.

Not everything.

"Water got here."
