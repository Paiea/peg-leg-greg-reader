# Chapter 51: The Traveler

Status: **TEMPORAL 50 NOW CANDIDATE / NOT ACCEPTED STORY AUTHORITY**

The road beyond Wicker Ford was not where I remembered leaving it.

This was technically unfair.

Roads had several decades to become different when nobody was looking.

Marta disagreed with my philosophical position by pulling the cart north at exactly the pace Orrin asked.

Good horse.

We had left Carrow before first light.

North Gate passed us through the eastern lane under three lanterns and more inspection than our cargo deserved.

Current repair crews had added another timber brace since the reopening.

A city worker tapped one wheel width against a measuring stick.

Orrin handed over the manifest.

I showed Bronze.

Nobody asked whether I remembered the gate collapsing later.

Healthy institution.

We cleared the wall before sunrise.

Cold became larger outside Carrow.

Inside the city, winter had corners.

Walls.

Cook fires.

Alleys where wind lost arguments.

North road had no interest in sheltering opinion.

By second bell my nose hurt.

By third, my left foot had become a separate political body demanding warmer socks.

I had warmer socks.

Preparedness won quietly.

Wicker Ford arrived around midday.

Same bridge.

Same apothecary storehouse.

Different clerk.

We stopped only long enough for Orrin to deliver one small package and let Marta drink.

I bought hot broth because future knowledge had not made cold less cold.

Then we continued north.

There.

New Current Greg geography.

The road narrowed after Wicker Ford.

Not dangerously.

Economically.

Less cart traffic meant fewer reasons to spend public money making mud feel respected.

Two packed wheel lines.

Frozen shoulder.

Stone markers farther apart.

Farm roads leaving at angles I did not know.

Later memory supplied names without surfaces.

Stonecross.

Old Fenn.

One hill where a courier station would later stand.

A raised causeway through the eastern marsh.

Except the causeway was not there.

I saw that before Orrin did because I had been looking for the absence.

East of the road, low winter reeds spread across flat wet ground.

Black water between them.

Ice along the edges.

Two narrow farm tracks entered the marsh and stopped being visible after perhaps a hundred yards.

Later causeway would cut almost straight through it.

Current marsh had not received the memo.

"What?" Orrin asked.

Face again.

"Road changes later."

He looked east.

"There?"

"Eventually."

"That's a marsh."

"Strong current observation."

He looked at me.

Then back at the road.

"We're using this one."

Correct driver.

Old Fenn crossing announced itself with stopped carts.

Bad sign.

Six of them lined the north approach before the mile house came into view.

Two freight wagons.

One empty timber cart.

A covered passenger coach whose horses looked personally betrayed.

One farm cart stacked with baskets under canvas.

And a narrow courier wagon parked so neatly it had probably arrived first.

A red board hung across the road.

**CART CROSSING CLOSED.**

Below that in smaller chalk:

**FOOT / LED ANIMAL BY KEEPER CLEARANCE ONLY.**

Orrin swore once.

Not angry.

Accounting.

"Formal enough for Sella?" I asked.

He pointed at the road keeper standing beside it.

"Ask him to sign your feelings."

Good.

We joined the line.

The crossing itself lay another fifty yards ahead.

Old Fenn was not a dramatic bridge.

Low stone arches over a broad slow channel where marsh water narrowed between two firmer banks.

Parapet barely knee high.

Road surface stone packed with grit.

Normally easy.

Today the north half shone.

Not snow.

Ice.

Clear enough to see dark stone underneath.

One freight wagon sat crooked just beyond the first arch with three men around its left rear wheel.

No one injured.

Wheel had slid against the parapet and stopped.

Useful wall.

A road crew worked around it.

Four people.

Two with shovels and baskets of dark grit.

One with a pry bar.

One woman kneeling on the bridge with both palms against the stone.

I looked longer than politeness required.

No visible construct.

No plane.

No light at first.

Then a thin dark line appeared through the frost around her hands.

Not melting outward quickly.

Something subtler.

The white surface loosened from the stone in a strip perhaps two feet wide.

One shovel worker scraped it up in sheets.

Interesting.

I had seen road heating later.

Copper bed lines.

Charged grit.

Expensive municipal systems in wealthier districts.

This was not that.

The woman moved three feet.

Hands down again.

Another strip released.

I did not know the method.

Excellent.

The keeper came down the line collecting names and destinations.

Heavy coat.

Red scarf.

Small road-office badge pinned to the chest.

When he reached us, Orrin handed him the manifest.

"Stonecross. Medicine."

The keeper read it.

Looked at the cart.

"No carts until I clear."

"Estimate?"

"After noon if surface comes clean and stone stays dry. Tomorrow if seep keeps freezing."

Orrin looked at the sky.

Grey.

No active snow.

"Mile house?"

"Floor only unless somebody dies in a bed."

Reassuring hospitality.

The keeper looked at me.

"Escort?"

I showed Bronze.

He wrote it beside Orrin's manifest number.

"Closure mark at the house. Guild delay?"

"Yes."

"They'll stamp it."

There.

Contract weather became paper.

I looked at the bridge.

"What happened?"

The keeper followed my eyes.

"Warm seep under north approach. Freeze after midnight. First freight tried before inspection. Rear end walked."

"Driver hurt?"

"Pride."

Acceptable injury.

"Stone damaged?"

"Checking."

He looked at me more closely.

"You planning to solve it?"

I considered.

"No."

"Good."

Professional relationship established.

He moved to the next cart.

Orrin looked at me.

"You make that face everywhere?"

"Apparently."

"Could be expensive someday."

Likely.

We took Marta and the cart to the mile house yard.

Mile house sounded generous.

It was a long stone building with a stable attached to one side and enough smoke from the chimney to suggest either warmth or structural concern.

Noll had been correct about the stew before I saw it.

I knew this spiritually.

Inside, every bench had people on it.

Drivers.

Two families.

Three couriers.

A shepherd with no sheep, which felt suspicious until I saw the dog under his chair.

One old woman asleep upright with a basket of onions in her lap.

Nobody looked like they had been waiting for Greg.

Good room.

Orrin found the keeper's clerk near the door.

Our closure was stamped.

Time recorded.

Contract proof acquired.

Then practical civilization ended.

"Beds?" Orrin asked.

The clerk laughed.

Noll's intelligence network remained strong.

"Floor after supper. Stable loft if you don't snore near horses."

"Price?"

She named both.

Floor cost less.

Stable loft smelled more.

We chose floor.

Marta chose stable without consultation.

Cargo stayed locked in the cart under the yard watch.

Orrin checked the seals twice.

I checked the axle pins.

He watched me do it.

"Satisfied?"

"For now."

"Professional distrust."

"Specific reputation."

He almost smiled.

We ate stew.

Noll had undersold the problem.

It contained cabbage, barley, and a piece of meat that had achieved legal distance from the animal kingdom.

"Bad," I said.

Orrin kept eating.

"Hot."

Correct evaluation.

At the next table, two freight drivers argued about whether Old Fenn should have been rebuilt last summer.

One said the north approach had iced every third hard freeze for twenty years.

The other said this was why competent people waited for inspection.

They were both right enough to continue eating.

A courier woman joined them without invitation.

"Road office asked for a raised east approach three years ago. City funded south culverts instead."

Interesting.

Not my future causeway.

Maybe ancestor of it.

Maybe unrelated.

Later history flattened budgets into infrastructure.

Current people remembered who lost the argument.

I listened.

Did not explain the causeway.

The woman working the bridge came in near sunset.

Hands red from cold.

Hair wet at the temples.

She bought tea and sat alone two tables away.

No robes.

No Guild token visible.

Road-office badge like the keeper's.

The courier woman called to her.

"How bad?"

"Surface is coming. Seep isn't."

"Morning?"

"Maybe."

Useful expert confidence.

I looked at her hands.

She saw me looking.

Danger.

"Need something?"

Direct.

"What were you doing to the stone?"

There.

Curiosity permitted.

She rubbed one thumb across the other palm.

"Loosening frost bond."

Not technique name.

Result.

"Heat?"

"A little. Mostly makes the stone stop holding the ice."

That was not how heat worked.

Therefore probably magic.

"Through the stone?"

She looked at my Bronze token.

Then my face.

"You support?"

"Some."

"Not the same work."

"I noticed."

She took a drink of tea.

I waited.

Important skill.

Eventually she added:

"If I heat the surface enough to melt everything, water runs into the joints and freezes deeper tonight. Then we break the bridge while fixing the road."

There.

Domain judgment.

"So you release the top layer."

"Enough to scrape."

"What do you call it?"

She shrugged.

"Road work."

Excellent magical taxonomy.

I left her alone.

Later Greg had known practitioners who would have called that three different things depending on school, region, and how much they wanted to charge.

Current woman had a shovel partner waiting outside.

Road work.

Good.

By dark, the bridge remained closed.

No dramatic reversal.

The keeper posted a new chalk line.

**REVIEW AT FIRST LIGHT.**

Orrin read it.

"Four days."

"Possibly."

"Contractually."

"Best kind."

We arranged blankets on the floor after supper.

Twenty people became twelve sleeping shapes and eight people pretending not to need sleep yet.

I had money for a better room if one existed.

It did not.

Another useful limit.

The old woman with onions snored like a sawmill.

Orrin lay on his back beside me.

"Still want road work?"

I looked at the ceiling beams.

Smoke-blackened.

Cracked plaster between them.

Someone's wet socks hanging from a line near the stove.

Later memory had reduced this whole route to distance.

Carrow to Stonecross.

Hours if roads improved.

A line on maps.

Current road had a keeper who closed it.

A woman who could persuade frost to let go of stone.

A clerk who stamped delay time.

Bad stew.

Twenty people on a floor because water had frozen in the wrong place.

"Yes," I said.

Orrin grunted.

Maybe asleep already.

Travel, apparently, included staying exactly where someone else told you to...
