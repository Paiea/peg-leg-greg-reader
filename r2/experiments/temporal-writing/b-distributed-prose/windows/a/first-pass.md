# Chapter 49: The Empty Board

The temporary Guild had too much wall and not enough work on it.

That was not literally true.

East Annex had vegetables on the other side of the canvas, two clerks arguing over reconstructed contract numbers, three clients waiting to be told whether their paperwork still existed, and enough damp coats to qualify as weather.

What it did not have was a job I wanted.

I read the board twice anyway.

One warehouse rat contract.

One two-day drain clearing with a note that said **strong stomach preferred**.

One north-gate cart escort already claimed.

Two roof-inspection jobs marked Silver.

A laundry boiler that had developed opinions.

I considered the boiler.

It had apparently boiled a copper fitting out of the wall yesterday.

I stopped considering the boiler.

Sella looked up from the intake table.

"You planning to stare one into existence?"

"I was hoping the board felt guilty."

"It doesn't."

"Bad institution."

"You came here voluntarily."

This remained one of her strongest arguments.

I checked the rat contract again.

Not because I had developed a passion for rats.

Because money still mattered and an empty morning remained an empty morning.

The rate was ordinary.

The client wanted it done before fourth bell.

The warehouse sat south of the dye lane.

I had done worse.

Then a man behind me said, "Taken."

Claimed ten minutes ago.

"Thanks."

# Chapter 50: The Seam

Arlo's kiln had developed a line of daylight.

This was undesirable in equipment whose main function involved keeping heat inside.

Ivena's note had been concise.

**Come before noon. Bring coat. Arlo is angry at masonry.**

Dena opened the workshop door before I knocked.

"Don't mention new kilns."

"Wasn't planning to."

"Good."

She stepped aside.

The workshop was colder than outside in the way only a room full of failed heat could manage.

Arlo stood beside the kiln with a mason I had never met.

The mason had one hand inside the firebox and an expression suggesting professional disappointment.

"There," Arlo said.

She withdrew her hand.

"I know where."

"It wasn't that wide last week."

"Cold moved the outer seam. Heat moved the inner. You fired through both."

"I fired because I own a kiln."

"You own several hundred bricks arranged as a kiln."

I liked her immediately.

Ivena stood near the workbench with a ledger open.

"Greg. Mira Vale. Mason."

Mira nodded once.

"Third owner?"

"Unfortunately."

"Good. Then you can tell him no."

Arlo looked betrayed before she even explained.

Mira pointed at the north seam.

A pale line ran between two courses of brick.

Not a crack through the brick.

Joint failure.

"I can pack it," she said. "You fire tonight, it opens again. Maybe smaller. Maybe larger. You want it repaired, you cool the whole body, pull this face, reset twelve to sixteen bricks, repoint the seam."

"How long?" I asked.

"Two days if he stops asking how long."

Arlo closed his mouth.

Useful mason.

"Cost?"

Mira gave it.

Not ruinous.

Not cheap.

Ivena already had the number written down.

Of course.

"Bell Tile?" I asked.

"One shelf tonight," Ivena said. "One tomorrow if they don't get a roof order."

"Bodies ready?"

Arlo answered.

"Seven."

"Service pressure?"

Dena leaned against the wash trough.

"Current customers covered through Fifthday if nobody breaks anything."

"And if somebody breaks something?"

"Then I become charming."

I had seen Dena charming.

Customers would survive.

Probably.

Arlo put both hands on his hips.

"This is why we need the better kiln."

There it was.

Dena looked at me as if I had personally summoned the sentence.

Ivena said, "No. This is why we need our current kiln not to have a hole in it."

"A better kiln wouldn't have this seam."

Mira made a sound.

Arlo turned.

"What?"

"Every kiln has seams."

"Not like this."

"Not until it does."

Masonry philosophy.

I walked around the kiln.

Later factories would solve this with better refractories, controlled expansion joints, standardized liners, and enough capital to make current Arlo physically ill.

Current problem was twelve to sixteen bricks.

The service reserve existed.

We had argued for it.

Kept money in the business instead of paying ourselves.

This was the part where responsible decisions stopped being theoretical and turned into a mason's invoice.

I looked at Ivena.

"Reserve covers it?"

"Yes."

"After Bell firing?"

"Yes."

"After charcoal next week?"

"Yes."

Arlo frowned.

"We still lose two days."

"We already lost them," I said.

He looked at me.

That was the actual problem.

Not whether to repair.

Whether anger could negotiate with elapsed time.

It could not.

I had tried.

"Repair it," I said.

Ivena nodded.

Arlo looked at the kiln.

Then Mira.

"Twelve bricks?"

"Maybe sixteen."

"You said twelve to sixteen."

"I still do."

He sighed.

"Fine."

Unanimous decision achieved through exhaustion.

Mira started marking joints with chalk.

Dena immediately began reorganizing the drying rack.

"These seven go Bell tonight. These four can wait. North dye house clean spare is still clean. Tannery swap Sixthday."

No one asked her to do that.

She had already been doing the actual service work while the owners argued with masonry.

Ivena noticed me noticing.

"She wants a second cart shelf."

Dena called from across the room, "I want one that doesn't bend."

"Different capital request," I said.

"Correct," Ivena said.

Arlo pointed at us.

"Nobody is allowed to enjoy this."

I enjoyed it quietly.

By midday the kiln door lay on the floor.

Mira had removed nine bricks and found three more joints beginning to powder.

Arlo stopped arguing after that.

Not because he was defeated.

Because evidence had improved.

We ate bread and cold sausage at the bench while the workshop remained too cold.

No distribution.

No new machine.

No brilliant invention.

The reserve became smaller.

The kiln would become less broken.

That was what the money had been for.

On the way out, Ivena handed me a folded repair receipt.

"Owner copy."

"Can we frame it?"

"No."

"It represents maturity."

"It represents brick dust."

Both could be true.

Outside, Deepwinter had started sleeting.

I tucked the receipt inside my coat and walked home through weather the business could not put on a balance sheet...

# Chapter 54: The Stone

"Drainage shoulder?"

Higher.

"Cart approach?"

Higher again.

# Chapter 56: The Draft

# Chapter 58: The Ring

Pell Orst sent the note because he was tired of owning my metal.

**DONE. COME GET IT.**

Reasonable.

I had left the useful brace ring with him long enough that it had become part of the shop furniture in my mind.

The ugly spare remained on my shelf.

I wrapped it in cloth and brought it with me for comparison.

Pell's repair shop smelled like oil, old ash, and confidence in small hammers.

Three brass tabs around a dark iron body.

One tab slightly newer than the others.

Not pretty.

Good.

No new payment.

I liked him more.

Different directions.

Pulled.

# Chapter 60: The Load

Slow.
