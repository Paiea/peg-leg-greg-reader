# Chapter 99: The Specialist

Meren Voss asked for me by name and then tried to pay me Bronze rate.

Progress had become insulting in more sophisticated ways.

The job sat three streets south of East Market in a printer's drying room where six wall wards kept paper moisture within a narrow range.

Not glamorous magic.

Important magic if your business owned two hundred sheets that wanted to curl.

Meren met me outside.

"You brought the ring?"

I stopped.

"Which ring?"

He stared.

Good answer.

"Three-tab brace. Pell Orst said you had one."

Carrow remained a village when information wanted something.

"Why?"

"Ugly anchors."

That got my attention.

Inside, the printer had installed a new iron shelving run across a wall that had been built around three different generations of timber reinforcement.

The ward return still worked.

The anchor geometry did not.

Meren pointed at a ceramic channel that now had to cross shelf iron, old oak, and one section of patched brick.

"Modern ring wants three clean points."

"Old one doesn't care."

"As much."

Correct.

I took the repaired three-tab ring from my field pouch.

I had started carrying it after Pell finished the repair because leaving a useful tool on a shelf forever had become a different kind of stupidity.

That did not mean it needed a destiny.

Meren had found a wall.

The ring had found work.

We spent forty minutes making the anchor ugly on purpose.

One tab on timber.

One on brick.

One through a small iron standoff Meren fabricated from shelf scrap.

The ring held the channel without forcing any one surface to pretend it was another.

Narrow advantage.

No mana.

No amplification.

Just tolerance.

"Later design stopped doing this," I said.

Meren looked at me.

"Why?"

"Cleaner buildings. Standard fittings. Easier inspection."

"Worse world."

"Different world."

He grunted.

We powered the moisture ward low.

Checked return.

Raised load.

One shelf bracket hummed.

Meren killed power immediately.

"Iron coupling."

Not my problem.

His.

He moved the standoff.

Tested again.

Clean.

The printer touched one sheet as if magic could be judged by paper texture in three seconds.

Probably could, in his domain.

"Good?" Meren asked.

"Give it an hour."

Also his domain.

We waited.

The paper stayed flat.

At closeout Meren counted the standard second-hand rate.

I looked at the coins.

Then at him.

"Tool fee."

He looked offended.

"Your ring?"

"My ring."

"I asked you to bring it."

"Yes."

"That's why you're useful."

"Exactly."

We stared at each other.

The printer watched with professional interest.

Meren added two copper.

"One."

"Two."

"One and lunch."

"Lunch where?"

"Not here."

Printer looked offended now.

We settled at two copper because hunger should not become contract language.

Specialized knowledge had become specialized billing.

Tiny step.

Real money...

# Chapter 100: The Invoice

The problem with charging a tool fee once was that I had to decide whether it had been legitimate.

Meren had paid it.

That did not answer the question.

I took the closeout to Sella.

"Do we have a category for privately owned specialist equipment?"

She looked at me over the ledger.

"We have several."

Of course.

"Small tool. Client-requested. Not consumed."

"Rental or handling?"

"I don't know."

"Then why did you charge?"

Administrative violence.

"Meren agreed."

"That is not a category."

I showed her the closeout.

She read it.

"Two copper."

"Yes."

"You are asking whether you committed accounting fraud for two copper."

"I prefer early detection."

Sella opened a thin rate sheet.

Private specialist tool.

Nonconsumable.

Minor job-use fee permitted if client or lead requests the tool and owner assumes maintenance liability.

There.

Civilization had anticipated me.

"You were fine," she said.

"Excellent."

"Write the tool on the issue copy next time."

"Less excellent."

"And maintenance is yours."

Right.

Money from an object also created obligation.

I took the ring to Pell Orst that afternoon.

Not because it had broken.

Because I had been paid for using it and suddenly pretending wear did not exist felt dishonest.

Pell checked the tabs.

One had a new bright line near the standoff bite.

"Fine," he said.

"How fine?"

"Use fine."

"Professional unit?"

"Very."

He charged one copper to dress the edge and re-oil the locking pin.

Half my tool fee gone.

Economics became less magical quickly.

Still positive.

I wrote the maintenance cost beside the job payment in my small book.

Not because I had become Ivena.

Because Ivena had become difficult to argue with.

Back home, the ring returned to the field pouch instead of the shelf.

No increase in power.

One more thing I could rationally bring to certain ugly walls.

That was enough...

# Chapter 101: The Route Board

West Cut finally got a survey because two merchants stopped agreeing about who was losing money.

Harl Fen explained this at East Annex while Pate tried to eat a posted notice.

"City didn't care about old alignment," Harl said. "Now North Gate traffic is up and West Cut carriers want rate relief."

"So?"

"So somebody has to know whether the cut can carry thaw carts before anyone funds repairs."

There it was.

An old geography thread moved because trade changed, not because Greg had remembered it.

The survey was four days.

Harl.

Pate.

A city works clerk named Senna Vey.

One Bronze field hand.

"Why me?"

Harl looked at me.

"You've walked it. You don't panic when old infrastructure disagrees with memory."

Specific enough.

"Rate?"

He gave it.

Better than ordinary escort.

Not Silver money.

I accepted.

Jorren was already on a grain run south.

No conflict.

We left next morning under thaw rain.

West Cut looked worse without snow hiding it.

Ditches full.

One culvert half blocked.

Ruts deep enough to become property.

Senna measured everything.

"People use this?" she asked.

Harl pointed at fresh wheel tracks.

Evidence.

By midday we reached the first retaining wall.

Later memory wanted it rebuilt in dressed stone.

Current wall was stacked field rock, moss, and stubbornness.

Senna touched one shifted course.

"Mark."

Harl marked.

No one asked whether history considered the wall important.

The road existed now.

That was enough...

# Chapter 102: The Cut

West Cut earned its name by removing every pleasant option from a hill.

The road narrowed between stone on one side and a long drop into alder on the other.

Pate disliked the drop.

Smart horse.

We walked the worst section instead of riding.

Senna checked grade with a weighted line.

Harl checked drainage.

I checked where a person could stand if a cart came the other direction.

Not many places.

"Passing bay?" I asked.

Harl pointed uphill.

"Was one."

The old bay had become a pile of slide debris.

Current problem.

We climbed over it.

Halfway across, the upper slope made a small sound.

Pebbles first.

Then wet soil.

"MOVE."

Harl's call.

We moved downhill along the road, not toward the drop.

A sheet of mud and fist-sized rock crossed where Senna had been standing.

No dramatic collapse.

Enough.

Pate pulled hard against his lead.

I did not Barrier the hillside.

Excellent professional decision.

When it stopped, Harl waited.

Not seconds.

Minutes.

Listened.

Then approached from the low side.

"New seep," he said.

Water ran through the exposed soil where none had been visible before.

Senna wrote.

"Repair?"

"Drain first. Wall maybe."

"Cost?"

"Ask after we know where water comes from."

Correct answer.

Later memory wanted a cut with a stone-lined upper drain.

Lead.

Not answer.

I kept it to myself until Harl asked.

"You remember a drain?"

"Later. Higher than this. Could be because of this. Could be something else."

Senna looked interested.

"Where?"

I pointed approximately.

"Not enough to dig from."

Harl nodded.

Future knowledge entered as search area, not plan.

We camped farther down the cut because the road crew shelter had lost half its roof.

Survey work remained glamorous...

# Chapter 103: The Spring

The water came from a spring nobody had mapped because it only existed during hard thaw.

Senna found the first sign above the road.

Not water.

Dead grass flattened in a narrow line beneath last year's leaves.

We climbed.

Twenty yards uphill the ground became soft enough that Harl stopped us.

"Spread out."

We did.

Pate stayed below.

Again, smartest.

A shallow basin under exposed roots held clear water.

No glow.

No monster.

No magical explanation required.

Just thaw pressure finding an old path.

Senna checked her historic map copy.

Nothing.

Harl checked older maintenance notes.

One entry from eleven years ago:

**UPPER WET AFTER LATE FREEZE. CLEARED BY MIDSUMMER.**

There.

Institutional memory existed in one bored worker's sentence.

"Drain alignment later?" Harl asked me.

I pointed farther east than before.

"I think."

"Think useful?"

"Useful enough to look."

We looked.

Found two buried stones under brush in a shallow line.

Maybe old drain edge.

Maybe boundary stones.

Senna refused to classify them until excavation.

Good.

The survey changed from "repair retaining wall" to "investigate seasonal spring and old drainage before wall work."

Different project.

Less satisfying.

More correct.

That evening Harl wrote the recommendation while I dried socks beside the fire.

Current professional life remained mostly discovering that the first problem was not shaped correctly...

# Chapter 104: The Brace

The three-tab ring became useful on West Cut because Senna owned a measuring frame designed by an optimist.

Three equal feet.

Flat ground assumed.

West Cut offered none.

She tried setting it twice.

One foot on rock.

One in mud.

One on the old retaining wall.

The frame leaned enough to make its sight line useless.

"We can hand-level," she said.

Harl looked at the slope.

"For twenty marks?"

No.

I opened my field pouch.

"I have something ugly."

Senna looked offended before seeing it.

The repaired brace ring fit around the lower frame post.

One tab caught rock through a cloth pad.

One tab took a short stake in mud.

The third rested against a wall joint through a wood block.

Ugly geometry.

Exactly what the old pattern tolerated.

"That's not survey equipment," Senna said.

"Correct."

"Rated?"

"No."

"Load?"

"Small. Static. We test before trusting."

Better.

We tested with weight.

Adjusted.

Tested again.

The frame stayed.

Senna checked level.

"Annoyingly good."

Professional approval.

We used it for six measurements.

Then moved.

The ring did not make the survey magical.

It saved time on bad ground.

At closeout Senna wrote **PRIVATE BRACE TOOL USED — FIELD STAND ONLY** on her copy before I remembered to ask.

Sella would be proud.

I charged one copper.

Not two.

Survey budget.

Pell's maintenance probably waiting.

Specialized leverage had margins...

Clear the blocked lower culvert immediately.

Excavate the suspected old upper drain line before wall reconstruction.

Restrict heavy thaw carts until seep behavior was understood.

No new retaining wall authorization yet.

One merchant objected.

"We paid for a repair survey."

Senna answered, "You paid to find out what repair is needed."

Good distinction.

"And?"

"We need more information."

Harl's field authority ended when the report did.

Could be weeks.

# Chapter 106: The Message

This made the envelope arrive with three route stamps and one irritated note from a Carrow clerk who had apparently been asked to find Lower Rook.

Inside, Mara wrote:

**Pell says your street is inconvenient. I told him streets do not have obligations to freight companies. He disagrees professionally.**

Pell remained alive and onion-adjacent somewhere west.

Good.

Mara had moved into route accounts often enough that her letters now contained places I did not know in current form.

Not invitations.

Work complaints.

A damaged wool seal at Bracken.

A captain who kept changing departure order after ledgers were closed.

One warehouse that charged storage by "day" while defining a day as whatever benefited the warehouse.

Then:

**I may be in Carrow after thaw. Not visiting. Work. Vey is reviewing whether the east packet handoff belongs here or farther north. If it becomes Carrow, I will probably be one of the people counting why.**

There.

Possible crossing.

Not scheduled.

Not romantic destiny.

Company routing.

She added:

**Do not rearrange your life around maybe. I learned that from you, unfortunately.**

I laughed.

Then obeyed.

I wrote back about West Cut mud, Noll's latest kitchen complaint, and the fact that my brace ring had now earned three copper and cost one in maintenance.

I did not explain why this mattered.

Mara could decide...

# Chapter 107: The Second Cook

The first time I saw Noll supervising someone, he was telling a boy not to salt soup from shoulder height.

"Why are you doing that?"

The boy froze with salt between two fingers.

"Chef does it."

"Chef knows how much is in his hand. You do not."

Noll took the salt.

Measured.

Returned it.

The Brass Spoon had moved him from trial cook to regular second cook weeks earlier.

Now Vessa had given him a kitchen runner for two evening shifts.

Not promotion ceremony.

More responsibility because there was work.

I was eating at the back table with Jorren.

"He looks angry," Jorren said.

"Professional."

"Same face."

Noll heard.

"I can poison both of you legally."

"Restaurant category?" I asked.

"Chef discretion."

We ate anyway.

The soup had correct salt.

The runner dropped one plate and did not die.

Vessa charged breakage to nobody because accidents happened.

I liked her more every visit.

Noll finished after us.

He sat for exactly three minutes before standing again.

"Inventory."

"You do inventory?"

"Now I do."

Independent life kept adding verbs...

# Chapter 112: The Lime Road

Lime dust got everywhere.

This became the defining fact of the escort before any danger appeared.

Three carts.

Covered barrels.

Two drivers.

Jorren rear.

Me front.

The road south-east stayed busy enough that thieves would have needed ambition.

Instead our problem was a wheel rim heating on the second cart.

Driver noticed smell first.

Jorren noticed smoke.

I noticed nothing until both had already stopped the cart.

Good world.

The iron rim had loosened against dry wood.

No magic problem.

We unloaded two barrels to reduce weight, cooled the wheel slowly, and waited for a roadside wheelwright from the nearest hamlet.

The client lost half a day.
