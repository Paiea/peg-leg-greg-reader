# Chapter 113: The Lime Yard

The wheelwright charged the client, not us.

This improved my opinion of the road immediately.

By the time the second cart could carry weight again, lime dust had settled into my sleeves, my hair, and one place inside my left boot that should not have been structurally available.

Jorren watched me take the boot off beside the ditch.

"Professional?"

"Extremely."

The drivers put the two unloaded barrels back aboard.

Neither asked us to help until the wheelwright had checked the rim a second time.

Good order of operations.

We reached Hollen Brick's south kiln yard after dusk.

The yard foreman who had requested us was shorter than Jorren and louder than both of us combined.

"All three?"

"All three," the lead driver said.

The foreman counted barrels before people.

Also good order of operations.

One lid had worked loose enough to dust the inside of its canvas cover. No lost barrel. No thieves. No heroic intervention.

The foreman marked the delivery late and complete.

Then he looked at Jorren.

"Culvert man."

Jorren nodded.

He looked at me.

"Guild-fire closeout."

Apparently that was my professional title now.

"Greg," I said.

"I know."

Worse.

The client put us in the bunk room behind the brick sheds and fed us stew thick enough to repair masonry.

Jorren ate two bowls.

I ate one and spent ten minutes cleaning lime from the seam of my sword belt.

Three-day escort.

One broken wheel.

Zero thieves.

Cargo delivered.

The work had still happened...

# Chapter 114: The Closeout

We returned with empty carts and one broken barrel hoop tied behind the rear axle.

Empty cargo traveled louder.

Every rut made the wagons complain about having nothing useful to hold down.

The wheel stayed cool.

I checked anyway.

The driver noticed on the third stop.

"You always do that?"

"Recently."

"Good habit."

Orrin had taught me axle pins months ago.

A wheelwright on this road had taught me hot rims yesterday.

Old Greg remained excellent company for some problems.

Current strangers kept improving him anyway.

Back at Carrow, Hollen Brick's clerk had already received the yard foreman's packet by faster rider.

Our closeout took twelve minutes.

Three days paid.

One delay recorded.

No deductions.

The clerk slid the copper across.

Jorren counted his.

I counted mine.

Not because I distrusted the clerk.

Because money deserved the dignity of being counted.

Outside East Annex, Jorren brushed white dust from my shoulder and made it worse.

"Pair again?" he asked.

"Apparently."

"You busy tomorrow?"

I thought about the unsigned Silver application in my room.

The filtration ledger I had not checked in several days.

Noll's evening schedule.

Lera still away in Dorrin.

"No."

"Good. I'm sleeping."

He walked toward East Market.

I went home, washed lime from my hair twice, and found a note pushed under my door.

The handwriting was Mara's.

**Vey has me in Carrow for two nights. Maybe three. Work first. Food after if schedules behave.**

Her life had arrived while I was elsewhere.

Correct...

# Chapter 115: The Clerk

Mara had three ledgers on the table when I reached the Vey packet office.

She did not look up.

"You're late."

"Your note said food after work."

"This is after some work."

"Terrifying distinction."

Then she stood and hugged me.

Work first.

Apparently not exclusively.

Her coat was better than the one I remembered from Halden.

Not expensive-looking.

Better fitted for weather.

Ink stained the side of her thumb.

A brass route token hung from a cord at her belt.

Current work had started leaving marks on her too.

"What are you doing?" I asked.

She pointed at the ledgers.

"Arguing with four warehouses and a bridge toll."

"Winning?"

"I don't know yet."

I liked that answer more than I expected.

Vey was reviewing its east handoff through Carrow. One clerk blamed North Gate delays. Another blamed warehouse transfer times. A third blamed wagon allocation and had apparently submitted numbers designed by personal hatred.

Mara wanted eyes on the gate tomorrow.

"Mine?"

"For the part you actually know."

"Which part is that?"

"Whether the gate behaves like people outside Carrow think it behaves."

Narrow expertise.

Good.

I carried one ledger downstairs because she handed it to me.

It was heavy enough to explain several trade disputes physically.

"Dinner?" I asked.

Mara checked the wall clock.

"One hour. Then I have to copy this before morning."

"Romance."

"Freight."

We went to eat...

# Chapter 116: The Handoff

The flour cart cleared North Gate in nine minutes.

Mara wrote that down.

A ceramic wagon took thirty-seven because its papers listed two axles and the wagon had three.

Mara wrote that down too.

"Which one proves your answer?" I asked.

"Neither."

"Excellent."

She ignored me and watched the next cart.

Vey's east handoff problem turned out to be several problems pretending to share a name.

Gate inspection varied by cargo.

Warehouse transfer varied by staffing.

One packet departure waited for a road clerk who was also responsible for collecting toll receipts from somewhere I had never been.

Mara kept asking questions until her first theory became less useful.

I contributed three things.

North Gate was not generally slow anymore.

Rain still made the inbound inspection yard miserable.

And the north-road cart queue changed shape when regional freight arrived in batches rather than evenly.

That was all.

I did not know Vey's optimal handoff.

Mara did not ask me to.

At lunch we sat on a low wall outside the packet yard with bread and hard cheese.

"You look pleased," she said.

"Watching someone else become less certain professionally is comforting."

"You are a terrible friend."

"Experienced."

She bumped my shoulder.

Then she went back to counting carts.

I went to the Guild and left her to her work...

# Chapter 117: The Dumplings

Mara met Noll through a kitchen hatch.

This was not how either of them remembered knowing each other.

The Brass Spoon was full enough that Vessa gave us a table near the wall and warned us the dumplings were running behind.

Noll appeared at the hatch carrying three plates.

He stopped.

"Mara?"

She pointed at him.

"You got taller."

"You left."

Apparently young Greg's social history remained communal property.

"I was gone four years."

"Still counts."

He vanished into the kitchen.

Five minutes later dumplings arrived that we had not ordered.

Vessa charged us for them.

I respected the institution.

Mara told me Vey's likely answer was not one new handoff site but a trial split: some east freight north of Carrow, some remaining inside the city until they had better numbers.

"Would that move you?"

"For a few weeks. Maybe."

"Where?"

"North first. West again eventually. Halden unless they change everything."

No promise.

No clean map.

I told her about the ferry and the lightfin migration.

She asked what the delay cost the caravan.

Not what the fish looked like.

Mara remained Mara.

"You should see Halden before I stop living there," she said.

Not a date.

Not a plan.

A possibility with a clock attached somewhere I could not see.

"I want to."

She looked at me for a second.

"Better answer."

I knew what she meant...

# Chapter 118: The Guest

Mara came to Lower Rook after dinner because she wanted to see the room I kept describing badly.

"Two windows," she said.

"I mentioned those."

"You mentioned wind."

"Related technology."

She inspected the sword rack.

The shelf with letters.

The repaired latch.

The table.

Then the bowls.

"You own bowls now."

"Travel changed me."

"Heroic."

I made tea.

We played one hand with Tavin's old deck.

Mara remembered the burned seven.

Not the rule.

The actual burn.

"You were losing," she said.

"Tavin claims that."

"Tavin was right."

Conspiracy expanded across geography.

The hallway clock struck late bell.

Mara looked toward the door.

Vey had paid for a room above the packet office.

She had work at first bell.

I wanted her to stay.

That was true.

So was everything else.

"Walk you back?"

"Yes."

No tragedy.

No test.

We walked three streets under cold lamps, and she kissed me outside a building paid for by her employer.

"Tomorrow?" I asked.

"If the ledgers stop fighting."

They did not...

# Chapter 119: The Trial Route

Mara left Carrow on a northbound packet with two route clerks and a box of copied toll records.

Vey had decided to test one part of the east handoff farther north.

Not because Mara chose it.

She had helped count why.

One wagon transfer disappeared.

One local cart leg became longer.

Carrow merchants complained before the trial began.

Merchants elsewhere probably celebrated in private.

Systems distributed inconvenience professionally.

At North Gate, Mara adjusted the strap on her ledger case.

"Back west after?" I asked.

"Maybe. They want three weeks of trial numbers first."

"Halden?"

"Unless Vey discovers another city needs me more."

Her life kept acquiring directions without consulting my memory.

Good.

Annoying.

Good.

She climbed aboard.

The packet driver checked his clock.

Mara leaned down once.

"Write before I have to complain."

"Threat received."

Then the packet left because schedules had no interest in emotional timing.

I watched until the road bent.

Then I went to Pell Orst's shop to have my brace ring inspected...

# Chapter 120: The Spare

Pell wanted my ugly spare.

This was suspicious because Pell had spent months treating the ugly spare as evidence that I should stop buying damaged municipal hardware.

"Why?"

"People ask."

"For ugly metal?"

"For old three-tab rings."

He put my repaired ring under a lens while he talked.

The tabs were still sound.

One edge showed fresh scrape from West Cut.

"Your survey use got mentioned," Pell said.

"By whom?"

"People who like buying things after someone else proves they are useful."

Market theory.

He offered ten copper for the spare.

I looked at it.

Unrepaired.

Useful as parts.

Potentially useful as itself if someone spent money first.

I had acquired it almost accidentally inside the old lot.

"Eleven."

"Nine."

"You began at ten."

"Negotiation."

We settled at ten copper and a free inspection after my next hard field use.

Not a fortune.

More than dead metal had been worth when I bought it.

I took the coins.

The artifact became lunch money and future maintenance.

History survived...

# Chapter 121: The Reed Camp

Edda asked for me because she wanted one more Bronze around reed cutters who had started losing food to stilts.

"Stilts?"

"Local name."

Old Greg knew a longer name involving marsh striders and salt glands.

Local name won immediately.

The animals were gray, long-legged, and ugly in the precise way wet birds resented being compared to mammals.

They were not hunting people.

They were stealing salted fish from work camps and occasionally charging anyone who stood between them and the theft.

A cutter named Jessa knew where the nests were.

Edda knew how to keep twelve workers from turning one animal sighting into twelve separate decisions.

I knew the shoulder twitch before a charge.

Those were different kinds of useful.

We moved the food higher.

Shifted one cutting line away from a nest.

Made noise before entering thick reeds.

One stilt watched us from knee-deep water and decided stealing somewhere else was easier.

No fight.

The cutters filled two wagons before the afternoon rain.

At the edge of the marsh, Jessa stopped me with one hand against my chest.

"Wait."

Nothing moved.

Then I saw a pale line traveling through the reeds against the wind...

# Chapter 122: The Reedline

"Don't cross when it's narrow," Jessa said.

The pale line was perhaps thirty feet long and only a few reeds wide.

It moved sideways through the marsh.

Not wind.

Not water I could see.

The reeds inside it bent lower as the line passed.

"What happens?" I asked.

"Legs go heavy."

"How heavy?"

Jessa shrugged.

"Enough to fall if you're carrying bundles."

I wanted mechanism.

Jessa wanted to get twenty people home before rain.

Her priority was better.

We waited.

The line widened.

Broke into several pale patches.

Jessa stepped across first.

Everyone followed.

Nothing happened.

Back on dry ground, I wrote in my field book:

**REEDLINE. LOCAL TIMING MATTERS. ASK BEFORE CROSSING.**

Then I stopped.

Old Greg might have learned a formal name later.

Current Greg had a rule that kept people upright.

Enough...

# Chapter 123: The Promise

I turned down four days of good escort pay because I had already promised Ivena an afternoon.

Sella looked at me across the temporary contract desk.

"You know this pays more."

"Yes."

"Considerably."

"Still yes."

Ivena was renegotiating kiln access with Bell Tile.

Arlo wanted me present because I owned part of the venture and because he had learned that entering price conversations alone made him generous in ways everyone else disliked.

"Can you move the meeting?" Sella asked.

"Probably."

"Then why not?"

Because I had said I would be there.

The answer sounded embarrassingly simple.

"I promised."

Sella handed the escort slip to the next Bronze who asked for work.

No one punished me.

No hidden better opportunity vanished forever.

Another person got paid.

That afternoon Bell Tile raised our per-batch firing rate by one copper and gave us first refusal on two low-demand morning slots.

Ivena got the morning slots.

Arlo complained about the copper.

I mostly stayed quiet.

My presence had not won the negotiation.

It had made the promise real...

# Chapter 124: The Bench

Five people had made one workbench ridiculous.

Arlo refused this conclusion until Dena put a wet return chamber on top of his alignment jig because there was nowhere else clean enough.

He stared at it.

"Second bench."

Ivena smiled without looking up from the ledger.

Pellin built most of it.

Arlo corrected one brace.

Then Pellin corrected one of Arlo's legs.

Growth.

The new bench was not beautiful.

It was flat.

That mattered more.

Dena claimed the end nearest the wash buckets.

Pellin claimed the end nearest the clay racks.

Arlo claimed both until everyone ignored him.

Sera, the woman helping two afternoons a week with cleaned returns and rack prep, wrote her name beneath one shelf because people kept moving her brushes.

No factory.

No new building.

One more place to put your elbows without ruining somebody else's work.

The venture had become more capable by four feet of wood...

# Chapter 125: The Question

The Silver application was still on my shelf.

This had become embarrassing mostly because nothing dramatic happened to it.

No one asked every morning.

Sella did not send reminders.

Meral did not appear in doorways to discuss destiny.

The paper remained paper.

I took it to Neris after a support block.

Not for permission.

I wanted one answer.

"If I apply as mixed sword and support, what are they actually judging?"

Neris wiped chalk from her fingers.

"Whether you can carry Silver responsibility without making Silver-sized trouble."

"Specific."

"Field judgment. Execution. Communication. Knowing what you own. Knowing what you don't."

"Reserve?"

"Matters. Isn't everything."

I looked at the form.

"Would you endorse me?"

Neris did not answer immediately.

Good.

"For support competency, yes. For judgment, I'd sign what I've actually seen."

"Which is?"

"Improving."

Cruel precision.

She handed the paper back.

One more current opinion.

Not an instruction...

# Chapter 126: The Signature

I signed the Silver application after breakfast.

This was less ceremonial than I had expected from nineteen.

I slept normally.

Burned one egg.

Ate the other two.

Read the form again.

Current evidence remained current.

Meral had signed a readiness note.

Neris was willing to stand behind the support work she had actually seen.

My sword no longer embarrassed Lera every minute.

People requested me for specific work.

Sometimes I knew what I was doing.

More importantly, I had become better at saying when I did not.

The fee would hurt.

It would not threaten rent.

Failure would be irritating.

It would not erase the work already done.

Old Greg knew he had been Silver young.

That fact had spent months trying to become an argument.

It was not one.

I signed **Greg**.

Current hand.

Then I carried the form to the Guild.

Sella checked every line before taking my money.

"Assessment scheduling is irregular," she said.

"Of course it is."

"Could be two weeks. Could be six."

Good.

Even ambition had to wait for somebody else's calendar...
