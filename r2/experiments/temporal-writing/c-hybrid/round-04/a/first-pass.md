# Chapter 165: The Inventory

Noll stayed annoyed for two days.

This was inconvenient because I had apologized correctly.

Apparently correctness was not the full transaction.

On the third night I went to The Brass Spoon before close instead of after.

Noll looked at me, looked at the dining room, and handed me a crate of onions.

"Those go down."

"Hello to you too."

"You know where down is."

I did.

The cellar smelled like vinegar, onions, old wood, and one sack of potatoes that had begun negotiating with death.

When I came back up, a junior kitchen hand was trying to slide a chipped bowl behind the clean stack.

Noll saw it.

"No."

The boy froze.

"It still holds soup."

"Then it can hold soup at your house. Not here. Put it in breakage."

The boy looked unhappy.

Noll looked more tired than angry.

He had a ring of three keys clipped to his belt now.

Closing lead.

Inventory.

Breakage.

The boring parts of authority had found him.

"You want help?" I asked.

"You are helping. Onions."

"I meant with the count."

"I know what you meant."

He gave me a slate anyway.

We counted oil, vinegar, dried beans, salt, flour, smoked fish, and spoons.

The spoon count was wrong.

Again.

"Domm," Noll said.

"You know that?"

"I know him."

At the end of the night he locked the rear door, checked it twice, and handed the key ring back to the owner.

"Next Thursday," he said.

"Dinner?"

"If you can manage it."

"I can send a runner if the Guild traps me in a warehouse."

"Excellent. Civilization advances."

He was still a little annoyed.

I preferred that to being forgiven on schedule...

# Chapter 166: The Second Carrier

The second outside customer lived east of Carrow.

Hesk lived north.

This became important immediately.

"No," he said.

Dena leaned across the workbench.

"You have a cart."

"A cart is not a direction."

"It moves in directions."

"Mine moves north on fourth-day freight and south on sixth-day returns. I am not taking six chambers east because you discovered geography."

Dena turned to me.

Ownership was a disease.

"He has a point," I said.

"You also have legs."

"Not freight legs."

The new customer was a dye house beside Old East Gate. Small operation. Reliable water demand. Bad local filter cloth. They wanted four chambers every five weeks and expected us to solve delivery because customers enjoyed tradition.

Ivena had already written three versions of a route schedule.

Arlo wanted to hire a second craft hand.

Dena wanted a second carrier.

Pellin wanted everyone to stop talking until the firing rack was empty.

Hesk wanted payment for having attended a meeting about a road he did not use.

He had also brought back the north mill's six empties without breaking one.

That improved his case.

We found another carrier through the flour exchange.

Marris Venn.

Two-wheel cart. East Gate twice a week. Carried flour in, soap and lamp oil out. She disliked fragile freight but liked recurring money.

Her price was higher than Hesk's.

Dena disliked this personally.

Marris disliked Dena within six minutes.

Useful symmetry.

Ivena put a crate-condition line on the handoff sheet.

Marris added a weather exception.

Hesk added, from the other side of the table, "Mud exception."

"You are not even on this route," Dena said.

"Mud is universal."

Nobody bought a cart.

Nobody solved logistics forever.

We acquired a second carrier and twice as many arguments about receipts...

# Chapter 167: The West Board

The first westbound Silver job I took was not actually in Halden.

This improved its chances.

City Works wanted a Guild support hand for a four-day drainage survey between Carrow and the west freight road.

One Silver.

Two Bronze.

One city surveyor.

No excavation beyond hand probes.

No independent authority over road closure.

The pay was ordinary for Silver and unusually specific about who could stop traffic.

I liked that.

Sella watched me read the contract twice.

"You're not supposed to marry it."

"I'm checking whether it wants me to sign anything exciting."

"It does not."

"Promising."

The city surveyor was Varin Cole, forty-something, narrow-faced, and already irritated because Guild staff had arrived seven minutes after he wanted to leave.

The older Bronze was Doss Fen.

Doss had been working west-road drainage longer than I had been alive this time.

He looked at my Silver cord.

Then at my boots.

Then back at the cord.

"You know ditch water?"

"Some kinds."

"Bad answer."

"Accurate answer."

He grunted.

The second Bronze, Mira Pell, was twenty and had brought enough waxed cord to restrain a horse.

Varin handed us route stakes.

"We are measuring three spill lines and checking two old culverts. Nobody repairs anything unless I say so. Nobody closes a lane unless I say so. If something magical begins behaving like it owns public property, the Silver gets to tell me whether it is my problem."

Doss looked at me.

"Congratulations."

We walked west...

# Chapter 168: The Ditch Hand

Doss believed three things about drainage.

Water went downhill.

People lied about where they had put it.

And the north ditch beside Marker Seventeen hated iron after rain.

The first two were engineering.

The third sounded like superstition until Mira put a probe rod into the wet bank and blue-white sparks crawled three inches up the shaft.

She let go.

"That," Doss said, "is why we use wood there."

Varin swore.

"The map says no charged bed within forty yards."

Doss shrugged.

"Map doesn't sleep here."

Varin did not enjoy this answer.

He also did not put the iron rod back.

Competence survived irritation.

We switched to ash probes.

The bank held old low charge after storms, probably from a buried line or mineral seam Varin's records did not include.

I could feel the load shift against a small plane if I held one near the wet cut.

Not dangerous by itself.

Potentially unpleasant if somebody drove a long iron stake through the wrong place.

Doss showed us three dark notches on an older wooden marker.

"Those mean don't be clever."

"Official city notation?" I asked.

"Official me notation."

Varin made a noise through his nose.

"You cannot maintain municipal records by scratching warnings into rotten posts."

"Been working."

"That does not make it a record system."

Both men were right enough to remain angry.

I marked the location on the Guild sheet and let Varin argue Doss into drawing it properly.

Doss's drawing looked like a dying spider.

Varin stared at it.

"This is why literacy was invented."

"Water can read it."

Mira had to turn away so they would not see her laugh...

# Chapter 169: The Long Way

On the second day the road split around a low ridge.

I knew the right fork.

Or I knew it later.

Stone causeway.

Good drainage.

Fast enough that old Greg had used it without thinking.

Current Varin stopped at the fork and swore.

The right road was open.

The left road had a toll post.

"Right is shorter," he said.

Doss shook his head.

"Not with the survey cart."

"It is dry."

"Top is dry."

Varin looked at me.

Silver cord had become an invitation to disappoint people.

I walked thirty yards down the right fork.

The packed gravel I remembered was there in pieces.

So was a wagon rut deep enough to hide my boot.

The road had not received the later stone bed yet.

I came back.

"Left."

Varin's jaw tightened.

"Toll is six copper for the cart."

"Yes."

"City will not reimburse it without a closure notice."

"Then don't ask me. Ask Doss."

Doss spat into the ditch.

"Left."

Varin paid the toll from the field purse.

He wrote my name beside it.

"If the right road is fine on return, I'm charging this to Guild caution."

"Fair."

It was not a threat.

It was accounting.

We took the longer road.

Nothing collapsed behind us to prove me wise.

No wagon appeared overturned in the rut.

No divine hand reimbursed six copper.

The left fork was simply slower, drier underneath, and expensive...

# Chapter 170: The Watch List

That night I opened my old notebook beside the inn stove.

Most of it had become current-life work.

Rates.

Names.

A sketch of Lera's foot placement.

Filter dimensions.

Mara's route marks.

Jorren's terrible spelling where he had borrowed the page.

Old future notes lived farther back.

I found the west road.

**RIGHT FORK STONED. RELIABLE.**

No date.

Of course no date.

Old me had known things the way people knew where their own kitchen was.

Nobody wrote down when the kitchen became a kitchen.

I added one word beneath it.

**WATCH.**

Doss came downstairs carrying two mugs.

He set one beside me and looked at the page.

"Gambling?"

"Poorly."

"You write down losses?"

"Sometimes."

He pointed at WATCH.

"Good system."

"You don't know what it means."

"Then I can't disagree."

He drank half his beer standing up.

"Knee bothering you?"

"Not yet."

"That means yes tomorrow."

He went back upstairs.

I closed the notebook before he could become correct twice...

# Chapter 171: The Halden Edge

We reached Halden's outer freight district without entering the city proper.

I recognized nothing for almost an hour.

Then I recognized a corner.

Not a building.

Not a sign.

A slope.

Two streets descending toward a long brick wall.

Old Greg had walked that slope hundreds of times later.

There had been a baker on the corner.

Current life had a rope yard.

Bundles of hemp hung from a rail where I remembered bread cooling in summer air.

The rope-yard woman watched me stare.

"You buying?"

"No."

"Then admire from somewhere less useful."

I moved.

Varin was measuring a drain outlet beside the freight wall.

Doss had found a cracked clay line under the shoulder.

Mira was arguing with a cart driver who believed orange flags meant decorative permission.

Halden remained busy without caring that I remembered a different version.

Our survey stopped three streets short of the central district.

No Mara.

No old addresses.

No pilgrimage.

At lunch Doss bought a meat pie and dropped half the filling down his sleeve.

"Professional," I said.

"Silver observation?"

"Extremely."

We spent the afternoon proving that the freight-wall drain belonged to Halden, the upstream ditch belonged to Carrow district maintenance, and the water itself recognized neither government...

# Chapter 172: The Pack

Rain started on the return leg.

Not dramatic rain.

Professional rain.

Enough to make straps swell, boots stay wet, and every hill become slightly more expensive.

By midday my left knee had begun reporting each downhill step individually.

I adjusted the pack twice.

Doss watched once.

At the next stop he took two iron survey stakes out of my load and strapped them to the cart.

"Those are mine."

"No. City owns them."

"I'm carrying them."

"You were."

"Doss."

He kept tying the strap.

"Need you walking at dusk. Don't need you honorable at noon."

I disliked how complete the sentence was.

Mira handed him a third stake.

Traitor.

I kept the measuring rod and one small tool roll.

My knee improved from complaint to muttering.

Two hours later Doss slipped in mud and sat down hard enough to invent a new expression.

Mira laughed first.

I laughed second.

Varin, who had spent four days preserving municipal dignity, laughed longest.

Doss stood with brown water to his thigh.

"Nobody writes that down."

Varin opened his field book.

"Public record."

We reached the inn after dark with wet boots, intact equipment, and one city employee threatening legal action over a puddle...

# Chapter 173: The Bad Batch

The filtration shop smelled wrong when I got back.

Too much hot clay.

Too little talking.

Then Arlo started shouting.

Useful diagnostic.

Eleven chambers from the last firing had hairline cracks around the lower collar.

Not visible warm.

Visible after cooling.

Pellin had shortened the dry hold because the firing queue was backed up.

Arlo had approved moving the batch forward because the north mill shipment was due.

Dena had packed six before checking all collars because she assumed the cooling rack meant finished.

Nobody had committed the entire mistake alone.

This made everyone less happy.

"You changed the hold," Arlo said.

"You told me the kiln had to turn!" Pellin snapped.

"I said we needed the slot, not that you could guess at moisture."

"You built the schedule."

"You run the clay."

"And Dena packed them."

Dena turned around slowly.

"Finish that sentence."

Pellin did not.

Ivena stood between the ledger and the door with both palms flat on the worktable.

"Nobody ships the north order today."

Dena opened her mouth.

"Nobody," Ivena repeated.

That one belonged to her.

We lost eleven chambers.

Two more needed reinspection.

The north mill received a one-week service credit.

Thirty-one copper disappeared between clay, firing time, and the credit.

Arlo was still angry.

Pellin was still angry.

Dena was still Dena.

I owned part of a business large enough to have expensive ordinary mistakes now.

Nobody celebrated...

# Chapter 174: The Clerk

Ivena hired the clerk before Arlo won the kiln argument.

Arlo considered this procedural betrayal.

"A clerk doesn't make chambers."

"Neither does writing the wrong customer on a return slip," Ivena said.

"We need firing capacity."

"We need to know which firing we're behind on."

Both remained true.

The clerk was Senn Marr.

Half days.

Four mornings a week.

Former grain-counter assistant with handwriting so clean it made mine look criminal.

Senn spent the first morning asking why the north mill receipt, east dye-house receipt, and Hesk's crate-condition slip all used different date formats.

Dena blamed civilization.

Hesk blamed Dena.

Marris blamed Hesk despite having met him twice.

Senn made one form.

Then discovered Dena had been charging two different return fees depending on whether she liked the customer.

"That is not a policy," Senn said.

"It was working."

"It was emotional taxation."

I left the room because I wanted to remember the phrase without becoming involved.

By the end of the week we had one route ledger, two carrier sheets, a visible firing queue, and the same kiln.

Arlo stood beside it with his arms crossed.

"Still too small."

Ivena did not answer.

The clerk had solved paperwork.

The kiln had not become less real because the paperwork improved...

# Chapter 175: The Freight Word

Tavin's letter arrived folded around a freight receipt.

Not mine.

His.

**Rell Ford now has me managing outbound yard two days a week because apparently competence is punished with chairs and signatures.**

I approved of the diagnosis.

He wrote about three damaged axle boxes, a merchant who believed wet canvas was a moral failure, and one driver who had tried to declare a mule as empty cargo.

Then:

**Saw your name on a Guild hold form attached to a Carrow warehouse packet. Not our yard. Yet. Your people use too many words. If work ever drags you here, I intend to complain professionally.**

No invitation.

No urgent problem.

No hidden destiny.

Tavin had acquired a yard and paperwork.

I wrote back:

**Congratulations on the chair. Sorry about the signatures. The mule was correct.**

Then I added:

**I also use too many words now. They pay me more for some of them.**

I almost wrote that I missed him.

Instead I wrote:

**Try not to become important. Jorren already failed.**

That was close enough for the mail...

# Chapter 176: The Return

Lera came back from Dorrin because Dorrin stopped paying her.

This was more satisfying than fate.

I found her in the Guild yard at seven in the morning arguing with the rack steward about practice blades.

"These are soft," she said.

"They are training steel."

"They are decorative disappointment."

The steward saw me.

"Silver Greg. Tell her."

Lera turned.

Her eyes went to the cord at my shoulder.

Then back to my face.

"You got promoted while I was getting rained on."

"Apparently."

"Annoying."

"Welcome home."

She hugged me once, hard, then pulled back and pinched the Silver cord between two fingers.

"This mean you're expensive now?"

"Sometimes."

"Good. Lesson price went up."

"That's not how promotion works."

"Worked immediately."

Dorrin had paid her better than Carrow.

Dorrin watch training had also taught her to hate clean drills.

"People shout," she said. "Doors open. Civilians walk where they're stupid. Rain exists. Carrow yard teaches as if everyone fights in a dry box."

"You sound happy."

"I made money. Different thing."

She picked up one of the soft practice swords.

"Tomorrow. Same hour. Bring bruises you don't mind replacing."

Then she resumed insulting the rack steward...

# Chapter 177: The Interruption

Lera's new drill had rules until it started.

Then other people were allowed to ruin them.

I cut.

Recovered.

Reset.

A junior yard hand walked across the edge of my line carrying three helmets.

I stopped.

Lera hit my forearm.

"Dead."

"There was a person."

"Yes. People are famously present."

Second run.

Cut.

Recover.

Someone behind me shouted, "LEFT!"

I turned.

Lera hit my shoulder.

"Dead."

"That was a call."

"From a man watching the wrong pair."

Third run.

A bucket dropped.

I ignored it.

Wrong choice.

Water spread under my lead foot and the next recovery slid six inches.

Lera did not hit me.

She watched me catch balance.

"Again."

By the sixth run my old instincts were angry because none of the interruptions respected combat grammar.

That was probably the point.

Probably.

Lera had not asked my opinion.

At the end she checked the bruise on my forearm with one thumb.

"Silver didn't fix that."

"Deeply disappointing."

"Tomorrow."

"You just got back."

"And already you're whining. Carrow feels normal."

She walked away before I could turn the morning into a lesson...

# Chapter 178: The Early Ditch

Jorren's road-office note arrived with mud on one corner.

His handwriting had not improved under municipal supervision.

This felt stable.

Most of the note concerned gravel counts, a clerk named Pessa who hated him, and a culvert crew that had adopted a three-legged dog.

Then:

**West Cut drainage phase moved up. Budget got released after spring washout numbers. We start next week instead of next season. If Carrow office asks why, tell them water is rude.**

I read that line twice.

West Cut.

Old Greg remembered the drainage works.

Not because they mattered to history.

Because years later he had complained about the new ditch line while walking home wet.

I remembered it as new then.

Maybe memory had compressed years.

Maybe the city had rebuilt it twice.

Maybe current Jorren's washout reports had moved money.

Maybe none of those.

I opened the notebook.

Under **RIGHT FORK STONED. RELIABLE.** I added:

**WEST CUT DRAINAGE: WATCH.**

Then I pinned Jorren's muddy note beside Mara's last route letter and went to work...
