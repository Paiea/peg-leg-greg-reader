# Chapter 206: The Fourth Batch

Batch four made it home whole.

This disappointed everyone differently.

Arlo wanted proof that outside firing capacity worked.

Pellin wanted proof that the new cross slats fixed transport.

The Hesk Street pottery owner wanted her cooling shelf back.

Dena wanted lunch.

Ivena wanted numbers.

We had four batches.

One clean.

One damaged by fast cooling.

One cooled properly and lost a chamber to cart flex.

One cooled properly and survived the trip home.

Arlo put both hands on the table.

"So it works."

Pellin said, "Something works."

"That is what I said."

"No. You said it like the kiln won a war."

The pottery owner, who had stayed because we owed her two copper for the slower shelf time, looked between them.

"Do either of you want my kiln or just each other?"

Nobody answered correctly.

She offered one regular firing slot every sixth day.

Eight weeks.

Slower cooling included.

One extra copper per firing for the shelf.

Arlo wanted two slots.

Pellin wanted another transport cycle before any regular arrangement.

Ivena wrote **ONE SLOT / SIXTH DAY / EIGHT WEEKS** and turned the page toward us.

"This is not ownership. It is not expansion forever. It is eight weeks of finding out whether we are paying for useful capacity or moving our mistakes three streets away."

Arlo frowned.

Pellin frowned too.

That felt healthy.

I signed after Ivena.

Dena signed third.

Arlo signed fourth and underlined his name for reasons known only to Arlo.

Pellin was not an owner, so he wrote **TRAYS FIRST** in the margin anyway.

The pottery owner took the page back.

"Good. Now remove your ceramics from my shelf."

Growth had acquired a schedule and an eviction notice...

# Chapter 207: The Wet Return

The south linen carrier refused a used chamber at the bathhouse.

Not because it was broken.

Because it was wet.

Dena held the wrapped ceramic under one arm.

"It's not leaking."

The carrier, a woman named Pera Sol, looked at the linen bundles behind her.

"Wet."

"Wrapped."

"Wet wrapped."

"That is more wrapped."

Pera did not move.

The bathhouse manager watched from the doorway with the expression of a man who had successfully made a problem belong to three other people.

Senn checked the route sheet.

Outbound condition.

Customer signature.

Return count.

No return-state rule.

"We can add one," he said.

Pera pointed at the cart.

"You can add twelve. The wet thing is not going beside clean linen."

Dena looked at me.

I had ownership.

I also had no cart.

"She's right about the linen."

"Traitor."

"Professionally."

We carried the chamber back ourselves.

Halfway home Dena announced that this was proof the company needed a horse.

"It proves we need a dry return."

"Horse can be dry."

"Horse can be wet."

"Bad horse."

The next morning Pellin built a cheap slatted draining stand for the bathhouse service room.

Pera approved nothing.

She said she would reconsider after the first dry return.

Dena wrote **PERSUADE LINEN TYRANT** on the route sheet.

Senn crossed out **TYRANT**.

Pera saw both versions the following week...

# Chapter 208: The Six Lines

Senn's first condition sheet had fourteen boxes.

Ivena stared at it long enough that the room became quiet.

Dena counted them.

"Why does weather get two?"

"Outbound weather and return weather."

"Weather knows which direction we're going?"

"The crate does."

Hesk wanted **CARRIER RECEIVED DRY**.

Pera wanted **RETURN DRY**.

Marris wanted **CUSTOMER PACKING ERROR**.

Dena wanted **CARRIER DAMAGE** in letters large enough to be read from a moving cart.

Senn added a line for transfer-yard count because of the missing empty.

The page became a confession under torture.

Ivena took the pen.

"If the form needs Senn standing beside it to explain the form, the form has failed."

Senn looked personally wounded.

"Some failures are user failures."

"Not today."

We cut fourteen boxes to six lines.

Crate count.

Visible condition.

Dry or wet.

Transfer count if used.

Customer sign.

Carrier sign.

Dena objected to losing the dedicated crime category.

The first sheet came back three days later with a muddy thumbprint through **VISIBLE CONDITION**, one line crossed out, and **DRY** written twice because Pera did not trust us.

Senn pinned it to the wall.

"Working?" I asked.

"Ugly."

"That wasn't the question."

"I know."

He left it pinned there...

# Chapter 209: The Laundry Wall

The water office found East Mill on a second sheet.

This one had been filed under bathhouse maintenance because municipal memory also made mistakes.

The old service line bent east after Hara Venn's parcel and crossed beneath what was now a laundry yard.

Issen arranged access for nine in the morning.

The laundry worker opened the gate at nine twenty and told us this was our punishment for arriving before breakfast last time.

Her name was Bessa.

She had been using one section of the east wall to dry aprons for six winters.

"Faster there," she said.

Renn looked delighted.

Issen looked at Renn before looking at the wall.

Good instincts.

Two narrow strips stayed free of frost even when the yard stones were white.

Bessa slapped a wet apron against one.

"See?"

"Every winter?" I asked.

"Every winter I have had aprons."

"Any change?"

"Sometimes warmer after heavy rain. Sometimes not."

Renn stopped smiling.

That answer was better than the one he wanted.

The old line on the sheet passed beneath the wall and turned toward a plugged utility mark near the lane.

I held a small plane over the upper joint while Bessa pressed another wet cloth lower down.

Faint load.

Not enough to move anything.

Enough to feel the same sideways preference I had felt in Hara's cellar.

"Same system," Renn said.

Issen said, "Same sentence you are not allowed to write yet."

Bessa pulled the apron down.

"Are you going to stop me using the wall?"

Nobody answered quickly.

Her face changed.

That was the first important thing we had done all morning.

"Not today," Issen said. "If current use becomes unsafe, you get told before anything changes."

"Current use is laundry."

"I noticed."

Bessa looked at the tools on the ground.

"Good. Move those. I need the line."

We moved them...

# Chapter 210: The Plug

The plugged fitting was mostly mineral stain.

That made Renn unhappy for nearly four minutes.

A water-office inspector named Olin scraped the white crust with a narrow knife.

"Old seep."

"From the conduit?" Renn asked.

"From water."

"Helpful."

"Water often is."

The fitting itself sat two stones below the laundry wall and had been mortared shut at least twice.

One patch was older than the other.

Olin wet the mineral deposit.

A faint metallic smell came up.

Not mana.

Not obviously.

He rubbed residue between two fingers.

"Something traveled with the water."

Renn recovered emotionally.

"Charge?"

"Mineral. Heat. Old treatment. Bad pipe. Pick one."

"Can we sample behind it?"

Issen and Bessa said "No" at the same time.

Olin laughed.

"Good office."

The city could seek an opening order later.

For now we photographed the fitting with measurements, copied the mortar layers, and left the wall closed.

Bessa hung a sheet over the spot before we finished packing.

"Still warm," she said.

The sheet steamed faintly in cold air.

Renn watched it.

"That is irritating."

"Laundry?"

"Evidence I can't touch."

Bessa handed him a basket.

"Carry this instead."

He did...

# Chapter 211: The Four Baskets

Noll bought four baskets of greens instead of six.

The supplier objected to arithmetic.

"Six ordered."

Noll turned one basket sideways.

The lower leaves were bruised dark and warm under the top layer.

He opened another.

Same.

"Four usable."

"Rain road."

"I know."

"Domm takes six."

"Domm is sleeping."

"Wake him."

Noll looked at the kitchen clock.

"No."

The supplier swore at him.

Noll paid for four.

Then the supplier left with two bad baskets and Noll inherited a lunch menu built for vegetables he had refused.

I watched him stand in the kitchen with both hands on the prep table.

"Correct decision?" I asked.

"Shut up."

That seemed fair.

He cut cabbage thinner.

Miko suggested using more beans.

Noll said the bean pot was already short because Domm had promised a staff meal to three masons repairing the rear chimney.

"Then less soup," Miko said.

"Lunch is soup."

"Then more water."

Noll stared at him.

Miko raised both hands.

"I have contributed."

They changed lunch to cabbage cakes and thin broth.

Customers complained about the broth.

One ordered a second cabbage cake.

Domm woke halfway through service, saw the bad-greens note, and said, "Should've taken five."

Noll almost threw a ladle at him.

Almost.

Authority had not made lunch easier...

# Chapter 212: The Second Close

Miko carried the dry-store key twice a week now.

He still wanted Noll to stop checking him.

Noll still did not.

On the third Thursday Miko counted salt correctly, opened the upper vent, locked the rear shelf, and forgot lamp oil.

Noll found it during final close.

"Again."

Miko swore.

"You said I could do this."

"You can."

"Then stop checking."

Noll went quiet.

Miko's face tightened before Noll answered.

"No."

"Then it's not mine."

"The key isn't yours."

Miko looked down at it.

"Then why do I carry it?"

Noll leaned against the shelf.

"Because I need you able to close this store before I can stop being the only person who can."

"But you still check."

"Because if you miss lamp oil, tomorrow starts stupid."

"So you don't trust me."

Noll exhaled through his nose.

"I trust you enough to let you make mistakes in my close. That's not the same as pretending they're not mine too."

Miko did not like the answer.

Neither did Noll, apparently.

They counted lamp oil together.

Miko kept the key for Saturday...

# Chapter 213: The Half Lesson

My left knee objected to downhill work before I did.

Not pain exactly.

Stiffness behind the kneecap after a long west-road day and two hours standing on cold stone.

I told nobody because it was not worth telling.

Lera noticed because Lera charged money for noticing things I considered private.

We were halfway through cut-recover footwork when she stopped me.

"Short today."

"Why?"

"Because your left side is arriving late."

"I can continue."

"Yes. Badly."

She cut the block at thirty minutes.

Then charged the full lesson rate.

I found this morally offensive.

"You did half a lesson."

"I made one useful decision."

"Expensive decision."

"Good decisions cost more after you become Silver."

"That is not how your pricing works."

"Today it is."

I wanted to argue until she made me do stairs.

So I paid.

On the walk home the knee loosened before the second bridge.

This did not improve Lera's personality...

# Chapter 214: The Five-Ton Plate

The Meren bridge carried heavier wagons later.

I knew that.

Current inspection plate said **FIVE TONS UNTIL SPRING REPAIR**.

Our dye wagon weighed a little over six.

The merchant pointed at the stone arch.

"That thing has held ten."

"Later."

He frowned.

I had not meant to say that aloud.

"Later what?"

"Later repairs. Different condition. Current plate says five."

"Can it physically hold us?"

Probably.

I had crossed it in my first life under loads that made this wagon look polite.

But I did not know which stones had been reset between now and then.

I did not know whether the current restriction was conservative or necessary.

I did know the number painted on the plate.

"Not on current certification."

The detour cost four hours and eight copper in road fees.

The merchant complained for the first two.

Then he got bored and complained about dye tariffs instead.

Nothing collapsed behind us.

No cracked bridge appeared to vindicate me.

We simply arrived later and more expensive than he wanted...

# Chapter 215: The Quarry Road

The detour passed an abandoned quarry road I did not remember.

Our driver did.

"Red stone," he said. "Closed when the east shelf went soft."

Old drill holes striped the cut.

Scrub had grown through the wheel ruts.

The later road in my head ignored the entire place.

The driver pointed ahead.

"Stay left after the birch. Right shoulder lies."

The merchant, still angry about eight copper, said, "Road looks fine."

The driver said, "Then walk on it."

He did not.

We stayed left.

Ten minutes later an empty handcart coming the other direction put one wheel onto the right shoulder.

The edge sheared away under it.

Nobody was hurt.

The handcart owner swore at the road.

Our driver stopped to help pull the wheel free.

The merchant looked at me.

"You knew?"

"He knew."

The driver grunted from the ditch.

"Everybody knows."

The handcart owner shouted, "I didn't."

"Then almost everybody."

We lost another twenty minutes helping him...

# Chapter 216: The Resin Chewers

I knew what was eating the support rope before Esa Porr finished swearing.

Pale dust under the coil.

Tiny crescents cut into the weather resin.

"Rope moths."

Esa looked at me.

"Resin chewers."

Same creature.

Different profession.

Old Greg had seen them ruin bridge line in wet country.

They ate the treatment first, nested between fibers, and left the rope looking useful long after weather could get inside it.

"Don't load that coil."

"Wasn't going to."

Esa already had bitterleaf oil warming in a small iron pot.

She brushed three neighboring coils, moved them to the high dry rack, and swept the pale dust into a covered pan.

"Three days before inner-wrap check," I said.

"Two here. Loft runs warm."

I looked at the roof vents.

She was right.

"Two."

"Good. Silver can count."

Old experience had found the creature.

Esa knew the building.

Neither of us required a calendar from the future to be useful...

# Chapter 219: The Road Notice

I learned about Jorren's washed shoulder after he had almost finished repairing it.

The notice outside the Guild said:

**WEST SPUR CLOSED — SLOPE LOSS / CITY CREW ACTIVE.**

Under that, in smaller hand:

**EXPECTED OPEN: THIRD DAY, WEATHER PERMITTING.**

Jorren's name sat beneath the engineer as field coordinator.

I had not known the shoulder failed.

I had not been asked to help.

On the third morning the notice changed.

**OPEN — LOAD LIMIT RESTORED.**

I walked that direction after lunch because curiosity is not the same thing as professional need and therefore harder to bill.

The repair was ugly.

Fresh gravel.

New timber edging.

Mud everywhere.

Jorren was arguing with a cart driver who wanted to stop on the new shoulder.

"Keep moving."

"I'm unloading two sacks."

"Then unload them fifty paces ahead."

"Why?"

"Because I enjoy repeating myself. Move."

The driver moved.

Jorren saw me.

"You inspecting?"

"No."

"Good."

A produce cart rolled over the repaired section.

Then a brick wagon.

Then a woman pushing a handcart full of onions.

Jorren went back to yelling at somebody else...
