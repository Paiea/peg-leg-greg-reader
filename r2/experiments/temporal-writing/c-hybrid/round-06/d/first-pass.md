# Chapter 242: The Archive Wagon

The job carried paper instead of freight.

Six lockboxes of municipal copies.

Three villages.

Seven days.

One city archivist who treated rain as a personal enemy.

My role existed because two of the copied records concerned old charged boundary markers and the city wanted a Silver hand present if anyone needed to expose or stabilize one.

The archivist was a woman named Pel Aro.

She inspected my pack before we left Carrow.

"Oilskin?"

"Yes."

"For the documents?"

"For me."

She stared.

I added a second sheet of oilskin.

We left by the east road with a driver, one Bronze guard, six boxes of dead people's handwriting, and no expectation of adventure.

This felt dangerous only to the archivist...

# Chapter 243: The Bell Field

The first village grew grass that rang when wet.

Not loudly.

A thin glassy chiming across the drainage meadow after rain.

I stopped beside the road.

Pel did not.

"Bellgrass," the driver said.

"Useful?"

"Tells farmers when the lower ditch is carrying charge."

That seemed useful.

Children had tied red cloth to three patches where the sound changed pitch.

The village ditch keeper walked out with a wooden rake and pressed one ringing clump flat until the note dulled.

"Too much seep," she said.

No spell.

No Guild involvement.

The grass was part warning, part nuisance, and apparently excellent goat feed after drying.

Old Greg had crossed this region many times later.

I remembered none of it.

The future had preserved roads better than grass...

# Chapter 244: The Toll House

There was a toll house on the east road that did not exist in my memory.

That was because it had burned down thirty years before Old Greg started using the route.

Current Greg had to pay two copper.

I objected on principle.

Pel objected because municipal archive wagons were exempt.

The toll keeper produced a book proving they were exempt only on official seal days.

"Today is an official seal day," Pel said.

"Seal is in the box."

"Which box?"

Pel became silent.

We spent fifteen minutes opening the correct lockbox under oilskin while the toll keeper watched with professional pleasure.

The seal was inside.

The wagon passed free.

I wrote **TOLL HOUSE / CURRENT** on my map.

Pel said, "It will be here tomorrow too."

"Probably."

She disliked probably...

# Chapter 245: The Widow's Bridge

The second village had a bridge everyone called Widow Sar's.

Widow Sar did not own it.

She owned the house beside it and had spent twenty years telling wagon drivers what the bridge could tolerate.

The posted limit said four tons.

Our archive wagon weighed less than two.

No problem.

A quarry wagon behind us did not.

The driver said he had crossed heavier loads before.

Widow Sar came out carrying a broom.

"Not since the north pier shifted."

"Looks straight."

"So do you from far away."

He did not enjoy this.

The village road hand confirmed the pier had moved after spring thaw.

The quarry wagon turned around.

Pel asked Widow Sar how she knew.

"Kitchen window."

"You can see the pier from there?"

"I can hear wheels change."

She went back inside.

Current infrastructure had acquired another expert nobody had certified...

# Chapter 246: The First Box

The first archive box contained a boundary record from eighty-two years ago.

The current mayor expected it to settle a pasture dispute.

It did not.

The record described a charged stone marker beside a walnut tree.

The walnut tree was gone.

The marker was gone.

Two current stone posts disagreed by eleven feet.

Both families had fences.

Both fences were older than either current owner.

Pel read the record twice.

"This proves the original line."

The mayor looked at the empty field.

"Where?"

That was the problem.

I could have told them where the later road boundary ran.

That road did not exist yet.

I kept my future out of other people's pasture.

The village scheduled a survey.

The cows continued trespassing impartially...

# Chapter 247: The Marker Below

The missing marker was under a manure shed.

Not metaphorically.

His grandfather had built the shed around it because moving it made his hands tingle.

That was good enough reason for a grandfather.

The current owner wanted the shed left alone.

Pel wanted the marker exposed enough to copy its inscription.

The surveyor wanted measurements.

I wanted everybody to stop leaning shovels against the charged stone while arguing.

We removed three boards instead of the floor.

I held a tiny plane while the surveyor brushed dirt from one edge.

Faint old charge.

Stable.

No revelation.

The inscription matched the archive copy.

The line moved seven feet from one current fence and four from the other.

Both families lost the argument they had hoped to win.

# Chapter 248: The Upstairs Bed

Village lodging had one bed left.

Pel took it.

Not because she was important.

Because she had six lockboxes of paper and refused to sleep where a leaking roof could reach them.

I got a cot in the stable loft.

The Bronze guard got hay and claimed this was better for his back.

At two in the morning a goat climbed the stairs.

Nobody knew why.

It ate part of the driver's glove before discovery.

My knee disliked the cot less than the goat disliked being removed.

By breakfast Pel had added **GOAT EXPOSURE** to her lodging complaints.

The innkeeper said the goat was not included in the room rate.

Pel said that made it worse...

# Chapter 249: The Reed Lights

The last village floated blue lamps on irrigation ponds at dusk.

I assumed festival.

Wrong.

The lamps were reed bundles tied around small stones that absorbed low charge during the day and released it slowly into the water overnight.

A local woman said it kept a biting water insect away from the shallows.

"Magic?" I asked.

She looked at the glowing pond.

"Probably."

Useful answer.

The Bronze guard wanted to know what the insects were called.

"Biters."

He tried again.

"Scholarly name?"

"Ask a scholar."

Nobody did.

We slept with fewer bites...

# Chapter 250: The Return Seal

Full pay.

# Chapter 255: The Road I Never Knew

A later road in my memory crossed the eastern hills in a straight line.

Current archive copies showed three older routes underneath it.

One through the bellgrass village.

One past Widow Sar's bridge.

One seasonal track closed whenever the low meadow flooded.

Old Greg remembered none of them because by the time he cared, the straight road had won.

Current people still lived beside the losing roads.

That seemed important.

Not because history needed saving.

Because a map built only from what survived had always been missing somebody...
