# PEG-LEG GREG — ADVENTURE GENERATION GUARDRAILS

Non-canon runtime guidance for using outward-adventure and myth-derived material without flattening PLG.

## Do not overcorrect

The story is ready for larger adventure. That does **not** mean every chapter now needs danger, travel, creatures, mystery, or supernatural escalation.

The ordinary life already built in Carrow is an asset. Adventure gains force because that normal exists.

Keep the contrast.

## One primary engine per arc

For a sustained adventure, choose one dominant story engine.

Examples:

- retrieval / escort with unstable romance;
- object / material acquisition that exposes a regional problem;
- travel through a dangerous ecological route;
- a local dispute involving an ancient or powerful being;
- a contract caught inside another family's or faction's conflict.

Secondary myth-derived textures may enrich the route, but do not make every seed fire at once.

A strong default is:

**1 PRIMARY SKELETON + 1–2 SECONDARY STRANGE ELEMENTS + ORDINARY TRAVEL LIFE**

## Preserve negative space

Not every rumor is true.

Not every old woman is supernatural.

Not every strange road contains a monster.

Not every local custom hides magic.

Not every suspicious client is concealing a mythic secret.

Not every shifter transforms on-page.

Not every powerful being needs to demonstrate maximum power.

Mystery survives only if the world also contains coincidence, fraud, misunderstanding, ordinary danger, exaggeration, and boring explanations.

## Greg competence rule

Do not create adventure by deleting Greg's experience.

Old Greg's practical survival knowledge is a real advantage.

When he knows a creature, route hazard, posture, track, ecology pattern, or adventuring problem from repeated experience, let him know it and sometimes be completely right.

The new frontier is not “Greg forgot everything.”

It is:

**Greg knows a lot, and the world still contains things he never personally encountered, misunderstood, or only knew through rumor.**

## Side-character gravity

At least some major adventure arcs should have a clear emotional center that is not Greg.

Possible centers:

- two people falling in love;
- siblings whose loyalty has broken;
- a parent and child conflict;
- a village dealing with an old obligation;
- rival local powers;
- a person deciding whether to return home;
- a shifter negotiating ordinary personhood and dangerous inheritance;
- a political conflict Greg only partly understands.

Greg remains protagonist because the reader lives through his perspective and choices, not because every important relationship points toward him.

## Avoid chosen-one contamination

Reject directions where:

- ancient beings were waiting specifically for Greg without strong prior causality;
- every rare artifact naturally belongs to him;
- every powerful person recognizes his special destiny;
- travel becomes a sequence of tests designed for him;
- mythic figures exist mainly to certify his importance;
- S-class becomes evidence that he sits near the top of cosmic reality.

Greg is interesting because of accumulated life, competence, contradiction, history, and relationships. He does not need cosmological endorsement.

## Ordinary travel is story material

Use the road itself.

Travel can contain:

- meals;
- bad beds;
- weather;
- sore bodies;
- cart repairs;
- bargaining;
- route choices;
- laundry;
- unfamiliar food;
- petty arguments;
- boredom;
- jokes that only become funny after days together;
- local work;
- somebody snoring;
- Greg discovering what an allegedly luxurious inn actually means outside Carrow;
- companions gradually becoming socially legible.

These scenes earn space when they create new relationship, place, consequence, humor, information, or pressure. Compress routine repetition that adds nothing new.

## Adventure should create reusable world

Whenever possible, a trip should generate assets the serial can reuse later:

- a town Greg now knows;
- a route;
- recurring contacts;
- a supplier;
- a rumor network;
- a regional custom;
- a species or phenomenon with established behavior;
- a person who can later visit Carrow;
- a debt or favor;
- a political name without starting a politics arc;
- a future reason to travel again.

This prevents the adventure from becoming disposable scenery.

## Expansion test

After developing an adventure possibility, ask:

1. Does this create a larger world or just a louder plot?
2. Is there enough ordinary human texture inside it?
3. Does Greg remain Greg?
4. Are other people allowed to own the emotional center?
5. Does the fantasy feel rarer and stranger rather than more game-like?
6. Will the arc leave something useful behind after Greg returns?

If most answers are no, mutate further.
