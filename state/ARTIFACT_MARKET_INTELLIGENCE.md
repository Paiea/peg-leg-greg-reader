# PEG-LEG GREG — ARTIFACT MARKET INTELLIGENCE

This file is a durable companion to `RHYTHM_ARTIFACT_ENGINE.md` and `ECONOMY_CONTINUITY.md`.

Its purpose is to make artifact acquisition an information, bargaining, provenance, and risk problem rather than a clean shop interface.

Exact manuscript prose remains authority. This is forward-writing logic, not a catalog of canon artifacts.

## Core rule: both upside and downside may be unknown

Do NOT assume unusual artifacts arrive with complete, accurate labels describing what they do.

The current market may be wrong or incomplete about:

- the upside;
- the downside;
- the trigger;
- the threshold;
- the reset condition;
- the intended use;
- the maker;
- the age;
- the provenance;
- the true rarity;
- the interaction with other magic;
- the reason previous users failed;
- whether a reported `curse` is even the real problem.

An artifact can therefore have multiple layers of uncertainty at once.

A seller may know one visible effect and nothing else. A former owner may know a dangerous rule but not understand why. Greg may remember the later famous use while being uncertain about this exact generation or condition. A specialist may correctly identify the mechanism but miss a later-discovered application. A rumor may preserve the right warning with the wrong explanation.

This is not noise. It is the acquisition game.

## Layered artifact knowledge

Treat artifact knowledge as separable layers rather than one binary known/unknown state.

Useful layers include:

1. **Observed effect** — what somebody has actually seen the object do.
2. **Marketed effect** — what the seller claims or demonstrates.
3. **Known constraint** — a practical rule people have learned to obey.
4. **Known consequence** — what happens when that rule is violated.
5. **Mechanism** — why the effect or consequence happens.
6. **Hidden upside** — a capability, application, interaction, or scale the present market does not understand.
7. **Hidden downside** — a danger, accumulation effect, incompatibility, or failure condition the present market does not understand.
8. **Provenance value** — maker, workshop, generation, historical identity, or later significance.
9. **Combination value** — what the artifact becomes when paired with another object, technique, environment, or Greg-specific workaround.
10. **Future reputation** — what people decades later eventually believe about the object, which may itself be partly wrong.

Different characters can know different layers.

## The artifact may be marketed for only one attractive thing

Do not make every dangerous artifact sit under a sign reading `FAILED CURSED ITEM, CHEAP`.

Often the seller is marketing the part they can prove.

Examples of shape, not canon:

- `rapid healing charm` while the light sensitivity is unknown or only rumored;
- `silent-step boots` while nobody realizes the deeper sensory cost;
- `perfect cooling stone` while the heat-transfer target is misunderstood;
- `unbreakable tether` while the release condition is poorly understood;
- `cheap ward plate` while nobody realizes it can block one extraordinarily specific magical phenomenon;
- `decorative failed lens` while its true value is that it reveals something current users do not know how to look for.

The reverse can also happen. An object may be known mainly for a dangerous reputation while its true upside has been missed.

That is especially valuable for Greg.

## Warnings can be correct without explanation

A warning does not need a theory to matter.

Information may arrive through:

- a former owner;
- a repairer;
- a widow;
- a porter who carried the body;
- a rival bidder;
- a dealer who refuses to stock it;
- an old guild rule;
- a scribbled note inside a case;
- an institutional restriction whose original reasoning is lost;
- a survivor who only knows `do not use it after noon`;
- a story Greg remembers later becoming a documented mechanism.

Greg is experienced enough to distinguish `I cannot explain this` from `this warning is worthless`.

He should sometimes obey an ugly practical warning before he understands the mechanism.

## People may have died without revealing the real rule

Death or disaster does not automatically make the constraint obvious.

A user may die while using an artifact and observers may conclude the wrong thing.

Possible shapes:

- the victim died in sunlight, but people blame overuse;
- the victim died after healing repeatedly, but people blame blood loss from the original wound;
- several users failed in the same city because of an environmental condition nobody connected;
- an artifact appears random because nobody noticed a cumulative threshold;
- only one class of user is vulnerable, so the market treats deaths as coincidence;
- a famous accident causes the item to be banned without anyone understanding its actual operating rule.

Greg's first-life memory may preserve the later explanation, or only enough pattern to know a present warning should be taken seriously.

## Greg's edge is partly information asymmetry

Greg does not need perfect knowledge.

His advantage can come from knowing one layer the present market lacks:

- a hidden upside;
- a hidden downside;
- the correct interpretation of a warning;
- the later maker reputation;
- a future use case;
- a famous interaction;
- a known historical accident;
- the fact that a supposedly defective generation is actually the desirable one;
- the fact that an item later becomes priceless for a completely different reason.

Sometimes Greg should know enough to want the object very badly while still not knowing everything about it.

That is stronger than either perfect foresight or total ignorance.

## Information itself has economic value

Artifact pricing should reflect what the current market believes, not the artifact's omniscient true value.

That means:

- an item can be expensive because the visible upside is impressive even if nobody knows the hidden upside;
- an item can be discounted because deaths, failures, rumors, or repair history make buyers nervous;
- an item can be wildly underpriced because the market understands neither the real upside nor the real historical significance;
- an item can be overpriced because the marketed effect is flashy but the hidden constraint ruins the practical value;
- a knowledgeable seller may charge much more than an ignorant estate liquidator;
- new information can change price faster than the object itself changes.

This creates a reason for Greg to care about who knows what, who is talking, and when information becomes public.

## Greg should manage his visible interest

Greg's attention can itself become market information.

Do not make him automatically explain what he knows to sellers, rivals, appraisers, or bystanders.

He may deliberately:

- keep his face neutral;
- inspect several worthless items first;
- criticize an irrelevant flaw;
- ask ordinary questions instead of the one that reveals his theory;
- leave and return later;
- let another bidder lose interest;
- avoid correcting a seller's mistaken explanation;
- withhold future-history knowledge;
- avoid naming the maker he recognizes;
- pretend the item is merely mildly interesting;
- negotiate around visible condition instead of hidden value;
- refuse to demonstrate a use that would raise the price;
- buy through another person when appropriate;
- walk away if visible eagerness would destroy the deal.

This should remain character-grounded rather than turning Greg into a flawless poker player. He can be too interested, get read correctly, overplay indifference, or discover that the seller already knows more than expected.

## Withholding information is allowed and often smart

Greg is not obligated to educate a seller about the item's full value before buying it.

If the seller is honestly selling an object under mutually understood terms, Greg may keep private:

- future knowledge;
- hidden applications;
- later maker reputation;
- combination ideas;
- historical significance;
- his own intended workaround.

This is ordinary bargaining information asymmetry, not automatically fraud.

But preserve ethical and practical complexity when relevant. Greg should not lie about immediate safety in a way that gets someone killed merely to lower a price, conceal an active danger he created, or invent false defects that cross into outright deception unless the story intentionally explores that choice.

## Seller behavior should vary

Do not make every seller ignorant, greedy, dishonest, or easily manipulated.

Possible seller states:

- knows only the marketed effect;
- knows the warning but not the reason;
- knows more than Greg expects;
- is deliberately minimizing danger;
- is honestly uncertain;
- inherited the object and wants it gone;
- values provenance more than capability;
- values capability but not historical significance;
- knows the item is dangerous and prices that risk in;
- suspects Greg knows something and raises the price;
- refuses to sell without disclosure or conditions;
- wants a favor, future percentage, first refusal, or information instead of only cash.

Artifact acquisition should create relationships and reputations, not vending-machine transactions.

## Hidden upside / hidden downside matrix

Useful artifact shapes include:

- **known upside / known downside** — straightforward but still tactically interesting;
- **known upside / hidden downside** — dangerous success;
- **hidden upside / known downside** — discarded or feared object Greg sees differently;
- **hidden upside / hidden downside** — highest uncertainty and strongest potential for layered discovery;
- **misidentified upside / misidentified downside** — the whole market model is wrong;
- **correct warning / wrong explanation** — people survive by folklore before mechanism is understood;
- **wrong warning / real hidden danger elsewhere** — dangerous confidence;
- **multiple real upsides with different constraints** — artifact deepens over time instead of being solved once.

Do not use the most mysterious shape every time. Variety matters.

## Acquisition scene test

Before shipping a meaningful artifact acquisition or bidding scene, ask:

1. What does the seller believe the object does?
2. What does Greg believe it does?
3. What does Greg know about the downside?
4. What remains unknown to both?
5. What information would change the price if spoken aloud?
6. Is Greg's visible interest itself dangerous to the deal?
7. Why is the current price rational from the seller's perspective?
8. Why does Greg consider the price good, bad, or urgent?
9. What could Greg be wrong about?
10. What new operating rule, economic pressure, or future relationship can this transaction create?

If all parties effectively share the same complete stat sheet, the artifact market is probably too flat.

## Forward effect

From the current Book 3 direction onward, artifact acquisition should increasingly contain:

- incomplete information;
- asymmetric knowledge;
- warnings without full explanations;
- market misvaluation;
- strategic silence;
- bargaining behavior;
- provenance questions;
- the risk that somebody notices Greg knows more than he should.

This should complement, not replace, the stronger artifact rule: important objects should change how Greg operates, and their acquisition should push his economic ambitions upward.
