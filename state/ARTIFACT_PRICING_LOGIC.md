# PEG-LEG GREG — ARTIFACT PRICING LOGIC

This file is a durable companion to `RHYTHM_ARTIFACT_ENGINE.md`, `ARTIFACT_MARKET_INTELLIGENCE.md`, and `ECONOMY_CONTINUITY.md`.

Its purpose is to keep artifact prices rational while preserving Greg's information advantage.

Exact manuscript prose remains authority. This is forward-writing logic, not a universal rate card.

## Core rule: price the visible value, not the omniscient value

The artifact market is not generally stupid.

If an artifact has an obvious, repeatable, demonstrable benefit, that benefit should usually be reflected in the price.

Examples:
- an artifact visibly closes wounds quickly;
- a barrier reliably stops arrows;
- a lamp produces strong light without fuel;
- boots clearly silence footsteps;
- a tool lifts loads ordinary labor cannot;
- a cooling stone demonstrably keeps food cold.

Those objects may still be underpriced relative to some deeper use, but they should not be cheap merely because Greg is the protagonist.

The market price should be based on what current buyers and sellers can reasonably observe, reproduce, believe, insure against, repair, use, and compare.

Greg's advantage becomes strongest where there is a gap between **market-visible value** and **latent value**.

## Five value layers

For meaningful artifact pricing, separate:

1. **Visible utility** — what the artifact obviously and repeatably does now.
2. **Visible risk** — known breakage, deaths, failures, upkeep, legal problems, activation limits, or other constraints buyers already understand enough to price.
3. **Latent utility** — hidden capability, alternate use, interaction, scale, future technique, provenance, or condition the present market does not recognize.
4. **Latent risk** — hidden downside, cumulative threshold, false diagnosis, environmental interaction, user-specific danger, or failure mode the market does not understand.
5. **Future / strategic value** — later maker reputation, future scarcity, later-discovered use, historical importance, compatibility with future objects, or a capability Greg values for reasons current buyers do not.

Price should mostly respond to layers 1 and 2, plus whatever portion of 3–5 has become credible public information.

## Obvious benefits should not receive bargain-bin pricing

Do not create easy deals by pretending sellers fail to notice spectacular visible effects.

If a healing artifact is known to heal rapidly, the market should value rapid healing.

If a weapon obviously cuts enchanted armor, buyers should care.

If a storage artifact reliably triples carrying capacity, merchants should care.

If a ward demonstrably stops a common dangerous magic, specialists should care.

Greg may still judge the price cheap because:
- he knows the effect scales further than people realize;
- he knows the supposedly narrow effect works against something far more important;
- he knows the maker becomes historically important;
- he knows a later technique removes part of the downside;
- he knows the item combines absurdly well with something else;
- he values the operating-rule change more than current buyers do;
- he knows the current market has misidentified what the artifact is actually doing.

But the visible part of the value should already cost money.

## Hidden upside creates real bargains

True underpricing should usually require an information gap.

Strong reasons an artifact may be underpriced include:
- the major upside is not known;
- the major upside is known only as rumor and has not been demonstrated;
- the artifact is being used for the wrong purpose;
- current users lack the technique needed to activate the valuable effect;
- the valuable effect appears only under rare conditions;
- the item's provenance is unknown or misidentified;
- a later-famous maker is currently obscure;
- a defect prevents the obvious intended use but enables a different use;
- the useful interaction requires another artifact or skill not normally paired with it;
- the current market does not yet have a problem that makes the capability valuable;
- a frightening reputation overwhelms interest before buyers investigate deeper utility.

The less visible the upside, the more plausible a deep bargain becomes.

## Why mispricing can survive

A valuable information gap needs a reason it has not already been arbitraged away.

Strong persistence reasons include:
- **activation rarity:** the useful effect appears only under conditions most owners never encounter;
- **missing complement:** the artifact becomes valuable only when paired with another object, technique, species, material, environment, or future invention;
- **wrong customer base:** current buyers value the obvious use, while Greg values an application outside the artifact's normal profession or social class;
- **poor authentication:** the object looks like common junk and its maker, generation, or provenance cannot be cheaply proved;
- **fragmented evidence:** failures and successes occurred in different places, under different names, or across records nobody has connected;
- **bad historical explanation:** people preserved the event but attached the wrong cause;
- **low reproducibility:** the hidden effect is real but difficult to demonstrate on demand, making buyers unwilling to pay heavily for it;
- **dangerous testing:** proving the upside safely would cost too much or risk death, so the market rationally discounts the claim;
- **future demand:** the capability becomes extremely valuable only after a later war, profession, creature migration, infrastructure change, legal change, or new technology creates demand;
- **institutional blind spot:** specialists evaluate the artifact according to intended use and therefore dismiss a cross-domain application;
- **social stigma:** the item is associated with deaths, criminals, failed adventurers, taboo practice, or embarrassment that suppresses demand even when capability exists;
- **maintenance bottleneck:** the current world cannot cheaply repair, recharge, or operate the item, while Greg knows a workaround or later route;
- **misleading success:** the obvious function is good enough that nobody has reason to search for another one.

Do not use `nobody ever tried` when thousands of rational people obviously would have tried.

The stronger the hidden value, the stronger the explanation should be for why the present market has not discovered or capitalized on it.

## Known downside can lower price rationally

An artifact can be visibly powerful and still sell below its apparent capability if the downside is severe enough.

Examples of market logic:
- excellent healing, but several users died under poorly understood circumstances;
- perfect concealment, but the user becomes unable to hear while active;
- extraordinary force, but activation breaks nearby glass and damages structures;
- valuable protection, but repair is rare and expensive;
- powerful effect, but the item is illegal in several districts;
- wonderful mobility, but failure causes catastrophic falls;
- reliable capability, but the user must accept a lifestyle-changing operating constraint.

In those cases, price is not low because buyers are foolish. It is low because many rational buyers conclude the package is not worth owning.

Greg may disagree because his use case, tolerance, old-life knowledge, body, allies, other equipment, or future workaround changes the equation.

## Unknown downside should not automatically depress price

If nobody knows the downside, the market generally cannot price it correctly.

An artifact with obvious healing and a completely unknown lethal light interaction may be expensive because buyers see only the healing.

This is important.

Hidden danger does not automatically mean cheap artifact.

The item becomes cheap only if some visible evidence of danger, failures, rumors, deaths, bans, poor reliability, or uncertainty has entered the market's beliefs.

Even then, the market may misprice the danger because it has the wrong explanation.

## Information confidence affects price

The market should distinguish between:
- demonstrated fact;
- specialist claim;
- repeated customer experience;
- documented accident;
- rumor;
- seller pitch;
- superstition;
- one unexplained death;
- famous provenance claim without authentication.

A credible demonstrated benefit commands more value than an unverifiable story.

A credible repeated danger depresses value more than one vague warning.

Authentication, reputation, demonstration, witnesses, documentation, and trusted specialists can therefore move price even when the artifact itself has not changed.

## Greg's valuation is not the market's valuation

For each important acquisition, distinguish at least three numbers conceptually even when the prose never states them explicitly:

- **market price** — what this seller can reasonably demand under current public knowledge;
- **Greg value** — what Greg would be willing to pay given his private knowledge, intended use, finances, and risk tolerance;
- **true value** — unknowable omniscient value, which the manuscript itself may not fully establish for a long time.

Greg does not need to know true value.

He only needs enough reason for his private valuation to differ from the market.

Sometimes he will be right.
Sometimes he will be partly right.
Sometimes the artifact will contain another layer neither Greg nor the seller knows.

## Bargaining follows the information gap

Greg's bargaining behavior should respond to what information would change the seller's price.

If the obvious benefit is already public, pretending not to notice it will not magically make the artifact cheap.

Better bargaining targets include:
- visible condition;
- uncertainty of provenance;
- repair cost;
- lack of authentication;
- inconvenient activation;
- limited current demand;
- known warning history;
- storage burden;
- legal risk;
- seller's need for liquidity;
- bundled lots;
- timing;
- the fact that Greg's *specific hidden use* remains private.

Greg can act uninterested to avoid revealing hidden value, but he cannot erase value everybody can already see.

## Acquisition-scene pricing test

Before shipping a meaningful artifact purchase, auction, bid, appraisal, or negotiation, ask:

1. What benefit is obvious to the current market?
2. What known downside is already priced in?
3. What upside remains hidden, disputed, misidentified, or undervalued?
4. What downside remains hidden or misunderstood?
5. Why is the seller's asking price rational under current information?
6. Why is Greg's private valuation different?
7. What specific information would raise the price if Greg revealed it?
8. Is Greg relying on actual asymmetric information, or merely on everyone else being inexplicably stupid?
9. If the item is spectacularly useful in an obvious way, is the absolute price high enough to reflect that?
10. If the item is deeply underpriced, what concrete information failure explains the gap?
11. Why has that information gap survived until now?

If questions 10–11 have no good answer, raise the price, weaken the hidden advantage, or improve the historical/market explanation.

## Forward economic effect

This logic should push Greg toward larger pools of capital.

Many of the best artifacts can be both:
- a bargain relative to their latent/future value;
- expensive enough in absolute terms that Greg cannot casually buy them.

That is the desired tension.

Greg should increasingly encounter situations where his thought is not `cheap item, easy win`, but:

**That price is brutal. It is also far below what this thing is worth to me.**

That creates urgency for better-paying work, reserves, bargaining skill, credit relationships, liquidation choices, travel, and economic optionality without turning the book into pure wealth accumulation.
