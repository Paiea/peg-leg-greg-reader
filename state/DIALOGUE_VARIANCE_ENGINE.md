# PEG-LEG GREG — DIALOGUE VARIANCE ENGINE

Durable craft logic for making recurring speech recognizable without making it mechanically identical from scene to scene.

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file. `state/voices/` owns recurring-character baseline voice evidence. `state/PROSE_PLAYBOOK.md` owns project-wide prose craft.

## Purpose

A character voice is not a fixed filter.

Use:

**SPOKEN MOMENT = BASE VOICE + RELATIONSHIP + CURRENT STATE + SCENE PRESSURE + SMALL HUMAN VARIANCE**

The baseline should remain recognizable across the manuscript. The surface realization should move.

Do not randomize for novelty. Vary only within plausible character range.

## 1. Base voice

The relevant `state/voices/<CHARACTER>.md` page supplies the stable center:
- cognitive habits;
- vocabulary and abstraction level;
- usual sentence length;
- confidence and certainty tolerance;
- humor source;
- information habits;
- what counts as a finished answer;
- professional or household lens;
- default directness, formality, patience, and social energy.

A baseline is a center of gravity, not a dialogue template.

## 2. Relationship modulation

People do not use one voice with everyone.

Before substantial dialogue, ask who the speaker believes they are talking to and what social relationship is active.

Possible changes include:
- speaking upward versus downward in status;
- customer voice versus coworker voice;
- stranger politeness versus household shorthand;
- authority performance versus private irritation;
- teasing permission;
- how much context is assumed;
- whether correction is blunt, softened, formal, indirect, or withheld;
- whether silence is comfortable;
- whether the speaker expects to be understood without explanation.

Repeated relationships may develop their own shared rhythm. Greg/Hessa, Greg/Lyssa, Greg/theatre peers, Hessa/subordinates, or two long-term coworkers should not sound like the same two isolated voice files merely taking turns.

Relationship rhythm is emergent. Do not create a separate relationship file unless the relationship is recurring and complex enough to justify durable memory.

## 3. Current-state modulation

Mood and cognitive load bend the baseline.

Relevant state can include:
- fatigue;
- embarrassment;
- anger;
- grief;
- distraction;
- attraction;
- pain;
- hunger;
- nervousness;
- excitement;
- shame;
- fear;
- being preoccupied by money, work, family, a mistake, or an unresolved argument;
- trying to impress, conceal, reassure, intimidate, or leave.

State should change rhythm before it changes identity.

Examples:
- a normally precise person may become clipped when exhausted;
- a terse worker may overexplain when defensive;
- a formal person may become more formal when threatened because formality is armor;
- a confident person may repeat themselves when frightened;
- Greg may fail to produce a dry comeback when something actually matters too much.

Do not make every mood produce an obvious speech tic.

## 4. Scene pressure

The task of the scene also changes speech.

A person may speak differently while:
- carrying something heavy;
- working with their hands;
- performing publicly;
- negotiating money;
- hiding information;
- being interrupted;
- speaking in front of a group;
- trying not to wake someone;
- rushing;
- being watched by authority;
- explaining their own domain;
- speaking outside their domain.

Let physical and social conditions affect sentence completion, response speed, and willingness to elaborate.

## 5. Small human variance

Preserve occasional productive imperfection.

People may:
- restart a sentence;
- choose a weak metaphor;
- explain past the useful stopping point;
- add a qualification after the answer is already clear;
- miss a joke;
- misunderstand a premise;
- answer the wrong part of a question;
- use a filler they do not usually lean on;
- tell a story instead of answering;
- become unusually terse;
- make an unexpectedly good joke;
- make a bad joke;
- abandon a sentence when the listener already understands;
- repeat themselves because they care, not because the prose forgot.

This is not permission for sloppy dialogue everywhere. Use human irregularity selectively so edited speech does not become uniformly optimized.

## Greg as comic gravity

Greg is the primary owner of PLG's compressed dry counterpunch register.

He often hears the structural absurdity in what somebody said, removes everything unnecessary, and returns the shortest useful or funniest version.

Other characters may absolutely be witty, dry, fast, or sarcastic. Do not make Greg the only funny person in Carrow.

But do not distribute his exact compression architecture evenly across the cast.

Supporting characters can be funny through:
- overexplaining;
- earnest literalism;
- bad metaphors;
- misplaced confidence;
- delayed comprehension;
- excessive formality;
- nervous qualification;
- storytelling;
- refusal to engage with Greg's joke;
- irritation arriving three beats late;
- practical indifference to a distinction Greg finds fascinating.

Greg is often funniest when the other person cannot, will not, or does not know how to play his game.

### Greg variance

Greg himself should not perform perfect Greg-ness every time.

His compression may weaken or change when:
- tired;
- genuinely angry;
- scared;
- embarrassed;
- talking to somebody he must carefully reassure;
- explaining a distinction he cares about;
- excited by a mechanism;
- deliberately making himself easier to understand;
- speaking to someone who did not follow the joke;
- with a person whose relationship permits softness or foolishness.

The absence of humor can itself become meaningful if Greg usually reaches for humor.

## Anti-sarcasm-academy check

A manuscript-wide failure mode is **comic register convergence**: everyone becomes equally good at short dry comebacks because the editor keeps sharpening every exchange.

When reviewing dialogue, ask:

1. Who owns the compression in this exchange?
2. Are multiple speakers landing similarly shaped short counters?
3. Is the supporting character funny in their own cognitive rhythm, or merely receiving Greg-shaped punchlines?
4. Would this person's response plausibly become longer, messier, more formal, more literal, slower, or less witty under the current relationship/state?
5. If Greg disappeared from the scene, would the scene still retain almost exactly the same sarcastic cadence?

If yes to 5, Greg's register may have leaked into the cast.

## Controlled-variance rule

Do not assign every recurring character one permanent quirk such as `rambling`, `formal`, `bad metaphors`, or `nervous fillers`.

Instead record tendencies in the voice page, then allow state and relationship to amplify, suppress, or occasionally invert them.

A useful target is:

**CONSISTENT PERSON, VARIABLE PERFORMANCE.**

Not:

**CONSISTENT GIMMICK, VARIABLE SCENE.**

## Editing rule

When a line is competent but too optimized, do not automatically make it longer.

First ask whether the speaker should:
- keep the clean line;
- miss the opening;
- take longer to reach the point;
- explain from a different professional or emotional frame;
- leave the thought incomplete;
- misunderstand Greg;
- refuse the premise;
- say something socially safer than what they mean;
- say too much because something else is on their mind.

Preserve speed and readability. Productive imperfection should create personhood, comedy, pressure, or relationship texture.

## Voice-page maintenance

When a recurring character has enough manuscript evidence, their voice page may record:
- baseline speech center;
- relationship-specific versions;
- common state-dependent shifts;
- what destabilizes or compresses their speech;
- what makes them unusually verbose, formal, blunt, hesitant, or playful;
- how they fail conversationally;
- whether they can actually keep up with Greg's compressed humor.

Record repeated evidence, not speculative personality matrices.

## Quick scene pass

For substantial dialogue, mentally resolve:

**WHO ARE THEY NORMALLY?**

**WHO ARE THEY TO THIS PERSON?**

**WHAT STATE ARE THEY IN RIGHT NOW?**

**WHAT IS THE SCENE MAKING HARD?**

Then allow one or two natural irregularities if the exchange is becoming too polished.

The goal is not noise. The goal is the feeling that each line was spoken by a person living through this particular moment rather than emitted by the project's dialogue style.
