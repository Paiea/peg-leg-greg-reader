# PEG-LEG GREG — ECONOMY CONTINUITY

This file is the durable economic-calibration layer for manuscript production, story control, and later editorial passes.

It is not a universal price list and does not outrank exact manuscript prose. Its purpose is to stop nominal coin amounts, wallet snapshots, and old scarcity beats from drifting away from the economic progression the story has already earned.

## Core direction

Greg's economic life should trend upward across the long serial.

That does NOT mean exponential wealth, RPG scaling, automatic raises, constant larger numbers, or every later purchase costing more. It means his economic position should gradually become more capable, more resilient, and more optional because of accumulated work, reputation, relationships, assets, knowledge, and access.

The desired long-range feeling is:

**Earlier Greg had to ask whether he could afford the thing. Later Greg increasingly asks whether the thing is worth the money, time, risk, storage, obligation, or opportunity cost.**

Temporary liquidity dips are allowed. Economic reset is not.

A bad purchase, travel choice, debt payment, missed job, or quiet work stretch can reduce cash on hand. It should not automatically restore early-book emotional poverty if Greg's broader earning capacity, social access, assets, and experience have improved.

## Separate five economic dimensions

Never use one purse number as a substitute for all economic reality.

Track these separately when relevant:

1. **Liquidity** — cash Greg can spend immediately.
2. **Earning capacity** — what kinds of paid work he can plausibly obtain, at what quality/rate/frequency.
3. **Assets / tools** — bag, holdfast, clothing, equipment, future magical items, and other durable things with use or possible value.
4. **Obligations / debt** — Vale debt, household responsibilities, promised work, repairs, recurring costs.
5. **Optionality / resilience** — whether Greg can refuse bad work, travel for pleasure, absorb a small loss, choose a better tool, wait for a better offer, or spend on something nonessential without the chapter treating it like disaster.

A low number in one dimension does not erase gains in the others.

## Economic progression shape

### Early-book mode: survival and immediate constraint
Small amounts can legitimately dominate decisions because Greg has little buffer, few routes to income, weak bargaining position, limited equipment, and unstable access.

### Middle-book mode: recurring access and selective constraint
Greg knows more people, has repeat work, understands Carrow better, has some equipment, can compare offers, and can sometimes choose leisure or refuse a bad fit. Small money still matters, but every bit/copper should not receive crisis-level emotional weight.

### Current / later mode: growing capacity without wealth fantasy
Greg can still be cash-light, indebted, annoyed by missed income, or make a purchase that strands capital. But ordinary low-tier prices should increasingly become background unless quantity, timing, principle, or cumulative cost makes them relevant.

Progression should show through:
- better or more trusted work;
- higher-value bounded tasks when earned;
- more reliable access to offers;
- ability to refuse poor-fit work;
- ability to spend on travel, tools, meals, gifts, or entertainment without every choice becoming a morality play;
- durable equipment and assets accumulating;
- larger purchases entering view naturally;
- debt becoming smaller relative to Greg's economic life even before it is literally gone;
- occasional losses becoming annoying rather than destabilizing;
- eventual use of larger denominations when exact prose earns the transition.

Do not force all of these at once.

## Prices should stay locally coherent while Greg changes

Do not create progression by inflating the whole world around Greg.

Bread does not need to become more expensive because Greg is more capable. A bath, simple meal, ordinary repair, common transport share, or cheap entertainment can remain cheap.

Instead, the story should increasingly expose higher-value layers:
- better tools;
- custom work;
- specialist labor;
- longer travel;
- valuable materials;
- substantial contracts;
- magical items;
- deposits / stakes / bids when established;
- major repairs;
- larger commercial obligations.

The economic ladder widens upward. The floor does not constantly rise.

## Denomination rule

Established currency families include copper / silver / gold, while recent prose also uses bits. Exact conversion rates are not durably established and must not be invented casually.

Until prose intentionally establishes conversion:
- preserve exact denominations as written;
- compare like with like where possible;
- use observed transaction anchors rather than fabricated exchange math;
- do not claim a bit is an exact fraction of a copper unless exact prose establishes it;
- do not use an unstated conversion to justify a price or emotional reaction.

If the story eventually needs conversion to make trade legible, establish it through an actual transaction or repeated ordinary usage, then record it here.

The current economic problem in Ch320 is not necessarily that one copper is an impossible portrait price.

The logic failure is treating **one copper = all current liquid cash = therefore one copper is intrinsically too expensive / Greg is effectively poor again.**

Correct framing:
- one copper can be a plausible price;
- Greg may be able to afford it in the literal sense;
- he can still decide he does not want the portrait one-copper badly;
- the choice should read as preference / optionality / opportunity cost, not early-book desperation;
- Lyssa should not automatically praise preserving the last copper as if spending it would be obviously reckless;
- Greg's current cash-light state should be acknowledged without letting it overwrite his broader progression.

If later price review concludes the portrait itself should cost more or less, change it against neighboring anchors, not merely to manufacture stakes.

## Emotional-weight calibration

Before giving a price narrative emphasis, ask:

1. Is this amount large relative to comparable established prices?
2. Is it large relative to Greg's current earning capacity, not only his purse at this exact second?
3. Does paying it block something else specific?
4. Is the choice important because of money, or because of value/preference/pride/risk?
5. Would Greg from 100 chapters earlier react differently? If yes, has the prose allowed his economic behavior to progress?

A price can matter without producing anxiety.

Useful reactions include:
- automatic payment;
- mild annoyance;
- bargaining for sport;
- deciding it is not worth it;
- paying because convenience matters;
- paying because he wants the thing;
- refusing because the seller's terms are bad;
- noticing a good deal without needing it;
- accepting a small loss and moving on.

Do not default to counting the purse after every transaction.

## Economic progression guardrail

Across roughly 20–40 chapter spans, inspect whether the economic story has actually moved.

Look for changes in:
- available job quality;
- pay size/context;
- frequency of paid opportunities;
- ability to say no;
- reserve / assets;
- debt pressure;
- discretionary spending;
- travel / tools / material access;
- social credit and referrals;
- consequences of losing a small amount.

The trend need not rise every span, but long stretches should not repeatedly reset Greg to the same tiny-stakes scarcity loop.

A downturn is strongest when the reader can feel what Greg had gained before losing or tying up some of it.

## Avoid super-scaling

Do not solve economic progression by making every later job pay ten times more.

Prefer staircase progression:
- lots of ordinary cheap things remain ordinary;
- Greg gets better at earning / choosing / saving / deploying money;
- occasional higher-tier transactions enter;
- capital purchases can tie up meaningful amounts;
- wealth changes social and practical options gradually;
- larger denominations appear only when the story's economic layer genuinely reaches them.

Even much later, Greg should still notice real prices. He just should not emotionally regress every time his purse happens to be light.

## Price-anchor discipline for new scenes

When a new chapter contains a meaningful price, wage, fee, debt credit, bid, or purchase:

1. Read the current money section of `MANUSCRIPT_STATE.md`.
2. Check this file's recent anchors.
3. Find at least one comparable prior transaction when practical.
4. Decide whether the new number is ordinary, favorable, expensive, specialist, subsidized, distressed, bundled, or otherwise context-specific.
5. Calibrate Greg's reaction against his current economic stage.
6. Record only materially useful new anchors here or in current state. Do not turn this into an exhaustive accounting ledger.

## Economic logic pass

A future dedicated pass should inspect earlier manuscript prices by broad era, not blindly normalize every number.

Goal:
- catch contradictions;
- identify accidental scarcity resets;
- identify transactions whose price category changed without explanation;
- preserve intentionally unusual deals;
- strengthen the felt upward progression of Greg's economic life;
- avoid rewriting hundreds of scenes into a rigid spreadsheet economy.

Prioritize scenes where money drives behavior. Background prices that create no contradiction can remain untouched.

## Current forward instruction

From Chapter 321 onward, do not use Greg's current `one copper + zero bits` snapshot as shorthand for destitution.

It is a liquidity fact after chosen spending and tied-up capital. It should create selective constraint, not erase earned economic progression.

Let future economic improvement emerge naturally through better access, better work, accumulated assets, reduced relative debt pressure, and occasional larger transactions. Do not immediately hand Greg compensatory cash merely to prove the new rule.
