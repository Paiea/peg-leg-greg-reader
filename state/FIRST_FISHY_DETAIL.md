# PEG-LEG GREG — FIRST FISHY DETAIL

Non-canon development guidance for the first serious outward offer.

The first thing that feels wrong should be human-scale and interpretable, not an obvious supernatural clue.

## Strong options

### The client is too careful about names
She gives location, timing, and payment clearly but avoids explaining the relationship between herself and the person to be found / retrieved.

Greg notices that she is not vague about the job. She is vague about why the job matters.

Working theory:
family embarrassment, old lover, inheritance, reputational mess, or a voluntary disappearance nobody wants described plainly.

### The target is described by function instead of relationship
`Bring him back.`
`Find out whether she is still there.`
`He is expected at the estate.`

Greg eventually asks the obvious question: expected by whom?

### The payment is structured oddly rather than simply high
Examples:
- generous advance but little completion bonus;
- client covers route, meals, and lodging unusually well;
- payment is higher if Greg does not ask the target certain questions;
- client values speed less than privacy;
- Greg is paid partly for accompanying another person rather than for retrieval itself.

This should suggest social risk before magical risk.

### The wrong person is nervous
The client seems controlled, but an intermediary / assistant / relative visibly hates the arrangement.

Greg notices the emotional mismatch and prices it as family or household conflict.

### The client does not actually need Greg's combat ability
This can make the offer more suspicious, not less.

If the job is supposedly routine, why specifically hire someone who can travel and handle danger?

The answer may still be mundane: bad route, unreliable escorts, need for discretion, or Person B refusing to travel alone.

## Preferred first use

Favor **relationship omission + oddly structured pay/access** over spooky clues.

The best Greg reaction is not `this is evil`.

It is closer to:

**This is somebody paying to avoid explaining something.**

That is fishy enough to notice and ordinary enough to accept if the rest of the terms make sense.
