## Local story / moʻolelo-like adventure engine
- An older passenger tells children **The Woman Who Returned the Water**.
- The story concerns **Iria**, an orchard-country woman in a dry year when families argued whether upper gates were withholding water or the spring itself was failing.
- Her grandmother gives her an older route preserved as a sequence of named places:
  1. **Lakeward shore**;
  2. **White Reed**;
  3. **Sleeping Stone**;
  4. **Three Sisters Hollow**;
  5. **Mirror Spring**.
- Iria carries a plain cup of lake water uphill and pours some out at each place, not as a magic refill ritual but as a story-form way of paying attention to where water signs change.
- In the story:
  - White Reed soil remains wet despite dry channel;
  - Sleeping Stone has moss on downhill face;
  - Three Sisters still holds frogs;
  - Mirror Spring is found partially blocked by storm-fallen root/stone.
- Iria clears enough for water to move and carries a black spring pebble back to the lake because `water remembers road both ways`; when a child objects that rocks do not remember, storyteller replies: `Then remember for rock.`
- Story ends socially rather than triumphantly: farmers still disagree about gate fairness even after spring blockage is found, so families establish marker stones and a norm that each family should walk the spring path before accusing another during future drought.
- Durable design intent: use Hawaiian/moʻolelo-like structural logic through place-name sequence, memory held in story, reciprocal journey, land/water observation, social obligation, and practical knowledge carried culturally. Do **not** paste Hawaiian gods/names/rituals into PLG or turn the book into borrowed sacred mythology.
