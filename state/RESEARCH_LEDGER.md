## Research principles

- Research real practice when it can replace generic invented texture.
- Prefer primary, institutional, expert, or strong craft sources where possible.
- Community anecdotes are useful for lived texture but must be labeled anecdotal.
- Do not paste long source text. Distill the useful principle and preserve the source link.
- Do not turn historical practice into mandatory fantasy-world history.
- Translate knowledge into behavior and constraints rather than exposition dumps.
- Keep contradictory evidence when it matters.
- If a fact changes with time, record the date checked.

## THEATRE — REHEARSAL NOTES, AUTHORITY, AND RECOVERY

**Question:**
How do actors in ensemble settings receive and use rehearsal notes, when are notes commonly delivered, who has authority to give them, and how do scene partners handle mistakes without making the mistake disappear?

**Source / authority:**
- Royal Shakespeare Company, `Stage management`
  https://www.rsc.org.uk/about-us/how-we-make-theatre/stage-management
- Royal Shakespeare Company, `Homework Help` — practitioner answers on rehearsal, notes, props, collaboration, repeated performance, and things going wrong onstage
  https://www.rsc.org.uk/learn/young-people/homework-help
- American Theatre, `On the Job` — professional-practitioner anecdotes including note timing and ensemble/company work
  https://www.americantheatre.org/2012/01/01/on-the-job/
- American Theatre, `Fiasco’s Rehearsal Room Joy and Rigor, Now Available in the Classroom`
  https://www.americantheatre.org/2019/12/17/fiascos-rehearsal-room-joy-and-rigor-now-available-in-the-classroom/
- Backstage, `Fill in the Blank` — working actors / coaches on line loss, objectives, scene partners, and recovery
  https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/fill-blank-15689/
- Kerry Hishon, Theatrefolk, `Communicating Rehearsal Notes in Three Different Ways`
  https://www.theatrefolk.com/blog/communicating-rehearsal-notes-in-three-different-ways

  https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/give-notes-60649/

  https://www.iamatheatre.com/professional-code-of-conduct

  https://www.pioneerdrama.com/Newsletter/Articles/Scene_Partners.asp

Sources rechecked / expanded 2026-08-31.

**Supported finding:**
- A common rehearsal pattern is to let a run-through complete while notes accumulate, then give verbal notes afterward; immediate physical review/fixes may happen after the run. This is a common practice rather than a universal rule.
- Note timing is itself a craft choice. A professional director quoted in American Theatre describes the difficulty of waiting until an actor is ready to hear a note. Not every survivable error benefits from instant correction.
- Rehearsal knowledge is collaborative and cumulative. RSC practitioners describe actors taking notes from the creative team and from other actors, collaborative rehearsal changes, and repeated work with text/props until choices become familiar.
- Professional theatre distributes practical responsibility. RSC stage management separates communication/cue work from props/costume responsibility. This supports domain-specific authority as a broad pattern, not copying a large-company org chart into Carrow.
- Experienced companies can develop very compressed director/actor shorthand. Scott Alan Evans describes long-running company relationships where a look or gesture can carry a note that would require more explanation with a new actor. Note delivery is actor- and situation-specific, and some adjustments may be given privately.
- Formal note authority is role-bounded in many contemporary companies. IAMA explicitly bars actors from giving fellow actors official notes, routes artistic notes through directors during rehearsal, and gives stage management a relay/maintenance role. Gettysburg similarly describes stage management as logistical conduit rather than an independent artistic director.
- Ensemble rehearsal is also peer learning. Fiasco practitioners emphasize needing other people, learning by watching others, and having room to fail together rather than treating rehearsal as isolated actor-to-director correction.
- Strong scene partners keep listening instead of anticipating known lines/choices. Several sources warn that thinking ahead or pre-empting a partner can disrupt the current scene.
- When a performer loses a line, practical advice emphasizes staying grounded in the scene objective / circumstances and using scene partners as anchors. Partners may ask a natural question, offer a re-cue, or make a limited improvisational bridge that returns the scene toward intended information without announcing the repair to the audience.
- Recovery does not erase the original error. The performance can be protected in the moment and the underlying timing, line, blocking, or choice problem can still be corrected afterward.

**Confidence / limits:**
Mixed institutional, professional-practitioner, trade, and theatre-education guidance. High confidence in the broad behavioral patterns: note timing varies, responsibility is distributed, ensemble shorthand develops, rehearsal is collaborative, and partners often cover live mistakes. Exact hierarchy and etiquette vary substantially by company, era, contract, director, actor, and production. Modern formal protocols are analogues for Carrow, not fantasy-world history.
