## High-value attribution findings

- Hazardous multi-person work with short commands needs heavier attribution because the reader must know who owns each physical system and decision.
- Stable two-person relationship scenes can remain light once rhythm is established; re-tag after interiority, movement, third-speaker entry, or subject pivot.
- Chapters 74-80: professional differentiation is often more valuable than bespoke diction.
- Chapters 81-82: long technical two-person scenes can breathe; re-tag at phase changes and after substantial interior/expository beats.
- Chapters 83-90: intimate/road two-person scenes can stay light; crowded scenes need tags mainly at returning-authority and relationship handoffs.
- Chapters 91-99: dense two-person instruction can stay nearly untagged when procedure keeps ownership stable; in three-person scenes tag the hinge, not every turn.
- Chapters 100-114: apparent attribution problems may actually be referent-continuity regressions. Check exact introductions before assigning identity.
- Chapters 115-124: crowd size alone is not an attribution problem. Anonymous background speakers can remain anonymous when individual ownership does not matter.
- Chapters 125-129: attribution and referent continuity are separate; failed replication should change claim strength; successful theatrical recovery does not erase a local rehearsal mistake.
- Chapters 130-138: shared short workflow words are not reliable voice identifiers; rehearsal and live performance have different continuity logic.
- Chapters 139-145: role labels are not identities; later naming can reveal overconfident pronoun normalization. Naming itself can reduce attribution load. Tour authority distributes by consequence rather than one hierarchy.
- Chapters 146-151: public presence does not automatically create live-show logic. Open rehearsal can still stop, diagnose, reset, and rerun. Paid performance cannot. A partner recovery may hide useful information in rehearsal but save the scene in performance.
- Chapters 152-155: not every omission needs recovery; shared workflow words inherit meaning from the active object/system; explicit refusal can bound otherwise aggressive social momentum; object custody can be real authority without exposition.
- Chapters 156-158: preserve downstream load, not every byte. Keyword prompts can retrieve function more cleanly than full-line feeding. Overchecking one dangerous fact can contaminate sequence. Backstage objects can carry attribution when handlers and routes remain visible.
- Chapters 159-163: private patron, public market, and worker-room audience are different dialogue environments. A patron is not automatically a scene partner. Venue can change the bill. Company fee, individual share, food, floor, tolls, stable cost, and collection money must not be collapsed. In crowded work scenes, jurisdiction plus object/space can carry attribution better than redundant tags.
- Chapters 164-167: prospecting and travel are dialogue environments too. Open-market interruptions can be speaker-clear through current transaction and position; collection mechanics can become performance mechanics; road authority stays with the person who owns physical consequence; and a referral card creates access to negotiation, not entitlement to a booking.
- Chapters 168-172: compact work dialogue can remain lightly tagged when transaction, object, physical system, or professional jurisdiction is already visible. Add attribution at genuine ownership resets, not mechanically after every terse reply. Booking terms, operating costs, road feasibility, and stage-effect craft remain separate voice domains even when several of those speakers share the same scene.
- Chapters 173-177: returning to a familiar place changes attribution context without erasing road-learned behavior. Stable Lyssa and Hessa two-person scenes can remain light; crowded backstage scenes remain clear when the active object routes ownership. Prepared effect, audience interpretation, and actual external magic must not be merged just because Greg sees an analogy. Intentional named-self lines such as imagined `Greg did magic.` and role distinction `Not Greg. Shopkeeper.` are not the same defect as the recovered first-person slips patched in Chapters 161-167.
