## Voice philosophy

People from the same town may sound like people from the same town. Do not manufacture maximal contrast or bespoke dialects. Important recurring characters should instead accumulate a slight persistent fingerprint across scenes.

Education/register may influence vocabulary and explanation style, but **education is not intelligence**. Trade expertise, household expertise, institutional education, theatrical experience, and street/local knowledge may produce different speech without ranking competence.

## High-value attribution findings

- Hazardous multi-person work with short commands needs heavier attribution because the reader must know who owns each physical system and decision.
- Stable two-person relationship scenes can remain light once rhythm is established; re-tag after interiority, movement, third-speaker entry, or a subject pivot.
- Chapters 74-80: **professional differentiation is often more valuable than bespoke diction**. Concise competent people can share Carrow language while remaining distinct because they decide different kinds of things.
- Chapters 81-82: long technical two-person scenes can breathe; re-tag at phase changes, after substantial interior/expository beats, and when authority re-enters.
- Chapters 83-86: intimate Greg/Lyssa and road Greg/Sevren exchanges need little tagging, but a third participant should trigger explicit ownership of the shared decision.
- Chapters 87-90: transient speakers can stay light when physically introduced immediately before their line. Highest-value tags belong to returning authority and relationship handoffs.
- Chapters 91-94: dense two-person instruction can remain nearly untagged when procedure keeps ownership stable. Differentiate experts by what kind of precision they own, not colorful diction.
- Chapters 95-99: one bare question may be the only ambiguous point in an otherwise clear three-person exchange. Tag the hinge, not the whole scene.
- Chapters 100-104: an apparent attribution problem may actually be a referent/continuity regression. Check earlier exact introductions before assigning identity.
- Chapters 105-109: once an unnamed recurring role's referent is source-settled, later pronoun drift is continuity noise. In crowded games, tag ownership pivots rather than every turn.
- Chapters 110-114: in crowded emergencies, transient workers can stay light when physically introduced; tag operational authority only where the safety decision would otherwise be unclear.
- Chapters 115-119: anonymous background speakers can remain anonymous when individual ownership is irrelevant. Tag when recurring authority enters that texture, a glance makes the wrong responder plausible, or a one-word reply has two credible owners.
- Chapters 120-124: **crowd size alone is not an attribution problem**. Workers can remain clear through physical introduction and local job ownership without bookkeeping tags.
- Chapters 125-129: attribution and referent continuity are separate problems; failed replication should change claim strength; successful theatrical recovery does not erase the local mistake.
- Chapters 130-138: shared short workflow words are not reliable voice identifiers by themselves; rehearsal and live performance have different continuity logic.
- Chapters 139-145 add three refinements. First, **role labels are not identities**: later naming can reveal that an earlier pronoun normalization was overconfident. Second, **naming itself can reduce attribution load**: once Davin, Serra, Iven, Marek, and Orin are named, crowded road scenes can stay light. Third, **tour authority distributes by consequence** rather than hierarchy: Teren routes show/company consequences, Davin physical transport, Nessa performed costume continuity, Serra playable actor relationship, and Marek performer/social momentum.
