# CHAPTER 181

## THE CALLER

Lyssa was gone before I woke, but she had left evidence.

A loose piece of blue thread, maybe the length of my hand, had escaped whatever bundle she was carrying and caught beneath the cup. I picked it up, considered it, and discovered that it was blue.

I had no further useful observations.

I put it back under the cup and left.

Carrow had already committed to being loud. A cart wheel complained somewhere toward the market. A man at the end of the lane was selling hot cakes with enough enthusiasm to make distance useless. Two women argued over a basket while a boy ran past with three prepared lamp wicks under one arm and nearly hit me.

"Sorry," he called without slowing.

East Market Hall's charcoal sign was gone.

Mostly.

GREG remained as a gray stain beside the latch. I rubbed it once with my thumb.

"Don't start."

Rinna was inside the door.

"I wasn't."

"You were touching it."

"That's not starting."

"Good. We need you."

That was rarely good.

"What am I?"

"Calling second and third."

"What happened to whoever calls second and third?"

"Not here."

"Who?"

"Jori."

"Do I know Jori?"

"No."

"Then how do I know his job?"

"You don't."

She handed me a folded page covered in lines, marks, names I knew, and names I didn't.

"Rinna."

"Yes."

"I don't know this."

"I know."

"That's comforting."

"You're not calling first."

"Why?"

"Because first is complicated."

"Also comforting."

She pointed toward the stage. "Pell will show you second. Veya knows third. Do not invent anything."

"I don't invent things."

Rinna looked at me.

"At work."

Then she left.

Pell was under the stage. I knew because his voice came through the ghost tube.

"Greg."

I looked toward the dark corner of the hall.

"Under here."

There was a square opening beside the stage steps.

"Why are you under there?"

"Tube."

"Why?"

"Ghost."

"I understand the nouns."

His head appeared in the opening.

"We moved the outlet."

"Why?"

"People could see it from the left benches."

"I thought they couldn't."

"They could."

"Did someone complain?"

"No. Nessa sat there."

That was worse.

He disappeared again.

Getting under the stage required more planning than Pell had apparently considered worth mentioning. I backed to the stage edge, lowered myself until I could sit, and set both crutches beside me. Pell took the left one without asking and leaned it against a beam below. I kept the other close until my intact right foot found the first step. Then I lowered myself one step at a time, right foot taking weight, left knee tucked clear, palms doing more work than I wanted.

The space beneath the stage was high enough for Pell to crouch. It was not high enough for me to do anything gracefully. Dust stuck to my hands before I was fully down.

"Second piece," Pell said.

"What is second?"

"The Dead Brother."

"That's cheerful."

"Ghost is the brother."

"Who is dead."

"Usually."

"Usually?"

"Don't worry about it."

I worried about it.

Pell showed me the page. Four marks mattered: curtain open, lamp lower, ghost, door.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"Why does Rinna have a whole page?"

"Other people."

"Ah."

He tapped the ghost mark.

"When Nessa says, 'I heard him on the stair,' you point at me."

"Why?"

"So I talk."

"You need me to point at you?"

"I can't hear her well from here."

"Then how do you know when to talk normally?"

"Someone points."

"Jori."

"Usually."

"Who isn't here."

"Yes."

I looked at the tube. "So my job is pointing."

"Second piece."

"Excellent."

"And curtain."

"Less excellent."

Pell grinned.

Getting out was the same problem in reverse. Pell passed my crutches up before climbing after me, and I used the stage edge, my right foot, and more shoulder than I liked to get back onto the boards.

Then the curtain became my problem.

It was not heavy. It was awkward. The pull ran along the wall beside the stage and around a wooden wheel overhead. Jori apparently stood there while watching the stage, watching the side door, listening for Nessa, and avoiding the minor professional embarrassment of dropping a curtain on an actor.

I tried once and let it out too quickly.

The curtain slapped the stage.

Everyone stopped.

Nessa looked up from the coat she was pinning.

"No."

"I noticed."

"Again."

Pell reset it.

The second attempt was worse in a different direction. The curtain crawled.

Sellen, waiting behind it, said, "I will die before the scene begins."

"You're not in this one."

"I'll die anyway."

"Quiet," Nessa said.

We tried a third time. The curtain opened at a speed nobody commented on.

Apparently that was success.

My right shoulder was already beginning to object.

"You pull with both hands?" I asked.

"Jori does," Pell said.

"I don't have both hands if I'm standing."

"You can sit."

There was a low box against the wall. I sat, set both crutches within reach, planted my right foot hard against the floor, and tried the pull again. The new angle let my leg brace me while my hands worked the cord instead of asking my shoulder to fight my entire balance.

"Better," I said.

Pell shrugged.

Nobody congratulated me for discovering sitting down.

Good.

The third piece was The Petition, which I knew because I was Third Man.

Apparently not today.

Veya took the page from me. "You know your own entrance."

"Yes."

"You're not in it tonight."

I stared at her.

"What?"

"You're calling."

"But I'm Third Man."

"Not tonight."

"Why?"

"Because Jori isn't here."

"Can I be both?"

"No."

"I could."

"No."

"I know the scene."

"Good. Then you know when Sellen enters."

There was a mark beside my old line.

NEIGHBOR.

"You want me to call Sellen."

"I want you to point at Sellen."

"Why is all theatre pointing?"

"Because if you shout his name the audience hears you."

Fair.

Across the backstage passage, Sellen was putting on the neighbor coat. He looked at me.

I pointed.

"Not now."

"I was practicing."

"Don't."

Veya drew a small line on the page. "Watch me. When I put the petition down, point to the reed. After the reed, Sellen. Do not anticipate."

"I don't anticipate."

Sellen laughed.

"Nobody asked you."

"You're not in the scene."

"I know."

That hurt more than it should have.

Before the doors opened, Nessa made me walk the backstage route once without the page. She needed to know whether I could get from the curtain box to the side gap quickly enough without blocking the costume change.

Barely.

The narrowest point was between a scenery brace and a basket of folded cloth. My left crutch cleared. The right one caught.

"Move the basket," I said.

"No."

"Why?"

"Quick change."

"Move the brace."

"No."

"Why?"

"Wall."

I looked at the passage.

"So I don't fit."

"You fit differently."

Not inspiring. Also true.

I turned sideways, planted both crutches ahead of me, braced through my right leg, and made two short hops through the gap.

Nessa watched.

"Do that."

"I hate that."

"Do it anyway."

By the third trip my palm was sore where the grip rubbed. I wrapped a strip of scrap cloth around it and kept moving.

The hall filled around us while everyone else continued having jobs. Nessa had yesterday's wrapped parcel open on a table. Inside were three narrow rolls of pale cloth and a packet of black hooks. The woman with paint on her wrists stood beside her, and the two of them were discussing whether the hooks were too bright while people crossed behind them with lamps, clothes, pages, and things I had learned not to touch.

Teren had also won something from the baker. Trays of small rolls occupied the back room.

"Four bookings?" I asked.

"Three evenings and an afternoon," he said.

"That's four."

He pointed at me.

"Don't."

I reached for a roll.

He slapped my hand.

"After."

"You're cruel."

"Work."

For ten minutes, while Rinna found change, work meant the front door.

A woman stopped with her daughter beside her. Behind them, three men were arguing about whether two copper was too much.

"Is the ghost real?" the woman asked.

Yesterday I would have said Pell.

Today I looked at the girl and said, "You'll hear him."

Her face lit up. The woman bought two tickets.

I felt guilty for almost half a second.

One of the men behind them asked, "Is it frightening?"

"Not very."

Rinna kicked my crutch.

"Ow."

"Sell."

"It's not frightening."

"People like frightened."

"Do you want me to lie?"

"No. Stop finishing every answer."

The men bought tickets anyway. One of them pointed at me and said, "If it's bad, I'm blaming you."

"That's fair."

Rinna sighed.

The young actor from yesterday passed us carrying two lamp stands.

"Move the ticket box with magic."

"No."

"Bad magic."

"Yes."

He kept walking.

That was apparently the local form of a paper-magic rumor now. Nobody gathered. Nobody asked for a demonstration. The ticket box remained exactly where ordinary hands had put it.

The first piece began without me.

Hara covered the first call. She had apparently done it before. I watched her more than the play because she barely seemed to move and still knew where everyone needed to be. She checked the stage, the side traffic, and the page without making any of it look like a decision.

Annoying.

When the piece ended, she handed me the curtain cord.

"Don't wrap it around your wrist."

"Why?"

She turned her hand and showed me a pale scar crossing the inside of her wrist.

"Ah."

Then she left to change costume.

Second piece.

The Dead Brother.

I sat on the low box with my crutches leaned within reach, the page on my knee, and my right foot set where I could brace for the curtain. Pell was beneath the stage. Nessa stood onstage in a dark dress I had seen hanging from a nail that morning.

The room quieted.

Curtain.

I pulled. Good enough.

Lamp.

I pointed, and the lamp boy lowered the left lamp too far. Nessa's face nearly disappeared.

I pointed up.

He stared at me.

I pointed up harder.

The lamp rose. Nessa kept speaking.

A chair crossed. The door closed. Nessa stopped beside the painted stair.

"I heard him on the stair."

I pointed at Pell.

Nothing.

I pointed again.

Still nothing.

Then my stomach dropped because I finally understood what I was doing. Pell was under the stage. I was pointing at the floor as though he could see through it.

There was a narrow gap beside the curtain. Jori had pointed through that.

I leaned to reach it, shifted too far, and caught the tip of one crutch in the curtain. The fabric twitched. Nessa looked toward the dark stair and never looked at me.

I freed the crutch, got my hand into the gap, and pointed.

The ghost spoke.

"Nessa."

I knew it was Pell. I knew he was crouched under the stage and that the leather tube ran beneath my feet to the outlet hidden behind the dark flat.

My eyes went to the stair anyway.

Nessa froze.

"You're dead."

"I know."

Someone in the audience made a small involuntary sound, not quite a scream. More like they had forgotten to breathe and remembered badly.

Then I remembered the door.

I pointed.

The door operator did not move.

I pointed again.

Wrong person.

The operator was on the other side. Veya had told me. I had forgotten it.

I reached across and tapped the wooden frame twice.

Apparently that was not the cue either.

The door stayed open.

Nessa crossed toward it. She was supposed to close it on the next line, but it was already open too wide. For one ugly second I could see part of the mechanism behind the painted stair from where I sat.

The audience could not.

Probably.

Nessa caught the door herself and slammed it.

The sound was better than the planned one.

The audience jumped.

Pell whispered through the ghost tube.

"Good."

I wanted to kill him.

The rest of the piece worked.

When the curtain closed, Nessa came offstage and walked directly to me.

"Wrong side."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Now."

"Good."

She kept walking.

Pell crawled out from under the stage.

"You pointed at the floor."

"You were under the floor."

"Yes."

"So it was reasonable."

"No."

"I hate theatre."

"No, you don't."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because you keep lying."

Then came The Petition.

I was still irritated that Tovin had Third Man. I had spent two days with that coat and that entrance, which apparently was enough for me to become possessive about a man whose principal problem was someone else's goat.

My goat.

The goat remained theoretical.

Tovin entered after Veya put the petition down and I pointed to the reed. The reed sounded. I pointed at Tovin.

"My neighbor's goat has eaten half my fence and all my patience."

Wrong.

He had reversed my line.

Sellen looked at me from the wing. I almost laughed.

Tovin kept going. Sellen entered on neighbor anyway. Nothing collapsed. The scene simply moved around the sentence.

I was offended.

Yesterday Sellen had used the father line. Today he replaced it with something else. The audience laughed, but less. Then Veya looked at him over the petition with exactly the right amount of exhausted contempt and got the bigger laugh without saying anything.

I sat behind the curtain, pointed when I was supposed to point, and discovered somewhere in the middle of it that I was enjoying myself.

That seemed unfair.

After the third piece, my right shoulder hurt enough that pretending otherwise had become stupid. I leaned both crutches against the wall and rubbed it.

Davin appeared beside me. I had not seen him all day.

"Pulling?"

"Curtain."

He looked at the cord and then up at the wooden wheel.

"Bad angle."

"I sat."

"Better."

"Thank you."

He reached up, touched the wheel, frowned, and started away.

"Are you fixing it?"

"Tomorrow."

"What's wrong with it?"

"Worn."

"Was it worn yesterday?"

"Yes."

"Why tomorrow?"

He looked back.

"Because today is today."

Then he disappeared toward the rear yard.

Cleanup took longer because somebody had spilled lamp oil near the prop shelf. Nothing caught fire.

That was the useful part.

Everything else kept happening at once. Nessa stopped cloth traffic through the spill while sand and rags came out. The three pale rolls went down, the black hooks went back into their parcel, and someone carried something else around the long way because Nessa pointed once and nobody argued. Elsewhere pages were being stacked, lamps were being handled, and the fake loaf vanished to somewhere I did not see.

When the floor was clean enough for Nessa, the cloth started moving again.

Rinna paid me one copper.

"Caller."

"I was bad."

"You were adequate."

"I missed the ghost."

"It arrived."

"I missed the door."

"It closed."

"Nessa closed it."

"Yes."

"That means I was bad."

"That means the show happened."

She put the copper in my hand.

"Jori back tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

I stared at her.

She smiled.

"Don't."

Outside, the charcoal sign had not been rewritten. Instead somebody had added a tiny drawing beside the gray GREG stain: a little piece of paper with lines around it as though it were flying.

I left it.

Carrow was cooling by the time I found Lyssa two streets from home carrying blue thread.

Not a bundle.

Six bundles, looped over her arm.

"You carried blue thread."

"I told you."

"I didn't understand the scale."

"Move."

I moved.

She shifted two bundles onto me.

"I have crutches."

"You have an arm."

"Pell said that about a tube."

"Who?"

"Nobody important."

We started walking again.

"What did you do?" she asked.

"Called."

"Called what?"

"Ghost."

She stopped.

"You summoned a ghost?"

"No."

"Good."

"It was Pell."

"I don't know who that is."

"Exactly."

She started walking. I carried two bundles of blue thread home because, despite several strong objections, I did in fact have an arm.

There was a note under the door.

Guild paper.

I knew before I opened it.

Lyssa set down her bundles.

"Paper?"

"Different paper."

I unfolded it.

Hessa's handwriting.

TOMORROW. AFTER MIDDAY.

NEW FINS.

DO NOT BRING PELL.

I stared at it.

Lyssa leaned over my shoulder.

"Who is Pell?"

"I may have mentioned him."

"To Hessa?"

"Possibly."

"Why?"

"I don't remember."

She took the note, read it once, and handed it back.

"She knows you."

"Unfortunately."

I put it beside my question paper.

ONE MOVEMENT.

NOT REPEATED.

ONE FIN WAS EASIER TO MOVE.

Now Hessa wanted new fins tomorrow.

My shoulder hurt. Blue thread covered half the table again.

Lyssa began unlooping the remaining bundles from her arm.
