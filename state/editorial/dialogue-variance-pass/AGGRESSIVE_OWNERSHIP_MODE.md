# PLG AGGRESSIVE DIALOGUE OWNERSHIP MODE

Status: **APPROVED / ACTIVE**

Governing authority: `state/DIALOGUE_VARIANCE_ENGINE.md`
Project-wide prose mirror: `state/PROSE_PLAYBOOK.md`

Purpose: eliminate reader backtracking caused by ambiguous speaker, addressee, action, reaction, pronoun, object/task, interruption, scene-reset, reported-speech, identity-label, or paragraph ownership. PLG intentionally prefers slight over-attribution and modest local scaffolding expansion over elegant ambiguity.

These fifteen updates are active for all future dialogue/variance review and any later reader-facing dialogue polish.

## 1. Forced re-anchor cadence

Re-establish speakers aggressively after interiority, narration, subject shifts, speaker returns, and crowded exchanges. Three-or-more-speaker scenes must not require turn-counting.

## 2. One dramatic owner per paragraph

Do not let another character's movement, reaction, look, or action silently ride inside the current speaker's paragraph. Split mixed-owner blocks.

## 3. Orientation scaffolding may expand

A clarity repair may add a small amount of plain observable prose around good dialogue. One to three orienting sentences or beats are acceptable when needed to establish who is facing whom, who owns the object/task, who a question targets, or who reacts.

This added writing is not considered bloat when it removes reader bookkeeping.

## 4. Decompress packed dialogue before rewriting speech

Preserve good spoken lines first. Split dense multi-speaker blocks, restore plain tags, add orientation where needed, and only then rewrite dialogue if the rebuilt exchange is still confusing or out of voice.

Do not later strip these clarity additions merely to recover word count.

## 5. Cold-read ownership gate

A reviewed chapter should support zero ownership backtracking at normal reading speed. Every substantial exchange must make speaker and action/reaction ownership effortless from local context, with the strictest scrutiny on three-or-more-speaker scenes and Greg-interiority interruptions.

If there is plausible hesitation, prefer an explicit tag, repeated name, paragraph split, or plain orientation beat.

## 6. Active-cast and re-entry anchoring

In substantial group dialogue, keep the active local cast legible. Anchor new entrants, meaningful exits, and previously silent/off-focus people when they re-enter the exchange after the reader could reasonably have dropped them from the scene.

Do not write roll calls. One plain tag or observable beat is enough.

## 7. Short-line attribution floor

Treat one-to-three-word replies such as `No`, `Yes`, `Good`, `Wait`, `Again`, `Fine`, and `What?` as high-risk ownership lines in three-or-more-speaker scenes.

Tag/name them by default unless the immediately preceding addressed line makes the speaker impossible to mistake. Do not permit anonymous short-line ladders to become a speaker puzzle.

## 8. Explicit addressee for shared-target speech

A clear speaker is not enough when a question, command, warning, correction, offer, or invitation could target several people.

Name or visibly orient toward the addressee. If someone else answers or interrupts, anchor that person explicitly.

## 9. Pronoun-collision rule

When two or more active characters could plausibly match the same pronoun, repeat the proper name, role, or unambiguous noun at the ownership point.

Repeated names are acceptable scaffolding. Resume pronouns only after the referent is secure again.

## 10. Object/task handoff continuity

When dialogue logic depends on who currently has a prop, tool, paper, artifact, money, food, equipment, or work task, keep that ownership locally visible.

State important transfers, re-anchor the current holder/operator when needed, and add a small spatial reset after meaningful movement if the technical exchange would otherwise become muddy.

Do not inventory the whole scene. Track only objects, positions, and tasks that are carrying dialogue logic.

## 11. Interruption and resumption anchoring

When one person cuts another off or unexpectedly takes the floor, anchor the interrupter. If the original speaker resumes afterward, re-anchor them too rather than making the reader preserve the abandoned turn map.

Do not rely on punctuation alone to show who interrupted whom.

## 12. Collective-reaction discipline

Collective reactions are safe only when they are genuinely collective and no later dialogue depends on one person's specific reaction.

If one person is about to speak, disagree, move, or take the task because of the reaction, name that person rather than hiding them inside `they`, `everyone`, or `the room`.

## 13. Scene and beat reset anchoring

After a scene break, time/location shift, long descriptive/action passage, or other substantial non-dialogue reset, treat the next dialogue as a fresh local ownership block.

Explicitly establish the first speaker and, when needed, the addressee or local cast. Do not rely on a speaker map from before the reset.

## 14. Reported and quoted speech separation

Keep the present speaker distinct from any person whose words they are reporting or quoting.

Anchor the current speaker, name the reported source when needed, avoid confusing nested quotation structures, and re-anchor the present conversation when the report ends.

## 15. Stable local identity labels

Within an active scene, do not rotate labels for the same person merely for stylistic variety.

Once named, prefer the name at ownership points. Use role labels when the role matters, not just to avoid repeating the name. Stable repeated labels are cheaper than making the reader wonder whether `Nessa`, `the clerk`, and `the woman` are different people.

## Repair priority

1. Preserve canon and scene outcome.
2. Preserve good spoken dialogue.
3. Split paragraph ownership.
4. Add plain `said` / `asked` attribution.
5. Clarify the addressee when necessary.
6. Repeat a name/role instead of relying on a colliding pronoun or rotating identity label.
7. Anchor interruptions, resumptions, and post-reset first speakers.
8. Add observable orientation scaffolding.
9. Restore cast/object/task continuity where it carries the exchange.
10. Separate present speech from reported/quoted speech layers.
11. Rewrite the smallest necessary spoken wording only if the rebuilt block still fails.

## Anti-regression rules

- Repeated `said` / `asked` is acceptable.
- Repeated names are acceptable.
- Stable identity labels are preferred over stylish label rotation.
- Do not vary tags for decoration.
- Distinct voice is not a substitute for attribution.
- Short dry lines do not get special permission to remain anonymous.
- Do not use invented gestures solely to avoid a tag.
- Do not rely on punctuation alone to carry interruption ownership.
- Do not assume a pre-reset speaker map survives a scene/time/location or long-action reset.
- Do not let group nouns conceal the individual who is about to own the next important beat.
- Do not compress a repaired dialogue block during the same clarity pass.
- Do not classify added ownership scaffolding as redundant prose solely because it repeats a name, addressee, object, spatial fact, or local cast fact.
- A silent or off-focus character should not reappear as a ghost speaker.
- The reader should never have to reread a line to discover who spoke, who was addressed, who interrupted, who resumed, who moved, who reacted, who is being quoted, or who currently owns the object/task driving the exchange.

## Future-pass instruction

When continuing the whole-manuscript dialogue/variance pass, apply all fifteen rules automatically. A chapter may receive a deliberate no-change verdict only after the cold-read ownership gate passes under this full aggressive mode.
