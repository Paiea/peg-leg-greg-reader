## Durable craft findings

### 1. First-contact perceptiveness should be expensive

A stranger can notice obvious behavior. They should not instantly possess the same interpretive shorthand as Greg's closest recurring relationships.

Useful first-contact evidence:
- where Greg is standing;
- what he is holding;
- what he just asked;
- whether he stopped moving;
- whether he is staring at the object;
- whether he is interrupting the work;
- whether he has started redesigning the process out loud.

### 2. Commercial boundaries are a distinct source of voice

Dorrin and Holl are positive references when they sound like people protecting transactions, customer information, sale bands, shop time, and product promises.

That is different from Hessa narrowing evidence and different from Antonius controlling exposure or repayment.

### 3. Worksite correction should often name the next action

Rusk is strongest when he says where the rocks can go, why Greg is carrying, or who needs another body. Mevi is strongest when she makes Greg put the sack down and shows the lift. Holl is strongest when he slides the tray back and tells Greg what part of the process he is actually willing to discuss.

### 4. Repetition can be healthy when the job justifies it

Do not confuse repeated short language with convergence automatically.

Healthy repetitions in this batch include:
- Mevi's immediate lift correction;
- Lessa's custody answers;
- Hessa's casting stop conditions;
- Pessa's road problem framing.

The issue is whether the repeated form carries a real decision or merely serves as familiar comic punctuation.

### 5. Mundane complications create voice for free

The wrong office, a chicken under someone's arm, storage fees on useless rocks, a hot wheel, a dog finding a pig, and an onion at lunch all force people to respond from ordinary priorities.

Protect these scenes from being over-polished into protagonist-centered banter.
