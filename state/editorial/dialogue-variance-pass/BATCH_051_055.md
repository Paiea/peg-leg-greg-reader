## Durable craft findings

### 1. A line should earn its knowledge from the scene

Before asking whether a line is funny, ask how the speaker knows the thing the line claims.

Strong evidence sources include:
- direct physical observation;
- repeated shared history;
- institutional records;
- explicit secondhand information;
- professional responsibility;
- something Greg just said or did.

A polished line that requires unexplained psychological access is more likely to flatten the cast.

### 2. Institutional memory is not personal intimacy

The Guild can know Greg is useful at lane support because reports say so. A field clerk can know he was involved in road work. That does not mean every Guild employee has a rich model of Greg's personality.

Let records create opportunities. Let relationships create intimacy.

### 3. Rules are often better than predictions

A bathhouse worker does not need to predict that Greg personally will wet a bandage. She can state the rule and the replacement-cloth charge.

A field lead does not need to predict that Greg will redesign a road. She can state who owns repair-plan changes.

This keeps authority reusable and believable.

### 4. Recurring friends should get more specific as they know Greg better

Jorren and Alden have earned perceptiveness. That should make their observations more concrete, not more generic.

Jorren can remember exactly what Greg promised at the bathhouse. Alden can see the hand switch. Specific history is stronger friendship texture than `the face`.

### 5. Literal `face` is not the same bug as psychological `the face`

Do not mechanically purge the word.

Berren saying `Face` because Greg shaved his beard is literal appearance and works. The problematic shorthand is when `face` becomes a universal explanation for hidden intention, the next question, or an entire mental state.
