## Durable craft findings

### 1. A specialist's stopping condition should come from what they protect

Holl should stop Greg from changing the fixture because Holl needs a clean enough answer before paying to change the shop. Hessa should stop shaping because minimal-draw success has not earned that permission. The protected object often supplies stronger dialogue than a personality read.

### 2. Simple social answers deserve to remain simple

Jorren can say a shirt looks good without performing analysis. Merrin can say `Nice.` Alden can say `It's a shirt.` Ordinary social asymmetry helps protect Greg's tendency to over-model without requiring everyone else to comment on that tendency.

### 3. Repeated `No` is safest when the noun changes

The keeper's kitchen `No`s work because each one protects something different: pot, knife, leg, pan distance, floor, cleanup. Repetition becomes flattening when the same `No` repeatedly means `I know the sentence you have not said yet.`

### 4. Visible setup can carry the joke

A blanket on the cot, a board on Greg's lap, a hot pan near his body, or a sticky tray on a bench already provides evidence. The supporting character often needs only to react to the setup rather than interpret Greg's personality.

### 5. Underperformance remains useful social variance

A friend, clerk, trainer, or healer does not need to maximize emotional or verbal response. Jorren's plain shirt approval and Hessa's plain `Good` can be more relationship-true than another polished comeback.
