# PAY / VALUE / RISK ENGINE

Status: **AUTHOR APPROVED**

Purpose: calibrate compensation to the actual value, scarcity, urgency, and risk of Greg's contribution rather than to his current purse or the denomination most recently used in prose.

This is not a wage table and does not establish currency conversion.

## Core rule

**Pay follows value created, moved, protected, unlocked, or risked.**

Greg's liquidity does not determine what his work is worth.

A cheap job can remain cheap even late in the book. A valuable job can pay silver or gold even early in the book.

Canonical early proof:
- ordinary Bronze/manual work can pay copper;
- Antonius pays Greg **6 silver** for the gauntlet appraisal because it is outside debt labor and materially useful to a valuable lending decision;
- specialist services around Greg already cost multiple silver;
- Vale's ordinary commercial world already contains gold-scale decisions.

Therefore the manuscript should not flatten all labor into one denomination band.

## Compensation axes

Before assigning pay, score the scene qualitatively across these axes.

### 1. Replaceability
Ask who else can do the job.

Low:
- generic porter;
- ordinary watch body;
- simple runner;
- extra theatre hands.

High:
- Greg-specific historical knowledge;
- unusual appraisal judgment;
- cross-domain coordination;
- trusted intermediary work;
- specialist magical/artifact inference;
- relationship/reputation access unavailable to a generic worker.

The less replaceable Greg is, the less plausible generic labor pay becomes.

### 2. Underlying value
Ask what quantity of money/property/operations Greg's work affects.

Examples:
- moving one cheap sack is low-value labor;
- preventing a silver/gold shipment from being stranded is not;
- restructuring a deal worth silver is more valuable than carrying the goods;
- identifying whether collateral can support a gold-scale loan can justify specialist pay even if the work takes an hour.

Do not pay only for time when the job exists because judgment changes a valuable decision.

### 3. Failure cost
Ask what happens if Greg is wrong or absent.

Low:
- another worker needs to finish the task;
- minor delay;
- cheap object damaged.

High:
- contract fails;
- valuable cargo is seized/lost;
- specialist time is wasted;
- business misses a deadline;
- artifact is damaged;
- people are injured;
- legal exposure increases;
- a client loses substantially more than Greg's fee.

Higher failure cost generally supports higher compensation.

### 4. Urgency
Urgent work can command a premium when the buyer has fewer alternatives.

Do not make every urgent job lucrative. Ask whether urgency actually weakens the buyer's alternatives or raises the consequence of delay.

### 5. Scarce trust / responsibility
Greg can be paid for being the person a party trusts to:
- hold an operational picture;
- connect two sides;
- keep authorities separated correctly;
- report accurately;
- carry sensitive information;
- coordinate specialists;
- make bounded decisions without overclaiming.

Responsibility is not ownership, but it has value.

### 6. Capital at risk
If Greg must advance money, hold inventory, post a bond, finance a purchase, or tie up scarce capital, compensation should reflect that capital risk.

A commission with zero capital exposure can be smaller than an ownership position while still being lucrative.

### 7. Physical / legal / reputational risk
Risk is part of price.

If Greg can be:
- arrested;
- fined;
- blacklisted;
- injured;
- robbed;
- retaliated against;
- exposed to criminal counterparties;
- deprived of future legitimate work;

then pay comparable to routine safe labor requires a concrete explanation.

## Legal / gray / illegal calibration

Crime does not automatically pay well.

Petty illegal work can still be bad money because:
- participants are desperate;
- goods are cheap;
- replaceability is high;
- exploitation is common;
- risk is misunderstood;
- the real reward is access/information/favor.

But meaningful criminal exposure needs a real premium.

### Suggested relative bands, not rates

**Petty illegal favor / cheap contraband**
- can remain copper or low silver when stakes and value are genuinely small;
- Greg should increasingly refuse this if risk outweighs reward.

**Real smuggling / gray freight / deliberate rule evasion**
- should normally move into silver-scale compensation when discovery has meaningful consequences;
- several silver may be plausible for bounded, replaceable work;
- higher value/risk should rise quickly.

**Serious illicit logistics / valuable contraband / institutional exposure**
- **tens of silver are a plausible operating band**;
- **30s+ can be entirely reasonable** when Greg's judgment, route, coordination, or discretion is important and the downside is real;
- do not treat 30s as a universal minimum.

**Major contraband / rare artifacts / dangerous routes / organized operations**
- gold-scale compensation can be plausible;
- Greg's cut may be gold even when the underlying transaction is much larger;
- gold pay must follow actual value/risk, not simply the word `crime`.

## Acceptance gate

Before Greg accepts paid work, ask:

1. What does Greg risk?
2. What does the client gain or avoid losing?
3. How replaceable is Greg?
4. What else could Greg do with the time?
5. Is cash the main reward, or are access, relationship, information, travel, training, or entertainment doing real work?
6. If the pay is below his current economic tier, why does he still say yes?

For serious illegal work, add:

7. Why is the risk premium worth possible arrest/seizure/retaliation?

If there is no good answer, raise the pay or change Greg's reason for accepting.

## Reaction gate

Compensation and emotional reaction are separate decisions.

A 1c theatre payment may please Greg because the theatre recognized his labor without being financially important.

A 3c commission may be respectable in an early rebuilding phase but trivial after Greg routinely earns silver.

A 10s job may feel huge at one point and ordinary operating money later.

Greg's reaction should track demonstrated earning capacity, not nostalgia for older scarcity beats.

## Progression rule

As Greg improves, the important change is not that every job pays more.

The change is that he gains access to **different classes of work**:

cheap labor -> skilled work -> specialist judgment -> commissions -> coordination -> management -> risk-bearing contracts -> capital deployment -> ownership.

Ordinary cheap work remains in the world.

Greg becomes less dependent on it.

## Waste rule

Higher earning power does not make Greg financially sterile.

He may:
- overbuy quality;
- fund curiosity;
- gamble/speculate;
- pay for convenience;
- treat friends;
- buy weird artifacts;
- deploy too much capital at once;
- accept a lower-return opportunity because he enjoys it.

Later waste should usually cost opportunity or flexibility rather than automatically resetting him to early survival poverty.

## Hard avoidances

Do not:
- infer pay from Greg's purse;
- make all late work expensive;
- make all crime lucrative;
- invent a copper/silver/gold conversion;
- use tiny pay as a progression beat after Greg has surpassed that tier;
- make Greg accept major legal exposure for routine-labor money without a concrete non-cash reason;
- confuse underlying transaction value with Greg's fee;
- confuse theoretical artifact value with liquid sale value.
