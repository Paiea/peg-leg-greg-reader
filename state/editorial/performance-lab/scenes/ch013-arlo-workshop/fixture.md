## SOURCE EXCERPT

"Fuck."

"Yes."

"Again."

"I already did it six times."

"Again."

"No."

"Arlo."

"No."

"You cannot show me this and then expect..."

"I can. Watch," Arlo said.

He began removing the test frame from the bench. I experienced actual pain.

"Stop."

"No."

"One more run. Different order."

Arlo put the frame on the shelf.

"Not today."

"Warm them first. Cold after. Change the input..."

"Greg, every one of those is a different test. I already spent two days answering this one."

I stared at him.

He smiled. It was not a pleasant smile.

"You understand how irritating this is?"

"Yes."

"Good," I said.

Arlo set the frame on a shelf. I looked at the six regulators.

"What changed?"

"Process."

"Which part?"

"Several."

"Which?"

"Mine."

I laughed. That was fair. Annoying. Fair. I had brought him the Tere set. I did not own what he learned from it.

He slid the notebook closer to himself.

"You can ask about the result. The process changes are mine until I decide otherwise," Arlo said.

I closed my mouth. Arlo wiped his hands on a cloth.

"First day I tried to make the regulator match the reference."

"Reasonable."

"No."

"Apparently."

"I changed too much."

I smiled.

He pointed at me.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say I have recently learned something similar."

"I don't care."

"Good story."

"No."

He leaned against the bench.

"I changed the clay. Then the winding. Then the firing. Then the etch. Then I had no idea which thing mattered."

"Yes."

"I made six worse."

"Excellent."

"Fuck you."

"Failure with information."

"They didn't have information. That's the point."

"Ah."

He picked up a regulator from a box under the bench. Cracked. The copper winding had darkened.

"I was changing the object because the object was wrong."

"Wasn't it?"

"Maybe. But then I used Tere's set on the same regulator three times."

I waited.

"Three readings."

"Different?"

"Enough."

That was interesting.

"The test."

"The test."

I looked at the ugly wooden frame on the shelf.

Arlo followed my eyes.

"I was holding the input lead by hand."

"Pressure variation."

"Maybe."

"Angle."

"Maybe."

"Contact area."

"Maybe."

"Heat from your fingers."

"Greg."

"Sorry."

"No, you're not."

"No."

He crossed his arms.

"So I stopped making regulators."

That sentence landed.

"What did you make?"

"The fixture."

Then he measured the same regulator twenty times. The spread dropped by more than half, not because the regulator improved, but because the measurement did.

"You separated measurement error from object variation."

"I stopped holding the fucking wire."

"Same thing."

Arlo pointed at the fixture.

"Call it what you want. That is what changed the readings."

Then he changed nothing except winding tension.

"How did you control it?" I asked.

Arlo pointed at another object. A stick. A spool. A hanging weight.

"It's temporary."

"It's beautiful."

"It's two pieces of wood."

"Three."

"Fuck off."

The copper wire ran from a spool, over a small peg, beneath a hanging weight, then toward the winding mandrel. Constant tension. Crude. Good. Very good.

"You built a tensioner."

"I hung a weight on a string."

"That is what a tensioner is if you are poor."

He laughed despite himself.

Nothing about the workshop had transformed. That mattered. A man stopped holding a wire. Then hung a weight from a string. History was insulting.

## DRAMATIC SCRIPT

### LOCKED
- Arlo owns the workshop process improvements and the decision about what process knowledge he shares.
- Arlo discovers that his hand-held test method introduced meaningful measurement variation.
- Arlo builds the crude wooden fixture and later the hanging-weight winding-tension control.
- Greg has the realization that mundane process control, not a grand invention, is the important development.

### REQUIRED
- Greg understands that asking for another run with changed order, temperature, or input would create different tests rather than simply repeat the current one.
- Greg understands that measurement spread dropped materially after Arlo stopped holding the lead by hand.
- Greg sees the hanging-weight tension control and understands what it does.

### MUST NOT DRIFT
- Arlo remains the owner of the process work. Greg does not become his boss or inventor.
- Greg may interpret future significance, but Arlo does not know the future importance of Arwick Works.
- The fixture and tension control remain crude, cheap workshop solutions.
- No new technical result is invented.

### STATE IN
- Greg is excited by the improved regulators and wants every variable immediately.
- Arlo is tired, proud of the work, protective of authorship, and mildly defensive about how crude the fixtures look.

### STATE OUT
- Greg accepts that Arlo owns the process and recognizes the importance of measurement/process control.
- Arlo continues to the next actual workshop problem rather than becoming Greg's exposition engine.

### ENVIRONMENT
- Active workbench with six regulators, Tere references, ugly wooden test fixture, notebook, cracked regulator, spool, string, and hanging weight.
