## Method

The audit included dramatic state, required result, ownership, performed behavior, relationship pressure where relevant, beat-level screenplay, speaker ownership, prose rhythm, semantic compression, and final source-win/edit judgment.

## Calibration result

This batch produced a genuine zero-edit result.

The governing rhythm distinction remains:

**A list is not automatically an inventory.** Recompose only when boundaries merely separate labels belonging to one already-formed semantic object. Preserve boundaries when they enact accumulation, timing, uncertainty, discovery order, physical sequence, escalation, changed conditions, cognitive overload, or comedy.

The full PERFORMANCE layer rejected every plausible surface-level rhythm candidate in this batch because the short boundaries carried dramatic or cognitive function.

## Representative adjudications

### Canon 156 / displayed 121 — THE ADVOCATE

The sequence beginning with `The Advocate had too many nouns.` followed by `Merchants. Order. Seal. Wagons. Chancellor. Authority. Bridge. Council. Crown.` superficially resembles an inventory-recomposition target.

**SOURCE WIN.** Greg is trying to memorize an unfamiliar role and is being overloaded by its legal nouns. The sentence restart cost is the experience being dramatized. Smoothing the list would make his cognition easier precisely where the scene requires it to feel difficult.

### Canon 173 / displayed 136 — THE LAMPLIGHTER

The sequence `Hessa drew mana. Varo marked metal. Nessa hid glass. Pell used a tube.` is not one pre-formed analytical inventory. Each clause names a different mechanism Greg is trying and failing to unify. The separation is evidence for the later realization that no single mechanism owns the theatrical effect.

**SOURCE WIN.** The distinctions must remain visible.

### Canon 174 / displayed 137 — THE RETURNER

The failed throw blank is read as `One line. A branch. A curve. Stop.`

**SOURCE WIN.** These are visual observations arriving in order, followed by the explicit epistemic stop `I knew nothing.` Recomposition would falsely make the pattern more coherent than Greg understands it to be.

The chapter also deliberately keeps the goose, courier seal, failed throw blank, troupe effects, and road-home markers as separate experiences. They do not become one synthesized idea yet.

### Canon 176 / displayed 139 — THE QUESTIONER

This chapter is the payoff for leaving those earlier examples separate. Greg arrives at Hessa with the blue lamp, throw clasp, fox, goose, audience interpretation, and his own external-work question partially conflated. Hessa's dramatic job is to separate them into three different problem classes before Greg can formulate the narrower question.

**SOURCE WIN.** Earlier recomposition would front-load the synthesis that this scene exists to earn.

### Canon 177 / displayed 140 — THE STAGEHAND

Repeated ghost tests and the chapter's explicit three lessons are sequential craft failures: leather routing, outlet contact, and actor anticipation. Each changes the next test.

**SOURCE WIN.** The screenplay makes the iteration structure especially clear. Repetition is experiment, not redundancy.

## Batch-level finding

The full-stack method is providing value even when it returns zero edits. Its strongest contribution in this stretch is protection against false-positive compression.

Displayed 106–140 moves through rehearsal, public performance, private performance, travel, market work, fair work, prepared stage effects, homecoming, medical questioning, and stagehand craft. Several prose surfaces look list-like or repetitive when isolated. Once converted into performed beats, most turn out to encode changed conditions, distinct task ownership, live spatial constraints, or knowledge that has not yet been synthesized.

This is evidence that the PERFORMANCE round trip should not be judged by edit yield alone. A correct `SOURCE_WIN` can be the higher-value outcome when a cheaper surface pass would otherwise smooth away the mechanism of a scene.
