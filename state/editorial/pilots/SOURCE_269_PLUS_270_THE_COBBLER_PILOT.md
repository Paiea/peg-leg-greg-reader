# CHAPTER 272

## THE COBBLER

Nobody came from the theatre in the morning.

I waited until breakfast to notice.

Healthier than waiting at the window.

Lyssa was already gone. The pale work had gone with her. The brown cloth remained folded on the shelf with the same three pins, apparently committed to surviving us both.

I ate bread.

Then I checked my right shoe because it had started making a noise.

A soft scrape on certain stones. Twice yesterday, once on the walk home.

The outer heel had worn thinner than the inner. One section of sole had begun to peel at the seam.

I pressed it.

It moved.

"Bastard."

The shoe did not apologize.

I had no left shoe for comparison. My right leg did all the walking my crutches did not remove, which was more than people imagined and exactly as much as the leather had discovered.

I put the shoe back on.

Second step: scrape.

Fine.

I knew two cobblers by location and none by name. The red-boot shop was closer.

That settled the medical referral.

The shop smelled of leather, pitch, and old dust.

A woman with gray hair braided tight against her head sat behind a low bench shaving the edge of a sole with a curved knife. Small round spectacles. One thumb wrapped in cloth.

She looked at my crutches.

Then the right shoe.

Then the empty space below my left knee.

"Which one?" she asked.

I liked her immediately.

"Right."

"Sit."

I gave her the shoe.

"Scrapes. Outer heel seam moving."

She bent the sole farther than I had dared.

"Started."

"Bad?"

"No."

"Expensive?"

"No."

Excellent shop.

She looked at the upper.

"Yours from new?"

"No."

"How long?"

I told her.

She pressed two fingers against the worn edge.

"You roll out. Heel first. Then across."

"Because of this?"

I tapped my left residual limb.

She shrugged.

"Maybe. Maybe you always walked ugly."

Better shop.

"Can you fix it?"

"Yes."

She named a small price.

"Do it."

"Today."

"How long?"

She looked at my bare right foot.

"You have another?"

"Shoe?"

"Foot."

I stared at her.

Her mouth moved once.

"No."

"Then you wait."

I laughed.

She pointed at a stool.

"There."

"I can come back."

"Barefoot?"

"I have crutches."

"And one foot."

"You are very focused on the count."

"Cobbler."

Fair.

I moved to the stool.

"Name?"

"Savin."

"Greg."

"I know."

Of course.

"How?"

"Mara talks."

"What did she say?"

Savin tapped the loose sole flat.

"Bronze with one leg buys onions and asks too many questions."

"Slander."

"You asked four already."

I stopped.

For almost a minute.

Savin lifted the loose section, cleaned beneath it, trimmed something I could not see, brushed in dark pitch mix, pressed the layers together, then set the shoe into a wooden clamp shaped like an argument.

"What's in the pitch?"

She looked at me over her spectacles.

I nodded.

"Right. Five."

A man came in carrying a child's boot split open at the toe.

"Busy?" he asked.

"Yes."

He put it on the counter anyway.

"Toe again."

"Same child?"

"Same wall."

"Tell him stop kicking it."

"I did."

"Tell wall."

The man sighed.

"Tomorrow?"

"Evening."

He left.

"Does the wall listen?" I asked.

"Better than children."

Six.

The pitch needed time. Savin started another job while mine sat clamped.

Across the street, six little glass bulbs hung from a line. Pale sparks floated inside them, brightening when cloud crossed the sun and dimming again when it passed.

I watched twice.

Savin noticed.

"Shade lamps."

"I didn't ask."

"Face did."

"How do they work?"

"Don't know."

She returned to the boot in her hands.

That was the whole answer.

A woman brought in two soft indoor shoes wrapped in cloth.

Savin felt the leather.

"Too wet."

"Can you save them?"

"Maybe."

"How much?"

Savin named one price, then another.

"If they dry straight, first. If they twist, second."

"Can you tell now?"

"No."

The woman left the shoes.

Savin did not explain uncertainty. The shoes were wet. They would dry into whatever answer they had.

A clicking sound came from beneath the bench.

Something black moved between two leather scraps.

Thumb-long. Six legs. Dull copper stripe down its shell.

"You have a bug."

"Many."

"That one has metal on it."

Savin looked down.

"Copperback."

Old memory arrived with grain sacks, harness pads, and a quartermaster swearing at eaten stitching.

"We called them that too."

Savin stopped working.

"Bite?"

"Thread. Treated thread, mostly. Waxed stitching. Pack seams. Sometimes leather binding. At least the ones I knew."

"People?"

"Not that I remember."

"Fly?"

I searched.

"Don't know."

She handed me a stiff scrap of paper.

"Move those."

I looked at my bare foot.

"You are assigning the barefoot one-legged customer insect work."

"You're sitting."

Difficult woman.

I shifted the scraps while Savin waited with an empty jar.

The copperback appeared.

I nudged it forward.

Savin dropped the jar over it, slid the paper beneath, then turned the whole thing upright.

Click.

Click.

"Sure thread?" she asked.

"Sure enough that old camps stored treated cord away from them. Not sure yours behave exactly the same."

Savin pulled a low box from beneath the bench.

One coil of waxed thread had rough sections. Another had several outer strands cleanly missing.

"Found two last week," she said.

"Thought mice?"

"Yes."

She pointed at three open coils on a shelf.

"What did you do?"

I remembered hanging tins. Closed metal boxes. Other things I did not trust enough to resurrect.

"Metal storage. Maybe height too. I don't remember what killed them, and I don't remember whether the container or the location mattered more."

Savin opened a rectangular tin and put the waxed thread inside.

Then two dressed cords.

She paused over plain pale thread.

"That?"

"I don't remember them caring about plain."

She left it out.

"Guild?"

"For three bugs in one cobbler shop? I'd ask somebody local before I ask the Guild."

"Good."

That was it.

No theory meeting.

No committee on beetles.

Savin put the jar where she could see it and returned to my shoe.

"Ready?" I asked.

"Now."

She flexed the sole, pressed the repaired edge, then drove three short nails into the heel and trimmed the heads flush.

"Thought pitch held it."

"Pitch holds. Nails keep edge honest."

I put the shoe on and walked three steps.

No scrape.

The heel felt firmer. Not corrected. Just attached to itself again.

"You didn't change the wear angle."

"No."

"Should you?"

Savin watched me stand.

"Maybe later."

"Why?"

She pointed at the heel.

"I see this once. Come back when it wears again. Then maybe I know if shoe made you roll or you made shoe roll."

"And if you change it wrong now?"

"You hurt."

There was the whole methodology.

I paid her.

Savin kept the copperback.

"What are you doing with it?"

"Watching."

"Feed it thread?"

"Maybe."

"Scientist."

"Cobbler."

"Right."

At the door, she called my name.

I turned.

She pointed at the heel.

"If it pulls soon, come back. Don't wait."

"I have one shoe. Motivation is excellent."

"People say that."

Outside, I walked slowly for half a street listening for the scrape.

Nothing.

By the next corner I forgot to listen.

That was better.

I needed salt, so I went toward the north fountain.

Only salt.

A man beside a covered handcart saw me before the salt seller did.

"Greg?"

"Depends."

He looked at my crutches.

"Bronze Greg."

"Less depends."

"Busy?"

I looked toward the salt stall.

"Violently."

He nodded at the cart.

"Sit with this half an hour."

"Why?"

"Lunch."

I looked across the street.

The cookstall was thirty steps away.

"You are paying me because lunch is there?"

"Back bench. Can't see cart."

"Front bench?"

"Full."

A complete economic system.

"What's in the cart?"

"Preserves. Plum. Pear. Pepper. Two onion."

"Explosive?"

"Pepper if you're weak."

He named a small amount.

"What am I doing?"

"Nobody takes jars. Tolan can take blue crate. My sister can add empties."

"Descriptions."

"Tolan: tall, blue cap, missing two front teeth. Neri: my sister. She'll tell you you're in her way."

Useful.

"Name?"

"Orin."

"Greg."

"I know."

Of course.

Orin left.

I sat on the fountain wall beside the cart.

My repaired shoe did not announce itself once.

A woman stopped to ask whether Orin had pear preserves left. I told her I was specifically being paid not to investigate the jars.

She called that stupid.

I told her I was new to the profession.

She left.

Then green light flashed beneath the cart.

Two little gray birds stood in the shade near the rear wheel. Their feather edges glowed faint green.

"Glowwings."

One tilted its head.

I knew the word because Lyssa had given it to me. Nothing else.

A man in a blue cap arrived.

When he smiled, two front teeth were missing.

"Blue crate."

"Tolan."

"Greg."

I hated local reputation.

He took the crate, retied the canvas, then glanced under the cart.

"Glowwings. Means rain."

I looked at the clear sky.

"Does it?"

"My mother says."

"You?"

"No."

He walked off with the crate.

Excellent local science.

A few minutes later a girl arrived with a basket on one hip.

"Where's Orin?"

"Lunch."

She closed her eyes.

"Again?"

"Neri?"

"Unfortunately."

She looked across the street and saw him eating.

The family disappointment was immediate.

"Tolan?"

"Took blue crate."

"Good. Move."

"Why?"

"Cart's mine too."

"Orin hired me to stop people taking jars."

"I'm adding jars."

I considered the contract.

"Allowed."

"Thank you, watchman."

She packed empty jars into a rear compartment and retied the canvas better than Tolan had.

Then she looked at me.

"Mara says you knew bugs at Savin's."

That had taken hours.

"Mara is a public emergency."

"Talking is her hobby."

"Copperbacks."

Neri nodded.

"Those eat wax cord."

I stared at her.

"You know them?"

"Orin had them in the shed last year. Lost two jar covers. Little clicking things under the shelf. Pera told him copperbacks."

"Pera?"

"West dye steps. Cord and net repair."

"What did you do?"

"Tin boxes. Swept. Bitterleaf at the wall."

"Which worked?"

Neri shrugged.

"Bugs left. Winter came too."

Good.

Messy, useful, local.

"Tell Savin," she said.

"If I see her."

Neri nodded as if information could survive not being delivered at a sprint.

Then she left.

Orin returned with half a flatbread folded around something oily.

"All good?"

"Tolan took blue crate. Neri added empties and judged you. Two glowwings lived under your cart briefly. No preserves stolen."

"Rain?"

"Tolan's mother says."

Orin looked up.

"She's wrong a lot."

He paid me exactly what he had named.

"Tomorrow?"

"You planning lunch again?"

"Usually."

"Bold."

"If you're here."

"If I'm here and free."

"Fine."

No standing job appeared. Good.

I finally bought salt.

At home I put it where salt lived and counted the money in my pocket.

Savin's repair had taken some. Orin had returned a little. Yesterday's theatre pay still existed.

Then I realized too much of it was sitting in my personal pile.

I had forgotten to split the caller pay when I came home tired.

I moved the household share where it belonged.

Less impressive.

More correct.

A knock came at the door.

A boy in a red scarf handed me a folded note.

"From Hessa."

I opened it.

**Tomorrow. Midmorning. If rested. Bring nothing.**

"Answer?" he asked.

"Yes, if rested."

He nodded.

"Coin?"

"She paid you?"

"Yes. Sometimes people pay twice."

"Not today."

"Fine."

He left.

Lyssa came home carrying a sack of food and a wrapped bundle that was none of my business.

I showed her the note.

"Magic?" she asked.

"Probably. Bring nothing."

"Rested."

"I guarded preserves for half an hour."

She looked at me.

"What?"

I gave her the short version while she unpacked onions, greens, and a small hard cheese.

When I got to the glowwings, she said, "They don't mean rain."

"Tolan already betrayed his mother."

"Sometimes before rain, bugs fly high. Glowwings eat them."

"They eat bugs?"

"Some."

"Green, sometimes blue, nest high, eat bugs. This is becoming an actual bird."

Lyssa looked at me.

"It was already bird."

I pointed at her.

"You know what I mean."

She cut the cheese.

I told her about Neri and the copperbacks.

"Tell Savin?" she asked.

"If I see her."

"Good."

No one in the room considered that urgent.

Hessa's note stayed on the table.

Tomorrow had its own work.

Tonight I had salt, a repaired shoe, a few coins, and one more local story about copperbacks than I had this morning.

Outside, the sky remained clear.

Tolan's mother lost this round.

I went to bed early anyway.
