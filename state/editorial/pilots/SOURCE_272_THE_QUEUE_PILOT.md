# CHAPTER 272

## THE QUEUE

The next morning, the water stopped.

Not all water.

Carrow remained committed to the concept.

Our building simply lost access to it.

I discovered this by lifting the kitchen pitcher and finding enough at the bottom to insult me.

"Lyssa."

She was pinning up her hair.

"Mm?"

I tilted the pitcher.

One sad mouthful moved.

"We are wealthy in dampness."

"Courtyard tap?"

I checked.

The courtyard tap coughed once and produced nothing.

Cala already stood there with a bucket. An older man from the rear rooms had two jars at his feet. The woman who shook rugs too early in the morning held a pot large enough to drown a child.

We stared at the tap.

"You broke it," Cala said.

"I arrived after you."

"Still possible."

The older man tried the handle again.

Hollow knock in the wall.

Nothing.

Rug Woman sighed.

"North cistern."

Cala groaned.

"What?" I asked.

"Public draw. Lane line dry."

"How far?"

"Not far."

This was a phrase invented by people carrying zero crutches.

Upstairs, Lyssa had already pulled out the larger household jar.

"I can go," she said.

"You have work."

"Later."

"I can get water."

She looked at the jar.

Then at both crutches.

Then at me.

"How?"

Excellent question.

I could not carry the large jar without giving one of my hands a second job it did not possess.

I picked up the small pitcher instead. Its loop could ride against my wrist while my hand stayed on the crutch.

"Civilization," I said. "I will find some."

Lyssa smiled.

"Bring it back full."

The north cistern was three streets away.

Not far.

I hated Cala.

The line began before I reached the square.

Buckets. Jugs. Shoulder yokes. A narrow handcart with covered pots. A boy holding a kettle. A woman with three blue bottles.

Nobody looked frightened.

Inconvenienced, yes.

Good.

The cistern sat under a stone canopy. Three bronze mouths fed a long trough. Four glass stones were set into the arch above them.

Three glowed pale white.

One was dark.

A man ahead of me noticed me staring.

"West feeder's out."

"Dark means out?"

"No feed."

"Pressure?"

"I think."

The woman beside him said, "Feed."

"Same thing."

"No."

The man shrugged.

"Water comes when they're lit."

For most people in line, that was enough engineering.

A city worker stood at the trough with a hooked iron key and chalk behind one ear. She called people forward, checked the mouths, and made marks on a board.

A boy tried to fill his kettle twice.

"Two fills only," she said.

"It's one kettle."

"You filled it already."

"For my aunt."

"Then back of line for your uncle."

He argued exactly long enough to discover she did not care.

The woman with blue bottles turned toward me.

"Your lane dry?"

I told her which.

"West feeder. Mine too."

"Since when?"

"Dawn."

"Happens often?"

"Enough."

She looked at my pitcher.

"That's all?"

"That's what I can carry."

Her eyes moved to the crutches.

Arithmetic, not sympathy.

"Jar at home?"

"Yes."

"Cart?"

"No."

"Neighbors?"

"Several, apparently."

"Then why are you here?"

I considered it.

"I wanted water."

She laughed.

"Fair."

By the time I reached the front, my right leg was mildly stiff from standing.

The worker looked at my pitcher.

"One?"

"One."

I hooked it against my wrist and tried to get it beneath the lower spout while keeping both crutches useful.

The geometry became bad immediately.

The worker watched for two seconds.

"Give it."

I handed her the pitcher.

She filled it and gave it back.

"Thanks."

"Next."

Perfect.

Then the dark stone above the arch flashed amber.

The line groaned as one organism.

One running mouth weakened.

The worker looked up.

"Two mouths. One vessel each until west returns."

Complaints started.

She ignored them.

I moved aside.

"Amber?" I asked.

"South unstable."

"Dark?"

"No feed."

Enough.

I left before I became a water-office volunteer.

The woman with blue bottles caught me near the edge of the square.

"You walk that back?"

"That was the plan."

"Bad plan. I have a cart."

The narrow handcart with six covered pots belonged to her.

"Soup?"

"Lunch pots. My kitchen's past your lane. Put it in."

I looked at the pitcher. Then at my right leg.

"Thank you."

She set it between two pots.

"Vela."

"Greg."

"I know."

"Theatre?"

"And Mara."

"Of course Mara."

We walked.

"Six pots?" I asked.

"Three shops. Two houses. One old man."

"Every day?"

"Most."

"What happens when water stops?"

"I swear. Then I get water."

A mature business system.

At my lane, Cala appeared pulling a low two-wheeled cart with three large household jars.

We stopped.

She looked at Vela's soup cart.

I looked at her water cart.

"Civilization," Cala said.

"I found soup."

Vela looked between us.

"You need more?"

Cala pointed at the jars.

"Rear man has cart. Maris has rope. We did one run. Yours is upstairs."

Rug Woman had become Maris while I was away.

"You filled ours?"

"Lyssa gave me the jar."

Of course she had.

I had gone out to fetch one pitcher and returned to discover the building had solved the larger problem without electing me to anything.

"Second run later," Cala said.

"What do I do?"

She looked at my crutches.

"Watch jars."

I stared at her.

"Again."

"What?"

"Nothing. I have experience."

Vela laughed.

She handed me my pitcher and pointed downlane.

"Green awning after the turn if you ever want soup."

"I'll remember."

"Don't need to."

She pushed on.

Upstairs, a full household jar stood beside the table.

Lyssa had left a note under it.

`CALA TOOK IT. DON'T GO AGAIN.`

I looked at the full jar.

Then at my small pitcher.

"Too late."

I drank some anyway.

Spite could be hydrating.

By midday the courtyard had become a water depot without anybody naming it one.

I finally learned the rear-room man's name: Olan. The low cart was his.

Maris's enormous pot was for laundry, not children.

Cala pointed at the cart.

"Olan pulls. Maris and I fill. Greg stays."

"Doing what?"

"People come for jars."

"I guard water."

"You sit."

"Prestigious."

"Sit."

They left.

Five filled vessels remained in the courtyard.

Nobody had labeled them.

A child came downstairs and pointed at one.

"Ours."

"Which one?"

"That one."

He had pointed at three during the sentence.

"Mark?"

He stared at me.

I inspected the jars.

Blue cord. Chipped handle. Tall narrow jar. Two nearly identical brown ones.

His mother's had none of the first three features.

"Go ask what mark is on yours."

He returned a minute later.

"White scratch."

One brown jar had a pale scratch near the base.

"That one."

He tried to lift it.

The jar remained committed to the ground.

I could not safely carry it upstairs either.

We looked at each other.

"Mama said bring it."

"Mama has unreasonable faith in both of us."

A young man crossed the courtyard carrying folded wood.

"Tavi," I called.

He stopped.

"Carry this upstairs for him?"

Tavi looked at the jar.

"Which floor?"

The boy held up two fingers.

Tavi sighed, set down the wood, and lifted the jar.

"Show me."

That became most of the job.

People came down. I figured out which jar belonged to whom. Someone else carried it when necessary.

When Cala, Olan, and Maris returned, they brought more water and worse news.

"South's dark," Olan said.

"North?"

"Two mouths."

Maris wiped her forearms on her skirt.

"Crew says west before evening."

Cala looked at the remaining jars.

"Good."

"Labels," I said.

"What?"

"Next run. Tie scraps on them. Every family here apparently bought the same brown jar."

Maris looked at the vessels.

"He's right."

Cala frowned at her.

"Don't encourage him."

We used old cleaning rags.

Red strip. Blue strip. One knot. Two knots. A piece with a hole.

Barely a system.

Enough.

On the next run, the child came down and said, "Red."

I gave him the red jar.

Tavi carried it upstairs when he passed.

No investigation required.

By late afternoon, the courtyard had enough stored water that people stopped talking as if every cup mattered.

Then the tap coughed.

Everyone turned.

Brown water spat once from the mouth.

Maris shut it immediately.

"Wait."

Nobody argued.

A few minutes later she opened it again. The water sputtered, cleared, then ran steadily.

Cala cheered.

Someone upstairs cheered because Cala did.

A voice from a rear window shouted, "Don't drink first draw!"

Olan shouted back, "We know!"

We let it run briefly into the drain.

Then the building returned to ordinary water.

I sat on the low step.

My right leg was tired. My shoe held. Hands fine.

I had done less hauling than everyone else.

Nobody seemed interested in measuring fairness by weight carried.

Lyssa came home near sunset and stopped when she saw rag markers still tied around several jars.

"What happened?"

"I invented labels."

Across the courtyard Cala said, "He did not."

"I improved labels."

"Also no."

Lyssa looked at me.

"Water?"

"Back."

"You went?"

"Once. Safely. Vela carried the pitcher back in her soup cart."

"Green awning Vela?"

I stared.

"You know her too?"

"Good soup."

"This city is hostile to anonymity."

Upstairs, the household jar was still more than half full.

I told Lyssa about the feeder stones while we ate.

"Three white. One dark. Then amber."

"Feed stones."

"Do they measure flow or pressure?"

Lyssa shrugged.

"Water."

I laughed.

That was enough engineering for dinner.

The building had lost water for most of a day.

We had dealt with it using a public cistern, one borrowed soup cart, Olan's low cart, Maris's rope, Cala's refusal to call organizing organizing, and some torn rags tied around clay jars.

The tap worked again.

Nobody needed a better ending.
