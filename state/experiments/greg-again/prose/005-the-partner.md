Arlo had sold one.

That was the first thing he told me.

I had not taken off the ruined coat yet.

I had barely gotten through the door.

He pointed at the bench.

A white-gray ceramic disk sat beside two shallow bowls. Purple wash in one. Paler purple in the other.

Not clear.

Cleaner.

The distinction had acquired eight copper.

I looked at the disk.

Then at Arlo.

Then back at the disk.

Eight copper.

Somewhere forty years ahead of us, people would buy improved versions of this by the crate.

Here, today, a dyer on Mill Street had paid eight copper for one ugly circle of ceramic that might crack before the week ended.

That should have felt small.

It did not.

It felt dangerous.

My brain started building a factory before Arlo finished telling me what the customer wanted.

Six more if the first one survived.

Six.

Not a market.

Not an industry.

Six.

I was already three buildings deep into a production plan.

Arlo watched my face.

"Don't."

I had not said anything.

"Factory."

I closed my mouth.

Apparently a month of working with me had produced predictive capability in other people.

Useful.

Rude.

I took off the ruined coat and hung it over the stool.

The acid damage had eaten a dark crescent through the sleeve. Neutralizing powder had stopped the worst of it, but the fabric smelled like wet stone and bad decisions.

Arlo looked at it.

"Saltback?"

Yes.

For money.

He looked around the workshop.

Then back at me.

"You know we're trying to make money here."

Eventually.

"I made eight copper today."

I had made considerably more than that.

He wanted to know what the coat cost.

Irrelevant.

His expression changed.

Right.

Not irrelevant.

I already owned the coat, which was not the same thing as the coat being free.

I liked Arlo less every week.

That was probably a good sign.

"Show me."

He did.

The disk was thinner than the last batch and visibly rougher.

Worse-looking.

Better-working, apparently.

Arlo had stopped trying to make the ceramic dense. The first versions held together nicely and filtered badly. This one let the wash move through its body instead of around it.

Different route.

Same destination, maybe.

He poured the purple liquid.

It seeped through slowly.

Too slowly compared with the later filters I remembered.

That thought arrived automatically.

I pushed it aside.

Later was not evidence.

This bowl was.

Twenty-seven percent less residue on this wash.

Four repeated tests.

Same general result.

The dyer had brought the wash herself and taken a disk this morning.

If it lasted a week, she wanted six.

I read Arlo's numbers twice.

Actual numbers were extremely inconsiderate to memory.

Memory liked destinations.

Numbers wanted the road.

"Why did the new mix work?"

Arlo corrected the question.

It had worked better.

Not worked.

Better.

Right.

He had changed the clay body first, then the firing schedule. He thought flow mattered more than the mana reaction I had originally obsessed over.

I thought surface area still mattered.

He agreed that it probably did.

Probably.

My favorite word.

The difference was that Arlo now had measurements attached to his probably.

I had a memory of an object that did not exist yet.

I was beginning to understand why this arrangement irritated both of us.

And why it might work.

The problem was that we did not actually have an arrangement.

That became obvious when I asked what he wanted to do with the six disks.

Arlo went still in a different way than he had when I accidentally used his family name.

Not suspicion.

Preparation.

He had been waiting for this.

"My workshop stays mine."

There it was.

The sentence changed the room.

I leaned against the bench.

His tools. His kiln. His repairs. His mixes. His firing schedule. His customers unless we explicitly agreed otherwise.

I had funded the beginning.

I had brought the shale and the remembered possibility.

Arlo had turned the possibility into something a woman had exchanged money for.

I had assumed we would eventually sort ownership out.

Business arrangements were famous for resolving themselves peacefully when ignored.

"I wasn't planning to take your workshop."

Arlo gave me the look.

Apparently I planned everything.

Unfair.

Then he reminded me about the cart of shale.

Less unfair.

He tapped the disk.

"This process is mine. My mixes. My firing schedule. My tools. I don't become your employee because you remembered a rock."

That one got through cleanly.

Good line.

Also correct.

I did not enjoy that combination.

A month ago I might have answered faster.

Current me had spent thirty days watching reality charge extra for confidence.

I sat on the second stool.

One leg had been repaired since my first visit.

Not by me.

Of course not.

Arlo's workshop kept existing when I left it.

That was the entire point.

"Fine. You own the process."

He blinked.

Too easy.

He had expected a fight.

I felt briefly superior for disappointing him.

Then I ruined it.

"What do I own?"

There.

Arlo relaxed by approximately half an inch.

Negotiation he understood.

Vague friendship had been the dangerous part.

My early investment came back first.

With return.

Reasonable return, according to Arlo.

We did not define reasonable because I enjoyed our partnership and wanted it to survive the next five minutes.

After that, I received a share on this formulation.

This formulation.

Not his workshop.

Not future things he invented.

Not every ceramic disk that happened to contain Kestrin.

This.

He tapped the sample again.

That boundary was better than what I had been about to propose.

I resented him for improving my position against him.

Then came percentages.

We spent twenty minutes discovering new ways to express disappointment with one another.

Vale expected money tomorrow.

That fact sat beside every number.

I had enough from the saltback contract to cover most of what I owed.

Most.

A beautiful word until attached to debt.

Every copper I left in Arlo's workshop made tomorrow worse.

Every copper I pulled out made the thing we were building weaker.

The future-value version of me wanted to reinvest everything.

The present-cash version wanted Vale to stop owning part of my week.

Both versions had excellent arguments.

Neither had enough money.

We ended with a deal neither of us liked completely.

That felt healthier than either of us liking it too much.

Arlo wrote the terms down.

I read every line.

He watched me.

"You trust me?"

Yes.

I also read contracts.

Those were separate systems.

He sighed.

I signed.

The project changed shape with the ink.

Not mine.

Not his alone.

A thing between us with edges.

That felt more real than the cart of shale ever had.

Then Arlo pointed at the order for six disks.

He could make them.

The problem was that making six meant not repairing lamps for two days.

Repairs already paid.

The disks might pay.

There it was again.

Future value.

Present cash.

I asked what six disks earned compared with two days of repairs.

Not enough.

Then charge more.

Arlo thought the dyer would refuse.

Maybe.

How many did she actually need?

Six, he said.

No.

She had asked for six.

Different question.

Arlo looked at me.

That one landed.

Four now.

If they worked, the next order cost more.

Why would she agree?

Because right now she had one.

If she truly needed six more, four additional working disks were better than zero while Arlo spent two days repairing someone else's lamps.

He thought about it.

"That's irritating."

Thank you.

Not a compliment, apparently.

Still useful.

We had been formal partners for less than half an hour and had already found the correct division of labor.

Arlo made things.

I made them annoying.

Promising.

I left the workshop with less control over the project and more confidence in it.

That was new.

Vale was due tomorrow.

That was not.

I counted what remained while walking.

Not enough.

Close.

Close was worse than far in some situations because close came with ideas.

I could ask Vale for another day.

I could work the balance.

I could sell something I owned.

That list was short.

I could gamble.

That option arrived immediately, cleanly, wearing the respectable clothes of competence.

I was good at cards.

Actually good.

The month had proved that too.

So had the losses.

The White Dog sat between Arlo's workshop and my room if I took a route that was technically shorter and morally dishonest.

I took it.

The front tables were half full.

Three faces I recognized.

None of them knew me forty years from now.

That remained refreshing.

I bought in with enough to close the debt gap and not enough to feel comfortable.

Deliberate constraint.

Very responsible.

For the first hour, the plan worked.

I watched more than I played.

A dockworker on my left hated being laughed at and overcorrected after embarrassment.

The woman across from me protected winnings once she was ahead. Her calling range tightened every time her stack crossed the same rough threshold.

A man two seats over talked more when he had nothing and less when he had something mediocre he wanted to become expensive.

People were systems with strong opinions about being called systems.

The useful part was remembering they were not simple ones.

Fold.

Small loss.

Fold.

Medium win.

Wait.

Another win.

I stacked the coins once.

Enough.

There it was.

Vale solved.

Food for several days.

A little breathing room.

Not much.

Enough.

I looked at the next hand.

Bad cards.

Fold.

Good.

I should leave.

The man two seats over increased his opening bet.

Again.

He had done that after the last loss too.

Interesting.

Was he chasing?

Maybe.

No.

Too neat.

He lost small.

Next hand, same larger opening.

The table noticed.

I noticed the table noticing.

There.

He knew they thought he was angry.

Or wanted them to.

I had enough money.

The problem was solved.

I stayed.

One hand.

Just enough to check the model.

There are thoughts you can identify as stupid while still admiring the engineering.

This was one.

He trapped me.

Not brilliantly.

He did not need brilliance.

I wanted the model to finish more than I wanted the money to remain mine.

A third of the margin disappeared.

Debt still solved.

Barely.

That should have been educational.

Instead it clarified him.

He was not chasing.

He was selling the appearance of chasing because the table wanted to believe losing had made him emotional.

Good.

I had been wrong in a more interesting direction.

One updated hand.

That sentence cost four copper.

I stood.

Finally.

The man looked up.

"Done?"

Yes.

"You sure?"

No.

That helped.

I left.

Outside, the air felt colder than it had two hours earlier.

I counted the coins under a lamp.

Enough for Vale.

Almost nothing afterward.

For perhaps fifteen minutes inside the White Dog, I had possessed debt repayment, food, training money, and margin.

Then understanding one stranger had become more interesting than keeping the margin.

That was the part worth remembering.

Not gambling is dangerous.

I had known that before nineteen.

Something more specific.

I could solve the stated problem and keep playing because the *model* was not solved yet.

Curiosity had a price too.

Of course it did.

I put Vale's money in a separate pocket.

Tomorrow, I would pay him.

Tonight, I had a partner.

A filtration process worth eight copper.

A coat with an acid hole in it.

Enough money to become poor honestly again by morning.

And one newly verified fact about myself.

Being smarter had not removed the idiot.

It had given him better arguments.
