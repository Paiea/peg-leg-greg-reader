# Chapter 9: The Bridge

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Batch rehearsal: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/007-010-story-batch.md`

---

It rained for two days.

Not continuously.

That would have been easier to respect.

The sky preferred intervals.

Rain hard enough to turn the road soft, then an hour of gray calm to convince everyone the worst had passed, then another sheet of water just as boots started drying.

By the sixth day, nobody protected their clothes anymore.

We protected socks.

Civilization had priorities.

My shaping drill had improved.

Not dramatically.

I could make the four-inch plane often enough that failure was no longer the default.

One breath reliably.

Two if I was rested.

Three once, after breakfast, on flat ground, with nobody trying to kill me and Meral insulting my wrist angle.

The important change was not duration.

I could choose the angle now.

Mostly.

That made the tiny thing feel less like a symptom and more like a tool.

Meral still refused to call it a barrier.

I had stopped arguing.

Also progress.

Jorren's left shoulder had a purple bruise from the road attack.

My lip had stopped hurting.

The passenger cart carried one new patch where my deflected bolt had entered the frame.

Nobody mentioned it anymore.

Action became history very quickly when a road still needed walking.

We reached the Brell River after noon.

I knew the crossing.

That was my first mistake.

Not this crossing exactly.

The place.

Forty years later there was a broad stone bridge here with three arches and a shrine niche cut into the north pier. I remembered crossing it during a supply march and complaining that the western parapet had been repaired with the wrong stone.

Very useful memory.

The bridge in front of me had no arches.

Wooden deck.

Stone footings.

Heavy timber rails.

Mud approaches cut into both banks.

No shrine.

No wrong stone.

Different bridge.

Same river.

I knew that immediately.

And still some stupid piece of my mind filed the crossing under familiar.

Future knowledge did not need to be explicit to be dangerous.

Sometimes it just made uncertainty feel smaller than it was.

The river ran high and brown beneath the deck.

Not flooding.

Close enough that branches moved past faster than I liked.

The south approach had taken damage.

Water had cut a shallow channel down the right side of the road. One edge of the packed earth had slumped toward the bank, exposing roots and pale gravel beneath the top layer.

The quartermaster stopped the caravan.

Good.

Meral walked the approach before anyone crossed.

Also good.

She drove the pointed end of her hammer into the road at six places.

Hard center.

Softer shoulder.

Very soft near the washout.

The bridge itself looked sound.

Approach was the problem.

I knew that too.

The first light wagon crossed without issue.

Then the passenger carts.

Then wagon two.

Each driver kept left on the approach, away from the slumped edge.

Slow.

One at a time.

Reasonable.

The rain began again as wagon four started across.

Of course.

I stood on the north side with Jorren, watching wheel placement.

The road behind us rose through trees.

Ahead, Brell Crossing was still several miles away.

Warm food existed there.

Maybe walls.

Possibly a chair.

The future stone bridge kept trying to occupy the same place in my head as the wooden one.

I pushed it out.

Present first.

Wagon five came down the approach.

Heavy load.

Barrels.

Two crates tied on top.

The driver kept left.

Too left, maybe.

The front wheel passed the soft edge safely.

Rear wheel followed.

The earth beneath it collapsed anyway.

Not dramatically at first.

A quiet sinking.

Then the wheel dropped almost to the axle.

The wagon leaned right.

Barrels shifted.

One rope snapped.

Everything happened after that.

The right rear wheel broke through the remaining shoulder.

Mud gave way beneath it.

The wagon slid toward the river.

The horses surged forward.

Driver yanked the reins.

Wrong direction.

Jorren ran.

So did I.

Meral shouted from the south bank.

"Cut the rear team loose!"

Motor interrupt.

Jorren reached the horses first.

Not cut loose.

Not yet.

One trace had wrapped under the tongue.

Cutting the wrong line could pull the team sideways with the wagon.

He saw that faster than I did.

Current experience.

He climbed onto the left side of the driver's bench instead.

The wagon shifted another inch.

The driver had one leg trapped beneath a fallen crate.

Good.

More problems.

The quartermaster threw a haul rope from the bridge deck.

I caught it.

No plan yet.

Just rope.

Meral reached us.

She looked once at the wagon.

Once at the bank.

Once at the bridge rail.

"Front axle. Two lines."

People moved.

That was the thing about good instructions.

They converted panic into jobs.

I passed the first line under the axle while another guard crawled beneath the safer left side.

Mud soaked both sleeves.

Nobody cared.

Second line went around the rear frame.

The river took another piece of bank.

The wagon dropped.

Jorren disappeared behind the tilted bench.

My heart stopped.

Then his head came back up.

"Still here."

Good.

Unnecessary line.

Worth hearing.

Meral anchored the front line to the bridge's stone footing.

The rear line needed a different angle.

There was one timber post near the road edge, part of an old approach rail.

Thick.

Wet.

Questionable.

We used it anyway.

The quartermaster had two draft horses brought forward to pull from the bridge deck.

The wagon team remained attached but slack, handlers holding them steady.

Jorren was working on the trapped crate.

The driver was conscious.

Swearing.

Also good.

Meral set a broad ward beneath the wagon's downhill side.

Not lifting it.

Catching movement if the bank failed again.

The pale surface appeared against mud and wood at an angle.

Strong.

Nothing like mine.

I felt the old envy immediately.

Then the bank cracked.

Not time.

The right side slumped another six inches.

Meral's ward flared.

Her knees bent.

The wagon stopped moving.

For now.

"Pull!"

The forward horses leaned into the line.

Rope tightened.

The front axle moved left.

Half an inch.

Rear line came tight around the wet post.

The rope started sawing sideways across the wood.

There.

I saw the failure.

Not the wagon.

The line.

If the rear rope climbed around the post, the angle changed.

If the angle changed, the wagon rotated downhill.

If it rotated, Meral's ward would take more load.

Meral already had enough.

I put my hand beside the post.

Plane.

Four inches.

Wrong scale.

No.

Right scale.

Right place.

I formed it against the rope where it wanted to walk sideways.

The plane appeared.

Clearer than before.

Small.

Angled down.

Rope hit it.

Pressure slammed through my wrist.

Not weight.

Direction.

The rope stayed in its groove.

One breath.

"Again!"

Horses pulled.

The wagon shifted left.

Two breaths.

My forearm shook.

Jorren got the crate off the driver's leg.

"He's clear!"

Three breaths.

Pain behind my eyes.

The plane flickered.

The rope climbed a fraction.

No.

I changed the angle.

Tiny movement.

The plane rotated.

The rope settled lower instead.

That felt different.

Not stronger.

Chosen.

Four breaths.

My magic failed.

The plane vanished.

The rope immediately started moving.

Meral saw it.

One hand left the broad ward.

A second narrow plane flashed beside mine had been.

Perfect angle.

No wobble.

She held both spells at once.

Show-off.

Professional.

Same thing from where I stood.

"Pull!"

The horses leaned again.

The front wheel climbed back onto firmer ground.

The wagon rolled six inches left.

Meral's lower ward stopped screaming.

Jorren dragged the driver across the bench and jumped to the bridge deck with him.

Now the cargo could lose.

That changed everything.

The quartermaster cut the rear lashings.

Two barrels rolled off the downhill side and vanished into mud.

One kept going into the river.

Nobody tried to save it.

Correct.

The wagon became lighter.

Another pull.

Front axle moved.

Rear wheel came out of the collapsed edge with a wet sucking sound.

The entire frame lurched left onto the road.

Meral dropped her ward.

Everyone staggered because several systems had been leaning against several other systems and suddenly none of them needed to.

The wagon stopped.

Actually stopped.

On road.

Mostly level.

The river continued downstream carrying one barrel toward somebody else's problem.

I sat in the mud.

Not intentionally.

My legs had apparently finished the meeting without me.

Jorren was on the bridge deck beside the driver.

The man's leg bent.

Good sign.

Painful sign.

Better than alternatives.

Meral came over.

She looked at my hand.

"Four."

I knew.

"Breaths."

Yes.

I tried not to smile.

Failed.

"Don't."

Apparently Arlo had started a school.

"Your angle was wrong at first."

Of course.

"Then you corrected under load."

There.

That mattered.

She held up two fingers.

"Next problem."

I waited.

"You braced where the rope was."

Yes.

"Brace where it's going."

I looked at the post.

At the polished wet groove the rope had cut.

Right.

Earlier.

Again.

Support seemed to consist largely of doing obvious things before they became obvious.

I had once been very good at that.

Current me was learning to arrive several seconds sooner.

The driver had a badly bruised shin and nothing broken.

We lost one barrel to the river and cracked another while unloading.

The wagon's right rear wheel needed a new rim segment before the return trip.

The approach needed repair before another heavy caravan came through.

The bridge itself had survived without caring about any of us.

I stood at the north end and looked back.

Rainwater ran through the fresh collapse.

In the future, there would be stone here.

Three arches.

Shrine niche.

Wrong-colored repair on the western parapet.

For years I had crossed that future bridge without wondering what had been underneath it first.

Maybe this bridge washed out next spring.

Maybe ten years from now.

Maybe somebody replaced it because a wagon finally went into the river.

I did not know.

The future gave me the answer without the history.

Again.

That was becoming a theme.

We reached Brell Crossing after dark.

Late.

Wet.

One wagon limping.

Nobody cheering.

Perfect arrival.

The town had an inn large enough to stable caravan teams and a common room warm enough that I briefly understood religion.

Jorren's caravan continued north at dawn after one repair day.

Mine did not.

My contract ended here.

The southbound return left the morning after his.

I had known this since Carrow.

It felt different with his bedroll leaning against the wall beside mine.

He was going farther.

I was going back.

His road did not exist to teach me anything.

Still irritating.

Meral sat across the room with her cords spread on the table, drying them one by one.

I held my hand beneath mine.

No cord.

Just memory of the angle.

Plane.

I reached.

A small translucent square appeared above my palm.

Four inches.

Steady.

I turned my wrist.

The plane turned with it.

Not much.

Enough.

Jorren noticed.

"That useful yet?"

I looked at him.

"It saved your driver."

"Meral saved the driver."

"I participated."

"Very heroic four inches."

I let the plane vanish.

My headache was smaller this time.

"Go north."

He laughed.

Tomorrow he would.
