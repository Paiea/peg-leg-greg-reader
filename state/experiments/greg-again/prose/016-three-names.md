# Chapter 16: Three Names

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/016-story-search.md`

---

Arlo had made four.

Not four disks.

Four actual units.

Ceramic housings. Bypass valves. Marked pressure tongues. Removable chambers. Ugly pipe joins that offended me less every time they survived use.

They stood along one wall of the workshop in a row.

The first had purple staining around the lower housing.

The second had a patched clamp.

The third had somebody's handwriting across the side.

**OPEN THIS ONE FIRST.**

The fourth looked suspiciously clean.

I distrusted it immediately.

Arlo was underneath a workbench when I arrived.

Not emotionally.

Physically.

His boots stuck out beside a box of brass fittings.

Ivena stood at the kiln table sorting washers into three piles.

She looked at me.

Then at the doorway behind me.

"Did you bring money?"

No hello.

Comforting.

"How much?"

"Wrong answer."

Probably.

I closed the door.

Arlo's voice came from beneath the bench.

"He's back?"

"Unfortunately."

"Good."

Ivena looked down at him.

"You two need better standards."

Arlo emerged holding a bent copper tube.

He had soot along one cheek and a small burn on his left wrist.

Nothing dramatic.

Workshop evidence.

He put the tube on the bench beside a page covered in numbers.

"Sit."

I remained standing.

That bought me perhaps two seconds of dignity.

Then I saw the second page.

Orders.

Not one.

Three names.

Ivena's dyehouse at the top, four units already marked delivered.

Two more businesses beneath it.

A wool washer on East Mill.

A soapworks near the lower bridge.

Six units total between them.

Deposits listed.

Dates listed.

My brain reached for production capacity before I had read the bottom line.

I stopped it.

Growth.

Painful but measurable.

"They came to you?"

Ivena gave me the look people had started using when I asked a question whose answer had been standing in the room before me.

"They came to me."

Ah.

She had shown the first units to people who actually used dirty process water for a living.

The wool washer had watched one run at her dyehouse.

The soapworks owner had heard about the bypass from a cousin who hauled ash.

No advertising.

No grand market discovery.

Workers talked.

Businesses watched one another.

The future remained disappointingly social.

Arlo slid the order page toward me.

"Six is too many."

"Six isn't many."

"For you."

Fair.

He pointed at the kiln.

The current firing schedule could produce two housings at a time without pushing temperature control into guesswork.

Three batches.

More if one cracked.

Brass fittings were now the slow part, not clay.

The bypass valves came from a machinist who had already raised his price after the second order.

Smart man.

I disliked him on principle.

"How much deposit?"

Arlo told me.

Enough to start.

Not enough to finish all six without using repair money or somebody adding capital.

I looked at the page again.

Future value.

Present cash.

Old enemies.

"How much do you need?"

Ivena answered this time.

Two silver.

I laughed once.

Neither of them did.

Right.

I counted what I had without taking the purse out.

Rent.

Food.

A little yard time.

The better sword had eaten most of the road pay.

Turning down the warehouse contract had eaten what could have replaced it.

Mara had not eaten anything.

Important distinction.

Time had.

I had chosen where to spend two days, and the purse had retained a very exact opinion about it.

"I don't have two silver."

Arlo nodded.

Too quickly.

He already knew.

That annoyed me.

"How?"

"You bought the sword."

"Everyone knows about the sword?"

Ivena shrugged.

"You walk around wearing it."

Disturbing breach in financial privacy.

Arlo reached for another page.

Not orders this time.

Terms.

I knew the shape before I read it.

That did not improve the experience.

"We need to change the deal," he said.

There are sentences that improve with context.

That one rarely did.

I read.

My original investment returned first from the first four units, as agreed.

Done.

My continuing share on this formulation remained intact for the work already sold.

Then a new line.

**Future units under revised production arrangement.**

I looked at Ivena.

She did not look away.

Good.

"Say it."

She folded her arms.

"I'm not your test bucket."

That was promising.

"I supplied the wash. I found the failure you didn't reproduce. I bought the catch basins. I changed the feed process. The bypass is in there because my workers were the ones standing beside the first version when it tried to spit hot residue at them."

Arlo opened his mouth.

Ivena held up one finger.

He closed it.

Excellent governance already.

"I brought the next two customers," she continued. "And I'm the one they trust enough to watch the thing run before they hand him money."

She pointed at Arlo.

He looked pleased to be included.

Then less pleased when she finished.

"So if this becomes something, I own part of the something."

There it was.

Clean.

Binding language had not happened yet.

But the room had already changed.

I looked at Arlo.

"You agree?"

"Mostly."

Ivena looked at him.

"Completely."

"Mostly with the number."

Better.

I sat.

The chair was new.

Or I had not noticed it before.

Either possibility was becoming common.

"What number?"

Ivena wanted one fifth of the current formulation venture going forward.

Not Arlo's workshop.

Not future ceramic work.

Not anything he invented unrelated to filtration.

This formulation and direct revisions descended from it.

One fifth.

My brain disliked the fraction immediately.

Not because it was unfair.

Because I knew what this could become.

Maybe.

That word had improved enough to become irritating in new ways.

I remembered later filters in warehouses, mills, field hospitals, alchemy shops, municipal treatment lines.

I remembered crates of cartridges stacked beside loading bays.

I remembered complaining about replacement prices in places where nobody cared how the first ugly ceramic housing had been invented.

One fifth of that future was enormous.

If this was that future.

If Arlo's two-chamber design led there.

If another inventor did not beat us.

If the whole thing did not become obsolete.

If my memory of who owned the industry was not another polished story told forty years later.

Lots of if.

Still enormous.

"Where does the fifth come from?"

Arlo had prepared for that.

Half from him.

Half from me.

I stared at him.

"Generous."

"I thought so."

"With my share."

"Half your generosity."

Ivena leaned against the bench.

"You can say no."

True.

I could.

The original agreement protected my share.

Arlo's process.

My early capital and remembered lead.

No Ivena ownership clause because neither of us had been clever enough to understand what she would become to the work.

I could enforce that.

Probably.

I could also put two silver into production, hire more kiln time, and argue that my capital replaced part of her leverage.

If I had two silver.

I did not.

Vale would lend it.

That thought arrived so smoothly I almost admired it.

Repay clean once, return with a real product, deposits, customers, and a growing order book.

Better terms this time.

Probably.

Borrow two silver to preserve a larger percentage of something I believed might become valuable.

Financially defensible.

Maybe even smart.

Also exactly the kind of sentence that had once ended with Vale owning part of my week.

I read Ivena's line again.

Not your test bucket.

The later history in my head had no Ivena in it.

No Arlo either, for that matter.

Just the mature object.

Arwick filters.

Clean name.

Clean history.

Somebody, later, would probably explain how the design emerged from a sequence of improvements in ceramic mana separation.

They might even be correct.

Technically.

No sentence in that history would include a dyer buying extra ash because she had learned exactly how much failure a catch basin should be allowed to hold.

That did not make her contribution smaller.

It made the history worse.

"One fifth is high," I said.

Ivena nodded.

"Yes."

No defense.

Good.

"One eighth."

"No."

"One sixth."

She looked at Arlo.

"He always do this?"

"Until food interrupts him."

I had eaten recently.

Unfortunate.

Ivena wanted one fifth because she was also putting in cash.

That part had not been on the first page.

Half a silver now.

Another half from the first month of her unit savings if the current run stayed stable.

There.

Different system.

Not just ownership for advice.

Ownership plus customer access plus process knowledge plus capital.

Arlo would put in repair revenue.

Less than he wanted.

Enough.

My contribution would be smaller.

Existing inventory share, some cash, and the original formulation rights.

My percentage still shrank.

The venture's ability to make six units increased.

Annoying relationship.

I countered at one sixth with a condition.

If Ivena brought future customers directly, she received referral margin on those sales in addition to ownership.

She thought about it.

Arlo thought about it longer.

That was useful information about who had expected what.

Ivena asked how long referral margin lasted.

Three orders per customer.

She wanted five.

Two.

"You just said three."

"Market moved."

She laughed.

Arlo put his head in his hands.

We settled on three.

One sixth ownership.

Equal dilution from Arlo and me.

Referral margin on the first three orders from customers she directly originated.

Her dyehouse did not count.

She objected.

It was the reason the venture existed.

Exactly why it did not count.

She called that convenient.

It was.

We left it anyway.

Then came the capital problem.

I could contribute twenty copper without making rent exciting.

Thirty if I became optimistic about food.

I chose twenty.

Arlo looked disappointed.

Not in me.

In arithmetic.

Good distinction.

The remaining gap came from delaying one batch of his lamp repairs and Ivena advancing more of the hardware cost against her future customer margin.

Nobody liked that either.

Excellent.

Arlo rewrote the page.

Three sections.

Three names.

I read every line.

Ivena watched me.

"You trust us?"

I looked at Arlo.

He started laughing before I answered.

Apparently some material survived repetition.

"Yes," I said. "I also read contracts."

Ivena considered that.

"Fine."

She read the whole thing too.

I liked her more immediately.

We signed.

Arlo first because it was his paper.

Ivena second because she took the pen from him.

Me third because apparently history could survive the indignity.

My share of a future thing became smaller.

The present thing became more capable of existing tomorrow.

I felt both facts.

That was probably healthier than pretending only one mattered.

Arlo took the page away before I could reread my lost percentage into a personal tragedy.

"If we're doing six, we need the lower shelf cleared."

I looked at the shelf.

Clay sacks. Broken molds. Three boxes of fittings. One object that might once have been a kettle.

"You changed my ownership and immediately assigned manual labor."

"Partnership."

Ivena handed me a bucket.

"Third name. Third pair of hands."

There was no argument available that improved my position.

I carried the bucket.
