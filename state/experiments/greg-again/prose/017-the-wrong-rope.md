# Chapter 17: The Wrong Rope

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/017-story-search.md`

---

On the seventh morning after Mara left, I had a blank sheet of paper, nine copper, and no useful excuse for either.

The paper was worse.

Money had rules.

Spend less. Earn more. Avoid lenders whose offices contained hanging meat.

Writing to Mara required content.

True content, specifically.

Inconsiderate standard.

I had placed the page beside Tavin's old cards before breakfast.

After breakfast it remained blank.

The cards had not helped.

Neither had staring at them.

I knew because I had tried both.

Arlo's workshop was easier.

Things there complained in measurable ways.

Two new ceramic housings were cooling beside the kiln when I arrived. Ivena had taken over one corner of the long table with order slips, wash samples, and a ledger Arlo claimed was making his workshop look respectable against its will.

She had added another customer name.

Not an order yet.

A tannery that wanted to see one of the soapworks units run first.

The venture was moving.

My purse was not.

That was not a contradiction.

It was becoming annoyingly normal.

I had twenty copper in the current production run, exactly as agreed. Arlo had repair money tied up in brass fittings. Ivena had advanced more hardware cost against future referral margin. Deposits covered enough to make stopping stupid and not enough to make any of us feel rich.

Good business, apparently.

I carried three clay sacks from the alley because ownership continued producing manual labor at a remarkable rate.

On the second sack, my shoulder reminded me that nineteen was younger, not mechanical.

On the third, Rusk appeared in the doorway.

I almost dropped it on his foot.

Not intentionally.

Mostly.

He looked exactly as amused as he had the first day I borrowed money from Vale.

"Greg."

I put the sack down.

"Rusk."

Arlo looked between us.

"Friend?"

"No."

Rusk nodded.

"Correct."

Ivena kept writing.

She had learned useful triage.

Rusk held out a folded note.

Vale's handwriting.

Short.

**Lower quay. Half day. Paid tonight. Bring Guild token.**

There was a number beneath it.

More than the gate work.

Less than the road contract.

Enough that my nine copper stopped feeling philosophical.

"What happened?"

Rusk shrugged.

"Traffic."

That was not an answer.

"Vale thinks you can be useful without being expensive."

That was.

I looked at the kiln.

Arlo did not need me.

Ivena looked up only long enough to point at the stack of empty crates by the wall.

"Move those before you go."

Ownership.

I moved the crates.

Then I went to the river.

The North Gate had not closed.

That would have been simpler.

Instead the city had restricted loaded wagons while Vessa's crews opened more of the eastern repair face. One heavy cart at a time through the narrowed lane. Inspections at both ends. Delays long enough that teamsters had begun finding routes that involved fewer officials and more water.

The lower quay had inherited the difference.

Carts stood nose to tail along the river road.

Porters carried sacks between wheels because waiting for a clear path had apparently been abandoned as a concept.

Three barges sat where I remembered two mooring places.

One had the yellow Vey Shipping mark on its side.

Another had the same mark painted smaller near the stern, newer and crooked enough that someone had done it from a ladder they disliked.

The company I remembered later had stone offices and schedules people complained about as if schedules were natural phenomena.

This version had two barges tied together because there was nowhere else to put them.

Present first.

Vale stood beside a stack of wrapped paper bales arguing with a merchant whose hat cost more than my sword used to.

Not the good sword.

That distinction still pleased me.

Vale saw me and ended the argument by turning away while the merchant was still talking.

Future Antonius had done that to ministers.

Apparently the technique required no capital.

"You came."

"You offered money."

"Reliable relationship."

The paper bales belonged to a printer upriver. Vale had financed the shipment. The printer had paid part. The balance depended on delivery. The barge carrying the paper was stuck outboard of another vessel because the quay was full.

Normally it would wait.

Today another cargo was arriving before dusk and the harbor clerk had started assigning penalties by the hour.

Vale wanted the paper moved to a warehouse before someone discovered that congestion was just another word for theft with witnesses.

"You need a guard."

"I have guards."

Two men stood near the bales.

One I recognized from Vale's office.

The other had shoulders designed for moving arguments physically.

"Then what am I doing?"

Vale pointed toward the water.

"Whatever she tells you."

A woman on the outer barge was shouting at three deckhands and one man on the quay with a boat hook.

Gray braid tied at the back of her neck. Sleeves rolled above both elbows. One bare foot on the rail because apparently shoes had lost the negotiation.

She pointed downstream.

Two men moved immediately.

The third asked a question.

She pointed downstream again.

He moved faster.

"Who is she?"

"Orra. Vey."

That was all Vale knew or all he intended to give me.

Fair.

I went down the loading steps.

Orra saw the Guild token first.

Then the sword.

Then me.

"Can you swim?"

"Yes."

"Good. Don't."

Useful introduction.

She put me at the upstream end of the inboard barge.

The arrangement took longer to understand than it should have.

The paper was on the outer boat.

The inboard boat carried grain and had arrived first.

To bring the paper barge against the quay, they needed to move the grain barge downstream far enough to open the berth, then warp the paper barge inward without letting its stern catch the current and swing broadside.

Simple.

The word arrived before I had earned it.

I kept it to myself.

Ropes ran from bow and stern to iron rings set into the quay wall. Another line joined the two barges. Men with poles controlled the gap between hulls while the crew shifted tension a little at a time.

I had seen river crossings.

I had fought beside river companies.

I had watched engineers move siege equipment across worse water than this.

None of those memories made me a riverman.

I knew that.

Very responsibly.

Then I started modeling the ropes.

The upstream bow line on the paper barge looked bad.

Not broken.

Furred white where wet fibers had opened around the outer twist.

It carried load every time the hull moved away from the wall.

I watched it tighten.

Ease.

Tighten again.

Orra came past with a short coil over one shoulder.

"That bow line wants replacing."

She glanced at it.

"Later."

"It's opening."

"I see it."

She kept walking.

I followed the line with my eyes.

Ugly rope.

Visible failure.

Very attractive problem.

Orra stopped two steps away.

"Watch the stern."

I looked downstream.

The paper barge's stern sat perhaps three feet farther from the quay than the bow.

Current pressed along the hull and peeled away in a widening seam of dark water.

"The rope is upstream."

"The river isn't."

Then she was gone.

I disliked how much information fit into four words.

Work moved.

The grain barge shifted first.

Poles down.

Stern line eased.

Bow line held.

Men on the quay hauled until the gap widened enough to move the paper barge inward.

For ten minutes, nothing became dangerous.

That was apparently the trick.

Vale's porters started passing bales across a loading plank as soon as the outer barge came within reach.

One bale at a time.

Heavy enough to make the plank bounce.

Light enough that two men could move one without machinery.

I stood where Orra had put me and watched the stern.

Mostly.

The ugly bow rope kept entering the edge of my vision.

The first six bales came across.

The paper barge rode a little higher.

The current changed against the exposed hull.

Barely.

I felt clever for noticing.

Then a cart on the river road backed into one of the quay posts.

Not hard.

Hard enough.

The horse jumped forward.

The cart wheel struck a stack of empty pallets.

The pallets went over.

One hit the man tending the downstream stern line.

He stumbled sideways.

Did not fall.

His hands came off the rope.

That was enough.

The line ran half a yard before he caught it again.

The paper barge's stern moved away from the quay.

Current entered the gap.

Fast.

The stern swung farther out.

The bow stayed close.

My brain found the geometry.

Pivot.

Bad.

I reached for the spare line beside me.

If we reinforced the stern and hauled it back before the angle increased, the hull would straighten.

I looped the spare around the nearest iron ring.

Orra shouted.

Not my name.

"OFF THAT LINE!"

I looked at her.

She was already moving toward me.

"Hold the stern," I said.

Wrong thing to say to a woman running toward you because you are doing something wrong.

"CUT IT!"

Hear.

Move.

Understanding could file a complaint later.

I dropped the spare.

Drew the sword.

The downstream stern line had gone hard as iron.

Not the spare.

The original.

Orra pointed at it.

"Cut the stern!"

Everything in me wanted to save the line.

A secured vessel was a controlled vessel.

That model had been useful in other places.

This was not another place.

I stepped outside the loop, raised the blade, and cut.

Wet hemp resisted more than training straw and less than fear expected.

The better sword entered cleanly.

Not through.

Half.

The barge pulled another foot away.

The remaining fibers screamed.

Second cut.

The line parted.

The stern jumped downstream.

For one very stupid fraction of a second, I thought I had just released a loaded barge into the river.

Then the hull turned.

Not broadside into the quay.

Away from it.

The bow line took load.

The paper barge swung around the fixed bow like a door opening into the current.

The gap beside the quay widened instead of collapsing.

The loading plank dropped.

One porter was still on it.

He went down with the outer end.

Orra reached him before I did.

She caught the back of his belt with one hand and the quay rail with the other.

The plank hit the water.

The porter hit Orra.

Both nearly went over.

I grabbed Orra's forearm.

Bad leverage.

Too much weight below us.

A deckhand caught my belt from behind.

Better leverage.

For half a second we became a very poorly designed chain of people.

Then the released stern line came back.

The free end had an iron eye spliced into it.

It whipped across the quay at shoulder height.

I saw it too late to move everybody.

Four inches.

That was what I had.

The support plane appeared almost flat beside Orra's head.

Not enough to stop iron.

Enough to change an angle.

The eye struck the plane.

Mana vanished out of me like a cough.

The little construct broke immediately.

The iron eye moved perhaps the width of two fingers.

It hit the wooden rail beside Orra instead of the back of her skull.

Wood exploded.

She flinched.

Did not let go of the porter.

Neither did I.

The deckhand behind me swore with professional depth.

We hauled the porter up.

He landed on the quay on top of my left foot.

Pain arrived with excellent timing.

The paper barge continued its swing.

Orra was already standing.

She pointed to the bow crew.

"EASE! NOT LOOSE!"

They eased.

She pointed to two deckhands on the grain barge.

"Poles down. Keep her off the stern."

They moved.

The paper barge passed the dangerous angle and entered slower water below the quay wall.

Only then did I understand what she had seen.

The stern line had stopped being a restraint.

It had become the hinge that would force the current to rotate the boat into us.

I had tried to strengthen the hinge.

Excellent.

Orra looked at the spare line I had looped around the ring.

Then at me.

"You nearly made it worse."

"Yes."

No defense available.

She nodded once.

Apparently accuracy had value even after the fact.

Then she looked at the cut end of the stern line.

"Good blade."

That pleased me more than it should have.

I put the sword away before pleasure generated another task.

The porter had a bruised hip and no interest in admitting it.

The loading plank had split one side rail.

Two paper bales had slid against the outer hull but stayed dry.

The ugly upstream bow rope still held.

I stared at it.

Orra noticed.

"Now we replace it."

I laughed.

She did not.

Work resumed.

Not immediately.

That mattered.

Orra inspected every ring and line before anyone moved another bale. The cart that had started the problem disappeared from the river road after three different people developed opinions about its driver.

Vale's guards moved the waiting crowd farther back.

I helped splice a temporary stern line under instruction I did not pretend to understand.

My hands were better at knots than nineteen-year-old me deserved and worse than the river crew considered acceptable.

Both could be true.

By dusk, the paper sat inside Vale's warehouse.

The barge had a new stern line.

The broken plank leaned against the quay wall with a chalk mark across it so nobody could accidentally decide it looked usable.

The lower quay remained crowded.

One emergency had not fixed traffic.

North Gate pressure continued arriving as carts, delays, extra river work, bad tempers, and people tying boats where boats normally did not fit.

That felt more real than a prophecy.

Vale paid me the number from the note.

All of it.

I counted once.

He watched.

"You still read contracts?"

"And count money."

"Comforting."

Orra came past carrying the damaged iron eye.

Vale asked whether I had been useful.

She looked at me.

I waited.

Domain authority had earned the answer.

"He tied the wrong rope."

Vale's mouth moved slightly.

Orra continued.

"Then he cut the right one when I told him."

That was apparently my review.

I had received worse.

Vale nodded.

No compliment.

No offer of future employment.

He went back to arguing with the printer's merchant.

Better.

I walked home with more money than I had that morning and a sore left foot.

The city smelled like river mud, horse sweat, hot bread, and smoke from somebody burning something they should probably have sold.

At my room, the blank page was still beside Tavin's cards.

I sat.

Nine copper had become more.

The page remained equally blank.

I picked up the pen.

Mara had asked for something true.

Not complete.

That helped.

I wrote.

**Mara,**

**I got paid today for a river job I understood less than I thought I did. A woman named Orra told me to cut a rope I was trying to save. She was right.**

I stopped.

Too much like a lesson.

I crossed out the last sentence.

Tried again beneath it.

**Arlo and Ivena have six units in production and another person asking questions. I mostly carry things and argue about fittings.**

True.

**Your mother gave me Tavin's cards. I remember burning one. I still don't remember why.**

Also true.

I looked at the first paragraph.

The crossed-out line stayed visible.

Good enough.

**I hope Halden smells better than the lower quay.**

**Greg**

I folded the page before I could improve it into dishonesty.

The packet office would open before breakfast.

I set the letter on top of the old cards so I would have to move one to forget the other.

Then I took off my boot and discovered the porter had left a purple footprint across three of my toes.

That, at least, required no interpretation.
