# Chapter 18: Six Weeks

The packet office opened before breakfast.

I arrived before the packet office.

That was irritating.

The door was still barred from inside. A painted board beside it listed destinations, departure days, weight limits, and enough small exceptions to prove that written communication had developed bureaucracy before civilization had developed good coffee.

Halden.

Seventh-day packet.

Vey Shipping carried the river leg.

Personal letters under one ounce went at the ordinary rate unless sealed for guaranteed delivery, insured for value, oversized, rerouted, held for signature, or addressed to someone important enough that ordinary rules became a personal insult.

Mara was not important enough for special postage.

That sounded worse than intended.

Good.

Normal postage.

The letter sat inside my coat.

I checked once.

Still there.

The packet clerk arrived carrying a ring of keys and bread folded inside paper.

She looked at me.

Then at the door.

Then back at me.

"We're closed."

"I noticed."

She considered this.

"You still standing here."

Also accurate.

She unlocked the door.

I followed after her because apparently this counted as permission.

The office was smaller than the later Vey packet halls I remembered. One counter. Two wall maps. Canvas mail sacks hanging from hooks. A brass scale with one foot shorter than the others and folded paper under it to compensate.

Present infrastructure.

Very glamorous.

The clerk put her bread beside the ledger.

"Destination?"

"Halden."

I gave her the letter.

That was the first difficult part.

Not writing it.

That had been worse.

Letting someone else take it.

The page stopped belonging to my room the moment her fingers closed on the fold.

She weighed it.

One copper.

I paid.

She stamped the front.

Current date.

Carrow mark.

Vey route mark beneath it.

Then she dropped the letter into a canvas sack with perhaps forty others.

No music.

No confirmation from the universe that I had done something emotionally responsible.

It landed against paper.

That was all.

"When does it leave?"

She pointed at the board without looking up.

Noon, assuming the lower quay remained usable.

Apparently yesterday had generated opinions.

"You hear about the barge?"

Now she looked up.

"Which one?"

Fair.

The lower quay had produced several bad stories recently.

I chose not to improve mine through repetition.

"Never mind."

She tied the sack once it was full enough, moved it beneath the Vey hook, and reached for her bread.

My letter had left my hands.

Not Carrow yet.

Enough.

Breakfast became possible again.

I ate standing beside a cookstall because sitting would have required finding another excuse not to do the second thing I had planned.

Money remained in my purse.

Not much by later-life standards.

Enough by current standards to become directional.

For most of the last month, every useful choice had failed at price.

Sword.

Food.

Rent.

Arlo.

Support instruction.

The better sword had already taken its turn.

The filtration venture had six units in production and three owners capable of having arguments without my supervision.

I had paid rent.

I had food.

The lower quay had paid me enough to stop pretending the fourth item on that list could wait indefinitely.

So I went to the Guild.

The red roof was still red.

I noticed that before I noticed the line at the front desk.

Bad habit.

Future objects kept trying to arrive before present people.

Sella had seven people waiting for her and the expression of someone personally betrayed by all seven.

I took a place along the wall.

A courier closed a road contract.

A woman with a bandaged wrist argued about whether a broken finger counted as contract injury when she had broken it after the contract technically ended but before leaving the client's property.

Sella said no.

The woman said the client's dog had caused it.

Sella asked whether the dog had been part of the contract.

The woman stopped.

Sella waited.

The woman swore.

Sella moved to the next person.

Institutions had violence too.

Mine arrived after twenty minutes.

Sella looked at me.

Her eyes went to the sword.

Then my face.

Then the purse when I put it on the counter.

Not literally.

Still irritating.

"Work?"

"Not today."

That got her attention.

I asked about the support instructor.

The one I had asked about before the road trip.

The one I had not been able to afford after buying the sword.

Sella reached under the counter and pulled out a different ledger.

Not the contract ledger.

Thinner.

Blue binding.

"Assessment first."

I looked at her.

"You didn't say assessment."

"I did."

No.

"You stopped listening when I told you the price."

Possible.

Annoyingly possible.

She opened the ledger.

Specialist instruction was not sold the way yard time was sold.

The Guild would happily rent me a patch of dirt and let me become worse at swordsmanship alone.

Support work involved other people's bodies, mana loads, ward interactions, healing delays, movement assumptions, and the general problem that a bad support mage could make competent people wrong at exactly the dangerous moment.

So the current Carrow Guild required an intake assessment before formal instruction.

Later it did not.

Or rather, later the process had been different enough that I had forgotten this version completely.

Future institution.

Present institution.

Different jobs again.

"Cost?"

Sella named the deposit.

Twelve copper.

Manageable.

Then the assessment balance.

Less pleasant.

Then the instructional block if I passed.

There was the familiar pain.

I could not afford the whole sequence today.

I could afford the door.

That was new.

"When?"

Sella turned the ledger toward herself and ran one finger down the page.

Then farther.

Then to the next page.

I disliked the motion more with every inch.

"Six weeks."

I laughed once.

She did not.

Of course she did not.

"Nothing sooner?"

Two local instructors could conduct basic intake, but both were on city support rotation through the North Gate works until the restriction order changed.

The specialist examiner came through Carrow on circuit.

Last circuit had been eight days ago.

I had been helping Mara pack.

Interesting collision.

Not a tragedy.

Just time doing more than one thing.

Six weeks.

I looked at the purse.

Money had finally stopped being the only problem.

Rude.

Sella tapped the ledger.

"Do you want the slot?"

That was the actual question.

Not whether six weeks was fair.

Not whether later Guilds had improved the system.

Not whether my first life had contained better access.

A slot existed in six weeks.

Deposit now.

Miss it, lose the deposit.

Show up, get assessed.

Pass or fail based on what I could do then.

Current body.

Current mana.

No historical exemptions for having once been impressive.

I counted twelve copper onto the counter.

Sella counted them again.

I had just counted them.

She worked for the Guild.

Different religion.

She wrote my name into the ledger.

**Greg. Bronze. Ward-support intake.**

I read it upside down.

"Ward support?"

"You make planes."

"I make several things."

This was technically true if failure counted as variety.

She held out her hand.

"Show me."

Here?

She looked around the Guild hall.

There were thirty people within range of making this embarrassing.

Apparently yes.

I placed my palm above the counter.

Small pulse.

Do not push.

Plane.

The construct appeared perhaps five inches wide on the first attempt.

Flat.

Pale enough that the wood grain remained visible beneath it.

I held it.

One breath.

Two.

Three.

Released.

No headache.

Sella watched the space where it had been.

Not impressed.

Not unimpressed either.

Clerk face.

Terrifying profession.

She wrote two more words.

**Low reserve.**

I objected.

She looked at me.

I reconsidered.

Accurate slander remained accurate.

Then she wrote the examiner's name.

My attention stopped.

**Kesra Dain. Silver.**

The Guild hall continued around me.

Coins.

Paper.

Someone laughing near the contract board.

A door opening behind Sella.

All of it became background.

Kesra Dain.

No title.

No later rank.

No black-and-gold expedition cord.

No six-page Guild history entry that somehow omitted every interesting thing about her.

Silver.

That was all the present had written beside her name.

Forty years later, Kesra would be one of the other six S-class adventurers on the continent.

One of seven.

My peer.

Eventually.

The word felt absurd from this side of time.

I remembered Kesra stealing dried pears from my pack during a three-week ruin survey and denying it while chewing.

I remembered her refusing every chair with a back because she claimed chairs were traps invented by people who wanted meetings to last longer.

I remembered a barrier line turning white under pressure while she laughed about something I could not hear.

I did not remember her as Silver.

Of course I didn't.

History had sanded that part away too.

Sella noticed me staring.

"Problem?"

"No."

She looked at the page.

"You know Dain?"

Not yet.

That answer almost came out.

I stopped it.

"I've heard the name."

Sella accepted this because people heard names.

Useful feature of civilization.

"She's good?"

Sella looked offended on Kesra's behalf.

"She's the examiner."

Not the question.

Also apparently the answer.

I looked at the date beside Kesra's name.

Six weeks.

First Deepwinter.

The seasonal word caught against something older.

Not Kesra.

Fire.

Red tile above us.

I looked toward the ceiling.

Nothing burned.

No smoke.

Just beams, plaster, and the faint water stain near the western wall that had probably existed for years and would someday acquire historical significance if enough important people complained about it afterward.

The Guild fire I remembered belonged to Deepwinter.

I was fairly sure of that.

Not a year.

Not a day.

A season attached to red tile becoming dark slate afterward.

If that memory belonged to this fire and this roof, then the fire sat inside the winter coming.

Months.

Not decades.

That was closer than I liked.

Still not proof.

A later roof and a remembered fire were not causation merely because future Greg had arranged them in that order.

I did not warn Sella that her roof might burn.

That would have been a remarkable way to lose access to specialist instruction.

The other winter memory took longer.

Red Winter.

East Verrel had been my original dating anchor because later everybody remembered East Verrel.

I had checked the current date against it on my first morning.

Four winters since.

The Red Winter I remembered sat six winters after East Verrel.

I counted again because I had learned something about confidence.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Not the winter coming.

The one after.

Outside the next year, unless one of the dates I thought was public history had shifted under me too.

Soren could remain wherever Soren currently was.

I did not need to hunt a man because a future scar had become impatient.

Good.

That thought actually deserved the word.

Sella had moved on from me mentally.

I could tell because she was already reaching for the next stack of contract slips.

"Anything else?"

I looked at the twelve copper now belonging to the Guild.

At the date.

At Kesra's name.

At my classification.

Low reserve.

Ward support.

Six weeks.

"No."

She tore off a narrow receipt.

Not instruction.

Not power.

A place on a list.

I put it in my coat beside the pocket where Mara's letter had been.

Empty pocket.

Full receipt.

Two different future things now moved without me.

One downriver.

One toward a date.

Outside the Guild, I almost turned toward Arlo's workshop.

Habit.

He and Ivena had work.

So did I, eventually.

The tannery wanted to see a unit run. Brass fittings remained irritating. Six orders still required hands, kiln time, clay, washing, drying, and all the ordinary labor that made remembered industries look cleaner from forty years away.

None of that required me this morning.

That was still strange enough to feel like forgetting something.

I checked the receipt again.

Six weeks.

There were several ways to waste six weeks.

I was experienced in most of them.

Training harder than my mana could support was one.

Buying things because waiting felt passive was another.

Inventing emergency roles for myself in a business that had stopped asking for them was probably a third.

I folded the receipt once and put it away.

Then I went to the yard.

Not for six weeks of preparation.

For today.

The tree behind the Guild still had the narrow strip of missing bark from my old sword.

The grooves in the dirt had softened under feet other than mine.

Someone else had been using the space.

Of course they had.

I drew the good sword.

One cut.

Recovered.

One small step.

Recovered again.

Then I put the sword away.

Enough.

There would be another day tomorrow whether I improved correctly or not.

I had a letter moving toward Halden.

A support assessment moving toward me.

A future S-class peer currently written in a ledger as Silver.

A Guild roof that might burn this winter.

A Red Winter that probably would not arrive until the next one.

And, for the first time since waking nineteen, a problem whose correct next action might actually be waiting.

I hated it immediately.
