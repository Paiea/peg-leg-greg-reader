# Chapter 19: Who Cleans It

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/019-story-search.md`

---

Five days passed without trying to kill me.

I noticed.

The first day I trained badly enough to remain alive.

The second I trained less.

That felt suspiciously like improvement.

The third I took a half-day warehouse count because twelve copper had recently become somebody else's twelve copper and the rest of my purse had opinions.

The fourth I did not go to Arlo's workshop.

That one took effort.

By the fifth morning, the receipt in my coat had softened along the fold and six weeks had become five weeks and two days without consulting me.

Time was moving correctly.

Rude system.

I was in the Guild yard trying to make one small support plane appear where I wanted it instead of where my hand happened to be when Ivena found me.

She did not greet me.

This had become reassuring.

"It works."

The plane vanished.

I looked at her.

Purple no longer owned her hands completely. Today they were only purple around the nails and one thumb.

Progress.

"The tannery?"

"It works."

Good.

I waited.

She looked annoyed that I understood there was more.

"That's the problem."

Of course it was.

I sheathed a sword I had not been using because carrying it while doing support work made me feel more competent than the evidence supported.

"How?"

"Come look."

Familiar sentence.

Last time it had ended with purple light crawling across a wet floor.

I followed anyway.

The tannery occupied a long stone building west of East Mill where the air became information before the sign did.

Lime.

Wet hide.

Old fat.

Bark liquor.

Smoke.

A smell with enough layers to qualify as architecture.

Ivena walked through it without reacting.

I tried the same.

The building won.

Inside, men worked around broad vats sunk waist-high into the floor. Hides hung from rails. Water moved through shallow channels dark enough that I stopped trying to decide what color it had begun as.

Arlo stood near the rear wash line beside one of our units.

Ours.

Still a strange word.

Ceramic housing.

Bypass valve.

Pressure tongue marked in black.

Removable chamber.

The ugly pipe joins had improved.

One was almost respectable.

I assumed Arlo had not made that one.

The tannery foreman stood beside him with both arms folded.

I had met him once when he watched Ivena's unit run.

He remembered me more clearly than I remembered him.

Common problem lately.

"Your man?" he asked Ivena.

"One of them."

Accurate and degrading.

Arlo pointed at the pressure tongue.

It sat low.

Not dangerous.

Wrong.

The feed through the unit had slowed until the bypass carried most of the line.

I crouched beside the housing.

No heat problem.

No mana crawling across the floor.

No cracked ceramic.

No dramatic failure at all.

"How long?"

The foreman answered.

First day, fine.

Second day, fine enough.

Third day, slower by midday.

They opened the bypass and finished the wash without it.

This morning Arlo pulled the chamber.

He showed me.

The lower half of the ceramic separator was packed with a gray-brown paste threaded with hair fine enough to disappear until it caught light.

Tannery water.

Of course.

Dyehouse sludge had taught us about heat, flow, concentration, and mana-bearing waste.

Tannery water had brought skin, hair, lime, fat, bark solids, and several things I decided not to identify while standing near breakfast.

The unit had worked.

Then it had continued working.

Then the part that caught dirt had become dirty.

I stared at it.

Future industrial history had omitted a remarkable number of obvious facts.

The foreman watched me looking.

"Can you clean it?"

"Yes."

Arlo made a small sound.

I looked at him.

"Probably."

Better.

The foreman did not look reassured.

"How long?"

That was the real question.

Arlo had already timed the first attempt.

Forty minutes to pull the chamber, scrape the loaded surface without damaging the pores, rinse it, check the lower seal, and reinstall it.

Longer because the housing bolts did not all like being removed after three days in tannery air.

Also information.

The foreman looked at Ivena.

"I lose a man for near an hour every third day, your eight-copper miracle can stay in your shop."

Eight copper had been the old disk.

The full unit cost more now.

Ivena did not correct him.

Good instinct.

"It saves wash," she said.

"Then sell me saved wash."

He pointed at the chamber in Arlo's hands.

"Don't sell me that."

There.

Outside mind.

Better than anything I had prepared.

I looked at the unit again.

In my later life, filters like this had been boring.

That was the achievement.

Cartridges arrived in crates.

Spent ones left in other crates.

Warehouses kept replacements on shelves.

Hospitals cursed suppliers when shipments were late.

Mills had maintenance schedules.

Nobody stood beside a clogged chamber asking who had invented the idea of cleaning it because by then the answer was a job title.

I had remembered the object.

Again.

Objects were apparently history's favorite liars.

"Spare chamber," I said.

Arlo looked at me.

"What?"

"Don't clean this one here. Swap it. Put a clean chamber in, take the dirty one back, clean it where you have tools."

The foreman unfolded his arms slightly.

Good.

Arlo did not.

Less good.

"They don't swap."

I looked at the unit.

"It's removable."

"This one is removable from this housing."

Ah.

He put the dirty chamber beside a clean one he had brought from the workshop.

Same design.

Same nominal width.

Same two locking grooves.

I tried to seat the clean one.

It stopped a finger's width high.

I turned it.

Worse.

Different unit.

Different firing shrinkage.

Different hand-finished lip.

Different brass collar.

The future had standardized several things without asking my permission to forget how.

"How far off?"

Arlo gave me three measurements.

Small differences.

Too small to matter until the object had to fit another object.

Then they became the entire problem.

"Template."

"Have one."

"Better template."

He looked at me.

Right.

Excellent contribution.

I tried again.

"Gauge the collar after firing. Pair the chambers to a finished size."

"Then this housing still needs the right chamber."

"Make three sizes."

"With three sets of collars?"

Yes.

No.

Maybe.

I could hear the mature factory in my head producing answers that required the mature factory.

Machined fittings.

Controlled firing.

Interchangeable parts.

Inspection gauges.

Suppliers who sold components to tolerances instead of to descriptions such as nearly the same as the last one.

I had skipped forty years of infrastructure in two sentences.

Efficient.

Wrong.

Ivena had stopped listening to me.

That was promising.

She was looking at the dirty chamber.

Then the clean one.

Then the foreman.

"What if you don't clean it?"

He looked at her.

"Then it stops."

"No. What if you don't clean any of it?"

Arlo understood first.

His face changed.

I got there half a breath later.

Ivena pointed at him.

"You take the dirty one. You bring back the same one clean."

Not interchangeable.

Matched.

Each unit kept its own spare.

Two chambers made for one housing.

Mark the pair.

Swap the matched spare in.

Dirty chamber came back to the workshop.

Clean it there.

Return it on the next route.

Crude.

Labor-heavy.

Completely achievable now.

The mature system in my head had tried to solve the forty-year problem.

Ivena had solved Tuesday.

Useful woman.

Annoying pattern.

The foreman looked at the unit.

"How long to swap?"

Arlo answered this time.

If the bolts behaved, ten minutes.

Maybe less once they stopped pretending every removal was an archaeological excavation.

He wanted a different fastener on the service side.

The current brass collar could be changed without changing the separator.

Ivena looked at me.

"That part useful?"

"Yes."

"Good. You can have one."

Generous.

The foreman still had not agreed to anything.

Correctly.

"Who watches the tongue?"

Ivena answered.

"Your wash man already watches the line."

The wash man, who had been close enough to hear all of this and had not volunteered for any new profession, looked over.

"I watch the vats."

Good correction.

He pointed at the pressure tongue.

"That thing means nothing to me."

Arlo moved toward it.

He explained the marks.

Black line meant normal.

Second notch meant slowing.

Lower notch meant bypass before pressure built.

The wash man listened for perhaps fifteen seconds.

Then he said, "Make slow yellow."

Arlo stopped.

"What?"

"Black is everything in here by second bell. Make the bad mark yellow."

I looked at the tongue.

He was right.

Of course he was.

Ivena looked pleased in the predatory way she got when a customer improved our product for free.

Arlo looked offended for three seconds.

Then he asked what color the bypass mark should be.

"Red."

Simple.

Current world.

Current worker.

No S-class memory required.

The foreman watched that exchange and unfolded his arms the rest of the way.

Not agreement.

Movement.

I could work with movement.

Ivena did not let me.

Also good.

She asked him what clean wash was worth per week.

Not the unit.

Not our costs.

Not the future.

His avoided disposal.

His recovered water.

The time his men did not spend changing wash early.

He named a number.

Ivena named a larger one.

He laughed.

She did not.

Arlo picked up the dirty chamber and stepped away from the negotiation with the relief of a man entering a cleaner kind of danger.

I stayed because apparently I enjoyed pain.

The foreman wanted to buy one unit outright and make maintenance our problem for a month.

Ivena wanted him to pay for the unit and maintenance.

He said then he was paying twice.

She said he was buying two things.

He said one of the things only existed because the other one got dirty.

She said his hides only got clean because his water got dirty and he still paid wash men.

He stared at her.

I nearly smiled.

Did not.

Relationship between seller and customer remained fragile enough without visible joy.

They moved numbers.

Slowly.

Not ownership.

Not percentages of imagined empires.

Actual copper.

Actual labor.

How often somebody walked from Arlo's shop to the tannery carrying a ceramic chamber full of material nobody wanted.

The route mattered.

The cleaning time mattered.

Breakage mattered.

If a spare cracked, the service stopped.

If the pressure tongue was ignored, the unit bypassed and saved nothing.

If Arlo spent an hour cleaning every chamber, he stopped making new units.

Recurring money had acquired recurring chores with impressive speed.

I did the calculation anyway.

Of course I did.

One unit under service.

Then four.

Then ten.

Then enough route density that one person could swap several on the same street.

Standardize later.

Central cleaning.

Replacement stock.

Scheduled runs.

A service network before the hardware became truly interchangeable.

The shape was good.

Very good.

My brain accelerated.

Ivena elbowed me.

Not hard.

Enough.

I looked at her.

She knew that face now.

Disturbing.

"One," she said quietly.

Yes.

Right.

The tannery foreman was not buying my future logistics system.

He was deciding whether to let one dirty ceramic box remain attached to one wash line for another week.

I slowed down.

One.

The agreement became smaller.

Better.

The tannery kept the current unit for a two-week service trial.

Not six.

Not a permanent order.

One.

We supplied a matched spare chamber.

We handled cleaning and return.

The tannery's wash man watched the pressure tongue after Arlo repainted the warning marks in colors that survived tannery dirt better than black-on-gray cleverness.

The foreman paid a small weekly service amount only if the unit remained usable through the work cycle.

If we missed a needed swap, he opened the bypass and did not pay for that interval.

He wanted that written.

Good.

So did I.

Ivena wrote it because her handwriting was better than Arlo's and more threatening than mine.

The foreman read every line.

Also good.

He added one sentence.

**TANNERY STAFF NOT RESPONSIBLE FOR CERAMIC BREAKAGE DURING NORMAL SWAP.**

Arlo objected immediately.

The wash man laughed.

We changed it.

Normal handling became defined.

Not well.

Well enough for today.

They signed.

No music.

No empire.

One dirty chamber sat on a cloth beside Arlo's boots.

Ivena folded the agreement and handed him a copy.

The foreman handed her the first service copper.

She did not hand any of it to me.

Correct.

It belonged to the venture.

Also irritating.

On the walk back, Arlo carried the dirty chamber in a lidded bucket.

Ivena carried the agreement.

I carried nothing.

This felt suspicious.

"Give me something."

Arlo handed me the bucket.

I should have specified.

It was heavier than expected.

The loaded chamber knocked once against the side.

I adjusted my grip.

Recurring value.

Very sophisticated.

Smelled terrible.

We reached the lower bridge and had to wait while two freight carts argued over one lane because a North Gate reroute had apparently taught the rest of Carrow to hate geometry.

Arlo looked at the stopped traffic.

Then at the bucket.

"If we do six of these, I'm not walking six chambers across the city every third day."

There.

Next constraint.

Not demand.

Not invention.

Route time.

Labor.

Cleaning capacity.

Spare stock.

The boring infrastructure that later history would compress into the sentence **filters became widely adopted**.

Ivena was watching the carts too.

"We don't do six."

Arlo looked at her.

"We have six orders."

"For units. Not service."

Different thing.

She pointed at the bucket in my hand.

"We learn one first."

I liked that.

Enough to distrust it less than usual.

"And if it works?" Arlo asked.

Ivena looked at me.

I had several answers.

Most of them involved future scale.

I chose the current one.

"Then we find out who cleans two."

She nodded.

Arlo did not.

He was looking at the bucket again.

"Not me."

Fair.

We kept walking.

Five days earlier I had paid twelve copper for a place on a list six weeks away.

Today somebody had paid the venture one copper because a dirty chamber would need attention again later.

Neither transaction had bought the thing people actually wanted.

Mine had bought access to future instruction.

His had bought somebody else's future labor.

I shifted the bucket to my other hand.

The smell followed.

History had remembered the clean water.

Somebody still had to carry the dirty part home.
