# Chapter 20: Ward Hand

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/020-story-search.md`

---

Sella found me work before I found lunch.

I had been trying to avoid both.

The work because I had trained that morning.

Lunch because lunch cost money.

One of those arguments was better than the other.

Sella stood in the Guild doorway with a folded contract in one hand.

"Greg."

That tone had never improved anybody's afternoon.

I stopped.

"How attached are you to the next six hours?"

"Emotionally?"

"Financially."

Worse.

I looked at the paper.

"What kind of work?"

"Ward hand."

I waited.

She did not elaborate.

Clerks believed nouns were complete systems.

"Meaning?"

"Meren has four warehouse seams overdue and no second hand."

That helped slightly.

"Meren who?"

"Voss."

Nothing.

No later history.

No famous expedition.

No statue.

Promising.

Sella continued.

"Routine commercial maintenance. You carry, scrape, mark, hold where she tells you, and do not improve anything unless she asks."

I looked at her.

"That last part was added for me."

"Yes."

Efficient institution.

"Pay?"

Five copper for the shift.

Six if the fourth site finished before evening bell.

Lunch not included.

Cruel profession.

"Why me?"

She looked at the yard behind me.

I followed her eyes.

The five-inch plane I had been practicing was gone now, but apparently its reputation had remained.

"You can hold a plane."

"Barely."

"Meren needs barely."

That was less flattering than accurate.

Two of the Guild's intake-capable support people were still tied up on North Gate city rotation.

That did not mean Carrow had run out of warders.

It meant everybody else with the right hands had inherited more of the boring work.

Doors.

Storage rooms.

Workshop dampers.

Old commercial ward seams nobody noticed until they stopped behaving quietly.

Meren could maintain them.

She could not conduct my formal support intake.

Different authority.

Different ledger.

The Guild had apparently invented specialization specifically to prevent me from getting what I wanted quickly.

I signed.

Sella took the paper back.

"Do what she says."

"You mentioned that."

"I know."

I liked Sella less every time she learned.

Meren Voss was waiting outside a warehouse off Copper Street with three cases of tools and the expression of a woman who had already been told I was temporary.

She was perhaps forty.

Maybe older.

Later life had damaged my ability to estimate adults who were younger than my first death.

Dark hair tied back with cord.

Gray work coat.

No visible Guild rank on the front.

A narrow copper tag at the collar carried the licensing mark instead.

Commercial warder.

Not adventurer.

Not instructor.

Not interested in becoming either before lunch.

She looked at my sword.

Then at the tool cases.

I moved toward the cases.

Good first answer.

The heaviest one contained ceramic plates.

Of course it did.

Current life had developed themes.

"Sella says you can hold a stable plane."

"For several breaths."

Meren looked at me.

"Several useful breaths?"

Different question.

"Probably."

She handed me the case.

"We'll find out."

The first warehouse stored lamp oil.

Excellent place to discover whether my definition of useful matched hers.

The ward seam ran around the inner doorframe between the receiving floor and the storage room.

Nothing dramatic.

A copper channel set into stone.

Ceramic bridge pieces at each corner.

Three flat anchor plates along the threshold.

The whole system existed to keep an accidental mana discharge on one side of the doorway from finding convenient metal paths into the other.

Not a fortress ward.

Not a battle barrier.

A commercial damper.

Later Carrow used better ones.

Of course it did.

Later versions were buried inside standardized frames, inspected through small glass windows, and replaced in sections without taking a chisel to the wall.

This one looked like somebody had taught masonry to resent magic.

Meren knelt at the lower corner.

"Case."

I put it beside her.

"Small scraper."

I opened the case.

There were four small scrapers.

I chose one.

Wrong.

She held out her hand without looking.

I replaced it with the narrow hooked one.

Correct.

We were making excellent progress.

The ceramic bridge at the lower corner had cracked through one mounting hole.

Still functioning.

That was the problem.

A dead piece was obvious.

A failing piece made people optimistic.

Meren scraped old seal compound from the edge, checked the copper channel with a dull metal probe, then pointed at a five-inch section beside the cracked bridge.

"Plane there. Flat."

There.

The thing I was being paid for.

I put my hand close to the stone.

Reached carefully.

No push.

No need for size.

A pale plane formed over the exposed seam.

Five inches.

Maybe slightly more.

I felt absurdly proud until Meren said, "Lower."

I lowered it.

"Closer."

Closer.

"Flat."

It was flat.

Apparently not enough.

I corrected the angle by perhaps two degrees.

She slid her probe beneath the cracked ceramic.

The copper channel hummed.

Very softly.

I felt the load touch the plane.

Not impact.

Pressure.

A small continuous insistence against something that had not existed a second earlier.

My body wanted to brace.

Wrong.

Old support habit arrived after the body did.

Let the construct take the load.

Do not turn my shoulder into part of the structure.

I exhaled.

The plane steadied.

Meren lifted the cracked bridge free.

Three breaths.

Four.

The pale surface began thinning at the edge.

She already had the replacement in her other hand.

Five.

The new bridge seated.

The hum dropped.

"Off."

I released immediately.

No headache.

Forearm tight.

Meren pressed seal compound into the mount.

"Useful breaths."

High praise.

I sat back on my heels.

"That's it?"

She looked at the next corner.

"For this one."

Right.

Support had always suffered from the terrible branding problem that success often looked like nothing happened.

We packed the tools.

The warehouse clerk signed one line.

Meren checked the time.

Then we walked two streets to the next site.

No celebration.

No advancement notice.

One cracked ceramic bridge no longer cracked.

Five copper remained theoretical.

The second site stored wool.

The ward seam had no damaged ceramic at all.

It had dirt.

Years of it.

Wool fiber.

Mud.

Grease.

Something black packed into the lower copper channel that Meren smelled and declined to identify.

My job became scraping.

Not magical scraping.

Scraping.

I spent twenty minutes on my knees removing industrial history from a groove the width of two fingers.

Later me had negotiated with dukes.

Current me found a button.

I put it on the ledge.

Meren glanced at it.

"Keep going."

Cruel woman.

The seam read clean once the channel was actually visible.

No replacement needed.

The warehouse owner complained about paying a maintenance call for dirt.

Meren showed him the signed work order where the problem was listed as intermittent seam bleed, not broken ceramic.

He said nobody had told him cleaning counted as maintenance.

She asked who he thought cleaned it.

I looked away.

Apparently the universe had developed one joke and intended to reuse it until I learned.

He paid.

We left.

Outside, Meren handed me a heel of bread from her coat pocket.

I looked at it.

"Sella said lunch wasn't included."

"It isn't."

"This appears to be food."

"You can owe me half a copper if that makes you feel safer."

I ate the bread.

Debt structure remained manageable.

The third site was an apothecary storehouse near the south market.

Not the later apothecary I remembered.

That one still did not exist.

This place had a painted green mortar above the door and enough dried root hanging from the ceiling to make walking through the front room feel like entering somebody else's pantry by mistake.

The back storage room held charged salts, bottled residues, catalyst powders, and several locked boxes that made my fingertips feel faintly warm from two steps away.

The threshold ward mattered more here.

Meren became less conversational.

Good signal.

The storehouse manager wanted the work done without moving the front rack.

The front rack blocked the left side of the ward frame.

Meren looked at the rack.

Then at him.

"Move it."

"It's bolted."

"Unbolt it."

"That takes an hour."

"Then the repair takes an hour longer."

He looked at me.

I was apparently the reasonable one by visual process of elimination.

Dangerous system.

"Can you reach around it?"

Yes.

Probably.

I could also cut around the frame, bridge the exposed section, and use a thinner tool from the side.

Later crews did worse things in collapsed ruins with active wards and dead people on the wrong side.

My brain began designing the shortcut.

Meren said, "No."

I had not spoken.

Disturbing.

"I was thinking."

"I know."

"You don't know what."

"You looked at the gap instead of the rack."

Fair.

She looked at the manager.

"Move it or we leave the seam closed."

State transition.

No negotiation with physics.

He swore.

Then found two workers and a wrench.

The rack moved.

Forty minutes.

Not an hour.

Everybody won except the workers.

Meren exposed the left vertical channel.

The complaint was heat.

One anchor plate warmed every few hours under no obvious discharge.

The storehouse manager had marked the times for three days because Meren had told him to.

Useful man after all.

The plate looked fine.

Copper clean.

Seal intact.

No scoring.

Meren tested the channel above it.

Then below.

Both normal.

She frowned.

I knew that frown.

Not her specifically.

Support people generally.

The expression you made when a system claimed nothing was wrong while continuing to be wrong.

I crouched beside her.

The warm plate sat at hip height.

Behind the moved rack, a newer iron brace had been drilled into the stone.

Not part of the ward.

Shelf support.

I touched it.

Cool.

Looked at the bolts.

Two passed close to the copper channel.

Not touching.

Probably.

Meren followed my eyes.

"Installed when?"

The manager said last month.

There.

Maybe.

I did not trust maybe enough yet.

"What is on the other side of this wall?"

He pointed through the room.

Mixing annex.

New lease.

Different tenant.

They had added a charge cabinet two weeks ago.

Better.

Meren stood.

"Show me."

We went around.

The mixing annex had its own ward line.

Newer.

Cheaper.

Badly placed.

Not dangerously.

Just lazily.

Its copper return ran along the shared wall before dropping toward the floor.

I looked at the height.

Same as the warm anchor on the other side.

Future instinct arrived cleanly this time.

Not memory of this building.

Shape.

Two support systems using the same structure without being designed together.

I had seen ruins do this.

Hospitals too.

Army camps every time somebody important decided one more ward could not possibly matter.

"They're sharing the wall."

Meren did not answer immediately.

Good.

She checked the annex line with a test stone.

The stone showed a faint pull toward the shared section.

Then she checked the old line from this side.

Same.

"Not sharing," she said.

Correction.

I waited.

"Coupling. Through the brace."

The shelf bolts were not touching the copper.

They did not need to.

Close metal.

Stone damp enough to carry a little charge.

Two ward returns pulsing at different times.

The new annex line was inducing just enough load into the old frame to warm the anchor.

Later installations used spacing rules for exactly this.

I remembered the rules.

I did not remember who had learned them first by setting two mediocre wards beside each other in a building full of expensive powder.

Probably people like Meren.

"If you open that plate live," I said.

She nodded once.

The induced load would have somewhere new to go.

Not catastrophic.

Could jump the open seam.

Could bite her hand.

Could trip the annex line.

Could spoil half a shelf of charged salts if everybody was unlucky in a very boring way.

I had been useful before she touched it.

That felt different from being useful after something broke.

Meren looked at the annex manager.

"Shut your cabinet down."

He objected.

Of course.

Different manager.

Same religion.

She showed him the test stone.

He stopped objecting.

The cabinet powered down.

The pull faded.

Back in the first storehouse, the warm plate cooled.

There.

Present proof.

Future lead survived.

Meren loosened the anchor.

"Plane."

I put it where she pointed.

Flat.

Five inches.

The line touched it.

Less pressure than the lamp-oil warehouse.

Different geometry.

My first instinct was to angle the lower edge toward the floor drain.

Later practice.

Give residual charge a preferred path away from the worker.

I moved the plane slightly.

Meren slapped two fingers against my wrist.

Not hard.

Immediate.

"Flat."

"Drain gives it somewhere to go."

"Drain is iron."

I looked.

Stone grate.

Iron frame beneath.

Right.

Later buildings isolated those.

This one did not.

I flattened the plane.

Current procedure.

Current materials.

Different world again.

Meren removed the anchor.

Nothing jumped.

She reseated it with a fresh insulating pad and marked the shared-wall coupling for a proper spacing repair on the next call.

Not today.

The annex tenant had not authorized wall work.

The old storehouse had not authorized rebuilding somebody else's ward.

The Guild contract covered diagnosis and safe restoration of this seam.

I knew three better technical solutions.

None had signatures.

Systems resisted through paperwork too.

We restored the line.

The anchor stayed cool for ten minutes.

Then fifteen.

Long enough for Meren.

She wrote the cause on the work order.

**INDUCED LOAD FROM ADJACENT INSTALLATION. TEMPORARY ISOLATION RESTORED. PERMANENT SPACING WORK REQUIRED.**

I read it upside down.

"That's ugly."

"It's accurate."

"I meant the system."

"So did I."

I liked her more after that.

We finished the fourth site before evening bell.

Barely.

No mystery.

No clever failure.

A cracked threshold plate in a cooper's storage room.

Meren pointed.

I held.

She replaced.

I released.

Done.

My plane was slightly steadier than it had been that morning.

Maybe.

I refused to promote maybe into progress without evidence.

My forearm hurt either way.

Back at the Guild, Sella paid me six copper.

Actual coins.

Not venture money.

Not future value.

Not ownership.

Mine.

I counted them.

Sella watched.

"You work for the Guild now?"

"One shift."

"Good answer."

I looked at her.

"Was that a test?"

"No."

Worse.

Meren signed the bottom of the work slip.

No rank change.

No assessment note.

No exemption from First Deepwinter.

I checked anyway.

She saw me.

"That wasn't instruction."

"I know."

"Wasn't assessment either."

"I know."

"Good."

Everybody in this building enjoyed saying that word at me.

She handed the slip to Sella.

Then looked back.

"You saw the coupled return before I opened it."

There it was.

The part my brain wanted to enlarge.

I stopped it.

"I've seen similar failures."

True.

Very true.

"And you tried to dump into an iron drain."

Also true.

She nodded toward my purse.

"Six copper seems fair."

I laughed.

She did not.

Of course she and Sella knew each other.

Meren left.

I stood at the counter with six copper in my hand.

Forty years later, nobody would have paid me to scrape wool grease out of a commercial ward channel.

They would have paid obscene amounts for me to decide which line mattered before a battlefield collapsed.

Same profession.

Not same work.

That thought was flattering enough to be dangerous.

I looked at my hand.

Five inches.

Several useful breaths.

One current warder telling me where to put them.

That was the work today.

Not a lesser version of support.

Support.

Current scale.

Current body.

Current pay.

Six copper.

Sella reached for the next contract.

I moved before she could find another use for me.

Outside, evening had cooled the street.

My forearm ached.

The support assessment was still more than five weeks away.

Kesra Dain remained a name in a blue ledger.

The tannery had not sent anybody running after me.

Mara's letter was still somewhere between cities.

Nothing had accelerated because I had finally found a useful shape for five inches of mana.

Good.

I bought dinner.

Not the cheapest dinner.

One better.

Six copper could survive the insult.

For the first time in either life that I could remember, somebody had paid me specifically because I could hold a barrier five inches wide.

Later history would have called that ridiculous.

Later history had not been there.
