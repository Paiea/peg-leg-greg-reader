# Chapter 24: The Watchman

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/024-story-search.md`

---

Pate heard them first.

One ear turned toward the road.

Then the other.

The mule lifted his head from whatever private argument he had been having with sleep and stood completely still.

I watched him.

Rainwater tapped from the quarry lip behind me.

Leaves moved in the dark below the overhang.

Nothing else.

Pate's nostrils widened.

There.

Not a sound exactly.

A pressure under one.

Rhythm too slow for rain.

Too irregular for wheels.

I put one hand on the sword beside me and listened harder.

Click.

Pause.

Click-click.

Stone touching stone somewhere down the Cut.

Then another sound joined it.

Hooves.

A lot of them.

I knew the noise before memory bothered giving me a picture.

"Fuck."

Pate agreed.

His lead rope tightened against the stake.

I got up.

Edda slept nearest the road, one hand under her cloak and the spear close enough that she could have reached it without opening both eyes.

I touched her boot.

Her eyes opened.

No confusion.

Useful woman.

"What?"

"Glasshorns."

She was upright before the second word finished existing.

"Here?"

"Coming through the Cut."

She listened.

The clicking had become easier to hear.

Horn against horn.

Hoof against limestone.

Not close yet.

Closer than I wanted.

"How many?"

"Don't know."

Edda looked at Pate.

The mule had stopped pretending he was brave.

His front feet shifted twice against the ground.

"Enough," she said.

That covered it.

Harl woke because Edda kicked his bedroll.

Less gently than I had touched hers.

"Up."

He made a sound that implied the road office did not pay him to be conscious at this hour.

"What happened?"

"Herd coming through."

That fixed him.

He sat up.

"What herd?"

"Glasshorns," I said.

Harl stared at me for one beat.

Then toward the dark road.

"They don't come this low."

The clicking answered him.

Harl stood.

Current evidence remained rude.

Edda pointed uphill from the overhang.

"We move the mule and gear above the road. Now."

Harl looked at the slope.

"Pate hates loose stone."

"Pate is about to hate something else more."

That also covered it.

We moved.

No shouting.

No fire to stamp out because the wet had denied us one earlier.

Bedrolls rolled badly.

Cook tin into pack.

Survey case first because Harl would apparently rescue it before any of us if forced to choose.

Pate did not need encouragement.

The mule had finally decided the slope was excellent.

Harl led him toward a narrow shelf where the quarry face stepped back above the Cut.

Loose stone slid under Pate's hooves.

He tried to climb too quickly.

Harl caught the halter near the cheek and slowed him.

"Easy."

Pate disagreed.

"Easy anyway."

The mule considered murder.

Then obeyed.

Edda took the rear of the load and kept one pannier from striking the rock face.

I carried the survey case.

Harl noticed.

"Don't drop that."

"I was planning to throw it at the herd."

"Don't."

"Glad we discussed it."

We got the gear onto the shelf.

The road below remained dark.

The sound did not.

More hooves now.

A low breathy grunt carried between the quarry walls.

Pate pulled toward the back of the shelf.

Harl did not tie him to the scrub pine behind us.

Good.

A frightened mule tied hard to a tree was how you converted fear into injury.

Harl wrapped the lead twice around his own forearm instead and stood near Pate's shoulder.

Also good.

I had spent enough years around bad animal handling to appreciate when nobody needed my advice.

Edda crouched at the edge of the shelf.

Spear in hand.

Not raised.

"What do they do?"

"Keep moving."

"If they see us?"

"Keep moving."

"If Pate panics?"

"That's more our problem than theirs."

She glanced back at the mule.

Pate had reached the same conclusion.

"Aggressive?"

"Not unless we make the road theirs and ours at the same time."

The first pale light appeared around the bend.

Not lantern light.

Too low.

Too soft.

A white-green smear moving between bare trunks.

Then another.

Then five more behind it.

Harl stopped asking questions.

Glasshorns came out of the dark in a line.

The name made them sound delicate.

They were not.

The first animal stood nearly as high at the shoulder as Pate, narrow through the belly and heavy through the chest, with a dark winter coat slick from rain. Two long horn sheaths swept back from its brow.

The horn itself was not glass.

People had been naming things badly for centuries.

The outer sheath was translucent enough that the faint light inside it showed through in cloudy bands.

Wet limestone made the glow look brighter.

The animal stepped into the open stretch below us.

Stopped.

Pate made a tiny sound deep in his throat.

The glasshorn lifted its head.

Edda's spear stayed low.

Good.

I had watched a Silver fighter once decide that a lead glasshorn stopping meant the herd was preparing to attack.

He had raised a shield.

The lead animal had interpreted this as a new piece of landscape occupying the route.

The rest of the herd had interpreted the lead animal stopping as permission to arrive all at once.

The shield survived.

His ankle did not.

The glasshorn below us smelled the air.

Its ears turned toward Pate.

Then toward me.

Nothing happened.

That was the difficult part for people with weapons.

Nothing happening could feel like an unfinished problem.

The animal lowered its head and kept walking.

The next three followed.

Then eight.

Then enough that counting became work I did not need.

Adults moved in a loose line two abreast where the Cut widened, narrowing without argument at the stone pinch below the overhang.

Smaller animals stayed inside the herd.

A young one had only short horn nubs with almost no light in them.

Another limped slightly on its right rear leg.

Nobody came to fix it.

The herd flowed around it anyway.

That was not kindness.

It was simply how bodies moving together learned to leave room.

Pate's breathing got worse.

Harl leaned against his shoulder and spoke quietly into one ear.

I could not hear the words.

Didn't need to.

The mule stopped pulling backward.

Edda looked over once.

Harl gave one short nod.

Three people doing three different jobs.

Nobody needed to become the solution.

A larger glasshorn came around the bend.

Male, probably.

The horn sheaths curved wider and the pale glow reached almost to the tips.

He had dried mud along one flank and a broken point on the left horn.

He stopped directly below our shelf.

Pate jerked.

Harl's boots slid half a step.

I moved toward the mule.

Harl shook his head once.

His animal.

Right.

I stopped.

The glasshorn looked up.

For a moment the pale horn light touched Edda's face, the spearhead, Harl's patched coat, and the underside of Pate's jaw.

The animal could see all of us.

Maybe smell the oat cakes too.

I had no idea whether glasshorns liked oat cakes.

Some questions did not deserve experiments.

The male snorted.

Scraped one front hoof across the road.

Edda's fingers shifted on the spear shaft.

Not raising it.

Ready anyway.

Then the herd called from farther ahead.

A low sound.

More felt than heard.

The male turned his head.

Walked on.

I let the breath out through my nose.

Edda heard me.

"You were worried."

"I said they weren't aggressive. I didn't say they were polite."

"Useful distinction."

Harl made a strangled noise that might have been laughter or Pate stepping on him.

The herd kept coming.

Twenty minutes.

Maybe thirty.

Time moved strangely when every pale horn arriving around a bend had the potential to be the last one and repeatedly was not.

We stood above the road until my legs began to stiffen.

Rainwater worked down the back of my collar.

Edda shifted her weight twice.

Harl's arm had to be numb from Pate leaning into him.

Nobody complained.

The last animal was a small female with one horn sheath clouded almost opaque.

She stopped to lick water from the quarry wall.

Apparently migration included refreshments.

Then she followed the others into the dark.

The clicking faded.

Pale lights disappeared one by one between the trees.

We waited anyway.

Old field rule.

The last animal was not always the last animal.

A minute passed.

Then two.

A single late juvenile came running around the bend hard enough to slide on the wet stone.

Edda looked at me.

I looked back.

"Now?"

"Still no."

The juvenile recovered, saw absolutely none of us because it was busy being late, and ran after the herd.

Its little horn nubs flashed twice between the trees.

Then the Cut went dark again.

Edda waited another full minute.

"Now?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"You wanted certainty, hire Harl."

Harl looked offended.

"I certify roads."

"Exactly."

Pate chose that moment to urinate on the shelf.

The discussion ended.

We did not move camp back down.

Edda made that decision before anyone suggested it.

The road could have the road until morning.

I returned to watch from the shelf.

Harl stayed awake another ten minutes because Pate refused to settle.

Edda sat beside me with her cloak wrapped around both shoulders.

"You've seen those before."

Not a question.

"Yes."

"A lot?"

I listened to the disappearing herd.

"Enough."

She looked at me.

The dark made lying easier and less useful.

"More than somebody your age should have."

There was no safe answer to that.

"I've had a complicated month."

Edda stared at me for another second.

Then snorted.

"That was a terrible answer."

"I know."

She did not ask again.

I liked her more for that too.

By dawn, the road looked different.

Not destroyed.

Used.

Hundreds of split hoofprints pressed into the wet center strip. Mud had been pushed to both shoulders at the narrow bend. A few pale hairs clung to a thorn bush near the overhang.

At the stone pinch, Harl crouched with his measuring rod.

He had been there long enough that Edda had eaten half her breakfast before I asked what he was doing.

"Measuring the same road after animals walked on it."

"Did it get wider?"

"No."

"Then I have saved you time."

Harl ignored me.

He measured the churned shoulder.

Then the distance from the rock face to the drop on the other side.

Then he walked fifty paces back and looked at the line of hoofprints again.

"They always use this?"

He asked me without looking up.

"I don't know about this exact Cut."

That mattered.

I knew glasshorns.

I knew later roads.

I did not know every year between them.

"They use limestone corridors after the first cold rains," I said. "Usually moving down from higher ground."

"Usually?"

"Usually."

"How long?"

"Movement can last days. Sometimes longer. Depends on the herd and weather."

Harl finally looked at me.

"That is extremely unhelpful."

"You asked an animal question."

"I asked a road question."

"The animals didn't."

Edda made a sound into her oat cake.

Harl wrote something on his board.

Not my answer.

Good.

He walked downhill to where the first fresh tracks had entered the Cut from a side slope.

Checked the ground.

Walked farther.

Came back.

"Current corridor use," he said.

Edda swallowed.

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I cannot call this reliable for pack traffic until we know where they enter and where they leave."

"Does that kill the route?"

"No."

Harl tapped the board with one finger.

"It makes the route seasonal until proven otherwise."

There it was.

Not monster attack.

Not road failure.

A herd had somewhere to be.

The road office had somewhere else it wanted people to be.

Same strip of ground.

Problem.

I left the interpretation there.

Harl did not need help having his own thought.

We packed the shelf.

Pate came down the loose stone carefully, then stopped at the road and smelled every hoofprint as if gathering evidence for a lawsuit.

Harl let him.

Edda checked the bend ahead before waving us forward.

The survey was not done.

We still had the quarry spur, the west slope, and whatever the rain had done beyond it.

My socks were almost dry.

My shoulders hurt from sleeping badly against stone.

The sword had spent the night unused.

Good sword.

Edda fell into step beside me after Harl and Pate moved ahead.

"You have first watch tonight."

I looked at her.

"I had second watch last night."

"And you found the herd."

"That is not a reason to give me more watch."

"It is exactly a reason to give you more watch."

I considered arguing.

Then Harl called back that the road narrowed again ahead and Edda moved past me to look.

Conversation over.

Apparently competence had consequences.

Fine.

I followed them west.
