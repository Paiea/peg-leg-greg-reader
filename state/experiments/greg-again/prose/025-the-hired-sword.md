# Chapter 25: The Hired Sword

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/025-story-search.md`

---

The glasshorns left the road before lunch.

Harl proved this by making us walk half a mile past the place where it was already obvious.

Surveying had standards.

The herd tracks ran west through the Cut in a churned double line, then climbed an old quarry spur where the limestone bank broke low enough for large animals to take the slope without developing opinions about engineering.

Harl followed them up.

Pate objected.

Edda sided with Pate.

"You have eyes," she said.

Harl crouched beside a split hoofprint pressed deep into the mud.

"Eyes are not measurements."

"That sentence is why nobody invites you anywhere."

"I was invited here."

"Paid."

"Better."

I carried the survey chain.

This had become my role through a process involving Harl handing it to me and never asking for it back.

The chain was heavier than it looked.

Most useful equipment was.

The quarry spur climbed twenty paces above the road and opened onto an old bench cut into the hill. We found more tracks there, fresh mud kicked over pale stone, then the herd line turned north through scrub and disappeared toward higher ground.

Harl walked another hundred paces anyway.

Edda looked at me.

"If he keeps going, do we have to follow him?"

"You're the lead."

"Bad answer."

"Accurate answer."

She sighed and followed Harl.

So did I.

Pate came because nobody had explained labor law to him.

The tracks thinned where the ground hardened.

Harl stopped.

He took out his board.

Made a mark.

Then another.

"Corridor ends here for the route."

Edda looked around at the obvious absence of road.

"Excellent."

"No. Useful."

"I hate both of you."

I had not said anything.

"Preventative," she said.

Fair.

We returned to the Cut.

The road west of the quarry changed character slowly enough that I did not notice until Harl stopped measuring width and started measuring places where width had gone missing.

The old quarry traffic had cut a shelf along the slope.

Later roots had lifted parts of it.

Rain had softened the outside edge.

One section was wide enough for Pate if Pate remained emotionally committed to being one mule.

Two animals could not have passed there without becoming a negotiation.

Harl looked at the shelf.

Then at Pate.

Pate looked at the shelf.

Then at Harl.

The mule had learned to distrust professional interest.

Edda went first.

No spear in hand.

She strapped it across her back and walked the narrow section with both hands free.

At the far end she turned.

"Good footing inside. Soft edge outside. Keep him high."

Harl nodded.

He shortened Pate's lead.

I moved behind the left pannier without being told.

There.

That was new.

Not the task.

The absence of discussion.

Pate stepped onto the shelf.

One hoof.

Then another.

He leaned slightly away from the drop because Pate possessed survival instincts and no romantic attachment to scenery.

The outer pannier brushed my shoulder.

I pushed it inward.

Not hard.

Enough to stop the load from swinging.

Harl kept Pate's head straight.

Edda watched the animal's feet from ahead.

Nobody spoke until we were across.

Then Harl said, "Again on the return."

Edda looked at him.

"I assumed we were going home eventually."

"I mean same positions."

"I know what you meant."

He stared at her.

She smiled.

The road widened after that.

For almost an hour it became what the road office had probably hoped to find everywhere.

Hard stone under thin mud.

Enough shoulder for an animal to step aside.

Drainage still working in places nobody had maintained in years.

A person could imagine couriers moving quickly there.

A person could imagine pack strings.

A person with a generous imagination and no cart experience could imagine carts.

Harl ruined that by stopping at every turn.

"No."

He said it at the first tight bend.

Edda leaned on her spear.

"Nobody asked."

"Someone in the office will."

He measured the inside radius.

"No carts."

I looked at the bend.

Later, this section had been wider.

Not dramatically.

Two feet cut from the inside bank, maybe three.

Enough.

Somebody had done that work.

I had remembered the result as geography.

Harl was looking at the labor that did not exist yet.

I left him to it.

We heard the traveler before we saw her.

Not bells.

Swearing.

Good sign.

A woman's voice came around the bend ahead.

"If you eat that strap, I am selling you to a soup pot."

Edda lifted one hand.

We stopped.

Harl frowned.

"Traffic?"

"Apparently," I said.

"Don't sound happy."

"You wanted a road."

"I wanted measurements."

Consistent man.

The woman appeared with a small brown pack horse behind her.

Not a Guild courier.

No badge.

No armed escort.

Heavy wool coat patched at both elbows, narrow leather message case strapped across her chest, one pack roll tied behind the saddle.

The horse had its head turned far enough to chew the loose end of a strap while walking.

Her threat had not been theoretical.

She saw us.

Stopped.

Then looked at Harl's survey board.

"Road office?"

Harl looked briefly pleased to be identifiable by suffering.

"Yes."

"Good. Fix the west shelf."

"We're surveying it."

"Then survey it faster."

She looked at Edda's Guild token.

Then mine.

Her attention stayed on my sword for half a beat longer than it stayed on me.

Reasonable priority.

Edda stepped toward the inside bank.

"We passed a shelf wide enough for one animal about two hundred paces back. We can give you the road there."

The woman looked behind us.

Then at her horse.

"He'll back."

The horse chose that moment to bite the strap again.

"Eventually," she added.

Edda nodded.

"We'll move."

No argument over who had right of way.

No demand that the traveler reverse half a mile because we had official paperwork.

Edda turned us around.

Harl opened his mouth.

Closed it.

Growth.

We walked back to the wider shelf.

Pate had opinions about reversing professional progress but accepted them privately.

At the turnout Edda put Harl and Pate against the uphill side.

I stood outside Pate's shoulder where I could keep him from swinging his load into the passing horse if either animal decided the other had insulted its ancestry.

The woman led her horse through.

The animals sniffed each other.

Pate flattened one ear.

The brown horse ignored him and tried to eat my cloak.

"No," I said.

The horse considered this new policy.

The woman pulled its head away.

"Sorry."

"He has range."

"He has crimes."

Harl had already taken out his board.

"How often do you use the Cut?"

The woman looked at him.

"When the north road is stupid."

"Frequency."

"This month? Third time."

That got his full attention.

"Loaded?"

"Messages and cloth. Nothing heavier than him."

She slapped the pack roll.

The horse did not react.

"West shelf?" Harl asked.

"Walk him across. Don't ride. Don't use it after hard rain unless you enjoy digging animals out of mud."

"How long after rain?"

"Depends on rain."

Harl looked offended by weather again.

"One day? Two?"

"If the wind comes through, one. If it stays cold and wet, two. Sometimes three."

"Glasshorns?"

She frowned.

"What about them?"

"Herd came through last night."

That made her look east.

"Then I'm early."

I noticed the wording.

Harl did too.

"You expect them?"

"Everyone with sense expects them after cold rain."

Edda glanced at me.

I declined to become smug.

Professionally.

The woman adjusted the message case across her chest.

"They usually cut north at the old quarry. If you camp in the Cut during first movement, that's your problem."

Harl wrote.

"How long does movement last?"

"Go ask a glasshorn."

I liked her immediately.

Harl did not.

"Useful," he muttered.

The woman heard him.

"I'm not the one getting paid to make the road useful."

Edda coughed into one hand.

I looked at Pate.

Safer.

Harl asked two more questions.

Where she entered from the west.

Whether she had seen carts.

No carts.

One donkey string three days earlier.

Two men on foot yesterday.

No one she knew used the Cut for heavy freight unless they wanted to arrive with less freight.

Then she left.

No name.

No prophecy.

No reason to become part of my life.

She had somewhere to be.

The brown horse tried to eat the strap again as they disappeared around the bend.

"Soup," she told it.

Her voice faded west.

Harl stood over his board.

Edda waited.

I waited too.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing," Edda said.

"You're both doing something with your faces."

"Weather."

"We're under cloud."

"Complicated weather."

I had used that excuse recently enough to respect the attempt.

Harl ignored us and resumed walking.

The west shelf was worse than the traveler had promised.

Not impassable.

Worse.

The rain had soaked the outer half into dark clay over stone.

Harl tested it with the end of his measuring rod.

The rod sank two inches.

He tried farther inside.

Less than one.

Pate sniffed the ground.

Then stepped backward.

"Smart mule," Edda said.

Harl did not argue.

We unloaded half the gear.

No debate.

Survey case to me.

Chain to Edda.

Harl took the stakes.

Pate crossed light.

Then we carried the gear over by hand.

This was adventuring.

I remembered fewer songs about it.

On the other side, Harl measured the soft section.

Then the slope below.

Then the drainage ditch that had filled with leaves and mud.

He made three marks on the board.

"Not after rain," Edda said.

Harl looked at her.

"I decide that."

"I know."

"Then stop deciding it before me."

"Measure faster."

He went back to measuring.

We continued west.

Nothing attacked us.

This became increasingly suspicious.

By late afternoon the Cut descended toward maintained road again.

The grass thinned.

Wheel marks returned.

A boundary stone appeared beside the ditch with the road-office mark cut deep enough to survive people forgetting the road existed.

Harl stopped there.

He touched the old mark once.

Then looked back east.

Four days of road sat behind us.

Broken crossing.

Usable ford.

Quarry corridor.

Glasshorn tracks.

Narrow shelf.

Wet clay.

A stranger already using the thing anyway.

"That's it?" Edda asked.

"That's the western connection."

"So yes?"

Harl looked offended.

"What kind of yes?"

She stared at him.

"Road yes."

"There is no road yes."

"There absolutely is."

"No carts."

"Fine."

"No general freight."

"Fine."

"Single light pack passage in dry conditions. Foot couriers. Experienced handlers. Ford instead of the stone crossing. Cold-rain restriction until the herd movement is clear. West shelf closes after hard rain."

Edda waited.

"So yes."

Harl exhaled through his nose.

"Useful under conditions."

I smiled.

He pointed at me.

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

He knew me now.

Disturbing.

We made camp near the maintained western road.

First watch belonged to me.

Edda did not explain why.

She only handed me the time cord and lay down.

Progress had become administrative.

Nothing happened on watch.

I stayed awake anyway.

That also counted.

The next morning Harl walked part of the west shelf again after the wind had dried it overnight.

His rod sank less.

He wrote that down.

We turned east.

The return took less time because Harl had already measured everything he intended to distrust.

We still stopped.

Just fewer times.

At the narrow shelf, nobody discussed positions.

Edda crossed first.

Harl took Pate's head.

I took the pannier.

Same positions.

At the quarry spur, fresh glasshorn sign still marked the road but no new herd had passed.

Harl checked anyway.

At the ford, Pate stopped at the water.

He looked at me.

I looked at him.

"No oat cake."

Pate remained still.

Harl sighed.

"You trained him badly."

"I saved your survey case."

"Edda saved my survey case."

"I carried it."

"After I told you not to drop it."

"Team effort."

Edda stepped into the water.

Pate watched her.

Then followed.

No oat cake.

I felt betrayed.

We reached Carrow on the fifth afternoon.

Five days, because apparently Harl hated the road exactly as much as Sella had predicted.

The West Gate guard checked our tokens.

Looked at Pate.

"Still kicks?"

"Only people," Harl said.

Stable facts mattered.

Inside the walls, city noise arrived all at once.

Carts.

Vendors.

Hammering.

Someone arguing about onions.

Smoke without a forest attached to it.

After five days of listening for water, hooves, rock, and animals in the dark, Carrow sounded irresponsibly confident.

The Guild smelled like wet wool and ink.

Sella looked up when we entered.

Her eyes went to Harl first.

"Road?"

Edda pointed at him.

"Don't ask yes or no."

Sella paused.

Then looked at me.

I shook my head.

"You'll regret it."

Harl put the board on the counter.

"Conditional light use. Foot couriers and single light pack animals in dry conditions. No carts. No general freight. Stone crossing fails loaded-animal use. Ford is viable. Seasonal glasshorn corridor through the quarry section after cold rain. West shelf becomes unreliable after hard rain."

Sella listened without interrupting.

Professional woman.

"Worth marking?"

"Yes."

Edda looked at me.

I looked back.

There it was.

Road yes.

Harl continued before either of us could speak.

"Worth opening as general freight? No. Worth courier notice while North Gate restrictions remain? Yes. With conditions."

Sella pulled the completed chit toward her.

"Five days?"

"Five."

She stamped it.

Money became real again.

The Guild paid the field contract exactly as promised.

No heroic bonus.

No glasshorn bounty because we had wisely failed to kill anything.

No additional hazard payment for Pate's personality.

Still enough that the three copper I had spent preparing stopped feeling like a personal attack.

I counted the coins once.

Then again because being paid did not excuse arithmetic.

Edda collected hers and leaned one hip against the counter while Harl signed the road-office copy.

Sella looked at him.

"If they authorize repair survey, same escort grade?"

"Same escorts."

My head came up.

Edda's did not.

Harl kept writing.

"If they're available."

Sella looked at Edda.

"You heard him."

"Unfortunately."

Then at me.

"You?"

I looked at Harl.

He still had not looked up.

Pate was outside tied to the rail, probably committing a crime against wood.

"If I'm available."

Sella made no promise.

Good.

Neither did I.

Also good.

I left the Guild with field pay in my pocket, mud dried to both boots, one pair of socks that had survived better than the other, and no new rank.

The last part did not bother me.

Lower Rook Street smelled different when I turned the corner.

Not smoke.

Soup.

The cookroom chimney was working again.

Someone had repaired the stove while I was gone.

Of course they had.

Noll was sitting on the front step with a bowl in both hands.

He looked at my pack.

"Road still there?"

I stopped.

Considered Harl's answer.

"Under conditions."

Noll stared at me.

"You sound terrible."

"Survey work."

"You surveying roads now?"

"No."

I shifted the sword on my belt.

"I stood near the man who did."

Noll nodded as if this confirmed something important.

"Skilled labor."

"Exactly."

He went back to his soup.

I went upstairs.

My room had remained exactly as badly arranged as I had left it.

The brass key sat on the table.

The marked cord stayed near the window.

No new packet waited under the door.

Good.

Five days did not have to pay every account.

I set the sword against the bed.

Counted the field pay one last time.

Then took off my boots.

Noll started snoring through the wall before I finished hanging the less-dead sock near the window.

I lay down.

For one stupid second I tried to remember whose watch came next.

Nobody's.

I slept.
