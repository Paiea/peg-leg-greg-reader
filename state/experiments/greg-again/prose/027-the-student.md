# Chapter 27: The Student

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/027-story-search.md`

---

The next morning I spent one silver on being told I was bad at something.

This was progress.

It did not feel like progress while Sella took the coin.

She held it up between two fingers.

"Instruction?"

"Yes."

"Sword?"

"Yes."

She looked at me.

Then at the coin.

Then back at me.

"Voluntarily?"

"I have made several financial mistakes recently. I wanted variety."

That almost got her.

Almost.

She slid the coin into the Guild tray and wrote my name into a narrow ledger.

The line beneath mine had a Bronze courier paying for climbing instruction. The line above belonged to someone named Fenn who had apparently purchased three hours of spear work and one replacement practice shaft.

Expensive morning for Fenn.

Sella blotted the ink.

"Lera has the east lane."

I paused.

"Lera."

Sella's eyes moved up from the ledger.

"You paid for instruction before learning the instructor's name?"

"I asked her one question."

"Did the question contain her name?"

"No."

"Then this is going very well."

I took my Bronze token back.

Lera.

Good.

That was one less person in Carrow stored under a profession.

The yard sounded almost exactly as it had the first time.

Wood striking wood. Boots in sand. Somebody swearing at a shield strap. An instructor counting in a tone that made eight sound morally disappointing.

The difference was me.

I knew that because I stepped through the doors without immediately cataloguing twelve things everyone else was doing wrong.

Only seven.

Growth.

Lera stood in the east lane with the same faded Silver leathers and the same short stick she had used to point at my feet weeks ago.

One side of her head was still shaved close. The longer side had been tied back badly enough that several strands had escaped.

She watched me approach.

Recognition arrived slowly.

Then all at once.

"Furniture."

"Greg."

"I liked Furniture."

"I have paid money now."

"That makes it worse."

She held out her hand.

I gave her the stamped instruction chit Sella had given me.

Lera checked the time mark and tucked it into her belt.

"Sword."

I touched the good sword at my hip.

"Mine?"

"Did you pay me to teach the rack?"

Fair.

I drew it.

The blade felt familiar now.

Not like an old limb.

Not even close.

But familiar in the honest way an object became familiar after enough mornings of putting it on, enough practice cuts, enough roads walked with the weight at one hip, enough times checking the edge before a job that never required drawing it.

Lera looked at the blade.

Her eyebrows moved.

"Better sword."

"Yes."

"You get better too?"

"Some."

"Show me."

She pointed at the post.

I took position.

Overhead cut.

No committee meeting.

Hands lifted. Weight settled. Hips joined late enough not to announce themselves and early enough not to sabotage the rest. Blade down through the marked line.

Recover.

Not beautiful.

Serviceable.

I knew that before Lera spoke.

"Again."

I did it again.

"Again."

Third cut.

She clicked her tongue once.

"Less furniture."

I lowered the sword.

"Thank you."

"Wasn't praise."

"I paid a silver. I'm taking what I can get."

She pointed with the stick.

"Side step."

I stepped left.

Small.

Enough to leave a line without trying to relocate to another district.

Right.

Better.

Lera's face remained professionally unrewarding.

"You practice?"

"Yes."

"Fight?"

"Some."

"Work outside?"

"Five days last week."

"That explains your feet."

I looked down.

"They're attached."

"Better than before."

Again, somehow not praise.

She moved behind me and tapped the outside of my rear ankle with the stick.

"You don't plant it as hard."

"Roads objected."

"Roads are good instructors."

"Cheaper than you."

The stick hit my calf.

Not hard.

Enough.

"Road doesn't stop you when you're stupid."

"Neither did you last time."

"You weren't paying."

That was difficult to argue with.

Lera moved in front of me.

"Cut."

I cut.

The stick touched my ribs before my sword returned to guard.

I stopped.

She had not moved quickly.

That made it worse.

"Again."

Cut.

Tap against my shoulder.

Again.

Cut.

I recovered faster.

The stick touched my forearm.

"You're hitting me after the cut."

"Yes."

"I noticed."

"Good."

"This lesson is already paying for itself."

She did not smile.

I was increasingly convinced this required active effort.

"What's wrong?"

"You think the cut ends when the thing gets hit."

"Often useful."

"Thing might have friends."

"Often inconvenient."

She pointed at the post.

"Again."

I cut.

This time I thought about the recovery before the blade landed.

That made the cut worse.

Lera hit me in the ribs.

"Excellent."

"That was worse."

"Yes. Now you know where the work is."

I stared at her.

There were instructors in my later life who could have delivered that sentence with enough dignity to get it carved into a school wall.

Lera scratched the side of her nose with the practice stick.

Better.

"Cut. Arrive. Recover."

"Arrive?"

"Blade gets where it's going. So do you. Then leave ready."

"Leave where?"

"Anywhere I can't hit you for free."

Practical education.

I liked it.

Which remained dangerous.

She had me cut again.

And again.

And again.

The first problem was that recovery had too many pieces.

Hands.

Feet.

Blade.

Eyes.

Breathing.

Weight.

All of them had been doing something after a cut already. The problem was that they had not been doing the same something.

My hands wanted guard.

My feet wanted to admire the successful attack.

My eyes wanted to find the next opening.

My shoulders wanted to remain involved in the previous one.

Fifty-nine-year-old me had opinions about all of this.

Nineteen-year-old me had calves.

Lera simplified.

"Don't fix everything."

I almost laughed.

"I've heard that before."

"Then somebody else got tired of you first."

She put a chalk mark on the post at chest height.

"Cut through that. Then give me your eyes before your sword stops moving."

"My eyes."

"Look at me."

"You're standing beside the post."

"Very good."

I cut.

The blade crossed the mark.

My eyes found Lera.

Her stick came low.

I stepped.

Too late.

Tap against the outside of my thigh.

Again.

Cut.

Eyes.

Stick high.

I ducked.

Wrong response.

The stick stopped against the side of my head.

"Dead."

"I dislike how often people say that to me here."

"Useful building."

Again.

This time the stick came toward my shoulder.

I saw her breath change first.

Small inhale.

Weight already settling through her left foot.

The shoulder was a lie.

Low strike.

I stepped before the stick moved.

Not far.

Just enough.

The padded end passed the outside of my knee.

My sword returned to guard.

Lera stopped.

I stopped.

That had been clean.

Not fast.

Not impressive.

Correct.

"Again," she said.

No praise.

Better.

We repeated it.

The next one hit me.

The one after that hit me too.

Causality remained committed to moderation.

By the tenth repetition I could cut, find Lera, and recover without turning all three into separate administrative processes.

That was when the west side of the yard changed.

Not louder.

Quieter.

People began moving equipment out of one lane without being asked twice.

A pair of Bronze fighters abandoned a sparring circle and carried their practice shields to the rack. One of the yard assistants rolled a low iron frame from storage and checked the pins with both hands.

Lera glanced over.

"Keep cutting."

I kept cutting.

This lasted four repetitions.

Then four people walked into the yard wearing Gold.

Not gold clothing.

Gold rank marks at collar and shoulder, worn enough that none of them looked recently acquired.

A woman with a long spear went first.

Behind her came a broad man carrying a shield with three repaired edge plates and no decorative surface left on the front. The third had a short sword and a coil of thin cord looped across one shoulder. The fourth carried no obvious weapon at all, just a narrow case under one arm and a pair of gloves tucked through his belt.

Nobody announced them.

Nobody needed to.

Gold.

I had seen Gold adventurers before.

Of course I had.

In my first life I had passed through Gold on the way to becoming something else.

I remembered contracts at that level. People. Arguments. Equipment. The particular kind of confidence that came after surviving enough difficult work to stop performing confidence for strangers.

But memory was cheating.

Current Greg had spent weeks surrounded mostly by Bronze workers, competent specialists, a few Silvers, and people whose jobs mattered more than their rank.

The gap had become theoretical.

Then four Golds began warming up thirty yards away.

The gap became physical.

The spearwoman did not move quickly.

That was the first thing.

She moved exactly as quickly as the next position required.

One thrust into the weighted frame.

Recover.

Shieldman crossed before the spear was fully back.

The unarmed man touched two fingers to the shield's rear strap.

A faint line of pale light appeared for less than a breath across the shieldman's outside foot and the sand beneath it.

Not a wall.

Not a dramatic spell.

Something about traction or load, maybe.

The shieldman received the frame's swinging return without giving ground.

The short-sword woman was already through the space the spearwoman had opened.

One cut.

She was gone before the weighted arm came back.

Then all four reset.

No discussion.

Again.

Different order.

The spearwoman moved second this time.

Shield first.

Cord came off the third woman's shoulder and caught one of the frame's upper hooks at exactly the moment its weight shifted.

The unarmed man's fingers moved.

A brief translucent shape appeared near the iron hinge.

Gone.

The frame turned six inches farther than its construction wanted to turn.

That was enough for the spear.

No one celebrated.

No one checked whether the others had seen what they did.

They were already resetting.

I missed Lera's stick.

It hit me in the ribs.

"Ow."

"You're paying me."

"There are Golds."

"Excellent eyesight."

"That support work."

"Not your lesson."

"Did you see the hinge?"

The stick hit my ribs again.

Same place.

"I am beginning to think this is personal."

"Stop watching them."

"I can see what they're doing."

That finally made her smile.

Very small.

Almost cruel.

"That has never been your problem."

I shut my mouth.

Reasonable.

She pointed at the post.

"Cut."

I cut.

Arrive.

Eyes.

Lera moved.

I read high.

Wrong.

Tap against my thigh.

Again.

Behind her, the Gold party ran the frame in another pattern.

I heard the shield strike.

Did not look.

Cut.

Arrive.

Eyes.

Breath.

Weight.

Lera's right shoulder shifted.

This time the shoulder told the truth.

I stepped outside the line.

Recovered.

Her stick missed.

My sword was ready.

There.

I felt the opening arrive.

Not a theoretical opening.

A real one.

Lera's stick had crossed low and her right side was open.

I knew three counters.

One belonged to a swordsman I had watched win a continental final thirty years from now.

One depended on a support brace I could not currently form quickly enough.

One was stupid.

I did none of them.

I held guard.

Lera's eyes changed.

"Good."

Actual praise.

Tiny.

I almost ruined it by becoming pleased.

"Again."

Of course.

We kept going.

The lesson ended with my shoulders burning and my one silver fully converted into corrections.

Lera took the practice stick under one arm and handed me the stamped chit back with a mark across the corner.

"What do I practice?"

"Same cut."

I waited.

"That's it?"

"No. You practice finishing it."

"Cut, arrive, recover."

"If you need the words."

"How many?"

She shrugged.

"Enough that you stop asking what comes after the cut."

"Very measurable."

"You want measurable, pay me another silver."

I looked toward the Gold lane.

They were done.

That surprised me.

No exhausted collapse. No final dramatic exchange. The shieldman was checking one repaired edge plate with his thumb. The spearwoman had a cloth around the head of the spear. The woman with the cord was rewinding it carefully, checking each span for fray. The unarmed man had opened the narrow case and was putting three little metal pieces back into fitted slots.

Maintenance.

Of course.

One of the yard assistants asked them something.

The spearwoman answered.

They left through the west door.

No one applauded.

A Bronze pair moved back into the lane ten seconds later.

I watched them go anyway.

Gold.

Really Gold.

Good enough that four people could make a complicated thing look less complicated by refusing to waste motion on it.

Old me had been above that.

Much above it, eventually.

The thought should have made current Greg feel small.

It did.

A little.

It also made the distance real.

That was better.

Lera tapped my sword with the stick.

"You done?"

"Apparently."

"Then sheath it before you cut somebody buying lunch."

I sheathed it.

Outside the yard, Sella was dealing with a man who had filled out the back of a form and somehow missed the front.

Balance restored.

She glanced at my marked chit.

"Worth it?"

I thought about the silver.

Two repaired boot heels had cost two copper.

Two pairs of socks had cost two more.

Lunch yesterday had been one.

One silver still hurt.

Good.

It should.

"Ask me when I stop getting hit after the cut."

Sella looked past me toward the yard.

"So no."

"Not yet."

She took the chit and stamped the ledger.

I left the Guild with less money, sore shoulders, and no new job.

That felt suspiciously responsible.

On Lower Rook Street, I did not practice the cut between carts.

I had matured.

Instead I waited until the small yard behind the building was empty.

Noll's laundry hung from one line.

Someone else's shirts occupied the other.

I found six feet of space between a rain barrel and the wall.

Raised the sword.

Cut.

Arrive.

Recover.

My eyes found the doorway.

Nobody there.

Again.

Cut.

Arrive.

Recover.

The first two felt slow because I was thinking about them.

The third felt worse.

The fourth nearly became clever.

I stopped it.

That might have been the most expensive part of the lesson.

Again...
