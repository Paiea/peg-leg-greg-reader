# Chapter 28: The Second Hand

Status: **EXPERIMENTAL SECOND-RUN PROSE / TRUE-FRONTIER STORY**

Story search: `state/experiments/greg-again/rehearsals/028-story-search.md`

---

Sella found me work the next morning because apparently spending money near her desk created a scent.

I had gone to the Guild to check the Bronze board.

That was my story.

It remained technically true even after Sella looked up and said, "Meren asked if you're available."

I stopped walking.

There were several good ways to react to being requested for paid work.

I chose suspicion.

"Specifically?"

"No, Greg. She described another very tall Bronze who can hold a five-inch plane and asks too many questions."

"Could happen."

"Not twice."

Fair.

I approached the desk.

"How long?"

"Four hours if nothing fights back."

"Wards fight back?"

"Buildings do."

"Pay?"

"Four copper. Five if the site reopens before midday bell."

Less than the last shift.

Less time too.

I had spent one silver yesterday learning not to finish a sword swing like an idiot.

Current economics suggested I should take work offered by people who had already learned my name.

"Where?"

Sella slid a folded work slip across the desk.

South quay counting house.

Freight transfer floor.

Routine threshold cycling repair.

That meant nothing useful yet.

I signed.

Sella took the slip back.

"Meren said bring gloves."

I held up my hands.

"Attached."

"Work gloves."

"I knew that."

"Then this is another strong morning."

Meren Voss was waiting outside a long brick counting house with a tool case at her feet and a cup of something steaming in one hand.

She looked at me.

"You came."

"Apparently I have developed a reputation for employment."

"Sella said you spent money yesterday."

I stared.

"There are no secrets in that building."

"There are. They're just not yours."

She handed me the smaller case.

Good to be back.

The counting house sat between two freight yards where wagons came off the south road and river carts went toward the quay.

Inside, clerks stood at tall desks moving numbers between ledgers while actual human beings moved everything else.

Sacks.

Crates.

Copper pipe.

Bundles of dyed cloth.

One cage containing six furious white birds whose economic purpose remained unclear.

The ward problem occupied a wide interior doorway between the receiving floor and the bonded storage room.

Unlike Meren's warehouse seams, this one opened and closed constantly.

Two copper rails ran vertically inside the stone jambs.

Ceramic teeth sat between them at handspan intervals.

A narrow horizontal bar crossed above the doorway with a sliding brass weight trapped inside it.

Every time a bonded load crossed the threshold, a clerk touched a stamped token to the plate on one side.

The rails brightened faintly.

The load passed.

The light died.

Simple enough.

Except the doorway had been closed since dawn.

Three freight crews were waiting.

Their opinion of maintenance was becoming architectural.

A thick-necked foreman pointed at the waiting carts.

"Can we use the east door?"

Meren did not look at him.

"For bonded freight?"

"It's a door."

"Very advanced observation."

I liked her immediately all over again.

The foreman did not.

Meren opened the case.

"East door isn't keyed to the bonded ledger. You move freight through it, the clerks have to break seal and recertify every load."

The foreman looked toward the clerks.

The clerks all became extremely interested in writing.

"How long?"

"If people stop asking me how long, shorter."

He left.

Professional authority had many forms.

Meren crouched by the left rail.

"The cycling gate isn't clearing between loads."

"Residual charge?"

"Probably."

Probably was healthy.

"Why?"

"That's what we're being paid to learn after we make the building useful again."

Also healthy.

She pointed at the lower ceramic teeth.

"Yesterday they replaced three cracked pieces. Gate worked for six cycles. Then the brass return started sticking halfway."

I looked up at the horizontal bar.

The trapped weight sat slightly left of center.

"Mechanical?"

"Maybe."

"Magical?"

"Maybe."

"Excellent profession."

"Gloves."

Right.

I put them on.

Meren removed two plates from the lower rail and exposed a narrow copper junction behind them.

"I need to cycle the gate manually. When I call there, plane across this gap. Flat. No brace. No push."

Five inches of exposed copper.

Convenient.

"How long?"

"Until I say off."

"Several useful breaths?"

That earned the smallest glance.

She remembered.

"Not this time."

She pointed at the brass return overhead.

"If I hold the cycle long enough for your plane to thin, we've already done it wrong."

Different job.

Good.

Meren stood and took a narrow forked tool from the case.

She pressed it into a slot beside the doorway.

The copper rails hummed.

"There."

I reached.

The plane formed.

Too high.

"Lower."

I dropped it.

The exposed junction pressed faintly against the construct.

"Off."

I released.

The brass weight slid three inches overhead.

Stopped.

Meren reset the tool.

"Again."

Hum.

"There."

Plane.

This time the right height.

"Off."

Release.

The weight moved another two inches.

Stopped.

Meren looked at the rail.

Then at the weight.

"Too slow."

I disliked that.

"The weight?"

"You."

Worse.

"Plane was stable."

"Eventually."

She crouched and checked the lower copper with a probe.

"There's a narrow discharge window after the cycle starts. You cover the junction before it dumps into the lower rail, it routes through the return. You form after, the rail already has it."

"How narrow?"

"Narrow enough that you're late."

Very measurable profession.

She stood.

"Again."

Hum.

I waited for the call.

"There."

I formed.

Faster.

Still late.

"Off."

The weight moved.

Stopped.

Again.

Again.

By the sixth cycle my forehead had begun to tighten.

Not pain.

Attention.

The plane itself cost almost nothing compared with holding one under load for several breaths.

The difficult part was making it exist now.

Not after I pictured it.

Not after I checked angle.

Not after I asked whether the five inches were exactly five inches.

Now.

Old Greg could have done that without thinking.

Current Greg thought too much because current Greg had to build the thing with hands that did not yet trust the instruction.

Familiar problem.

Different body part.

Meren reset.

"There."

Plane.

"Off."

Gone.

The brass weight reached center.

Meren did not praise me.

She did something better.

She turned away before the next cycle.

"Again."

She started checking the upper rail while I watched the forked tool in her left hand.

Hum.

"There."

Plane.

She did not look.

"Off."

Release.

The weight slid past center.

Stopped.

I looked at Meren.

She was already reading the upper contact with the probe.

Trust, commercial edition.

Again.

We ran twelve cycles.

On the thirteenth, Meren stopped calling.

She pushed the forked tool in.

Hum.

I formed the plane when the tool seated.

Her head turned.

Not angry.

Interested.

The return weight moved.

"Off."

I released.

"Why then?"

"The hum starts after the lower contact closes. Your hand closes the contact before the rail answers."

She looked at the tool.

Then at me.

"Again."

She seated it.

Plane.

Hum.

"Off."

Release.

The brass weight slid cleanly to the far stop.

The rails went dark.

Silence.

Meren pulled the tool out.

"Good."

Two actual Goods in two days.

Dangerous trend.

I resisted the urge to become unbearable.

Mostly.

"I believe this qualifies me for a promotion."

"To what?"

"Second hand."

"You are already the second hand."

Right.

Title acquired.

Meren opened the upper bar.

Inside, the brass return weight ran on a dark leather strap around two small ceramic rollers.

One roller had a hairline crack.

Not enough to break.

Enough to drag.

The strap had polished one side bright from rubbing against it.

Mechanical.

Mostly.

Meren touched the cracked roller.

"There."

I leaned closer.

"So yesterday's ceramic replacements weren't the cause."

"No."

"But they changed the load enough for this to become visible."

Meren glanced at me.

"Probably."

Healthy woman.

She replaced the roller.

Then cleaned the track.

Then made me run the manual cycle six more times while she watched the return.

By the sixth, the plane appeared before she would have said there.

Small.

Flat.

Gone the instant the return cleared.

My head felt fine.

That was the interesting part.

Not more mana.

Less wasted time spending it.

The foreman reappeared.

"Done?"

Meren looked at the doorway.

"Testing."

He made a face.

"Looks done."

"Then you don't need me."

He left again.

I was learning many trades today.

A bonded clerk brought over a test crate with a lead seal and a ledger token.

Meren reinstalled the lower plates.

The clerk touched token to plate.

Rails brightened.

Crate crossed.

Light died.

The brass weight returned all the way home.

Again.

Second crate.

Clean.

Third.

Clean.

On the fourth, the return hesitated.

Barely.

Meren saw it.

So did I.

The foreman did not.

"Open?"

Meren said no.

The foreman's shoulders rose.

"It worked."

"Four times."

"We have twenty carts waiting."

"Then you'll be happy I didn't let you discover the fifth one with bonded freight in the doorway."

He looked at me again.

Still apparently the reasonable one.

I shook my head.

Never volunteer for that title.

Meren reopened the upper bar.

The new roller was fine.

The leather strap was not.

One section had stretched slightly where the old roller had dragged against it.

Under normal return it passed.

Under a full gate cycle it slipped.

She held the strap between two fingers.

"Replacement."

The foreman asked whether that was really necessary.

Meren handed him the stretched strap.

He stretched it.

Then compared it with the spare from her case.

He stopped asking.

Useful man after all.

The new strap went in.

Eight test cycles.

All clean.

Meren finally nodded to the bonded clerk.

The waiting carts began moving.

One token.

One brief glow.

Load through.

Dark.

Next.

The first freight crew crossed without knowing I had spent half an hour creating and deleting five inches of pale nothing so their paperwork could remain boring.

Support branding remained terrible.

Meren packed the tools.

I took the heavier case this time without being told.

Outside, the midday bell had not rung yet.

Five copper.

Excellent.

Meren handed me my work slip.

"Sella pays you."

"You requesting me again?"

She looked at me.

Too eager.

I regretted the question immediately.

"If I need a second hand."

There.

Not friendship.

Not destiny.

Employment.

Better.

I carried the case to her cart.

At the Guild, Sella counted five copper onto the desk.

I counted it because I had learned from Antonius Vale that trusting arithmetic and trusting people were separate hobbies.

Sella watched.

"Correct?"

"Tragically."

She pushed the ledger toward me.

I signed.

Five copper earned.

One silver spent yesterday.

My economy remained committed to keeping me literate.

Outside, I held my hand at waist height.

No exposed copper.

No Meren.

No reason.

I formed the plane anyway.

There.

Gone.

Again.

There.

Gone.

The third one appeared fast enough that I felt the timing before I saw the pale surface.

Tiny.

Still tiny.

But magic had started obeying a little sooner.

I stopped before turning a paid lesson in timing into another unpaid injury.

Then I went to buy lunch...
