# Chapter 35: The Pair

Sella had started putting me and Jorren on the same side of the counter.

I noticed because she did it without asking.

This was how institutions became dangerous.

Small assumptions.

Then ledgers.

Then somebody invented a form.

Jorren leaned one elbow against the counter beside me while Sella read a work slip.

Neither of us had come in looking for the other.

He had arrived first.

I had arrived hungry.

Apparently those conditions now produced employment.

"Two days," Sella said.

Jorren looked at me.

I looked at him.

Sella continued before either of us could become difficult.

"Hill relay west-southwest. Courier line. Storm check, post replacement, continuity test."

"Road?" Jorren asked.

"Mostly."

Bad word.

"Pay?"

She named it.

Good enough that two days felt like work instead of charity.

Not good enough to explain the mostly.

I looked at the slip.

"Lead?"

"Meral."

That changed the shape immediately.

Jorren saw my face.

"You know her."

"She taught me several ways to be disappointed in four inches of magic."

Sella did not look up.

"Five now."

I stared at her.

"What?"

"Your intake note says five."

Of course it did.

The Guild had documentation for my humiliation.

Jorren smiled.

"Progress."

"Institutionally."

Sella slid the slip across.

Two Bronze names already copied onto the bottom.

Mine.

Jorren's.

Not separately.

One under the other beneath **FIELD HANDS**.

I looked at her.

"You assigned us before we agreed."

"I penciled you."

"That feels different to you?"

"Very. Ink costs money."

Jorren took the page.

"Why both of us?"

Sella finally looked up.

"Because you've finished work together."

Simple.

I waited for more.

There wasn't any.

No declaration that the Guild recognized our tactical compatibility.

No assessment of party structure.

No inspirational speech about trust.

We had signed the same completion lines enough times that when somebody needed two Bronze bodies, Sella had put our names beside one another.

Administration had beaten friendship to the conclusion.

Rude.

"Meral asked for us?" I said.

"Meral asked for two people who could walk uphill, carry replacement posts, and follow instructions."

Ah.

"You thought of us."

"Eventually."

Jorren coughed into one fist.

Traitor.

Sella's mouth moved approximately one human emotion.

Then the Guild door opened behind us.

Meral came in carrying a coil of marked cord over one shoulder and three ceramic caps under her arm.

Same practical coat.

Different road dust.

Her hair had been cut shorter since I last saw her.

She saw me.

Then Jorren.

Then the work slip in his hand.

"Those two?"

Sella nodded.

Meral considered us.

Not warmly.

Not coldly either.

Inventory.

"He still thinks too much?"

She pointed at me.

Jorren answered before I could.

"Yes."

Good reunion.

Meral shifted the ceramic caps into one hand.

"You still take second watch?"

That was to Jorren.

"Usually."

"Good."

Then back to me.

"You still spend mana proving points?"

"Less."

She waited.

"Usually."

"Good enough."

A terrible phrase.

I missed it.

We signed.

Sella inked over our penciled names.

Apparently commitment had a stationery budget after all.

The relay station sat less than a day's walk from Carrow if you believed maps.

Maps remained optimistic about elevation.

The first half of the route followed a maintained courier road through farms and low stone walls.

The second half became a hill path pretending its relationship with carts had ended mutually.

Meral carried the diagnostic case.

Jorren and I carried everything heavier.

Two replacement relay posts.

Three ceramic heads.

Copper strap.

One box of anchor spikes.

Food.

Rope.

A folding shovel that seemed designed specifically to catch on brush.

No mule.

I missed Pate.

Not personally.

Structurally.

The relay line appeared before the station did.

Low stone posts every few hundred yards where the ground allowed it.

Not identical.

That surprised me.

Later courier lines used standardized waist-high pylons with sealed copper cores and numbered access plates.

These were older.

Some stone.

Some wood wrapped in copper strap.

One was built into the remains of a field wall.

Another leaned because the hill had apparently become tired of supporting it.

Each carried a shallow ceramic head aimed toward the next visible point.

Line of sight.

Mostly.

Cheap distance signaling.

Not messages themselves.

A pulse chain.

Station-to-station confirmation that a courier ward packet had passed one point and reached the next.

Later versions were boring enough that nobody thought about them until they failed.

Current versions still looked like somebody had invented the idea separately at every hill.

I liked them immediately.

Dangerous.

Meral stopped at the leaning post.

"Don't touch it."

I had not moved.

"I wasn't."

"Your face was."

Jorren looked at me.

"There is a face."

Apparently everyone had unionized.

Meral set down the diagnostic case.

She clipped one marked cord to the copper strap and unrolled it toward the next post.

Not physically all the way.

The cord itself carried a faint blue tracer once she pressed two fingers to the first mark.

The light reached three handspans.

Stopped.

"Dead here?" Jorren asked.

"No."

Meral moved the clip six inches lower.

Light ran farther this time.

A yard.

Then faded.

She scraped dirt from the stone with her thumb.

"Ground moved. Strap didn't. Contact is intermittent when the post leans."

That fit.

Current infrastructure.

Current failure.

I looked uphill.

The next relay point was visible through two bare-limbed trees.

Later systems would have compensated for the lean electronically.

Not electronically.

Magically.

Future language had contaminated me.

They used a shaped correction plate at the ceramic head.

I could picture it.

Thin brass crescent.

Three notch settings.

Quick field fix.

Current post had no correction plate.

Fine.

Meral pointed at the shovel.

"Dig downhill side."

Jorren took it.

I grabbed the post before she could tell me not to.

She looked at my hands.

"Hold. Don't lift."

Different instruction.

I held.

Jorren dug.

The hill had opinions.

Roots.

Stone.

Wet clay beneath the topsoil.

Fifteen minutes later the post came back toward vertical one inch at a time while we packed stone beneath the downhill base.

No magic.

Meral retested.

Blue tracer ran the length of the visible cord this time.

Then jumped.

Not through air.

My eyes did that part.

The ceramic head brightened once.

Farther uphill, the next post answered with a pale blink.

Good.

One repaired.

We kept walking.

Three posts later, the storm damage stopped being polite.

A pine had come down across the path.

Not a huge tree.

Huge enough when horizontal.

Its upper branches had crushed one relay post flat and dragged the copper line down the slope.

The ceramic head lay broken in four pieces among needles.

Meral crouched beside it.

"Replacement."

There.

The reason we had carried the stupid things uphill.

Jorren and I moved the new stone post off my shoulder and onto the path.

My left arm immediately became happier.

We cleared branches.

Cut nothing larger than necessary.

The fallen trunk itself could stay.

Courier path went around it easily enough.

Relay sightline did not.

The next station uphill was hidden behind the pine crown.

Meral walked twenty yards left.

Then right.

Looked uphill.

Back down.

"Old position is dead."

Jorren wiped pine pitch from one hand.

"Move it?"

"Have to."

She looked at the slope.

This was where later memory became useful enough to become dangerous.

Relay placement rules.

Height over grade.

Clearance angles.

Maximum offset before pulse loss.

I remembered those.

Later rules.

For standardized heads.

Not these.

Good.

I kept my mouth shut.

Meral walked another line.

She held one ceramic cap at shoulder height and sighted across its shallow groove.

Jorren followed with the replacement post.

I carried the diagnostic case now.

At forty yards downslope and left, she stopped.

"Here."

The new position sat on a patch of exposed rock beside a shallow drainage cut.

I looked at the stone.

Good base.

Looked at the drainage.

Bad water.

Maybe.

"Rain?" I asked.

Meral looked down.

Current eyes.

The cut had fresh debris along one edge.

Pine needles caught knee-high on a bush.

Higher flow than the channel currently suggested.

She nodded once.

"Too wet."

Moved again.

Clean contribution.

No lecture.

We found a second position twelve yards higher.

Worse rock.

Better drainage.

Clear sight both directions once one low branch came off.

Jorren dug the socket.

I held the post.

Meral set the copper strap.

Then she handed me one ceramic cap.

"Hold that."

I held it.

"Level."

I leveled it.

"No."

Of course.

She turned my wrist perhaps three degrees.

"Level to the line, not the hill."

Right.

The ground lied.

The next post did not care.

She fixed the cap in place.

Then pulled two narrow metal tabs from the diagnostic case and touched them to the ceramic contacts.

Nothing.

Again.

Faint blue.

Gone.

Meral frowned.

I knew that expression too.

System says yes and no in alternating breaths.

She retested downhill.

Good.

Uphill.

Bad.

"Next head?" Jorren asked.

"Maybe."

Meral looked through the sight groove.

The next relay post was visible.

Technically.

A thin branch crossed the line.

Not enough to matter.

Probably.

Wind moved it.

The next head flashed once.

Then failed to answer.

Meral moved our new cap one notch sideways.

Retested.

Good.

Wind moved the branch again.

Bad.

There.

Not the branch itself.

Something about movement.

Later lines ignored leaves.

These older ceramic heads apparently did not like a wet branch carrying charge across the pulse corridor.

I reached for that conclusion.

Stopped.

Current proof first.

"Can we tie the branch back?"

Meral looked at me.

Then uphill.

"For a test."

Jorren climbed the lower trunk without waiting for my expertise.

Correct choice.

He looped rope around the branch and pulled it three feet out of the sightline.

Meral tested.

Blue pulse.

Uphill answer.

Again.

Answer.

Wind.

Still answer.

Meral looked at the wet branch.

Then the old ceramic head.

"That's stupid."

Excellent technical conclusion.

"Later heads don't care," I said.

Silence.

Ah.

Too much.

Meral turned slowly.

Jorren remained in the tree.

He had the dignity to look interested from above.

Meral's expression did not become suspicious.

Worse.

Professional.

"Later than what?"

I considered several lies.

None improved with inspection.

"Complicated month."

From the tree, Jorren made a sound of genuine pain.

"He still says that?"

Meral asked.

"Constantly."

I pointed at the relay head.

"Can we agree the branch is stupid?"

Meral watched me for another second.

Then the system won.

Good professional.

"Temporary tie. Record vegetation interference. Crew comes back with a cutter and tests after dry weather."

No interrogation.

No revelation.

She wrote it down.

Jorren tied the branch off properly and climbed down.

By then the light had begun changing.

Not dark yet.

Hill dark.

Different thing.

The relay station itself sat another hour uphill.

Small stone hut.

One room.

Copper mast through the roof.

Two shuttered windows.

Nobody lived there.

The station existed so nobody had to.

Meral checked the door seal.

Unbroken.

Unlocked.

Inside smelled like cold stone and old lamp oil.

One bench.

One relay cabinet.

One emergency cot with mouse evidence.

Excellent accommodations.

The main cabinet still held charge.

Meral tested the downhill line.

Good.

Uphill line.

Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

Different problem.

The dead side continued toward the next ridge.

Not our whole contract today.

Meral looked outside at the remaining light.

Then at us.

"Camp here. Check uphill at first light."

Jorren nodded.

I nodded.

No argument.

There was room on the floor.

Barely.

We unpacked.

Jorren claimed the cot until he saw the mouse evidence.

Then generously offered it to me.

I declined his sacrifice.

Meral lit one low lamp.

Not a mana lamp.

Oil.

The relay cabinet clicked once in the wall as a downhill courier pulse passed through.

Tiny blue line across one plate.

Then quiet.

I sat with my back against the stone beside Jorren.

Meral was writing the day's repair notes at the bench.

Our names appeared twice.

I could see them from where I sat.

Greg.

Jorren.

One line under **hands**.

Another under **field crew**.

Nobody had asked us what we were.

Apparently the work had answered first.

Outside, somewhere beyond the ridge, the uphill relay line remained dead.

Tomorrow's problem.

Not tonight's.

I was getting better at that distinction...
