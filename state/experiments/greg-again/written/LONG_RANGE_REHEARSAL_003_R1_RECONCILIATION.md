# WHAT R1 ALREADY SOLVED THAT R2 SHOULD KEEP

## 1. Artifact knowledge is layered, social, and incomplete

R1's mature artifact market brain already rejected universal identification.

Useful surviving principles:

- Greg does not know every artifact.
- Recognition may be exact, maker/family, class, technique, story, pattern, or none.
- observed effect, marketed effect, known constraint, known consequence, mechanism, hidden upside, hidden downside, provenance, combination value, and future reputation are different knowledge layers.
- different people may know different layers.
- repairers, former owners, widows, porters, dealers, Guild records, creature handlers, appraisers, and specialists may hold operationally correct information without a complete explanation.
- a warning can be correct even when the explanation is wrong.
- death or disaster can preserve a warning while obscuring the real rule.

This is unusually compatible with the Seven Lamps iceberg.

Strong R2 translation:

> **PRACTICAL KNOWLEDGE MAY BE TRUE BEFORE THEORY IS TRUE.**

An old repair rule, seller warning, regional taboo, inherited activation sequence, creature-handler habit, or Guild restriction may encode a real deep-magical constraint without anyone knowing which underlying principle makes it true.

Do not make every such rule a hidden Seven Lamps clue. The world needs ordinary engineering, superstition, fraud, and coincidence too.

## 2. Artifact markets should be rational under current information

R1 already had the right economic safeguard:

> **PRICE THE VISIBLE VALUE, NOT THE OMNISCIENT VALUE.**

The market should not become stupid so Greg can get bargains.

Visible repeatable benefits usually cost real money.

Deep bargains require a credible information gap such as:

- hidden use
- wrong intended customer
- future demand
- missing complement
- poor authentication
- misunderstood provenance
- dangerous testing
- fragmented evidence
- bad historical explanation
- maintenance bottleneck
- legal or social stigma
- an obsolete or failed design whose defect matters to Greg

The Seven Lamps architecture strengthens this because two current schools may reasonably assign very different value to the same object while both are acting intelligently inside partial models.

Greg's future knowledge may let him see a later use or later maker significance without knowing the complete mechanism.

Sometimes he gets the bargain.

Sometimes the present seller knows more than he does.

Sometimes both are missing another layer.

## 3. Strong artifacts can be expensive bargains

The best acquisition pressure is often not `cheap junk, easy profit`.

It is:

> **That price is brutal. It is also far below what this thing is worth to me.**

This matters for the 5–10 year economic arc.

Future Greg should need larger pools of capital, better access, credit, brokerage, commissions, ownership, liquidation choices, storage, transport, and reputation to act on the best opportunities.

Do not make artifact value wait for Greg to become rich. Seeing something he cannot yet buy can itself create durable economic direction.

## 4. Artifact downsides should rewrite life, not trade stats

R1's mature artifact engine correctly preferred objects that rewrite operating rules across:

- time
- space
- body
- environment
- social identity
- economics
- risk
- logistics
- tactics

That principle survives intact.

A Seven-Lamps object becomes interesting when its deep mechanism produces a practical interface problem, not because the story labels it `Lamp X artifact`.

An object may make one action absurdly easy while changing Greg's travel, sleep, clothing, storage, social exposure, movement, maintenance, or contract selection.

The downside should create decisions.

Do not make every severe downside secretly trivial once Greg understands it.

## 5. Magical ecology is a high-confidence competence reservoir

R1's plot brain already recognized that Old Greg's decades of field life should make creature / environment knowledge unusually durable compared with historical trivia, market forecasts, or social chronology.

Strong R2 translation:

When Greg personally encountered, hunted, avoided, traveled around, worked beside, or repeatedly studied a creature or field condition in his first life, let him sometimes know exactly what to do.

Useful clean-win shapes include:

- do not run
- do not draw a weapon
- wait for the territorial display to finish
- move sideways instead of backward
- identify nesting / mating / feeding behavior
- know the safe route
- recognize a bluff
- recognize when the frightening animal is not the dangerous one
- redirect or coexist rather than kill
- know when violence is actually necessary

This is not omniscience.

Farmers, handlers, hunters, shifters, children, locals, traders, and specialists may know their ecology better than Greg.

But `forty years of adventuring` should mean something.

## 6. PvE does not require an enemy

Magical ecology should create problems through:

- migration
- nesting
- feeding
- roads crossing territory
- salvage near a den
- storage attracting a magical scavenger
- river entities changing routes
- herds moving through farmland
- weather / mana interactions
- dangerous but normal animal behavior

This is one of the strongest bridges between normal fantasy adventure and R2's existing no-master-villain philosophy.

## 7. Economic progression should ratchet upward

R1's economy brain contains a powerful long-serial rule:

Greg's economic life should accumulate memory.

Separate:

- liquidity
- earning capacity
- assets / tools
- obligations
- optionality / resilience

A low purse does not automatically mean Greg is economically back at Chapter 3.

Over years, the emotional question should gradually move from:

> **Can I afford this at all?**

toward:

> **Is this worth the money, time, risk, storage, obligation, or opportunity cost?**

Ordinary low-tier prices do not need to inflate as Greg progresses.

The ceiling widens upward through better work, larger contracts, artifacts, specialist labor, travel, capital, ownership, and market access.

Use staircase accumulation: plateaus, purchases, dips, recoveries, and new tiers.

Do not make economic progression a smooth number-go-up graph.

## 8. Support thinking naturally becomes leverage without forcing support career

R1's progression brain correctly identified that elite support was never merely `cast buffs`.

At peak, support means making the whole situation unfair through:

- information
- positioning
- timing
- preparation
- tools
- resource allocation
- coordination
- protection
- reducing failure modes
- creating options
- knowing when another specialist should own the problem

This survives beautifully in warrior-first R2.

Greg can remain a swordsman / adventurer and still increasingly think like an S-class support practitioner.

That may make him good at:

- expedition composition
- brokerage
- introductions
- assigning specialists
- field logistics
- artifact combinations
- route planning
- contract reading
- party positioning
- deciding what **not** to fight

This should not automatically turn him into a boss, merchant prince, or party leader.

It means forty years of support work create transferable leverage instincts.

# RECURRENCE STRENGTHENED BY R1 RECONCILIATION

## STRONG GRAVITY — artifact/economy coupling

Artifacts become a more powerful long-range engine when the best opportunities are not only rare but **capital-constrained**.

Greg's future knowledge gives him private valuation differences.

Money determines whether he can act before the window closes.

That naturally pushes him from survival wages toward better work, commissions, brokerage, ownership, credit, and larger economic optionality without making wealth itself the plot.

## STRONG GRAVITY — ecological fieldcraft as clean expertise

Among future-memory domains, practical magical ecology should have a relatively high clean-hit rate where Old Greg had repeated firsthand experience.

This gives warrior/adventurer Greg satisfying competence while still leaving local specialists and genuinely unknown beings room to surprise him.

## STRONG GRAVITY — economic state must change across years

A 5–10 year R2 cannot plausibly keep Greg emotionally and structurally trapped in the same copper-scale scarcity loop.

He may be broke sometimes.

He may lose capital.

He may suffer expensive injuries or failed purchases.

But his access, assets, earning capacity, reputation, and optionality should have longitudinal memory.

## STRONG GRAVITY — support mind without support identity

Greg's old support career should increasingly appear as leverage, coordination, systems judgment, and social/field geometry even in futures where he remains primarily a warrior.

This is a better inheritance than forcing him to repeat Old Greg's profession.

# SURPRISING CONVERGENCE 7 — MARKETS CAN STORE DEEP MAGIC AS FOLK KNOWLEDGE

The Seven Lamps do not need scholars to foreshadow them.

Markets and work can preserve fragments of truth:

- `this maker's red-cased pieces hate river iron`
- `never recharge that model after noon`
- `the widow won't sell the second one`
- `those plates only work in old stone houses`
- `handlers say the shed antlers spoil if stored beside ward glass`

Those are example shapes, not canon facts.

The crucial structure is:

> **A PRACTICAL RULE CAN SURVIVE WITHOUT ITS CAUSAL THEORY.**

Later, apparently unrelated traditions may reveal that several such rules touch the same deeper magical principle.

That is a much more natural iceberg reveal than a lost textbook.

# SURPRISING CONVERGENCE 8 — GREG'S ECONOMIC ASCENT CAN MEASURE TEMPORAL DISTANCE

Money is not only constraint.

It is a clock.

At nineteen, a few copper can determine his week.

Years later, ordinary copper can become background while a rare artifact, expedition stake, workshop share, salvage claim, specialist repair, or transport commitment becomes the thing that hurts.

The world did not inflate around him.

Greg's operating scale changed.

That gives the reader an intuitive way to feel years and accumulated competence without chapter headings announcing `three years later`.

Do not lock exact denominations or conversion mathematics until R2 prose earns them.

# SURPRISING CONVERGENCE 9 — SUPPORT + PARTY + SEVEN LAMPS PRODUCES TRANSLATION VALUE

If different traditions solve similar problems through different partial magical models, a mature Greg party can become stronger because Greg recognizes **functional equivalence** before he understands deep theory.

He may not know why two techniques are related.

He may know that one person's strange regional method can fill the same tactical need as a familiar support technique.

That makes Greg valuable as a practical translator / coordinator between specialists.

It also keeps other specialists authoritative in their own craft.

This is especially strong because it grows from Old Greg's support habit of asking what the team needs rather than who gets credit.

# UPDATED TEMPORAL ECONOMIC PRESSURE

## Current / nineteen

Liquidity remains genuinely constraining. Greg can still care about individual copper because current earning capacity and reserve are small. Future knowledge can create opportunities he cannot yet finance.

## Roughly twenty

Repeat work and better field usefulness should plausibly improve access. Some ordinary early prices begin becoming less narratively dominant even if Greg is not wealthy. A first meaningful artifact or market opportunity may be expensive enough to require planning rather than impulse buying.

## Early twenties

Greg can plausibly have multiple economic layers at once: cash, equipment, venture equity, artifact value, obligations, social credit, commissions, or travel commitments. One low-liquidity week should no longer erase his broader position.

## Mid twenties

In stronger futures, Greg can face genuinely larger allocation decisions: expedition stakes, rare objects, specialist repair, ownership, storage, travel, or capital tied up in something uncertain. The question becomes what deserves resources.

## Later twenties

Greg may have access to transactions and opportunities that nineteen-year-old Greg could barely imagine financing while still living in a world where an ordinary meal remains an ordinary meal. Wealth need not become enormous for optionality to become dramatically different.
