# Scene 1 — Training Hall spar

## Minimum scene packet

### State-in

- Greg is nineteen, Bronze-ranked, physically whole at this early point, and carrying decades of first-life combat/support knowledge.
- His present body has technique memory but not Old Greg's conditioning, reinforcement, recovery capacity, or S-class chassis.
- He has recently spent scarce money on better equipment and paid practice access.
- Movement feels emotionally valuable because it restores a familiar Greg-role.
- The hall/yard contains marked lanes, other trainees, practice weapons, posts, sand, and supervision at ordinary guild distance.

### Actors

- **Greg:** wants proof that old understanding can accelerate present skill. Owns his body report, choices, observations, and mistakes.
- **Jorren:** a stronger, better-conditioned Bronze trainee who expects Greg to be ordinary. Owns his attacks, adaptations, irritation, and decision to continue.
- **Guild environment:** lane boundaries, footing, fatigue, noise, equipment rules, and paid time constrain both men. It does not rearrange itself for Greg.

### Hard constraints

- No magic rescue.
- Greg may recognize technique before his body can exploit it.
- Jorren is not a training dummy and adjusts when Greg demonstrates knowledge.
- Greg must take real contact and physical cost.
- The fixed canon outcome is preserved: Greg's reading and timing eventually win one exchange, but the larger discovery is that knowledge does not replace conditioning or recovery.
- No injury outcome beyond ordinary early-training pain/fatigue.

### Required outcome

Greg is initially outperformed physically, learns Jorren's attack preparation, manipulates an expected line, and wins a bounded exchange. The success encourages more training and overconfidence instead of solving his body/career problem.

### State-out

- Greg has evidence that old combat reading transfers.
- He also has evidence that present conditioning is a hard limit.
- Jorren no longer reads him as an easy novice.
- Greg interprets the useful success more loudly than the physical warning.
- Money, training appetite, and recovery pressure tighten.

## REHEARSAL: three possibilities

### T1 — Technique without capacity

Greg immediately sees several openings but repeatedly arrives late because his present feet, lungs, and recovery cannot execute the answer. He earns one clean touch only after shortening the problem to a single anticipated entry.

**Useful discovery:** recognition and execution can diverge visibly without narration explaining the whole concept.

### T2 — The taught mistake

Greg notices Jorren loads one shoulder before committing. He deliberately gives Jorren the same tempting target twice. The first costs Greg a hit. The second lets him arrive inside the attack and take the exchange.

**Useful discovery:** Greg's tactical intelligence includes accepting bounded cost, while Jorren's belief about Greg changes through evidence.

### T3 — Fatigue changes the geometry

Greg performs well early, then fatigue shortens a recovery step and turns a safe distance into contact. He wins later only by reducing movement and making Jorren cross the distance.

**Useful discovery:** footing, distance, and body state change the available strategy. The environment is causal rather than decorative.

## Selected alternative

**T2 with T3's fatigue constraint.**

It best preserves the fixed outcome while giving both fighters active beliefs. Greg cannot simply be secretly superior. He spends one hit to teach Jorren a false expectation, then uses timing rather than athletic dominance. His failing recovery step remains the evidence that the victory is narrow.

## One HIGH-FIDELITY PERFORMANCE

**Representation:** explicit performed exchange.  
**Uncertainty tested:** Can Greg credibly win one exchange while being physically inferior, without Jorren becoming stupid?

### Blocking and behavior

1. Greg and Jorren begin near the center of a sanded lane. Greg's point is slightly low because his shoulder is already warm. Jorren keeps more weight forward than Greg expects from a cautious first exchange.
2. Jorren tests with a quick cut toward Greg's outside shoulder. Greg sees the shoulder tighten but retreats too late. The practice blade lands. Greg's knowledge predicted contact; his foot did not create enough distance.
3. Greg resets closer to the lane's worn edge. He rolls the struck shoulder once. Jorren reads the movement as discomfort and raises pressure.
4. Greg shows the same outside opening. Jorren commits harder. Greg attempts the inside step, but fatigue makes the rear foot drag. He avoids the full strike but takes another glancing contact. Jorren now believes the opening is real.
5. Between exchanges, Greg stops trying to recover backward. He shifts his starting distance half a foot closer, reducing the step his body must complete. This looks worse to Jorren because Greg appears easier to reach.
6. Jorren's eyes settle on the shoulder. His front foot plants. His striking shoulder tightens before the blade moves.
7. Greg does not retreat. He turns just enough for the line to pass, closes while Jorren's weight is committed, and places his practice edge at Jorren's throat/chest boundary before Jorren can recover.
8. Both stop. Greg's legs begin trembling after the action rather than during it. Jorren first looks at Greg's blade, then at the false opening, reconstructing what happened.
9. Greg smiles because the trap worked. He does not volunteer that his body could not afford the cleaner version of the same solution.
10. On reset, his breathing remains too high. The hall has given him two results: the old read is real, and the old body is not. He emotionally selects the first.

### PERFORMANCE evidence

- **Opponent state tracked:** confidence → confirmed target belief → committed expectation → revised respect.
- **Body state tracked:** warm shoulder → late retreat → dragging recovery foot → reduced movement solution → post-exchange tremor/high breathing.
- **Spatial state tracked:** center lane → worn edge → deliberately shortened distance → inside line.
- **Mistake with consequence:** Greg's first failed trap costs contact; it also makes the later deception credible.
- **Environmental interaction:** sand/worn lane and available distance determine whether Greg can retreat or must close.
- **Surviving discovery:** Greg's intelligence is expressed by changing the geometry to match his current body, not by overriding the body.

## One experimental prose render

Jorren looked at my practice token, then at me, and decided the order mattered.

Fair.

He had thicker shoulders, better legs, and the settled stance of a man who expected the next minute to confirm the previous nineteen years.

I lifted the practice sword.

My shoulder was already warm.

Not injured. Not even close. Just used enough that it had begun submitting complaints in writing.

Jorren stepped in.

His front foot landed. His right shoulder tightened.

I knew the cut before it started.

My rear foot did not care.

The blade struck the outside of my shoulder with a flat wooden crack.

Pain arrived second. Irritation won the race.

Jorren stepped back. “Again?”

“Obviously.”

I rolled the shoulder once.

His eyes followed it.

Good.

We reset nearer the worn edge of the lane. Sand had been pushed thin there by years of people discovering that painted boundaries were more durable than balance.

Jorren came faster.

Same foot. Same shoulder. Same line.

I left the opening for him and stepped inside.

That was the plan.

My rear foot dragged.

That was the body.

I turned enough to keep the blow from landing cleanly, but not enough to preserve dignity. The practice blade clipped my ribs.

Jorren smiled.

Not cruelly. Worse.

Correctly.

He thought he had found the answer.

We separated.

My lungs had joined the shoulder's administrative revolt. I could keep retreating and prove that nineteen-year-old legs became sixty-year-old legs if criticized firmly enough.

Or I could stop asking them for distance.

I moved half a step closer.

Jorren blinked.

There. A question.

Then he saw the shoulder again and answered it himself.

He planted.

The right side tightened.

This time I did not go backward.

His blade crossed where I had been. I turned inside the line, close enough to smell leather oil, and put my practice edge against the hollow below his throat.

We stopped.

Jorren looked down.

Then at my shoulder.

Then at me.

“You did that on purpose.”

“Eventually.”

My legs began shaking.

Quietly, which I appreciated.

I lowered the sword before they could make the result look negotiable.

Jorren's expression had changed. Not admiration. Nothing that expensive.

Revision.

He had believed I was easy. Then injured. Then predictable.

Now he had paperwork.

“Again?” he asked.

My breathing said no.

My shoulder said absolutely not.

The old part of me smiled.

“Obviously.”

# Scene 2 — Gambling table

## Minimum scene packet

### State-in

- Greg has discovered that his old experience reading people makes early card rooms profitable.
- Winnings have already shifted in his mind from food and rent to capital, training, equipment, and permission.
- He moves among rooms/tables because repeated opponents can notice that he studies them.
- His current liquidity matters; losses can create debt and creditor control.
- Greg believes information can make a bad situation solvable if he stays long enough.

### Actors

- **Greg:** wants both money and a complete model of the table. He knows he should leave when the table becomes wrong.
- **Disciplined player:** protects larger pots and notices Greg's attention. Owns deliberate pattern changes and the decision to exploit Greg's need to finish the read.
- **Pressure player:** attacks visible weakness and increases the cost of uncertainty.
- **Unstable/lucky player:** behaves inconsistently enough to contaminate Greg's model.
- **Dealer/table:** controls turn order, pot visibility, card delivery, and the conversational pauses in which players observe one another.
- **Credit presence:** makes staying possible after cash discipline should have ended the scene.

### Hard constraints

- Do not invent a game-specific rules engine. Track only observable wagers, private cards, folds/calls/raises, pot size, and incomplete information.
- Greg is genuinely good. The loss cannot require sudden incompetence.
- Other players observe Greg observing them.
- Hidden cards remain hidden until the hand resolves.
- Fixed canon outcome: Greg stays after recognizing the table is wrong, chases a complete read, loses materially, and accepts/creates debt.
- Winning earlier remains real; this is not a moral fable where gambling never worked.

### Required outcome

Greg's accurate partial reads produce enough success to make continued play rational-looking. A disciplined opponent detects what Greg is studying and changes behavior at the expensive moment. Greg treats the resulting uncertainty as a reason to buy more hands instead of leave, ending in loss and debt.

### State-out

- Greg has lost more than intended.
- His model of one opponent was weaponized against him.
- He correctly understands the local mistake but underreads the larger money/compulsion problem.
- Credit converts a bad night into future obligation.
- Gambling remains a real competence and a dangerous operating system.

## REHEARSAL: three possibilities

### G1 — Correct read, wrong price

Greg correctly predicts multiple players but the cost of testing the final uncertainty grows faster than the informational value. He loses through escalating commitment, not deception.

**Useful discovery:** incentives and pot size can defeat an accurate model.

### G2 — Tell belongs to a different state

Greg identifies a reliable gesture but mislabels its cause. The player protects winnings, not strong cards. When stack size changes, the same gesture predicts something else.

**Useful discovery:** behavior can be real evidence while Greg's explanation is wrong.

### G3 — The observed observer

A disciplined player notices Greg tracking a repeated protective behavior. He preserves it during cheap hands, then reverses it once Greg has enough confidence and the pot is large. Greg recognizes the manipulation one hand too late and stays to rebuild his model.

**Useful discovery:** knowledge is reciprocal. Greg is not outside the system he studies.

## Selected alternative

**G3.**

It most strongly differentiates gambling PERFORMANCE from physical PERFORMANCE. The causal state lives in nested beliefs:

- Greg believes the disciplined player protects strong positions.
- The disciplined player believes Greg has learned that pattern.
- Greg believes later behavior confirms the model.
- The opponent spends cheap hands strengthening Greg's confidence.
- Greg's need to know whether he was fooled becomes the lever that keeps him seated.

## One HIGH-FIDELITY PERFORMANCE

**Representation:** explicit table performance with public actions and private beliefs kept separate.  
**Uncertainty tested:** Can Greg lose credibly while remaining observant and competent?

### Table state and behavior

1. Greg begins with enough cash for several ordinary hands and a private stop boundary. He has already categorized three opponents provisionally: disciplined protector, pressure attacker, unstable noise.
2. In small pots, the disciplined player repeatedly pauses before committing more money, then releases weak positions. Greg sees the same thumb movement against the card edge before protected bets.
3. Greg wins two modest hands by avoiding the pressure player's attacks and extracting value from the unstable player. His model earns money.
4. The disciplined player notices that Greg watches hands during betting and faces only after decisions. He repeats the thumb movement in another cheap fold.
5. A larger pot develops. Greg holds a playable but non-dominant private position. The disciplined player performs the familiar pause and thumb contact, then makes a controlled wager.
6. Greg interprets the wager as protection and applies pressure, expecting the player to preserve rather than contest the pot. The player calls/continues without visible distress.
7. Greg experiences two competing explanations: the model is wrong, or the player is defending exactly as predicted. Prior success makes the second feel cheaper.
8. The pot grows beyond Greg's planned information budget. The unstable player exits. The pressure player adds conversational distraction, asking Greg whether he has finally found something.
9. Greg commits again. The disciplined player reveals a stronger position at resolution. Only then does the thumb go still.
10. Greg reconstructs the deception. The opponent did not hide the tell. He sold it.
11. Greg's remaining cash is below the amount he mentally needs to verify the new model. Credit is available. He knows leaving is correct.
12. Greg stays because the next hand now appears uniquely informative. He converts uncertainty into justification, loses further, and signs/accepts an obligation.
13. His post-scene diagnosis is locally accurate: he stayed after the table changed. It remains globally incomplete: he believes the failure was not updating fast enough rather than needing the model to close.

### PERFORMANCE evidence

- **Game state tracked:** cash boundary → modest wins → larger pot → testing cost exceeds plan → credit extends play.
- **Knowledge tracked:** private cards unknown; public bets observed; Greg's classifications provisional; opponent infers Greg's observation method.
- **Belief recursion tracked:** Greg models opponent; opponent models Greg's model; planted confirmation increases Greg's confidence.
- **Deception tracked:** the tell is not erased but deliberately preserved as bait.
- **Conversational timing:** table talk arrives while Greg holds competing explanations, increasing pressure without supplying information.
- **Mistake:** Greg confuses the value of resolving uncertainty with the value of the money required to resolve it.
- **Surviving discovery:** gambling activates Greg's systems intelligence and his inability to tolerate an unfinished model at the same time.

## One experimental prose render

The table became wrong twenty minutes before I left it.

I knew this because I wrote rules for other people much more easily than I followed them.

The man across from me had a careful stack and a careful face. Neither meant much. His thumb did.

Before larger bets, he touched the edge of his cards once. Not tapping. Pressure. A small confirmation that the cards remained where he had put them and the world had not rearranged itself while he considered the price.

Twice, he folded after doing it.

Useful.

The man to my left attacked whenever somebody looked uncertain. The player beside him had no stable relationship with probability, money, or the truth.

Also useful.

I won two modest hands.

Nothing dramatic. The right amount. Enough to prove the table could be read without becoming interesting enough to be remembered.

Then the careful man touched his cards.

The pot was larger now.

He bet.

Protection.

I increased the price.

The man to my left smiled. “Found something?”

“Several things.”

“That sounds expensive.”

He folded.

The erratic player lasted one decision longer, complained about the cards as if they had been raised badly, and left the pot to us.

The careful man called.

That created a problem.

Either I had misunderstood him, or he was protecting exactly what I thought he was protecting. Those possibilities required opposite decisions and looked annoyingly similar from the outside.

He touched the cards again.

There.

Confirmation.

I put more money in.

He matched it.

No rush. No satisfaction. His breathing did not change.

The pot had crossed the line I had set before sitting down.

Lines established before evidence were hypotheses.

That was true.

It was also the sort of sentence a man invents while stepping over one.

The careful man showed his cards.

Better.

Not barely.

My model broke all at once, which meant it had probably been broken in pieces while I was admiring it.

His thumb rested beside the cards.

Still.

I looked at him.

He looked back.

“You saw me watching,” I said.

“I saw you learning.”

The man to my left laughed.

I counted what remained.

Enough to leave.

Not enough to answer the new question.

That difference became the whole room.

The next hand would tell me whether the careful man had planted the gesture from the beginning or only changed after noticing me. One hand. Cheap information.

I reached for the cards.

Later, when the cash was gone, the credit felt temporary because numbers written on paper always looked less physical than coins.

I signed.

The careful man had already left.

That offended me more than the loss.

I had solved him, eventually.

He had solved the table.

The table included me.

# Post-generation canon comparison

## Methodological limitation

The blind artifact was committed before Chapter 5 was retrieved **in this run**, so no regeneration or canon-guided revision occurred afterward.

However, Chapter 5 had already been opened during the preceding Epoch 3 investigation in the same broader conversation context. The exact prose was therefore not cleanly unknown to the model. This is material contamination, especially for the Training Hall result, whose selected exchange resembles canon closely.

Treat this as a workflow/domain-behavior test, not a scientifically clean blind authorship comparison. A future clean control would need scenes never previously exposed in the active context.

## Scene 1 comparison — Training Hall

### Canon control

Canon begins with solo repetition and lets Greg feel brilliant before his legs start shaking. The spar with Jorren then unfolds across multiple exchanges:

- Greg reads Jorren's high guard, planted commitment, and tightening right shoulder.
- Jorren hits Greg first.
- Greg makes him miss, touches ribs and wrist, takes another shoulder hit, then uses the expected shoulder line to step inside and reach Jorren's throat.
- They continue. Greg wins and loses additional exchanges.
- A dragging foot eventually causes a hard loss.
- Jorren recognizes that Greg fights like an old man and refuses another round after observing his breathing and shaking legs.
- Greg hears confirmation more loudly than warning and buys more training.

### What generic PERFORMANCE added usefully

- It made continuous spatial causality explicit: center lane, worn edge, shortened starting distance, retreat failure, inside line.
- It tracked Greg's body as changing state rather than a static limitation.
- It gave Jorren a belief model: easy novice → confirmed shoulder opening → committed expectation → revised assessment.
- It made Greg's tactical adjustment legible as changing the geometry to fit his current body rather than simply being cleverer.

### What the experimental prose lost or distorted

- It compressed canon's richer exchange sample into one designed trap, making the bout feel cleaner and more authored.
- Canon gives Jorren more independent agency after the throat touch: he keeps fighting, wins exchanges, notices Greg's state, offers a hand, names the old-man quality, and ultimately refuses.
- The experimental ending has Greg accept another round verbally, while canon's actual state-out depends on Jorren declining. That shifts safety/authority toward Greg and weakens the opponent.
- Canon's solo-training prelude supplies the crucial false-confidence rise before the spar. The experimental scene begins too close to the tactical proof.
- Canon is funnier and less mechanically explanatory. The experimental render's “administrative revolt” language is voice-compatible but slightly over-composed.

### Outcome judgment

The PERFORMANCE packet improved local physical legibility, particularly distance and body-state causality. The prose render did **not** improve the whole scene. Canon has better escalation, opponent autonomy, humor, and longitudinal consequence.

Because of prior exact-prose exposure, the apparent accuracy of the selected shoulder-bait/throat-touch sequence is not valid evidence that generic PERFORMANCE independently discovered canon's solution.

## Scene 2 comparison — Gambling table

### Canon control

Canon keeps the card action compressed and focuses on Greg's systems failure:

- he recognizes within twenty minutes that the table is wrong;
- one player is unreadable because drunken intention changes;
- another is good, another better, another lucky;
- Greg loses, loses more, then recovers half;
- he sees a possible line: one player protects large pots, another attacks weakness, and the lucky player is overconfident;
- he believes he can turn them against each other;
- he stays, owes the table, and signs a note;
- Antonius later identifies the deeper error: Greg thinks explaining a mistake means he can afford it.

An earlier table also establishes that Greg can read who protects winnings, chases losses, bluffs from embarrassment, watches cards, watches the pot, and watches him. Greg moves rooms because people-reading works both ways.

### What generic PERFORMANCE added usefully

- It naturally represented information as asymmetric rather than physical.
- It tracked public actions, private cards, Greg's provisional classifications, an opponent's model of Greg, and the increasing price of uncertainty.
- It treated cash and available credit as state transitions, not background stakes.
- It found a domain-appropriate failure: Greg confuses the information value of another hand with the economic value of paying for it.
- Conversational pressure arrived while Greg held two competing explanations, which is genuinely different from the Training Hall's distance/timing pressure.

### What the experimental prose invented or overcommitted

- Canon does not establish a disciplined player deliberately planting or reversing a thumb tell. That is a plausible PERFORMANCE discovery, but it is unsupported invention rather than recovered canon behavior.
- The invention makes the opponent more agentic but narrows the cause of Greg's loss to one elegant counter-read.
- Canon's messier table is stronger: unstable intention, skill differences, luck, partial recovery, several interacting player tendencies, and Greg's desire to turn the system. No single opponent needs to mastermind him.
- The experimental prose delays Antonius's sharper diagnosis. “The table included me” is useful, but canon's “being able to explain a mistake means you can afford it” connects gambling to Greg's entire financial operating system.
- Canon achieves more causal reach with fewer card-specific beats.

### Outcome judgment

The gambling PERFORMANCE is more independently informative than the combat result. It correctly generated belief recursion, hidden information, incentives, attention, and bankroll/credit state without gambling-specific machinery.

Its prose is vivid and coherent, but not better than canon. The high-fidelity simulation overfit the loss into a clean duel of observers. Canon preserves a noisier multi-player system and connects the loss more powerfully to debt, self-explanation, and Greg's broader compulsion.

# Cross-domain evaluation

## Did generic PERFORMANCE simulate the domains differently?

**Yes.**

Without combat-specific or gambling-specific code, the same generic contract activated different causal state:

| Training Hall | Gambling table |
|---|---|
| body fatigue and pain | bankroll and credit |
| distance and lane position | pot size and information price |
| foot placement and recovery | bets, folds, and hidden cards |
| opponent commitment timing | nested beliefs and deceptive signaling |
| contact and balance | uncertainty and incentive |
| environmental geometry | conversational/decision timing |

That distinction was useful and emerged from scene pressure, actors, hard locks, and required outcome rather than a domain module.

## Did it improve the prose?

**Locally, sometimes. Overall, no clear win over canon.**

- Training PERFORMANCE improved physical continuity and made the winning exchange easy to visualize, but the render reduced Jorren's autonomy and canon's broader escalation.
- Gambling PERFORMANCE improved the visibility of belief recursion and economic state, but invented an overly neat planted-tell mechanism and lost canon's messy system-level strength.
- Canon was generally more compressed, funnier, less explanatory, and better connected to chapter-scale consequences.

The best use of generic PERFORMANCE here is as an **error detector and causal evidence generator**, not an automatic replacement-prose engine. The surviving discoveries worth carrying into prose are small: body state must change available geometry; observers can observe Greg; money determines how long uncertainty can be pursued. The full simulated mechanism does not need to appear on the page.

## Was the inference cost worthwhile?

**Worthwhile for the cross-domain architecture test; not worthwhile as a routine full-prose replacement pass.**

Two compact PERFORMANCE runs were enough to show that the generic layer can track materially different state without new machinery. The prose comparison also exposed the main cost: high-fidelity simulation tends to make causality cleaner, more explicit, and more duel-shaped than PLG canon benefits from.

A cheaper production use would preserve the PERFORMANCE evidence and let the renderer use only one or two surviving causal constraints.
