# Chapter 1: The Boy

I woke because my back didn't hurt.

That was wrong.

Not alarming at first. Waking normally involved a short negotiation with several pieces of me that had outlived their warranties. Lower back. Left shoulder. Right knee. The rib that clicked if I slept twisted. Nothing dramatic. Just attendance.

This morning nobody showed up.

I lay still.

Then I moved my toes.

All of them answered.

I bent one knee. Then the other.

Too easy.

I sat up fast enough that the room tilted and my body came with me instead of filing three objections on the way.

That got my attention.

The room should not have been familiar.

Cheap plaster. Narrow bed. One chair with a coat hanging over it. Washbasin with a crack down the side. Sword in the corner.

I knew the crack.

I had cut my thumb on it once.

I looked at my thumb.

No scar.

Wrong hand, maybe.

I checked the other.

Nothing.

I sat there with both hands in front of me.

They were young hands.

That is a stupid sentence until you have known your hands for fifty-nine years.

The knuckles were wrong. Too clean. The right index finger was straight. I had broken that finger on a contract and refused to let a healer reset it because we had another descent the next morning. It had leaned inward ever since.

I bent it.

Straight.

I bent it again as if the finger might admit something under pressure.

Still straight.

"No."

My voice was wrong too.

I got out of bed.

No stiffness.

I crouched.

Lower.

Stood.

Did it again.

Then, because apparently the first serious thing I would do with a miracle was test whether I could jump, I jumped.

The floorboards complained.

A man on the other side of the wall shouted, "Some of us are sleeping!"

I knew the voice.

The name was gone.

That bothered me more than the jumping.

I stood very still.

I knew he snored. I knew he borrowed soap. I knew he once threw a cup at someone in the hallway and missed so badly that the cup became my problem. I could not remember his name.

Forty years of memory and apparently soap theft had survived better than nouns.

The mirror above the basin was spotted at the edges.

I leaned toward it.

Nineteen.

I did not know that immediately as a number. I knew the face first.

Mine.

Dark hair. Too much of it. No gray. No line beside the mouth. No scar under the chin. The shoulders under my shirt were broad enough to make nineteen-year-old me optimistic and narrow enough to make fifty-nine-year-old me realistic.

I pulled the shirt up.

Flat stomach.

"Oh, that's unfair."

The boy in the mirror looked pleased.

I checked my teeth.

All present.

For perhaps two minutes I behaved with very little dignity.

Then I saw the sword again.

Cheap steel. One-handed. Leather grip darkened by use. A nick near the guard from the time I had used it to split kindling because finding an axe had seemed like more work.

I remembered loving that sword.

That offended me enough to make the room feel real.

I picked it up.

The balance was bad. Too much weight forward. The grip was slightly narrow for my hand.

I had spent decades around excellent weapons. I had held swords worth more than this building. I had watched people who could cut through plate, stone, enchanted hide, and once, memorably, a dining table because a duke's son had misunderstood how close he was standing.

I knew what good movement looked like.

That was not the same as knowing what my body was about to do.

I raised the sword and cut down.

The blade pulled farther forward than I wanted.

My shoulders tightened late. My rear foot moved after the rest of me had already committed. The tip passed close enough to the chair that the coat stirred.

I stopped.

"Huh."

I tried again.

Slower.

Better.

Again.

This time I concentrated on the feet and made the cut worse.

I lowered the sword.

That was interesting.

Not good. Interesting.

I remembered being a warrior first. Not a particularly elegant one. Strong enough. Fast enough. Brave enough, unfortunately. I had made Silver before finally admitting that every swordsman I respected was better at the sword than I was.

Then Barrier happened.

Then support magic.

Then decades of becoming something else entirely.

By the end I had been one of seven S-class adventurers on the continent.

Support.

The word mattered.

I had spent half a lifetime beside people who actually mastered this.

I knew how they trained. Or pieces of it. I knew what they complained about. I knew what they noticed. I knew that elite fighters conserved movement while beginners decorated it. I knew good footwork made violence look smaller. I knew people lifted their shoulders when they were afraid and held their breath when they were about to do something stupid.

I also knew my first cut had almost hit a chair.

Both facts were going to have to coexist.

I set the sword down.

Magic next.

That should have been easier.

I closed my eyes and reached for mana.

In my memory the motion was not really a motion. More like deciding to move a hand and discovering the hand had already begun.

Here I got a faint pressure behind my eyes and tingling in my palm.

I pushed harder.

Pain sharpened behind my forehead.

I stopped.

"Shit."

That frightened me.

The sword was optional.

Magic was not.

Not to the man I remembered being.

I stood in the middle of the room with a nineteen-year-old body, a bad sword, and almost no usable mana answering a mind that remembered shaping barriers over entire battle lines.

The absurdity should have crushed me.

Instead something else arrived.

Possibility.

Dangerous amount of it.

First I checked the room.

Three copper in the coat.

Bronze guild token in the pocket.

Training leathers in the wardrobe.

A bent lockpick I did not remember owning and probably should not have owned.

Receipts.

Two letters.

Dates.

I did not trust the dates yet.

I went to the window.

Carrow was outside.

Younger.

The north side of the street had only one proper stone building. The apothecary I remembered was not there. The Guild roof still had red tile. The Exchange was missing completely.

A cart rolled through mud where I remembered paving.

Earlier.

The thought came cleanly enough that I distrusted it.

So I checked.

I found a guild notice beneath the bed.

Another in the coat.

A letter from Mara, whose handwriting I recognized before I remembered whether I liked her.

I compared dates against the siege of East Verrel, because everybody remembered the siege of East Verrel. I counted backward.

Then did it again.

Nineteen.

I sat on the edge of the bed.

Nineteen.

The room got very quiet.

My first thought was not noble.

My first thought was that I had forty years back.

My second was that I could do everything better.

The certainty of that thought should have warned me.

It did not.

I stood and looked at the mirror again.

Young body.

Old memories.

I knew what professions became valuable. Which wars happened. Which people mattered. Which mistakes ruined guilds. Which discoveries changed entire industries. Which monsters were worse than the contracts claimed. Which supposedly brilliant fighters were actually held together by preparation, healers, money, and three very competent people nobody remembered afterward.

I knew magic.

I knew people.

I knew how the world worked.

This was going to be easy.

Then I remembered my mother.

The thought hit hard enough that I sat back down.

For several seconds I let myself be stupid about it.

Maybe.

Maybe the reset went far enough.

Maybe she was somewhere across the city buying bread. Maybe my father was arguing with someone about tools. Maybe there was a house I could walk to and knock on the door and watch two dead people become alive because I had been given enough time to reach them.

I knew the answer before I checked.

I checked anyway.

My parents had already died.

Years before nineteen.

The miracle stopped exactly short of them.

I stared at the floor.

There were things I had forgotten about grief because grief itself had gotten old. It had worn smooth. The edges had gone away.

Apparently the edges were still stored somewhere.

I sat there until the neighbor started snoring again.

Still no name.

That helped, strangely.

Memory was not a perfect archive.

Good.

If it had been perfect, I might have trusted it.

I found paper.

Old habit.

I wrote three headings.

WHAT I KNOW

WHAT I REMEMBER

WHAT I CAN CHECK

Then stared at them.

The categories began failing immediately.

Did I know the Northern Gate collapsed in the year I remembered, or did I remember the anniversary people celebrated later?

Did I know a merchant family became rich from river shipping, or remember the version of their history they paid to have repeated?

Did I know Soren died in the Red Winter, or only that I had met him after it with a scar he claimed came from there?

Memory kept handing me outcomes with the causes rubbed off.

I wrote one sentence under all three headings.

**USE THE FUTURE AS A LEAD. MAKE THE PRESENT PROVE IT.**

That was sensible.

I liked it.

Which made me suspicious.

I put the paper down.

The sword waited in the corner.

I picked it up again.

One cut.

Worse than I wanted.

Again.

Better.

I tried a side step the way I remembered S-class duelists doing it, small and almost lazy, barely enough to move the target.

My feet crossed.

I nearly fell into the basin.

Water jumped over the rim.

I caught myself on the wall.

The cracked basin did not survive the correction gracefully. A thin line of water began crawling down the outside.

I stared at it.

"Excellent."

Three copper.

Broken basin.

Almost no mana.

Bronze token.

Bad footwork.

Forty years of useful information, probably.

This felt more familiar.

I dried the floor with the coat I had not yet decided whether I still liked, dressed, tied the sword at my hip, and stepped into the street.

I had spent decades learning that the fastest way to understand a system was to find where it resisted you.

The Guild had resisted me in both lives.

It seemed polite to begin there.
