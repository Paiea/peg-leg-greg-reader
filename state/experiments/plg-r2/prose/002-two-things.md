# Chapter 2: Two Things

The Guild had not gotten smaller.

I had gotten used to larger rooms.

That distinction took me almost the length of the entrance hall to understand.

The old Carrow Guild smelled like wet leather, cheap lamp oil, metal polish, and too many people who believed coming back alive qualified them to shout indoors. The contract boards took up the far wall. Bronze on the left. Silver farther in. Gold behind a waist-high rail I remembered not existing later.

I stopped in front of it.

Gold.

There had been a time when that rail would have mattered to me.

Later, people moved when I walked through rooms like this.

Now a courier nearly hit me with a bundle of forms.

"Move."

I moved.

Good start.

The clerk behind the front desk had ink on two fingers and the expression of someone who had already explained the same rule six times that morning.

Young.

Not that young.

Twenty-two, maybe.

I knew her.

That was becoming an unpleasant sentence.

Not personally. Not yet.

Future Sella had run half of this building through a combination of memory, threat, filing systems, and the ability to make adventurers feel ashamed of wasting her paper.

Present Sella had one loose strand of hair stuck to her cheek and was arguing with a Bronze fighter about a missing stamp.

"You signed the declaration," she said.

"I signed the form."

"The declaration is on the back."

"Why is it on the back?"

"Because paper has two sides."

"Nobody said anything about the back."

"The arrow says TURN OVER."

"Small arrow."

Sella looked at me because I had started laughing.

The Bronze fighter looked too.

I stopped.

"Sorry."

"You need something?" Sella asked.

There were several possible answers.

Proof that time had moved backward.

An explanation for why my mana barely answered.

A list of future dead people.

Training.

Money.

A better sword.

Someone to tell me that I was not losing my mind.

I put the Bronze token on the counter.

"What does this currently get me?"

Sella picked it up.

"Currently?"

"Yes."

She turned the token over.

"Basic yard access. Contract board. Guild beds if available. Discounted healer. One assessment each quarter. You missed your last one."

I had.

Probably.

"Training?"

"Yard's included. Instructors aren't."

"Rates?"

She pointed at a board three feet to my right.

I had walked past it.

I read the prices.

Then read them again.

"That's obscene."

"That's one silver."

"Exactly."

Sella looked at my coat.

Then the sword.

Then me.

"You came here with three copper, didn't you?"

I stared at her.

"Why three?"

"You have the face."

Apparently I had always had several faces and everyone else had been cataloguing them without permission.

"I have enough," I said.

I had three copper.

"For the yard," she said.

"Yes."

"Which is free."

"Exactly."

She slid the token back.

"Next."

I stayed there.

"I also need the date."

That earned me a longer look.

"The date."

"Full date."

She told me.

Same year as the notices.

Same year as Mara's letter.

Same year as the version of my face I had been trying not to touch every ten minutes.

My stomach turned over once.

Sella had already looked down at the next form.

For her it was Tuesday.

That helped.

I stepped away from the counter.

The Bronze board was full.

Rat-cellar clearances. Road escorts. Two crop-guard jobs outside the south wall. Missing pack lizard. Something called a saltback infestation that paid embarrassingly well until I remembered saltbacks reproduced in walls and excreted acid when frightened.

No.

I had died enough times near acid without making it permanent.

The yard was behind the main hall.

I heard it before I reached the doors.

Wood striking wood. Boots in sand. Someone swearing. An instructor counting repetitions without any sympathy for whether the person performing them had stopped enjoying numbers.

The sound hit me harder than I expected.

Not nostalgia.

Something closer to hunger.

I pushed through the doors.

Three practice lanes. Two sparring circles. A row of posts. Weapon racks. Sand dark where people had poured water over their heads.

Bronze fighters everywhere.

Bad guards. Big swings. Too much shoulder. Feet planted too wide. One boy kept turning his hips before his blade moved, announcing every cut to anyone who had eyes.

I saw all of it at once.

That was intoxicating.

For five minutes I became insufferable in complete silence.

I knew so much.

The man on the nearest lane was wasting force.

The woman with the shield was stepping too deep.

Two fighters in the back were both watching the weapon instead of the chest.

A boy with a spear kept recovering his rear hand to exactly the same place.

I could fix half this yard.

Probably.

Maybe.

I picked up a wooden sword from the rack.

The first overhead cut pulled me forward.

Less than this morning.

Still enough.

I reset.

Again.

My rear foot landed late.

Again.

I thought about the shoulder.

The hips got worse.

I thought about the hips.

My grip tightened.

I loosened the grip and forgot what my left foot was doing.

By the tenth repetition I had successfully improved four separate pieces of the movement and made the movement itself worse.

A voice behind me said, "You trying to fight or assemble furniture?"

I turned.

The instructor was a woman in faded Silver leathers with one side of her head shaved close and the other tied back.

I did not recognize her.

Good.

She pointed at my feet with a stick.

"Again."

I cut.

"Too much."

"Which part?"

"All of you."

"Specific."

"You want specific, pay me."

Fair.

She moved on.

I watched her correct the next person with three words.

"Heel down. Again."

The movement improved.

She corrected another.

"Stop reaching. Again."

Improved.

I looked at my sword.

There were too many things I could work on.

That was the problem.

I waited until she passed again.

"Question."

"One."

"If you had a beginner for a month and they could practice one attack and one way to not get hit, what would you give them?"

She stopped.

That got more attention than any of my earlier competence had.

"Why only two?"

"Because I'm currently bad at twenty."

She looked me over.

"Overhead cut. Side step."

I almost objected.

Overhead cut was obvious.

Side step was barely a technique.

Which was probably why the answer irritated me.

"Which side?"

"Both."

"That's three things."

She stared at me.

"Fine," I said.

She moved away.

I raised the sword.

Overhead.

No flourish.

No transition.

No clever change of angle.

Just raise, cut, recover.

Again.

Again.

Again.

My shoulder started burning before I learned anything profound.

Good.

I put the sword down and practiced stepping.

Small movement.

Enough to leave the line.

I knew what elite fighters made it look like. Almost nothing. A shift of weight and suddenly the attack had missed by the width of a hand.

Mine looked like I had remembered an appointment somewhere else.

Too far.

Again.

Feet crossed.

Again.

Too slow.

Again.

Better.

A training sword touched my ribs.

I flinched and turned.

The fighter holding it was broad through the chest, maybe twenty, with a practice blade in one hand and the expression of someone who had been waiting to see whether I noticed him.

"You keep stepping with nobody attacking you," he said.

"That was the idea."

"Doesn't work if nobody's attacking you."

He had a point.

I disliked him immediately.

"Hit me again."

He blinked.

"What?"

"Same line."

He shrugged.

Then swung.

I saw the shoulder load.

Saw his weight shift.

Saw exactly where the blade would pass.

My brain supplied the answer before the sword moved.

Side step.

My body supplied it afterward.

The wooden blade hit my ribs.

Hard enough to sting.

The fighter laughed.

"Again," I said.

"You sure?"

"Same line."

He hit me again.

This time I moved early.

Too early.

He changed the cut and hit my shoulder.

I looked at him.

He grinned.

"You said same line."

"You lied."

"We're practicing fighting."

Fair again.

"Name?"

"Jorren."

Distinct enough.

I repeated it once.

"Greg."

"I heard the clerk."

"Of course you did."

He lifted the sword.

"Again?"

I should have said no.

I had already decided on deliberate practice. Narrow scope. One attack. One evasion. Repetition before complexity.

This was an excellent plan.

Then Jorren smiled like he thought he was better than me.

"Again."

We went for perhaps four minutes.

I learned several useful things.

First, Jorren was better than me.

Not dramatically. He was Bronze. He had the same collection of broad beginner tools I remembered having at that age, but his body knew them now. Guard. Step. Cut. Recover. He did not need to hold committee meetings with his ankles.

Second, I could read him.

His right shoulder tightened before a committed cut. He breathed in before changing direction. When I pressured his sword side, his first answer was usually to move backward rather than around.

Third, knowing those things was not the same as having an answer to them.

I made him miss twice.

Once because I stepped correctly.

Once because he slipped.

I preferred the first.

He hit me six times.

One of them I saw so early that I became offended before it landed.

That was a new experience.

The last exchange was the worst.

Jorren's shoulder tightened.

Heavy cut.

I stepped outside.

Correctly.

For once, beautifully.

His blade missed.

The opening appeared.

I knew exactly what a good swordsman would do with it.

I attempted something that was not an overhead cut.

My sword wandered sideways through an ugly little counter I had not practiced once.

Jorren recovered faster than I did and tapped my forehead.

"Dead," he said.

I stood there.

He lowered the sword.

"What was that?"

"Ambition."

He laughed.

I did too.

Then my ribs started hurting where he had hit me the first time.

That ended the lesson better than wisdom would have.

I returned the practice sword.

Across the yard, Sella was holding a short baton.

That made me stop.

She stood in a line with three guild clerks, a porter, and a woman from the healer desk. The Silver instructor moved down the row.

"Guild staff drill," Jorren said.

I had forgotten that existed.

"Since when?"

He looked at me.

"Since always?"

Right.

Present knowledge.

The instructor swung a padded stick at Sella's shoulder.

Sella stepped aside.

Small.

Clean.

No wasted distance.

The stick missed by inches.

She returned to position.

The instructor nodded once.

I stared.

Jorren followed my eyes.

"You know her?"

"Eventually."

"What?"

"No."

Sella stepped again.

Clean again.

No theory.

No visible existential crisis about foot placement.

Just a woman following a basic instruction because the Guild required her to do it twice a month.

I felt something petty wake up in me.

That was useful information too.

"How long has she been doing that?" I asked.

Jorren shrugged.

"Staff drills? Months. Why?"

"No reason."

Sella caught me looking.

Her face said she already had enough work.

I looked away first.

Interesting.

The yard bell rang.

My free hour was over.

I had accomplished less than I expected and learned more than I wanted.

At the desk, Sella held out her hand.

"Token."

I gave it to her.

She stamped something in the register.

"Next free yard block is six days."

"Six?"

"Bronze basic access."

"I need daily."

"Then pay."

There it was.

I looked at the rates again.

One silver for instruction.

Copper for additional yard blocks. More for practice gear if I broke anything. More for healer access. More for better food if I wanted the body to recover. Mana conditioning would cost too once I found someone willing to teach a nineteen-year-old whose mana currently answered like a sleepy cat.

I had three copper.

Two now, after buying breakfast.

"There a cheaper yard?"

"Outside."

"With posts?"

"Trees."

"Level ground?"

"Mostly."

"Someone going to complain if I hit them?"

"The trees?"

"People."

"Depends how accurate you are."

I looked at her.

That was almost a joke.

Future Sella existed in there somewhere.

Present Sella had already reached for the next form.

I left.

Outside the Guild, I stood beneath the red-tiled roof and inventoried the problem.

Training cost money.

Magic would cost money.

Food cost money.

Apparently being young again had not removed the world's commitment to invoices.

Fine.

I had future knowledge.

Not answers.

Leads.

Somewhere in Carrow was something cheap now that became expensive later.

Something useful nobody understood yet.

A person before they became important.

A process before anyone bothered to fix it.

I could find one.

I had done harder things.

That confidence returned easily.

Too easily.

I noticed that.

Then ignored it.

On the walk toward the east market, I practiced the side step every time nobody was close enough to see.

By the third street I had nearly walked into a vegetable cart.

The merchant shouted after me.

I kept going.

Two things.

Overhead cut.

Side step.

Money first.

Then everything else.
