# Chapter 3: The Borrower

Future knowledge was much easier to spend before I tried to buy anything with it.

The east market gave me three possibilities in the first hour.

One was wrong.

One was real but useless for another twenty years.

The third was a pile of gray stone chips beside a masonry stall.

Kestrin shale.

I stood in the street looking at it while a porter dumped another basket onto the pile.

Cheap. Brittle. Mildly mana-reactive. Mostly used where nobody cared if the material cracked.

Later it would matter.

I knew that.

Probably.

The word had become irritatingly necessary.

I remembered pale filtration housings stamped with the Arwick mark. I remembered somebody explaining that old Kestrin waste turned out to bind alchemical residue under heat. I remembered buying the finished filters by the crate because they were cheap enough that nobody important bothered to think about them.

That was all.

No formula.

No inventor.

No exact year.

I had the ending of a story and none of the useful middle.

Still better than two copper.

"How much?" I asked the stall owner.

She followed my finger to the shale pile.

"For what?"

"The shale."

"Why?"

"I want it."

"Why?"

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

Fair question.

"Testing."

"You an artificer?"

"No."

"Builder?"

"No."

"Mage?"

"Not usefully."

She folded her arms.

"Two silver for the cart."

I had two copper.

"How much for a bucket?"

That got a smile.

Much more sensible.

One copper.

I bought the bucket.

Growth.

The old version of me would have bought the cart because scale felt like confidence. The current version had just been hit in the ribs by a man named Jorren and nearly walked into vegetables while practicing footwork.

A bucket was enough to be wrong with.

I carried it through the market.

That lasted six minutes before the handle started biting into my fingers.

Kestrin shale was cheap partly because stone was heavy.

I had known this in principle.

Principles had excellent posture. Reality made you switch hands.

The name Arwick came back slowly.

Not the man.

The stamp.

Three lines inside a square.

Arwick filtration. Arwick regulators. Arwick something else.

I remembered seeing the mark on expedition gear decades later.

So I asked.

Current Arwick Works was not works.

It was a rented room behind a cooper.

The man inside was younger than I expected, which was becoming a useless observation. Everyone was younger than I expected. He had dark hair tied behind his neck, a burn scar across two fingers, and a lamp regulator opened across his bench.

He looked at the bucket.

Then me.

"No."

"I haven't asked anything."

"You're carrying rocks into my workshop."

"Shale."

"Wonderful."

"Kestrin."

That changed his face slightly.

Not recognition.

Technical interest.

Good.

"Do you work with mana-reactive ceramic mixes?"

"Sometimes."

"Have you tried powdered Kestrin in a porous body?"

"Why would I?"

There it was.

The dangerous moment.

My memory wanted to become authority because I knew the finished world.

I made myself stay with what I actually had.

"Trace mana. Cheap structure. High surface area if powdered. I think heat changes how it binds residue."

The man looked at the bucket again.

"Think?"

"Yes."

"Based on?"

"Something I saw a long time ago."

He looked at my face.

"You're nineteen."

"It was a very formative childhood."

He did not laugh.

I liked him more.

"Name?" I asked.

"Arlo."

Good.

Not proof.

But good.

"Arlo Arwick?"

He went still.

That was a mistake.

I knew it before he asked.

"Who told you my family name?"

No sign on the door.

No maker's stamp visible from where I stood.

Excellent work, Greg.

"Cooper next door."

"Mell doesn't know my family name."

"Then someone else."

"Who?"

I looked at the lamp regulator.

"Can you test the shale?"

Arlo's expression said the subject had not changed.

Mine said I hoped persistence counted as strategy.

He finally took one chip from the bucket.

Turned it over.

"Six silver."

I laughed.

He put the chip back.

"Then no."

"For a test?"

"For my time. Materials extra."

"That's robbery."

"Then go to a cheaper artificer."

I did not know a cheaper artificer.

Worse, I did not know whether the Arwick name mattered because of Arlo, his children, a buyer who kept the name, or a coincidence large enough to make me look stupid.

Future knowledge had given me a door.

The door charged six silver.

"I'll come back."

"Without rocks."

I took the bucket.

"The rocks are the point."

"That's what worries me."

Outside, I sat on the back step of the cooper's shop with the bucket between my feet and counted money I did not have.

Two copper.

Training would cost.

The test cost six silver before materials.

Food mattered if I intended to train every day.

Mana conditioning would cost once I found a teacher.

Rent would eventually become interested in me.

My second life had lasted less than two days and already required financing.

That part felt familiar.

There were several lenders in Carrow.

I knew one.

That was not the same thing as trusting him.

Antonius Vale's future reputation had accumulated so many stories that I no longer knew which ones were true. He had financed merchant houses, private wars, guild expansions, political campaigns, and at least one marriage. Possibly two marriages. The second story was better and therefore less likely.

I remembered the man.

That was enough to be cautious.

It was not enough to stop me.

His current office was behind a gambling room near slaughter lane.

I went to the wrong building first.

That improved my mood.

The right place had no polished stone, no private guards in matching coats, no quiet front room designed to make wealthy people feel observed.

There was a ham hanging from the ceiling.

I stared at it.

Antonius Vale came through the back curtain.

Black hair.

Cheap coat.

Lean.

Same eyes.

I had seen those eyes across negotiating tables when cities were involved.

Now there was a ham over his head.

I smiled.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

"Not yet."

His eyes sharpened.

Too much.

"I need a loan."

That fixed the problem.

Everyone became ordinary around money.

"How much?"

I had calculated six for Arlo, two for materials, four for training, three for food and margin, another five because things always cost more than the plan, and then doubled it because I did not want to come back.

"Forty silver."

The man beside Antonius laughed.

Antonius did not.

"Collateral?"

"No."

"Property?"

"No."

"Family?"

"Dead."

"Guarantor?"

"No."

"Rank?"

I put the Bronze token on the table.

The other man laughed again.

I was beginning to dislike him.

Antonius picked up the token.

"Forty silver."

"Yes."

"For what?"

"Training. Food. An artificer test."

"Of what?"

I put one Kestrin chip on his table.

He stared at it.

"Rock."

"Shale."

"That improves things."

"I think it has a useful filtration property nobody is pricing yet."

"Think."

"Yes."

"And the artificer?"

"Interested enough to charge me six silver."

"That means he is interested in six silver."

I liked present Antonius too.

Annoying day.

He pushed the Bronze token back.

"No."

Of course.

Forty-year reputation did not exist.

S-class did not exist.

I was a nineteen-year-old Bronze asking a lender for forty silver to test rocks.

Good.

Present proof.

"Ten."

Antonius looked at me.

"You changed quickly."

"I asked for what I wanted. Now I'm asking for what you might give me."

That got the smallest shift in his mouth.

"Term?"

"Ten days. Twenty percent."

"Eight days. Thirty-five."

"Twenty-five."

"Thirty-five."

"Thirty and ten days."

"Thirty-five and eight."

I looked at him.

He looked back.

Old Greg had negotiated with people who wanted borders.

Current Greg needed ten silver.

Different leverage.

"If I fail?"

"You work the balance."

"Rate?"

He named it.

Bad.

Not impossible.

"Type of work?"

The man beside him smiled.

I looked at Antonius.

"Legal."

"Mostly," he said.

"No."

That surprised him.

Good.

"Legal work. Labor, collections, delivery, bookkeeping if you trust me enough to let me touch numbers. No body disposal."

The other man laughed for the third time.

Antonius glanced at him.

"Rusk, stop encouraging him."

Rusk.

I knew the name now.

Future? Maybe.

Present first.

Antonius counted ten silver onto the table.

"Eight days. Thirty-five percent. Legal labor if you fail. You miss a day, the work rate drops."

"That's predatory."

"Yes."

"Good."

That came out before I could stop it.

Antonius's fingers paused on the last coin.

"Good?"

"Clear terms."

He pushed the money across.

The weight of ten silver hit my palm.

My brain changed immediately.

Two copper had been survival.

Ten silver was possibility.

Possibility had always been expensive for me.

I went straight back to Arlo.

That part was sensible.

I paid six silver.

Also sensible, if the test mattered.

I paid for better clay and reagents.

Still defensible.

Then I returned to the masonry stall.

The woman saw me coming and smiled.

"Bucket?"

I looked at the pile.

A bucket was enough to test.

We had already established that.

The whole cart was two silver.

If the first tests worked, I would need more.

If they did not, the shale remained cheap.

The rational decision was obvious.

"Cart."

She smiled wider.

Forty minutes later the cart blocked the alley behind Arlo's workshop.

Arlo came outside.

He looked at the cart.

Then at me.

Then back at the cart.

"No."

"We may need volume."

"We need a bowl."

"Eventually."

"Greg."

"Storage?"

"Not here."

The carter wanted another three copper to wait.

Another four to take it somewhere else.

I owned no somewhere else.

The cooper objected to the alley.

A woman trying to pass with a basket objected to me personally.

Arlo stood in his doorway with both arms folded.

Ten silver had lasted less than an hour.

"How much of this do you actually need?" I asked.

"I told you."

"Tell me again."

"A bucket."

I looked at the cart.

The cart appeared larger now.

"Excellent."

Arlo closed his eyes.

I paid the carter to move the shale to a cheap storage corner behind the masonry stall I had just bought it from.

The stall owner charged storage.

Of course she did.

By the time I got back to Arlo, my investment in nearly worthless rock had generated transportation fees, storage fees, and enough public humiliation to qualify as education.

Arlo was grinding one of the original chips.

"You rich?" he asked.

"Temporarily."

"That's not usually how rich works."

"You'd be surprised."

He measured powder into a ceramic mix.

I watched his hands.

He knew what he was doing.

I knew what the finished technology looked like decades later.

Those were not competing facts.

They were different jobs.

That helped.

The first sample cracked in the kiln.

The second warped.

The third came out intact and did nothing useful.

Arlo wrote each result down.

I started suggesting changes.

He ignored the first two.

The third made him pause.

"Why lower the heat?"

I searched memory.

A woman with burned fingertips swearing about old Kestrin mix.

A white filter housing.

Someone saying heat ruined the binding.

Or activated it.

"I don't know."

Arlo looked at me.

"You said lower it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I remember heat mattering and no longer trust which direction."

"That sentence is useless."

"It is more honest than the useful-sounding version."

He stared at me for a moment.

Then split the next batch.

One hotter.

One lower.

Both failed.

Good.

Not financially.

Epistemically.

That word did not improve my financial condition.

By dusk, Arlo had one result worth repeating and no result worth selling.

I had borrowed money, spent most of it, acquired an inconvenient amount of shale, and learned that remembering the future did not include remembering how the future had been made.

Eight days had become seven.

I should have gone home.

Instead I went to the strip of dirt behind the Guild where nobody charged for trees.

My shoulder hurt from carrying the shale bucket.

My hands hurt from the handle.

Good conditions for sword practice if the goal was stupidity.

I raised the cheap sword.

Overhead cut.

The blade dragged forward.

Again.

Side step.

Too wide.

Again.

Overhead.

Again.

Side step.

My feet crossed once.

I stopped.

Reset.

Again.

There were at least fifteen things I wanted to fix.

Grip. Recovery. Hip turn. Breath. Weight. Range. Timing. How the sword stopped. How the foot landed. How quickly I could move again after both.

I picked two.

Cut straight.

Step small.

Everything else could wait until tomorrow.

I practiced until the light went bad.

On the walk home I found myself calculating how much gambling capital I would need to clear Antonius in three nights.

I knew enough to recognize the thought as dangerous.

I also knew enough to make it sound reasonable.

That was going to be a problem.
