# Chapter 4: Thirty Days

A month later, Sella stopped asking for my guild token.

That was how I knew time had passed.

Not the calendar.

Not the tally marks on the paper beside my bed.

Sella looked up when I entered, reached beneath the counter, and slid the yard register toward me before I spoke.

"You're late."

"For what?"

"Being irritating before breakfast."

I signed the register.

Her finger landed on the wrong line before mine did.

I moved down one.

She looked at my signature.

"Your handwriting got worse."

"Training."

"Your hands?"

"Everything."

That was mostly true.

The first week had changed almost nothing except my appetite.

The second had changed my feet.

The third changed the sword.

Not broadly.

That part mattered.

If someone asked me to perform a proper sequence of basic cuts, I still looked like a Bronze fighter who had spent too much time thinking about the sequence before starting it.

If someone asked me to thrust, my front foot wandered.

If someone asked me to parry from the left, I occasionally tried to solve the incoming blade with an explanation.

But the overhead cut had become mine.

Not excellent.

Not Silver.

Not secretly S-class.

Mine.

The blade went where I meant it to go often enough that I no longer had to supervise every joint on the way down.

The side step had changed too.

Small now.

Usually.

I still stepped too far when tired.

I still crossed my feet if something surprised me from the wrong side.

But on an expected line, I could leave the line without turning the movement into travel.

That had taken hundreds of repetitions.

Possibly over a thousand.

I had stopped counting after the number became an excuse to feel accomplished.

The practice itself was less interesting than the evidence.

My right palm had a new hard patch beneath the first finger.

The cheap sword's grip had darkened.

A patch of dirt behind the Guild had two shallow grooves where my feet kept landing.

The tree I used as a target had lost bark in one narrow vertical strip.

Jorren had stopped laughing at the overhead cut.

He still laughed at almost everything else.

That morning he was already in the yard.

New boots.

I noticed because his old pair had a split at the inside heel.

"You got the escort," I said.

Jorren looked down at the boots.

"How did you know?"

"You said the caravan paid an equipment advance if they took you."

"Three weeks ago."

"Yes."

"You remember that but forgot my name twice."

"Priorities improve with evidence."

He shook his head.

The caravan left in nine days.

That had been Jorren's month.

While I was buying yard time, losing money, winning it back, moving shale I did not need, and trying to convince my feet that four inches was a meaningful distance, Jorren had been collecting the contract credits he needed for a north-road escort.

He had done it without me.

Obviously.

Still worth noticing.

"Spar?" he asked.

"Two exchanges."

"Why two?"

"Because I have a contract."

"You?"

The amount of surprise irritated me.

"Yes."

"Doing what?"

"Saltbacks."

Jorren stared.

Then laughed.

I had forgotten I told him, during our first week of training, that saltbacks were worth paying someone else to deal with.

"Financial circumstances changed."

"You got poorer?"

"I got more informed."

"Same thing?"

Increasingly.

The shale project had survived.

That was expensive.

Arlo's first repeatable filtration improvement had been small enough to be irritating and real enough to keep funding. He had changed the clay mix without asking me. That improved the result more than two of my suggestions combined.

I had congratulated him and been privately offended.

He had also sold three repaired lamp regulators and used some of his own money to buy better measuring glass because, as he explained, the project belonged to his workshop too if I expected his name on it.

That conversation had improved Arlo considerably.

Mostly by removing me from the center of him.

Antonius had been paid on the first loan.

Then I had borrowed again.

Smaller.

Then paid that.

Then borrowed against two gambling nights I was very confident would go well.

One did.

One did not.

The third loan was currently attached to part of my week.

This was technically progress because the total balance was lower than the first time.

Antonius disagreed with my definition.

My mana response had improved from almost nothing to a small, stable pulse I could hold for several breaths.

That had cost money too.

Everything useful had developed a price.

So I had taken the saltback contract.

Five silver plus salvage.

Two creatures confirmed in an old bathhouse cellar near the south wall.

Potential third.

Acid hazard.

The listing had remained on the board for six days.

Sella had circled the pay twice.

"Guild added hazard money," she told me.

"Because nobody took it?"

"Because the bathhouse owner added three silver."

"Because nobody took it."

"That too."

I had taken it.

Jorren looked toward the desk.

"You going alone?"

"No."

That had also taken me a month to improve at.

"I need someone who has actually fought one recently."

Jorren stopped smiling.

"I've done saltbacks."

"I know."

"How?"

"You complained about the smell."

"That could be anything."

"You complained specifically that acid getting into leather smells worse than acid getting into cloth."

He thought about it.

"That's true."

"Interested?"

"Split?"

"Three to me. Two to you."

"Two-fifty."

"I found the job."

"Sella found the job."

From the desk, Sella said, "Correct."

Neither of us had realized she was listening.

Jorren smiled.

"Two-fifty."

I looked at Sella.

"You're damaging my leverage."

"Your leverage was a piece of paper on my wall."

"Two-fifty," Jorren repeated.

"Fine."

Sella stamped the contract.

Then held it instead of giving it to me.

"Acid kit."

"We know."

"Do you have one?"

Jorren did.

I did not.

Sella kept holding the paper.

I looked at her.

"How much?"

She pointed toward supply.

Of course.

The kit cost four copper.

My expected profit decreased before I left the building.

Good adventure.

The staff drill started while Jorren checked his straps.

Sella handed the contract to another clerk and walked around the counter.

"You're training?" I asked.

"Twice a month. Same as last month."

"I thought that was optional."

"It is if I want to fail the emergency certification."

She joined the line.

The same Silver instructor held the padded stick.

I watched despite myself.

Sella stepped.

Clean.

Small.

Other side.

Less clean.

Again.

Better.

She caught me watching on the fourth repetition.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You have the face."

I hated this city.

"How often do you practice the step?"

"When I'm told to."

"Outside drill."

"Why would I do that?"

That answer offended me at a level I could not defend.

She stepped again.

The padded stick missed her shoulder by inches.

I had practiced the same basic idea hundreds of times.

I was better at it now.

I was fairly sure.

The fact that I needed to check annoyed me.

"Greg," Jorren said.

He was already at the gate.

"Contract."

Right.

Fantasy violence before petty competition with a clerk.

Priorities.

We walked south.

The bathhouse had closed a year earlier after a pipe collapse flooded the lower rooms. The owner now stored old furniture and heating stone in the basement because apparently the universal response to unusable space was to fill it with objects nobody wanted.

The cellar door smelled wrong before Jorren opened it.

Mineral.

Rot.

Something sharp underneath.

"Acid," he said.

"I know what acid smells like."

"This kind?"

I paused.

"No."

"Good."

He handed me a strip of waxed cloth.

"Wrap your sword hand."

"Why?"

"Because if it spits on the hilt, you'll drop the sword."

That had not occurred to me.

Current experience.

I wrapped the hand.

The stairs went down beneath a low stone arch.

Too low.

I looked at my sword.

Jorren saw it.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Face."

"Stop learning that."

My best attack required the sword to travel above my head.

The ceiling had different plans.

A month of deliberate practice had met architecture.

Excellent.

We descended.

The first saltback clung to the far wall.

About the size of a large dog. Six legs. Broad body. Gray-white mineral plates crusted over the spine. The mouth was small until it opened.

Then it was mostly mouth.

I remembered larger ones from somewhere.

Dungeon colony, maybe.

The memory supplied an image of a shield dissolving at the edge.

Useful warning.

Not useful technique.

Jorren whispered, "Don't rush it."

"I wasn't."

"You leaned forward."

"Observation."

"With your entire body?"

I ignored him.

The saltback's side sacs moved.

There.

I knew that.

Maybe.

"Spit before charge?" I whispered.

Jorren shook his head.

"Usually after."

Good correction.

The creature dropped from the wall.

It hit the floor hard and came toward us faster than something shaped like a rock should move.

Jorren stepped left.

I stepped right.

Small.

Clean.

The saltback passed between us.

For one beautiful half-second, the line was perfect.

Its neck seam opened beneath the raised plates.

Overhead cut.

I lifted the sword.

The ceiling stopped me.

The blade struck stone.

Pain ran down my wrist.

The saltback turned.

Jorren shouted.

I moved before I understood the word.

Side step.

Too large.

My boot hit an old bench.

I went sideways over it.

Acid hit the wall where my chest had been.

Stone hissed.

I landed on one knee behind the bench.

Jorren cut at the creature's rear leg.

His sword skidded off mineral plate.

The saltback spun toward him.

I stood.

Wrong room.

That was the entire problem.

My two good movements were not failing because they were bad movements.

The room had priced one of them out.

We did not need a better Greg.

We needed a taller ceiling.

"Stairs," I said.

Jorren blocked another charge with the bench.

"What?"

"Bring it to the stairs."

"Why?"

"Ceiling."

He looked up.

Then at my sword.

"Your fucking overhead thing?"

"Yes."

The saltback hit the bench.

Wood cracked.

Jorren stopped asking questions.

He kicked the broken bench toward the creature and backed toward the stairs.

I moved beside him.

The saltback followed.

Its sacs tightened.

"After charge," Jorren said.

Right.

It came.

We split.

I stepped.

Small.

The creature passed.

Jorren slapped its side with the flat of his sword and shouted.

It turned toward him.

The stairwell arch rose higher than the cellar ceiling.

Enough.

The saltback charged again.

Jorren moved late on purpose.

The creature committed.

I raised the sword.

No shoulder flourish.

No complicated angle.

Feet where I had put them a thousand times.

Blade straight.

Down.

The cut struck the neck seam behind the head.

The first impact jarred my arms.

The blade bit.

Not enough.

The saltback screamed.

I tried to recover.

That was the movement I had not practiced.

The sword stayed low for half a beat too long.

The creature twisted.

One side sac contracted.

I saw it.

Knew what was coming.

My feet moved.

Almost.

Acid sprayed across my coat sleeve.

Heat arrived immediately.

I shouted and ripped the arm free before the cloth could stick.

Jorren's sword came down sideways into the damaged seam.

Again.

The saltback collapsed.

We stood over it breathing.

My sleeve smoked.

Jorren looked at the creature.

Then at me.

"Your cut is stupid."

"It worked."

"You hit the ceiling first."

"Different room."

"Then you almost got sprayed."

I pulled the ruined coat off and threw it onto the stone.

The acid kit Sella made me buy came out of my pocket.

I poured neutralizing powder over the sleeve.

Four copper suddenly seemed reasonable.

"Recovery," I said.

"What?"

"The cut is fine. The recovery is bad."

Jorren stared at me.

"There are two more down here."

Right.

Lesson later.

Monster now.

The second saltback was smaller and smarter.

It never gave me the stairwell.

Jorren killed it.

That mattered.

I helped by keeping its attention on the side with more room and by shouting when the sacs tightened. I did not land the finishing blow. I did not need to.

The third was not there.

Good.

We checked anyway.

Twenty minutes later we came back into daylight carrying two acid glands in sealed jars and smelling like mineral rot.

The bathhouse owner counted five silver onto Sella's counter.

I watched two and a half move to Jorren.

Four copper had already gone to the acid kit.

My coat was ruined.

The waxed hand wrap needed replacing.

My wrist hurt from striking the ceiling.

Net profit remained positive.

Adventure was less profitable after contact with arithmetic.

Sella looked at the torn sleeve.

"Kit?"

"Used it."

She nodded once.

No congratulations.

Good.

Jorren left to meet the caravan quartermaster.

His life continued.

I stood at the counter with my share of the silver and found myself looking toward the training yard.

Overhead cut.

Side step.

One had killed a monster.

The other had kept acid off my chest.

A month ago both had been ideas.

Now they were things my body could do.

Not perfectly.

Enough to matter.

That felt different from remembering.

It felt owned.

Sella slid a folded note across the counter.

"This came for you."

Arlo's handwriting.

I opened it.

**STOP BUYING SHALE. COME SEE THIS.**

I smiled.

Sella looked at the expression and sighed.

"What now?"

"Potentially expensive progress."

"You say that like it's good."

I put the two silver in my pocket.

My burned coat smelled worse every second.

My wrist hurt.

I needed to practice recovery.

Antonius expected money in two days.

Arlo had apparently done something without me.

Jorren would be gone north next week.

Sella had stepped around a padded stick twice this month and somehow made that irritating.

The world had moved while I was busy learning two things.

Good.

I went to see what it had done.
