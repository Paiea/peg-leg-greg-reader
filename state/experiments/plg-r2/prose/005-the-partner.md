# Chapter 5: The Partner

Arlo had sold one.

That was the first thing he told me.

I had not yet taken off my ruined coat.

"You what?"

"Sold one."

He pointed at the bench.

A white-gray ceramic disk sat beside a shallow bowl of purple liquid.

Cleaner liquid filled another bowl.

Not clear.

Cleaner.

The distinction had acquired money.

"To who?"

"Dyer on Mill Street."

"For how much?"

"Eight copper."

I stared at him.

"Eight."

"Copper."

"I heard the unit."

"Then why are you repeating it?"

Because forty years later people bought improved versions by the crate.

Because I had borrowed money to fund this.

Because I had spent more than eight copper moving shale I did not need.

Because somewhere in my head I had already constructed a manufacturing chain, licensing structure, supplier agreement, and three reasons Antonius Vale should not be allowed anywhere near any of it.

Arlo had sold a disk for eight copper while I was in a basement hitting a saltback.

"Why?"

"Because she wanted one."

"Why only eight?"

"Because it costs less than two to make and she doesn't know if it will survive a week."

"Did you tell her what it does?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"All of what?"

I stopped.

Good question.

We did not know all of it.

We knew the latest mix pulled more residue from a cheap wash than the control. We knew it did the same thing on three batches. We knew Arlo had changed the clay body, grain size, and firing cycle enough that my original memory was now mostly a direction the project had once traveled through.

We did not know durability.

We did not know scaling.

We did not know whether the effect mattered enough outside this one process to create the future market I remembered.

Arlo had eight copper.

I had theories.

"Show me."

He did.

That was another difference from the first week.

He no longer explained every movement.

He expected me to keep up where I could and ask when I could not.

The new disk was thinner.

The surface looked rougher.

"You made it worse," I said.

"Visually."

"On purpose?"

"I stopped trying to make the ceramic dense."

"Because surface area."

"Because flow. Surface area came with it."

Different route.

Same destination, maybe.

He poured the purple wash.

The liquid passed through slowly.

Too slowly for the later filters I remembered.

The bowl beneath filled with a paler version.

"How much?"

Arlo handed me a page.

Numbers.

Actual numbers.

I had liked numbers more when they belonged to campaigns and other people's payrolls.

"Twenty-seven percent?"

"On this wash."

"Repeatable?"

"Four times."

"And the dyer?"

"Brought the wash yesterday. Took a disk this morning. If it doesn't crack, she wants six more."

Six.

Not a kingdom.

Not an industry.

Six disks.

I smiled.

Arlo saw it.

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're doing in your head."

"Scaling."

"No."

"You don't know what I was going to say."

"Factory."

I shut my mouth.

He pointed at the ruined sleeve.

"What happened?"

"Saltback."

"Why were you fighting saltbacks?"

"Money."

Arlo looked around his workshop.

"You know we are trying to make money here."

"Eventually."

"I made eight copper today."

"I made two and a half silver."

"How much did the coat cost?"

I looked at the sleeve.

"Irrelevant."

"How much?"

"I already owned it."

"That is not an answer."

I liked him considerably less.

He had acquired confidence while I was gone.

Good.

Probably.

"What do you want?" I asked.

"For the disk?"

"For this."

I gestured between the bench, shale samples, kiln, bowls, notes, and the future I could almost see sitting behind all of it.

Arlo went quiet.

That was the first time I realized we had never actually discussed ownership.

I had funded tests.

I had brought the material and the original hypothesis.

Arlo had done almost all the work.

He had changed the process.

He had found the first customer.

I had assumed the business arrangement would become obvious later.

Business arrangements were famously good at becoming obvious on their own.

"I want my workshop," Arlo said.

"You have it."

"Mine."

Ah.

"I wasn't planning to take it."

"You plan everything."

"That's unfair."

"You bought a cart of shale before one test worked."

Fair.

"I mean I own the process," Arlo said. "My mixes. My firing schedule. My tools. If this becomes something, I don't become your employee because you remembered a rock."

There it was.

A month ago I might have been offended.

Current me was offended too, but with better supporting arguments.

"I paid for the first tests."

"Yes."

"And the materials."

"Some."

"Most."

"At first."

He pointed toward a new measuring glass I had not bought.

"I paid for that. I paid for the last clay. I found the dyer."

"With money from repairs."

"My repairs."

"I'm agreeing with you."

"You look like you're negotiating."

"I am always negotiating."

"That's the problem."

Maybe.

I sat on the edge of the second stool.

One of the legs had been repaired.

Not by me.

Another change.

"Fine. You own the process."

Arlo's expression changed.

He had expected resistance.

That pleased me more than it should have.

"I get what?" I asked.

There it was again.

He relaxed by half an inch.

"You get your investment back first."

"With return."

"Reasonable return."

"Define reasonable."

"No."

"Good instinct."

"Greg."

"Fine. Keep going."

"Then a share on this formulation if it sells. Not my whole workshop. Not anything else I invent. This."

He tapped the disk.

That was better than what I had been about to propose.

I disliked that.

Also good.

"How much share?"

We spent twenty minutes insulting each other with percentages.

At the end we had something neither of us loved.

That was usually evidence of a functioning agreement.

Arlo wrote it down.

I read every line.

He watched me.

"You trust me?"

"Yes."

"You read that like you don't."

"Those are separate systems."

He sighed.

I signed.

The project changed shape with the ink.

Not mine.

Not his alone.

A thing between us with boundaries.

That felt more real than the cart of shale ever had.

Then Arlo said, "Dyer wants six by next week."

"Can you make six?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"If I don't repair lamps for two days."

There was the cost.

"How much do the repairs make?"

He told me.

The six disks would not beat it by enough.

"Charge more."

"On an unproven product?"

"Yes."

"She'll say no."

"Then make four."

"She asked for six."

"Ask what she actually needs."

Arlo looked at me.

That one landed.

Good.

"Four now," I said. "If they work, the next six cost more."

"Why would she agree?"

"Because if she needs six, she needs four more than she currently has."

He thought about it.

"That's irritating."

"Thank you."

"Not a compliment."

"Still useful."

We had become partners approximately twelve minutes earlier and already disliked parts of each other's expertise.

Promising.

I left with less control over the filtration project and more confidence in it.

That was new.

Antonius was due tomorrow.

That was not.

My share of the saltback contract covered most of what I owed.

Most was a terrible amount of debt to have.

I could ask for another day.

I could work the difference.

I could sell something.

I could gamble.

The last option arrived wearing a clean shirt and carrying excellent references.

I was good at it.

Actually good.

The last month had proved that.

I had lost money too.

Also proved.

The White Dog sat between Arlo's workshop and my room if I took a slightly dishonest route.

I took it.

The front tables were half full.

I recognized three people.

None knew my future.

That remained refreshing.

I bought in with enough to solve the debt and not enough to feel comfortable.

That was intentional.

For the first hour, the plan worked.

I watched.

Folded.

Lost two small hands.

Won one larger pot when a dockworker tried to bluff after being laughed at.

Won another because the woman across from me protected winnings and stopped calling marginal bets once she was ahead.

People were systems with opinions about being called systems.

The trick was not believing they were simple ones.

By the second hour I had enough.

Debt solved.

Food for several days.

Maybe a little yard time.

I stacked the coins once.

Then looked at the next hand.

Bad cards.

I folded.

Good.

Next hand.

Interesting table behavior.

The man two seats over had changed his bet sizing after losing twice. Was he chasing or manufacturing the appearance of chasing?

I watched.

One hand.

He lost small.

Next hand, same pattern.

I stayed.

The debt remained solved.

I knew I should leave.

I also almost understood him.

There are thoughts you recognize as stupid while still admiring their construction.

This was one.

I stayed another hand.

He trapped me.

Not brilliantly.

I had simply wanted the model to finish more than I wanted the money to remain mine.

I lost a third of the profit.

Now the debt was still solved.

Barely.

That should have been the end.

Then I understood what he had done.

Not chasing.

He increased his opening bets after losing because he knew the table expected emotion. He was selling the story of a man tilting.

Good.

I wanted one hand against the updated model.

That sentence cost me another four copper.

I stood.

Finally.

The man looked up.

"Done?"

"Yes."

"You sure?"

I was not.

That made leaving feel better.

Outside, I counted what remained.

Enough for Antonius.

Almost nothing afterward.

I laughed once.

A month of being smarter had produced a more sophisticated version of the same idiot.

Progress.

I paid Antonius the next morning.

He counted the coins.

"On time."

"You sound disappointed."

"I had work for you."

"Next mistake."

His eyes lifted.

"You planning one?"

"Historically."

He finished counting.

"Balance cleared."

The words felt good.

Clean.

I should have gone directly to the yard afterward.

Instead I stopped at a smith.

My cheap sword had a fresh mark near the upper third from the cellar ceiling.

Not dangerous.

Probably.

The grip was still narrow.

The balance still dragged forward.

I had learned enough in a month to feel exactly how bad it was.

That was unfortunate.

The smith handed me three replacements.

First too heavy.

Second decent.

Third balanced beautifully.

I raised it.

Overhead cut.

The blade stopped where I wanted.

No fight against the wrist.

No correction at the end.

My body had spent a month learning one line, and for the first time the weapon followed that line instead of making me bargain with it.

"How much?"

The smith told me.

No.

Absolutely not.

I had just cleared Antonius.

I needed food.

Training.

Mana work.

My coat needed replacing.

Arlo would probably discover another expensive question by lunch.

I put the sword down.

Then picked it up again.

Quality mattered.

That was true.

A better-balanced weapon reduced bad compensation.

Also true.

I was practicing hundreds of repetitions. Small mechanical differences multiplied.

True.

Buying the correct tool early could save months of learning around the wrong one.

Very true.

I had enough money if I was willing to redefine enough.

That was where the argument became suspicious.

I bought the sword.

Walking home, I developed an excellent explanation for the purchase.

By the second street, I almost believed it.

By the third, I remembered I needed breakfast.

I checked my purse.

One silver.

Four copper.

A new sword.

No debt.

An ownership share in a filtration process that had earned eight copper.

A healing acid mark on my forearm.

One overhead cut.

One side step.

And, apparently, the financial instincts of a man who had once been rich enough to forget what breakfast cost.

The second life was going extremely well.
