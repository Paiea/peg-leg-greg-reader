# CHAPTER 239

## THE DISTRIBUTOR

The new beans were gone when I woke.

I noticed because they had spent the previous day traveling farther than I had without becoming any more informative.

The brown cloth was still near the wall.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf with three loose threads, which made it the most stable project in the apartment.

Lyssa was gone.

No note under the door.

No theatre coat on the chair.

No paper from Hessa.

No Vale clerk waiting in the room with a book large enough to ruin breakfast.

I ate bread.

The last onion remained untouched.

It had survived through discipline and cowardice.

Someone knocked before I finished.

I looked at the door.

The door had not been part of my plan.

The knock came again.

I got the crutches, crossed the room, and opened it.

A boy stood on the landing with a stack of folded handbills under one arm.

Not Olin's boy.

Younger than Davin, older than a child who should have been trusted with that much paper.

He looked at me, then at my crutches.

"Greg?"

"Unfortunately."

"Rinna says if you're free, come before midday."

I waited.

He waited.

"That's all?"

He looked down at the top handbill as though more instructions might have been printed there.

"She said if you're free."

"Not dying?"

"No."

"Building on fire?"

"No."

"Someone missing?"

"No."

"Excellent message."

He frowned.

"She said you'd ask things."

That sounded like Rinna.

I took one of the handbills from the stack.

He pulled it back.

"Those aren't yours."

"Good boundary."

He left.

I closed the door.

The morning had acquired a shape.

Not an emergency.

Not a mystery.

Work, probably.

I finished breakfast, drank water, and left the onion alive.

Outside, Carrow had the washed-blue sky that made every roofline look sharper than it was. A wagon full of split wood occupied most of the lower lane. Two women were already arguing with the driver about whether most of the lane meant all of it.

Nobody needed me.

I went around.

My shoulders felt normal after yesterday's market. Right hand normal. Neck fine. Residual limb comfortable. Left crutch tip held.

The theatre side door was open when I arrived.

Inside, Rinna had turned one table into paper.

Handbills in stacks.

Handbills folded.

Handbills not folded.

A bowl of paste that nobody seemed to be using.

Three short lengths of string.

One pencil.

Marek sat on the edge of the stage folding paper badly enough that the corners never met.

Nessa was doing something with a dark green coat near the rear passage.

Jori and Davin were carrying a narrow door frame across the stage.

Teren was nowhere visible.

Rinna looked up.

"Free?"

"Until somebody improves my life."

"Good."

"I hate that word."

"Five places."

She pointed at five stacks.

"Cookshop near South Steps. Cobbler on Red Lane. Lamp-glass woman. The little tea room by the old fountain. Bread stall at West Gate."

"Handbills."

"Remarkable."

"How many?"

"Five each."

Marek held up a folded bill.

"Unless they love theatre."

Rinna said, "Five."

Marek lowered it.

I looked at the stacks.

"The boy brought these?"

"He brought some. You're taking the rest."

"Why me?"

"Because Davin and Jori have the door. Nessa has coats. Hara and Pell are with Teren. Marek has north."

Marek said, "I have the better route."

"You have the shorter route," Rinna said.

"Better."

Rinna ignored him.

"Ask before you leave them. If they say no, bring them back."

"Do I explain the plays?"

"If they ask."

"Do I promise anything?"

"No."

"Do I argue?"

"No."

"Do I perform in the shop?"

Rinna looked at me.

Marek said, "Yes."

Rinna said, "No."

I took the stacks.

Twenty-five sheets weighed almost nothing.

Twenty-five sheets plus two crutches still required somewhere to put them.

Rinna slid a narrow strap bag across the table.

"Use that."

I looked at her.

"You own a bag."

"We own many things."

"Somebody should tell Nessa."

Nessa, from the rear passage, said, "I know every bag."

Of course she did.

The strap sat high enough across my chest that the paper rested against my right side without catching the crutch. I adjusted it once.

Rinna watched.

"Works?"

"Yes."

"Bring the bag back."

There it was.

The real responsibility.

The first cookshop took five.

The woman behind the counter read the top line, looked at me, and said, "Last one brought people who ordered water."

"I apologize for the arts."

"They bought soup after."

"Then we saved the economy."

She put the handbills near the door.

I left before the claim could be examined.

The cobbler on Red Lane took three.

I had counted five onto the counter.

He pushed two back.

"Three."

"Rinna said five."

"My wall says three."

I looked at the wall.

The wall already held a wrestling notice, two room advertisements, a lost-dog sketch, a church supper announcement, and something written in letters so faded I could no longer tell what had once been urgent.

"Strong authority."

"Three."

I put two back in the bag.

The route had changed already.

Good.

Not everything became more convenient because I had done it once.

At the tea room, I made my mistake.

The owner said yes while carrying a tray.

I reached into the bag, counted five against my thumb, set them on the side table, and moved away so she could pass.

A girl sweeping near the wall looked at the stack.

"Seven."

I stopped.

"What?"

"Seven."

I counted.

Seven.

Apparently my thumb had become generous.

The owner did not stop moving.

The girl handed me two.

"Rinna will be devastated."

"Who?"

"Correct."

I put the extras away.

The girl resumed sweeping.

Useful correction.

No ceremony.

By the time I reached West Gate, the bread stall had no room.

Not no room metaphorically.

The seller had hung strings of small notices from two poles, and every available section fluttered with rooms, work, performances, sales, missing things, found things, lessons, debts, and one very aggressive drawing of a goat.

He looked at my handbills.

"No."

"Five?"

"No."

"Three?"

"No."

"One heroic handbill?"

He pointed at the goat.

"What am I supposed to cover?"

The goat did have presence.

I put the handbills back in the bag.

"Fair."

I had one stop left.

Lamp glass.

The shop was narrow, bright in the dangerous way of rooms where most surfaces could cut you, and warmer than the street. Small panes leaned in racks along one wall. Chimneys, globes, and shaped pieces sat behind the counter.

The woman behind it looked up when I entered.

Her eyes went to the bag.

"Theatre?"

"Handbills."

"How many?"

"Five unless your wall has a goat."

She looked toward the front window.

"No goat."

"Excellent."

She came around the counter and pointed at a board beside the door.

"Bottom."

I put five there.

I was straightening the stack when she said, "Wait."

I stopped.

Not fear.

Not yet anything.

Just a word.

She looked past me through the front glass, then back.

"For Rinna."

I took my hand off the handbills.

"What?"

"A man came yesterday."

Yesterday.

After Lyssa and I had bought oil from Olin, maybe before, maybe while I was sleeping in a chair.

The ordinary day remained ordinary.

This was new.

"What man?"

"Brown coat. Dark hair. Narrow face."

My attention tightened.

"Quiet voice?"

"Yes."

"Did you know him?"

"No."

"Did he give a name?"

"No."

She folded her arms.

"He asked about the theatre."

I did not ask what I wanted to ask first.

Bren.

Mason's Cut.

Cart.

Money.

Instead I heard Rinna asking a boy for exact words.

"What did he ask?"

The woman thought.

"First if I supplied the hall."

"What did you say?"

"That I sell glass."

Good answer.

"Then?"

"He asked, 'Do they take deliveries through the front or the back?'"

I repeated it.

"Do they take deliveries through the front or the back?"

"Yes."

"Exact?"

"As exact as I remember."

"Anything else?"

"He asked who opens for deliveries."

That was more.

My hands tightened slightly on the grips.

"What did you say?"

"That if he needed glass, I could sell him glass."

I almost smiled.

"Did he buy any?"

"No."

"Did he threaten you?"

"No."

"Offer money for an answer?"

"No."

"Ask when the theatre is empty?"

"No."

"Ask about Rinna's money?"

"No."

"Ask about the cart?"

"No."

"Say Bren?"

"No."

She watched me.

"Should he have?"

"No."

The answer came faster than I intended.

I corrected it.

"I don't know."

Better.

She nodded once.

"Tell Rinna."

"I will."

I looked at the board of handbills.

One corner of the top sheet had curled.

I pressed it flat.

The woman said, "Five is fine."

It took me a moment to understand.

"Right."

Theatre.

Handbills.

The job still existed.

I left.

Outside, the street had not changed because one man had asked a better question.

A porter was unloading two crates of clay lamp bases.

A woman was trying to persuade a child to wear both shoes.

Someone farther down the lane shouted about fish.

I stood against the wall for a moment and considered going directly back.

Then I looked at the bag.

Two extra from the cobbler.

Five refused by the bread stall.

The route was not finished merely because something more interesting had happened.

That was an irritatingly useful fact.

I went back to the theatre after finishing the loop I had already finished, which was to say I walked there carrying seven handbills nobody wanted.

My right palm was warm. Shoulders worked. Nothing unusual.

Marek arrived from the north at nearly the same time.

His bag was empty.

I looked at it.

"Nobody refused?"

"One place took eight."

"Rinna said five."

"They asked."

"Did you invent demand?"

"They wanted more."

I could not prove otherwise.

Inside, Jori and Davin had the narrow door frame upright.

It was not attached to anything.

Marek looked at it.

"Door."

Jori said, "Frame."

"Door soon."

"Maybe."

Marek nodded as if the distinction had been settled in his favor.

Rinna was at the paper table.

I put the bag down.

"Seven back."

She looked at me.

"Seven?"

"Red Lane took three. West Gate said no."

"Why?"

"Goat."

Rinna waited.

"Wall full."

"Better."

I slid the unused bills onto the return stack.

Then I said, "Lamp glass had a message."

Her hand stopped.

Not dramatically.

Enough.

She took the pencil.

"Tell me."

I did.

Brown coat.

Dark hair.

Narrow face.

Quiet voice.

Unknown man.

No name.

Asked whether she supplied the hall.

Asked, exact as remembered: "Do they take deliveries through the front or the back?"

Then asked who opens for deliveries.

No purchase.

No threat.

No offered money.

No question about empty hours.

No cart question.

No money question.

No Bren.

Rinna wrote the wording down.

Marek had stopped pretending not to listen.

Nessa came closer from the rear passage, still holding the green coat.

Jori did not. He was checking the frame.

Davin held it steady.

The theatre did not become a meeting because information had entered the building.

Rinna read what she had written.

"Description consistent with Olin's."

I said, "Not confirmed."

"No."

"Question is new."

"Yes."

Marek said, "Front or back."

Rinna looked at him.

"Yes."

"That's not money."

"No."

"That's doors."

"Excellent work."

Marek smiled.

Then stopped when he realized she had not meant it as praise.

I said, "It adds access."

Rinna looked at me.

I tried again.

"It adds a question about delivery access."

"Better."

Annoying.

Accurate.

Nessa set the green coat on the table edge.

"Did she answer?"

"No."

"Good."

Rinna wrote one more line.

NO USEFUL ANSWER GIVEN.

Nessa said, "We already tell deliveries where to come."

Rinna nodded.

"We keep doing that."

Marek frowned.

"Nothing changes?"

Rinna looked around the theatre.

"Something already changed. We know another question."

"That's it?"

"For now."

I understood the disappointment.

A pattern had moved.

The body wanted an action to match it.

Lock something.

Move something.

Follow someone.

Invent a guard.

Go find Mason's Cut and stare at every brown coat until the city became evidence.

Rinna capped the pencil.

Marek frowned.

"So nothing changes?"

"Not because he asked a question."

Nessa set the green coat on the table edge.

"Back latch sticks."

Rinna looked at her.

"Still?"

"Still."

"Then fix the latch."

Jori said from the stage, "After frame."

That was the right size of reaction. A stranger had asked about doors. The theatre still had one door that actually stuck.

The theatre had acquired one more fact about an unknown man and still had a bad latch.

Both could be true.

I helped with the frame.

Not because the pressure demanded labor.

Because the frame needed holding while Davin changed the lower brace.

Jori had one side. I took the other from a seated position near the stage edge, keeping the weight where my shoulders could manage it without turning the crutches into furniture.

Davin loosened two screws, shifted the brace, and said, "Now."

Jori moved.

I held.

Marek offered advice.

Nobody accepted it.

The frame settled square.

Davin checked the lower corner.

Jori checked the top.

They disagreed by less than a finger.

They adjusted it anyway.

Nessa carried the green coat past us.

"Tomorrow."

I looked at her.

"What?"

She stopped.

"You're wearing this tomorrow."

I looked at the coat.

Dark green.

Short enough not to reach my knees.

"Why?"

"Teren."

That explained nothing.

Rinna, from the table, said, "Pages."

She held up a folded set.

I released the frame when Jori told me to.

Then I crossed to her.

The top page had a role marked in the margin.

MESSENGER.

I looked at Rinna.

"That's insulting."

"You brought a message."

"Once."

"Today."

"That is how names start."

Marek said, "Message man."

I pointed at him.

"No."

Rinna handed me the pages.

"Tomorrow morning. Rehearsal first. Performance if Teren keeps it paired."

"How many lines?"

"Eight."

"Entrances?"

"Three."

"Running?"

"No."

"Good."

Rinna looked at me.

I sighed.

"Still hate it."

The piece was called The Missing Key.

I did not read farther while standing.

That was how mistakes began.

I put the pages in the now-empty handbill bag.

Rinna took the bag back out.

"Mine."

I held the pages.

Of course.

At the end of the afternoon she paid me the normal copper.

Handbills and frame work had apparently become a day.

I put the coin away.

No debt calculation followed.

The debt remained smaller than it had once been and larger than I wanted it.

Outside, Marek walked with me for half a street.

"Back door."

"Question about back or front."

"Same thing."

"No."

He thought.

"Mostly same."

"No."

"Annoying."

"Yes."

He turned north.

I went home.

The apartment was empty.

The new beans were still gone.

The brown cloth remained.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf.

Three loose threads.

No one had repaired my life while I was distributing paper.

I set the pages from The Missing Key on the table.

Eight lines.

Three entrances.

Messenger.

I read them once while bread warmed near the pan.

Then again because the second entrance came sooner than I expected.

No magic.

No Vale.

No answer about the man.

Lyssa returned after I had eaten half my bread.

She had no beans.

No bundle either.

She looked at the pages.

"Theatre?"

"Tomorrow."

"Today?"

"Paper."

She looked at the handbills I was not carrying.

"Successful?"

"Seven survived."

"Poor things."

I told her I had a role tomorrow.

"Messenger."

She looked at me.

I waited.

She did not say oil man.

I respected her restraint.

Then she said, "Fits."

"Relationship damaged."

She took bread.

I looked at the brown cloth.

Still there.

Then at the pages.

Lyssa took another piece of bread and leaned over the first page.

"Eight?"

"Eight."

She handed it back.

The beans stayed wherever tomorrow had put them.
