# CHAPTER 245

## THE SPENDER

The right crutch grip had turned.

Not much.

Enough.

I noticed it because the seam that normally sat along the underside had moved against the base of my thumb sometime between yesterday's wall and this morning's bread.

I turned the crutch in my hand.

The grip did not move.

Only the wrapping had.

This was better than the crutch breaking and worse than the crutch being free.

I pressed the edge with my thumb.

No tear.

No exposed wood.

No sharp tack.

The wrap had simply shifted around the handle and tightened unevenly.

Ordinary wear.

I hated ordinary wear.

It had no villain.

Lyssa was already gone.

The dark cloth from last night was not on the table. The brown cloth was.

Three pins.

Same fold.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf.

Three loose threads.

My better shirt had a blue paint streak on the sleeve because I had spent yesterday improving theatre property by damaging my own.

I wore it anyway.

A blue streak was not structural.

I had learned that word.

I ate bread, drank water, and checked the grip again as though a second inspection might produce a different economy.

It did not.

The right handle felt slightly thicker on one side and slightly thinner on the other.

No pain.

No weakness.

Right palm normal after sleep.

Shoulders normal.

Right leg normal.

Residual limb comfortable.

Left tip boring.

The crutch worked.

I could ignore it.

That was how maintenance became repair.

I took both crutches and left.

The repairer's shop was open.

He was working on somebody else's walking stick when I arrived, which offended me in a small private way.

I had become accustomed to shops existing specifically at the moment I needed them.

The man behind the counter sat with the stick across his knees. A brass-colored ferrule lay beside him. He was shaving a thin curl from the end of the wood with a small knife.

He looked up.

Then at my crutches.

"Tip?"

"Grip."

He put the knife down.

"Which?"

"Right."

He pointed at the bench.

"Sit."

This relationship had not grown warmer.

It had grown shorter.

I sat.

The other customer was an older man in a dark cap who stood near the door with his hands folded over his stomach. He looked at the walking stick, not at me.

The repairer finished the shaving, fitted the ferrule, twisted it once, removed it, shaved another sliver, then fitted it again.

The older man said, "Too tight?"

"Was."

"Now?"

The repairer knocked the ferrule once against the heel of his hand.

"Now not."

Strong sales language.

He fixed it in place, handed the stick over, and made the man test it on the floor.

The man leaned on it.

Turned.

Tapped it twice.

"Good."

"Pay."

I liked this shop.

The older man paid and left.

The repairer looked at me.

"Right."

I handed him the right crutch.

He turned it over once.

Then twice.

His thumb found the shifted seam immediately.

"You twist."

"I walk."

"You twist too."

"Accusation."

"Grip."

He held the crutch horizontally and showed me where the wrap had crept.

The material around the handle was dark and smooth from my palm. One edge had shifted far enough that the overlap no longer sat evenly beneath the wood.

"Replace?" I asked.

"No."

That improved my mood.

"Why not?"

"Still good."

That improved it further.

He picked at the end of the wrap with a blunt tool.

The material lifted.

"Loose here. Tight here."

He pointed to opposite sides.

"Take it off?"

"Part."

"How much is part?"

He looked at me.

"Enough."

Apparently Jori had relatives.

He loosened the outer turns without unwrapping the entire handle, pulled the material flat, then worked it back around with even tension.

I watched for maybe thirty seconds before deciding I understood the process.

This was false.

"Could I do that next time?"

"Yes."

I waited.

He kept working.

"That sounded too easy."

"It is easy."

"Then why am I paying you?"

"Because you brought it here."

Fair.

He pressed the seam down with his thumb and reached for a small tack.

I looked at the tack.

"That was there before?"

"No."

"Why now?"

"Because it moved."

"Is the tack going into my hand?"

"No."

"Useful standard."

He set it where the underside overlap met the wood, well away from where my palm closed, then drove it flush with two light taps.

He ran his thumb over it.

Then mine.

"Feel?"

I rubbed the underside.

The tack was there if I searched for it.

Not if I gripped normally.

"No."

"Good."

He handed the crutch back.

I closed my right hand around it.

The handle felt narrower than it had this morning.

Not actually narrower.

Even.

My palm noticed the change because it had spent too many hours learning the wrong one.

"Stand."

I stood.

Both tips on the floor.

Weight through the grips.

No slip.

No edge under the thumb.

I took two steps.

Turned.

Took two back.

The repairer watched my right hand rather than my leg.

I appreciated the accuracy.

"Turn again."

"Which way?"

"Both."

I turned left.

Then right.

The wrap stayed where it belonged.

"Fine," he said.

"I enjoy how moved you are."

"Other one."

I looked at the left grip.

"What about it?"

"Give."

I handed him the left crutch.

He checked the wrap.

Pressed the seam.

Twisted the handle in both directions.

Then handed it back.

"Fine."

"Should we do that one too?"

"No."

"For balance?"

"No."

"Matching?"

"No."

He looked almost offended.

"If good, leave."

That was a cruel policy for a man who took money for repairs.

I looked at the right grip.

"How long will it last?"

He shrugged.

"Until it doesn't."

Hessa and this man should never meet.

Or perhaps they had and the universe had wisely kept it from me.

I paid him.

The amount was ordinary.

The loss felt personal.

Not because it was large.

Because it had been money before I woke and was now grip.

Necessary grip.

Still grip.

I put the remaining coins away.

I had bought no new ability. The crutch did not become faster. I did not become taller. I had paid to make one irritating seam return to where it used to be.

Maintenance had terrible marketing.

The repairer had already picked up another piece of work from behind the counter.

No farewell.

I left.

Outside, the right grip felt too new for perhaps half a street.

Then my hand stopped noticing.

That was what I had paid for.

Carrow was brighter than yesterday.

The rain had cleaned nothing important.

Mud remained in the joints between stones. Wagon wheels had redistributed it. The air smelled of damp wood, cooking fat, and something sharp from a dyer's lane I did not take.

I had no theatre call.

Rinna had said not for me yet.

No Hessa note.

No reason to invent one.

Vale existed.

The debt also existed.

I had just spent money maintaining the thing that allowed me to move around the city and earn more money.

This was probably defensible.

Antonius would still prefer the money.

I stood at the next corner longer than necessary.

Home was one direction.

Vale's office was another.

I had not been promised work.

That mattered.

I went to Vale anyway.

Not because the repair had ruined me.

It had not.

Because debt did not become less real on days when nobody summoned me.

The office door was open.

The ink-fingered clerk was at the front desk with three stacks of paper arranged around him.

He looked up when I entered.

Then looked past me.

"No bundle."

"I did not bring one."

"No, ours."

"Ah."

He went back to the papers.

I waited.

He looked up again.

"What?"

"Work?"

"No."

That had been fast.

"None?"

"None for you."

Important distinction.

I looked at the three stacks.

"Those appear extremely work-shaped."

"They are."

"And yet."

"And yet."

I was beginning to suspect every profession in Carrow shared one teacher.

"What are those?"

"Accounts."

"I have handled accounts."

"These need decisions."

"I can make decisions."

"That is the problem."

I leaned on the repaired right grip.

It did not move.

"What is suitable?"

"Nothing today."

No apology.

No promise about tomorrow.

No attempt to create work because I had arrived with debt.

Correct.

Still annoying.

"What about outgoing packets?"

"Done."

"Returned acknowledgments?"

"Done."

"Sealing?"

"Tomorrow, maybe. Not today."

There it was.

Maybe.

The most expensive word in Carrow.

I looked toward the rear.

Antonius was not visible.

"Is he here?"

"Yes."

"Does he have work?"

The clerk looked at me.

"For you?"

"That is usually the relevant category."

"No."

I nodded.

Then stayed where I was.

The clerk finished a line.

Dipped his pen.

Wrote another.

Eventually he said, "Standing there doesn't create it."

"I was testing."

"Result?"

"Negative."

"Good."

I hated that word.

I turned to leave.

"Greg."

I looked back.

The clerk pointed at my right crutch.

"New?"

"Adjusted."

"Tip?"

"Grip."

He nodded.

That was all.

Apparently the office kept its interest in my equipment at the level of whether I could continue being useful.

Consistent.

Outside again, I had accomplished something important.

I had confirmed I could not earn debt credit today.

This felt like paying for information.

I had not paid for the information.

Small victory.

My stomach interrupted before I could develop a philosophy around unemployment.

I walked toward the cookshop district.

Not the theatre cookshop.

Another one.

I had eaten there once before, far enough back that I could not remember what I had ordered and the woman behind the counter did not remember me at all.

Healthy relationship.

The board listed stew, beans, and a meat pie that cost more than I wanted it to.

I looked at the pie.

Then at the stew.

Then at my money.

The grip repair had already happened.

The debt still existed.

Hunger also existed.

Three systems.

Only one could be solved with stew.

I bought stew.

The woman put it in a heavy bowl and pointed to an open place near the wall.

I sat.

A laborer across from me had sawdust on both sleeves and one red mark across his forehead where something had rested there.

He ate without speaking.

I respected him.

For almost five minutes, nothing happened.

Nobody asked me to perform.

Nobody asked me to test magic.

Nobody asked me to compare account lines.

Nobody asked whether the theatre used its front or back door.

I ate stew.

It was too salty.

I finished it anyway.

The laborer left before me.

A woman took his place, put a basket under the table, and began eating beans.

Carrow replaced people quickly when seats were useful.

I paid attention to the right grip when I stood.

Still flat.

Still even.

The tack did not touch my hand.

The repair was becoming invisible.

Good repair.

I left the cookshop with less money and more body.

That was the entire exchange.

On the way home I passed the lane toward the theatre.

I did not take it.

No call.

No maybe transformed by proximity.

Not for me yet had been clear.

I kept walking.

A handcart blocked half the street near a cooper's yard while two men rolled a barrel toward it.

I waited because there was nowhere sensible to go.

One of the men said, "Other side."

The other said, "I know."

"You are on this side."

"I know where I am."

They moved the barrel.

I passed.

Carrow remained capable of operating without me.

At home, the room was still empty.

The brown cloth remained in the same place.

Three pins.

Same fold.

The dark cloth from last night was still absent.

That established only absence.

My damaged shirt remained on the shelf.

Three loose threads.

My better shirt still had blue paint on the sleeve.

I took it off.

Looked at the paint.

Considered rubbing it.

Did not.

Growth through laziness.

I changed into the damaged shirt carefully enough not to pull the cuff.

Three threads remained three.

I set the better shirt on the chair.

Then I counted my money.

Not the debt.

My money.

Repair.

Stew.

What remained.

The one copper from yesterday had not become a debt payment.

It had joined the rest and then helped pay for ordinary life.

This was not failure.

It was also not progress against Antonius.

Both could be true without producing a speech.

I put the coins away.

The right crutch leaned against the wall.

I looked at the grip.

For a moment I could see the new tack because I knew exactly where it was.

Then I stopped looking.

Lyssa came back near dusk.

No dark cloth.

No bundle.

She carried two pieces of fried dough wrapped in a clean cloth.

This was immediately more relevant.

She put them on the table.

Looked at me.

Then at the shirt I was wearing.

"That one?"

"Temporary."

"Why?"

I pointed at the chair.

Blue streak.

She looked at it.

Then at me.

"You didn't rub it."

"I have matured."

"No."

"Correct."

She sat.

I reached for the cloth.

Lyssa slapped my hand once.

Not hard.

"Mine."

I withdrew.

"Hostile household."

She unwrapped one piece of fried dough.

Then the other.

Then pushed the cloth toward the middle.

Shared, apparently.

I took one.

She noticed the right crutch when I reached for it to shift the chair.

"Grip."

"Yes."

"Bad?"

"Moved."

"Fixed?"

"Adjusted."

"Paid?"

"Yes."

She ate.

"Vale?"

I looked at her.

"How did you get there?"

"You paid."

Fair.

"No work."

She nodded.

No sympathy ceremony.

No question about the balance.

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

She made a face.

"Exactly."

We ate the fried dough.

It had sugar on one side and too much oil on the other.

Excellent.

After a while I said, "The repairer says I twist the right grip."

Lyssa looked at my hands.

"When you turn."

I stopped chewing.

"You knew?"

"I see you walk."

"Why did nobody tell me?"

"Problem?"

"No."

"Then why?"

I looked at the repaired grip.

This household had standards.

I did not like them.

"You could have mentioned it."

"You could have asked."

"I did not know to ask."

"Then not useful."

I considered arguing.

She was already eating again.

I let it go.

Not because she was right.

Because I wanted the last piece of fried dough and conflict had become strategically unsound.

She reached for it first.

Of course.

I looked at her.

She broke it in half.

Gave me the smaller half.

"Generous."

"Eat."

I ate.

The room darkened.

The brown cloth remained folded.

The damaged shirt remained damaged on my body.

The better shirt remained blue on the chair.

The dark cloth remained elsewhere.

My debt remained smaller than it had been and larger than I wanted.

No work had appeared because I needed it.

The right grip held because somebody who knew grips had fixed what was actually wrong and left the other one alone.

By bedtime I had stopped noticing it completely.

That was the most useful thing I bought all day.
