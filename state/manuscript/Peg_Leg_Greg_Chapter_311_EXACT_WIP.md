# CHAPTER 311

## THE RETURN CART

Sevren found me three days later because apparently he still knew how.

I was outside a fruit stall arguing with a pear.

The pear had a dark spot near the stem.

The seller said it was fine.

I said that depended on price.

The seller said one bit for three.

I said I only wanted one.

She said that sounded like my problem.

Then somebody behind me said, “You got worse.”

I turned.

Sevren stood in the lane with a courier bag across his chest and dried mud up both boots.

The bag was not the same one.

Or it was the same one after surviving a small war.

The flap had been replaced with darker leather. One side seam carried two rows of pale stitching. The old brass buckle remained. So did Sevren.

Mostly.

His hair was longer. His face was thinner. There was a healed cut across the bridge of his nose that had not existed the last time I remembered looking closely.

I stared.

He stared back.

The fruit seller held up the pear.

“You buying?”

“No.”

She put it down.

Sevren said, “You lost.”

“I declined.”

“You argued with fruit.”

“I negotiated.”

“You bought nothing.”

“Excellent negotiation.”

He grinned.

That was familiar enough that the lane rearranged around it.

Not literally.

A woman with a basket had to step around both of us because we were standing in the way.

She said so.

We moved.

Sevren looked at my crutches, then my shoulder bag, then me.

Not checking.

Inventory.

“You busy?”

“Currently?”

“Until dark.”

That narrowed the question.

“No.”

“Want a cart seat?”

I looked at him.

“Where?”

“Three Wells.”

I knew the name. East road, beyond the low farms, not far enough to be a journey unless the horse hated everyone.

“Why?”

“Because there is a seat.”

“That is an answer to what.”

“It is the answer I have.”

He shifted the courier bag higher.

“Outbound freight pays the cart. Return freight pays the cart. Front bench has one empty place. Carter says he does not care if you do not touch anything.”

“Do you need me to do anything?”

“No.”

“Carry anything?”

“No.”

“Deliver anything?”

“No.”

“Sign anything?”

“No.”

“Lie to anyone?”

He considered.

“Not currently.”

Better.

I looked east.

The city wall was not visible from this lane. Too many roofs. Too many signs. Too much Carrow between me and outside.

I had left the city since the leg.

Not often.

Once with Sevren, actually.

That had been earlier, when a cart seat had felt like a body problem with scenery attached.

This morning my residual limb felt ordinary. Hands good. Shoulders good. Right leg good. Crutches sound. No rain threatening. No appointment. No work.

One copper and one bit remained upstairs.

Neither had to move.

“What time back?”

“Before dark if the return load is ready.”

“If not?”

“After.”

“That is not a time.”

“It is a road.”

Still him.

I glanced toward home.

Sevren followed the look.

“You need permission?”

“No.”

“Then why did you look?”

“I need water.”

“Reasonable.”

“And bread.”

“Less exciting.”

“And I should tell Cala.”

“There it is.”

“Shut up.”

We walked back together.

Sevren matched my pace without announcing it.

He had learned that months ago.

Maybe longer.

No hovering. No reaching for a crutch. No walking half a step too slowly in a way that made everybody aware he was walking half a step too slowly.

He just shortened.

That was friendship with mileage.

At the house, Cala was cleaning greens at the table.

Tavi was under it.

Of course.

Lyssa was out.

Also normal.

Cala looked at Sevren.

“Road.”

Sevren nodded.

“Unfortunately.”

She looked at me.

“Going?”

“Yes.”

“Back?”

“Before dark if the road obeys him.”

Sevren said, “It will not.”

Cala wrapped two pieces of bread in cloth anyway.

I filled the small water bottle that fit in the shoulder bag.

Tavi emerged from under the table and stared at Sevren’s boots.

“Horse?”

Sevren looked down.

“Probably.”

Tavi pointed.

“Bad.”

“Yes.”

Good conversation.

I checked the bag.

Water.

Bread.

Narrow scrap.

Charcoal.

No fixed holdfast.

The bad tool remained upstairs where expensive decisions belonged.

I put one folded cloth inside too.

Sevren watched.

“For what?”

“Seat.”

He nodded once.

No joke.

That meant he remembered.

The first road cart I had ridden after losing the leg had taught me more about plank benches than any person deserved to know.

We left.

The freight yard had moved.

Not the whole yard.

The entrance.

A new gate had been cut through the east wall of the compound, wide enough for two carts to pass without negotiating ancestry. The old gate still existed twenty yards down and had been half blocked by stacked wheel rims.

I stopped.

Sevren kept going two steps, noticed, and came back.

“What?”

“That was not there.”

“The gate?”

“Yes.”

“Three months.”

I looked at him.

“Three months?”

“Maybe four.”

“How long have you been gone?”

“Not gone.”

“You have not been here.”

“I have been here.”

“Not where I was.”

“That is different.”

Annoyingly true.

Inside the yard, three long carts waited under a roof. Two had high canvas covers. Ours was open, with bundled reed frames tied along one side and six sealed clay jars nested in straw toward the back.

A broad woman in a brown vest stood beside the horse checking a strap.

Sevren pointed.

“Ressa. Cart.”

The woman looked at him.

“Sevren. Noise.”

Good.

Then she looked at me.

“Seat is front. Crutches under, not across.”

“Under what?”

“Bench. Strap there.”

She pointed.

A leather loop had been fixed beneath the front bench, probably for poles, tools, or anything else long enough to become a problem.

I liked Ressa immediately.

“Do you want them from me?”

“No.”

Also good.

I got up myself.

Right foot on the iron step. Left knee clear. Both hands on the cart frame. Push. Turn. Sit.

Cleaner than the old days.

Not graceful.

Grace had no contractual role.

I folded one crutch, then the other as far as their ordinary construction allowed, slid both beneath the bench, and secured the shafts with the leather loop. Tips pointed inward. Handles within reach if I leaned.

Ressa checked the strap.

Not me.

The strap.

“Fine.”

Sevren climbed up beside me.

The bench was narrow.

I took out the folded cloth and put it beneath the back of my left thigh and residual limb so the plank edge did not press where it had no business pressing.

Sevren looked at it.

“You plan now.”

“I suffered before.”

“Same thing, slower.”

Ressa climbed to the driver’s board.

The horse started before anybody made a speech.

Carrow moved past us.

Freight yard.

Potters.

A row of low sheds.

The repaired crossing from weeks ago appeared under the wheels and vanished behind us without comment.

At the east gate a clerk checked the cart board, touched a small brass witness bead beside Ressa’s name, and watched it flash green.

Common transfer mark.

Nothing mysterious.

The gate opened.

Then Carrow ended.

Not dramatically.

Wall.

Road.

Fields.

Sky.

The first thing outside the city was cabbage.

Acres of it.

I had expected something better.

Sevren leaned back against the cart side.

“You look disappointed.”

“Cabbage.”

“You knew there were farms.”

“I expected the first view to show ambition.”

“It feeds people.”

“Practical ambition.”

He closed his eyes.

The road ran between low stone walls darkened by recent rain. Water sat in the deeper wheel ruts, but the center had dried. Every few hundred yards a shallow irrigation channel crossed beneath the road. Small square control stones at the field edges showed dull blue faces when water pressure held and gray faces when gates were closed.

I knew the family.

Different regions used different markers.

Old Greg had mostly cared whether the road flooded.

Current Greg watched one farmer kick mud away from a control stone so she could see its face.

Blue.

She kept walking.

That was the whole system I needed.

A mile farther, a cart passed us going west with stacked baskets under netting.

Ressa lifted two fingers.

The other driver did the same.

Sevren slept.

I looked at him.

Actually slept.

Mouth slightly open.

Courier bag clutched under one arm.

I considered speaking.

Friendship won.

Barely.

The east road flattened after the first rise.

My body settled into the cart rhythm better than it had months ago. Right foot braced lightly against the front board. Left knee bent comfortably. Residual limb supported by folded cloth instead of hanging against the bench edge. Hands mostly idle.

Road vibration still traveled through everything.

Cart seats remained a form of punishment.

But it was ordinary punishment now.

I could look outside.

That felt new enough that I did not explain it to myself.

Three Wells was not a village.

It was a road knot.

One inn.

One stable yard.

One long low relay house with a blue roof.

Three stone wells around a broad packed yard, which suggested somebody had named the place during a period of unusual honesty.

Ressa drove straight to the relay house.

Sevren woke before the cart stopped.

I resented this.

“You were asleep.”

“No.”

“You were making sleep noises.”

“Road thinking.”

“That is snoring.”

He jumped down.

Ressa unfastened my crutch strap before I reached for it, then stopped.

“Want them?”

“Yes.”

She handed them up one at a time.

No grabbing me.

No instructing me how to use objects I owned.

I set both crutch tips on the packed yard, turned, lowered my right foot to the iron step, then ground.

Fine.

Sevren was already halfway to the relay house.

“Wait.”

He turned.

“Why?”

“I do not know where I am going.”

“Food.”

“Excellent.”

That was not where he was going.

He pointed to the inn.

“I have handoff.”

“Then why did you say food?”

“For you.”

“Ah.”

He left.

Independent life remained rude.

I went to the inn.

The front step was too high for comfort and had a wooden half-step set beside it.

Not for me.

For everybody with short legs, old knees, heavy baskets, barrels on shoulders, children, and perhaps basic architectural regret.

I used it.

Inside, the room smelled like boiled meat, wet wool, and smoke.

A woman behind the counter said, “Stew or bread.”

“Both?”

She looked at me.

“That is allowed.”

Good place.

I paid nothing because Sevren had apparently arranged a road meal with the cart.

Or the cart had.

I did not know.

I asked before eating.

“Passenger meal included?”

The woman pointed toward the relay house.

“Courier seat?”

“Yes.”

“Yes.”

Good.

No accidental debt to stew.

I took a bowl and sat near the window.

Three Wells continued without me.

A stable boy carried two empty buckets past the window.

Ressa argued with a man over the reed frames.

A woman in a gray coat came out of the relay house, checked the sky, went back inside.

Two children rolled a wooden hoop through the yard until it hit one of the wells.

No blue charm.

No magical disaster.

Just bad aim.

Sevren returned ten minutes later and dropped into the chair opposite me.

He had a wooden token in one hand.

Round.

Dark.

A thin silver line crossed it from edge to edge.

He put it on the table.

I looked.

“Return?”

“Proof of handoff.”

I knew broad relay tokens.

Carrier takes sealed packet.

Receiving station witnesses intact handoff.

Token records or marks transfer enough to settle payment disputes.

Different houses used different versions.

I did not touch it.

Sevren ate half my bread.

I objected.

“That was included.”

“For passenger.”

“I brought passenger.”

“I am passenger.”

“You are cargo with opinions.”

“Then charge freight.”

He took another bite.

I moved the rest of the bread.

He smiled.

We ate.

For a while neither of us said anything.

That was also familiar.

Sevren had never needed every silence filled.

Outside, Ressa’s argument ended because the reed-frame man walked away carrying nothing.

Possibly victory.

Possibly lunch.

I nodded toward Sevren’s bag.

“New?”

“Old.”

“New flap.”

“Yes.”

“Who did it?”

“Woman in Bell Ford.”

“Good?”

“Still attached.”

High praise.

He stirred the stew.

“You still at theatre?”

“Sometimes.”

“Guild?”

“Sometimes.”

“Vale?”

I looked at him.

“How do you know Vale?”

“You complained about Vale before.”

“That was a long time ago.”

“You complain with continuity.”

Fair.

“Still Vale.”

“Lyssa?”

“Yes.”

He nodded.

No interrogation.

Again.

I looked at his boots.

“You?”

“What?”

“Road.”

He chewed.

“More east now.”

“I noticed.”

“North board got crowded.”

“With couriers?”

“With contracts.”

That meant something to him.

I waited.

He continued because he wanted to, not because I asked correctly.

“Short city work pays badly if everybody has legs.”

I looked down.

He pointed his spoon at me.

“Not about you.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He ate.

“East relay lost two regulars. One married into a mill family. One broke a wrist and decided paper was safer.”

“Is paper safer?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“So you took the runs.”

“Some.”

“Better pay?”

“Yes.”

“Worse beds?”

“Much.”

There it was.

The old Sevren equation.

I smiled.

He noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You have the face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you turn somebody else’s bad mattress into a lesson.”

I was offended.

“That is Lyssa’s accusation.”

“She is correct often.”

“Disgusting.”

Sevren finished the stew.

Then he said, “I am out more.”

Not apology.

Not announcement.

Fact.

“I noticed eventually.”

“Eventually?”

“I had other things.”

“Clearly.”

He pointed at my hair.

My hair had grown since the old date haircut.

The joke no longer made sense.

That made it better somehow.

I said, “You could have come by.”

“I did twice.”

I stared.

“When?”

“Once you were at theatre. Once Cala said you were at Guild.”

That was rude.

Not Sevren.

Time.

He had come.

I had been elsewhere.

The city had not failed to deliver him to me.

I had missed him by living.

I did not say that.

It sounded like something written on a bad wall plaque.

Instead I said, “You could have left bread.”

“I ate it.”

“Of course.”

He leaned back.

“I am here tonight.”

“Then leaving?”

“Tomorrow east.”

“For how long?”

“Three days if wheels remain round.”

“And after?”

“Carrow one day. Maybe two.”

A schedule.

Not a disappearance.

Not mine.

I nodded.

Sevren picked up the dark relay token and rolled it across his knuckles once.

The silver line flashed pale.

Not activation.

Just light.

“You ever want the longer seat?”

I looked at him.

“What longer seat?”

“East loop. Full day.”

There it was.

Not work.

Not yet.

“Why?”

“Because you keep looking at roads like they insulted you.”

“That is not a reason to spend a day on one.”

“It is a little.”

“Is there work?”

“For me.”

“For me?”

“No.”

“Pay?”

“No.”

“Cost?”

“Usually passenger bit if the cart is full contract. Sometimes nothing if seat is already covered.”

I had one bit.

He saw me calculate.

Not the amount.

The category.

“Not offering now,” he said.

Good.

That mattered.

“What are you doing?”

“Asking whether I should ask if there is one.”

Also familiar.

I looked through the window.

Ressa was tightening one of the return straps.

Beyond the yard, the east road kept going.

Fields.

Low hills.

Places I knew by old memory, places I knew wrongly, places that had not existed yet, places I had never seen at all.

Current Greg had spent a great deal of time learning one city.

That did not make the road less real.

“Ask,” I said.

Sevren nodded.

No promise.

No date.

No plan.

Then he stole the last piece of my bread.

I should have expected it.

The return freight was four wrapped wicker panels and two sacks that smelled sharply of dried mint.

Ressa made Sevren check the load ties.

Not me.

I appreciated the clarity.

The ride back began warmer.

Clouds broke over the fields. Road water turned silver in the low sun. One irrigation gate had opened since morning, and a thin sheet of water ran bright between rows of cabbage.

Still cabbage.

I had become more forgiving.

Sevren stayed awake this time.

We talked about nothing important.

A courier in Bell Ford who had lost a boot in mud and delivered the packet anyway.

A horse that refused one bridge but would cross the river twenty yards downstream.

A road inn that served eggs blue because of a harmless heating stone problem and insisted the color was traditional.

I believed half of it.

That was enough.

At the first rise toward Carrow, a small brake bead beneath Ressa’s board flashed amber as she pulled the long lever.

The cart slowed smoothly.

Common assist.

No drama.

Sevren kept talking through it.

Good fantasy, apparently, was sometimes something nobody stopped a story for.

By the east gate my back was tired.

Right thigh mildly stiff.

Residual limb warm but not angry.

No skin pain I could detect.

No hand trouble.

The folded cloth had earned permanent employment.

Ressa stopped inside the freight yard and handed me my crutches after releasing the strap.

“Same seat next time?”

“Ask me after my spine forgives this one.”

She nodded.

Reasonable.

Sevren swung his courier bag over his shoulder.

“Food?”

“I just spent half a day eating your bread.”

“My bread?”

“Exactly.”

We left the yard together.

At the next junction he turned south.

I stopped.

“You are not going home?”

“One delivery.”

“Of course.”

He walked backward three steps.

“If there is a longer seat, I ask.”

“If there is a seat.”

“That is what I said.”

“No, you said if there is a longer seat. There may be a shorter seat.”

He stared at me.

Then pointed south.

“I have work.”

“Coward.”

He turned away.

I called, “Sevren.”

He looked back.

“Good to see you.”

His expression changed just enough to make the sentence embarrassing.

“Yeah,” he said.

Then, because mercy was not a durable part of his character:

“Hair is worse again.”

“Fuck you.”

He left laughing.

I went home.

Cala was at the table.

Tavi was asleep with his head on folded arms.

Lyssa was cutting dark cloth at the far end.

She looked up.

“Three Wells?”

“Yes.”

“Worth it?”

My back hurt a little.

My right thigh wanted a different chair.

There was road dust on both trouser cuffs.

Sevren had stolen my bread.

“Yes.”

Lyssa nodded and went back to cutting.

No debrief.

No question about the green vest.

No question from me.

I put the folded seat cloth near the door so I would remember it.

Then I sat down.

Outside, a cart rolled past toward somewhere else.
