# CHAPTER 313

## THE LANDING

The cupboard arrived sideways.

That was the first problem.

The second problem was that it was taller than the stairs.

Not actually taller.

Buildings would have been difficult if furniture had to be shorter than stairs.

It was taller than the useful part of the turn.

There was a difference.

I discovered this because somebody shouted, "Stop," directly outside the door.

Then somebody else shouted, "I am stopped."

Then Cala said, "Greg."

I was drinking water.

This was apparently suspicious.

"What?"

"Move."

I moved.

Not far.

The front room was already mostly clear.

Cala had pushed the low stool against the wall and lifted Tavi's wooden cup off the floor.

The door stood open.

Beyond it, the gallery had become furniture.

A tall dark cupboard leaned across the landing at an angle that suggested optimism had recently died.

Its bottom rested on a folded blanket.

Its top was somewhere above my head, pressed close to the outer wall.

Two men held it.

One below.

One above.

A woman stood between them with both hands on her hips.

Another woman was halfway up the next stair, saying things that were not helping.

Tavi stood behind Cala and whispered, "Big."

Correct.

The cupboard was dark brown, nearly black where the wood had been oiled.

Two front doors.

Brass latch.

Rounded feet.

A narrow red stay bead sat above the latch, dull and quiet.

Ordinary travel furniture.

The bead kept the doors from swinging while the cabinet was moved.

It was not strong enough to make the thing lighter.

That would have been too convenient.

The woman with her hands on her hips looked at me.

"You live here?"

"Usually."

Cala pointed at her.

"Neris."

Neris pointed at the cupboard.

"Mine."

"Congratulations."

She did not look congratulated.

One of the men grunted.

"Can we?"

"No," said the man above.

"Still no?"

"Still wall."

The lower man looked offended by architecture.

Cala stepped onto the gallery.

I followed more slowly.

Right crutch.

Left crutch.

Right foot.

The usual.

I kept enough distance from the cupboard that nobody could accidentally turn me into a brace.

The building stair rose from the courtyard, reached our gallery, then turned sharply to continue upward.

The landing was wide enough for people.

Wide enough for baskets.

Wide enough for the red pot, if anyone became criminal.

Not wide enough for Neris's cupboard in its current relationship with geometry.

The top corner was caught under the low inner beam.

The bottom had not cleared the outer rail.

If they lifted the bottom, the top hit beam.

If they lowered the top, the bottom hit rail.

A beautiful arrangement.

"Door?" I asked.

Neris frowned.

"Which?"

"Room."

She pointed up.

"Third."

Of course.

The cupboard had already reached the second-floor landing and decided that was sufficient achievement.

The woman above the turn said, "I told her take the feet off."

Neris looked up.

"You said after it was here."

"I meant before this."

"That is not what after means."

The upper man laughed once.

The cupboard shifted.

Everybody stopped laughing.

The red stay bead flashed faintly.

The doors remained shut.

Good bead.

The lower man said, "Blanket's walking."

I looked down.

The folded blanket under the lower feet had crept half a hand toward the edge.

Not dangerous yet.

Unhelpful.

Cala said, "Tavi inside."

Tavi disappeared.

No argument.

The cupboard had authority.

Cala looked at me.

"Can you watch the stair?"

"Which part?"

"People."

That I could do.

I moved to the courtyard-side edge of the landing, outside the cupboard's possible fall path, and turned enough to see the lower stair.

A boy was already coming up with a basket.

"Wait."

He looked at the cupboard.

"Why?"

I considered the question.

"Because we have temporarily become a wall."

He sat on the third step.

Reasonable.

Another woman reached the bottom of the stairs, saw the situation, and turned around without asking.

Experience.

Behind me, Neris said, "Again."

The lower man lifted.

The upper man guided.

The cupboard rose perhaps two inches.

The top corner touched beam.

"Stop."

"I am stopped."

The lower man said something about the beam's parents.

Cala leaned against the doorway.

"Who brought it last time?"

Neris said, "My brother."

"Where is he?"

"North yard."

"Useful."

"He was until today."

I could see the cabinet's back now.

Three narrow iron straps ran vertically.

Between them, near the lower third, was a pale green square no bigger than my palm.

A dry plate.

Common enough.

Keep clothes from taking damp in bad rooms.

The broad family had been around before my first life ended, though this one looked older and simpler than the ones I remembered.

Nothing about it mattered to the stair.

I did not ask.

The lower man tried to slide the blanket.

The cabinet moved a little.

The red stay bead blinked again.

The upper woman said, "Take doors."

Neris answered immediately.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because pins are sealed."

That made sense.

Some older cupboards used the dry plate and door stay on a shared fitting line. Remove a door carelessly and the line might need reseating.

Not impossible.

Not worth doing on a stair landing because somebody had become impatient.

The upper man said, "Feet?"

Neris looked at the lower feet.

"Maybe."

"Do they unscrew?"

"I don't know."

The woman above said, "They look like feet."

This was technically true and professionally useless.

I looked at the lower right foot.

Rounded wood.

No visible pin from this angle.

The blanket hid the other.

I said, "Can you tilt enough to see under without moving the top?"

The lower man looked at me.

"Maybe."

Neris said, "Don't lift yet."

Good.

She crouched beside the lower foot.

The gallery was too crowded for me to join without becoming part of the problem.

She pulled one corner of the blanket back.

The foot ended in a dark iron plate.

Four small studs.

No screw stem.

"Fixed," she said.

"Looks fixed," I agreed.

The upper woman said, "Hit it."

Four people told her no.

Community.

The boy on the stair began laughing.

"Quiet," I told him.

He laughed quieter.

Progress.

For the next few minutes, the cupboard remained exactly where it was while everyone developed theories.

Angle it more toward the courtyard.

No, rail.

Raise the top.

No, beam.

Turn it face-down.

No, landing width.

Take the rail off.

Cala said no before anyone finished that sentence.

The outer gallery rail had a blue maintenance seal at the nearest post.

I knew the broad category.

Not decorative.

Not ours.

Good enough.

Neris rubbed her forehead.

"I measured the room."

Cala said, "Not the stair."

"I know that now."

The lower man shifted his grip.

"Need rest."

That mattered.

Everything else stopped.

They lowered the cabinet until its weight settled more fully onto the blanket and one padded corner block the upper man had wedged beneath the side.

Both carriers stepped back.

Hands flexing.

Shoulders rolling.

Nobody pretended tired arms were a moral failure.

I liked professionals.

The upper carrier came down two steps and sat.

The lower one leaned against the wall.

Neris went inside our room without asking.

Cala handed her water.

Apparently permission had arrived earlier than I had.

I stayed by the stair.

The boy with the basket said, "Can I go under?"

"No."

"I fit."

"That is not the question."

"I fit a lot."

"Impressive."

He looked pleased.

I had made an error.

Another man came up from the courtyard carrying three bundled lengths of reed.

He stopped beside the boy.

"Moving?"

"Not currently."

He looked at the cupboard.

"Ah."

That was the full diagnosis.

He sat too.

We had become a waiting room.

The carriers drank.

Neris came back out.

"Could bring it down."

The lower carrier nodded.

"Easy."

"Then what?"

Nobody answered.

That was the actual problem.

Not getting it off the landing.

Getting it through the turn.

I looked at the geometry again.

Old Greg had moved siege chests through ruined towers.

Dragged altar stones through broken corridors.

Helped evacuate a library where the shelves had been more valuable than three of the librarians.

He had also destroyed a lot of doors.

Power was useful.

Power encouraged bad carpentry.

This cupboard did not need S-class force.

It needed six inches of somewhere.

I looked at the landing.

Then the room behind me.

Our doorway opened inward.

The cupboard was currently trying to turn entirely within the gallery.

That was why the outer rail and inner beam were fighting over it.

I looked at Cala.

She was already looking at me.

"Inside?" I asked.

She glanced at the cupboard.

Then at our doorway.

Then at the floor.

Neris followed.

"What?"

Cala said, "Use room."

The upper carrier stood.

He looked through our open door.

Then back at the cupboard.

"Back corner in?"

"Maybe," I said.

He ignored me and looked at Cala.

Correct choice.

Cala moved the stool farther.

"Nothing break."

"Excellent operating standard," I said.

She pointed at me.

"You too."

Unfair.

The plan was not complicated.

That did not make it easy.

Instead of forcing the cupboard to turn entirely on the landing, they could reverse the lower half into our front room through the open door.

That would give the top more angle beneath the beam.

Then rotate the cabinet around the doorway.

Then bring it back out facing the upper stair.

A room as temporary turning space.

Not magic.

Not brilliant.

Just part of the building nobody had counted because it had been labeled house instead of stair.

The lower carrier looked at the threshold.

"Blanket catches."

Cala disappeared inside.

She came back with the thin old floor mat from beside the basin.

"Over."

He laid it across the threshold.

The mat made a smoother bridge over the raised wood.

Neris looked at Cala.

"Clean later."

"Yes."

The boy on the stairs said, "I can help."

"No," said everyone.

He accepted this with less grace.

I moved farther down the gallery to keep the path clear.

My job remained people.

I preferred it.

"Lower first," the upper carrier said.

The two men took positions.

Neris stood at the side, one hand near the cabinet but not yet touching.

Cala stayed inside our room to watch the threshold and table corner.

The upper woman finally came down from the third-floor stair.

"What do I do?"

"Nothing," said Neris.

I felt represented.

They lifted.

Not high.

Just enough to take weight off the corner block.

The lower feet slid on the blanket.

The blanket reached the threshold mat.

"Slow."

They went slow.

The bottom of the cupboard crossed into our room by perhaps half a foot.

Then a foot.

The top moved away from the beam.

"Stop."

They stopped.

Neris checked the stay bead.

Still red.

Still holding.

She put her hand flat against the side.

"Now turn."

This was where it became ugly.

The lower man needed to step inside our room while the upper man guided the top across the landing.

The cabinet could not simply spin.

It had to pivot around one padded lower corner while sliding the other.

The kind of movement that made diagrams look dishonest.

I watched.

The right upper corner passed beneath the beam.

Barely.

The left lower foot caught the threshold.

"Stop."

Everyone stopped.

Cala crouched.

"Mat folded."

It had.

One edge had rolled under the iron foot plate.

The carrier could not pull it free while holding weight.

Neris looked around.

Her eyes landed on me.

"No."

I had not spoken.

"You looked useful."

"This is discrimination."

"Hook mat."

That I could do.

I checked the cupboard.

Checked the path.

Then used the lower curve of my right crutch to catch the loose mat edge from outside the load path.

Not pulling the cabinet.

Not bracing it.

Just the mat.

"Ready?"

"Yes."

I drew the mat flat.

The folded edge came free.

Cala pressed it down with her palm after the foot cleared.

"Good."

I withdrew the crutch.

My contribution to furniture moving had peaked.

The cabinet turned another few inches.

The top cleared the beam.

The lower half went deeper into our room.

For one alarming moment, the whole cupboard stood diagonally across our doorway with one carrier inside, one outside, Neris trapped on the wrong side, and Cala telling Tavi from somewhere behind the table that no, he could not touch it.

Then the upper corner passed.

The cabinet faced the upper stairs.

Everyone exhaled.

The boy below clapped.

"Do not reward them yet," I said.

The upper carrier laughed.

They moved the cupboard back out of our room.

Across the landing.

Then onto the next stair.

The problem was not over.

It had merely become someone else's floor.

Neris went first.

The upper woman followed.

Both carriers climbed.

The cupboard rose one step at a time.

Red stay bead quiet.

Dark doors closed.

At the third-floor turn, somebody shouted, "Stop."

The lower carrier shouted, "I am stopped."

I looked at Cala.

She looked at me.

We both waited.

Then Neris shouted down, "Fine."

The cupboard disappeared upward.

The stair became stairs again.

The boy with the basket stood immediately.

"Finally."

"You are welcome for our wall service."

He passed me.

The reed-bundle man followed.

Three more people appeared from nowhere.

Traffic had memory.

Within a minute, the landing was ordinary.

Except our floor mat was crooked.

The stool was against the wrong wall.

The front room smelled faintly of old wood oil.

And a long dark scrape crossed the threshold where one iron foot plate had dragged before the mat flattened.

Cala looked at it.

I looked at it.

She rubbed it once with her thumb.

Surface only.

"Fine."

"Good."

She pointed at the mat.

I moved it back with one crutch.

Tavi emerged.

"Big gone."

"Yes."

He looked up the stair.

"Again?"

"No."

This disappointed him.

*

Neris came down perhaps half an hour later.

No cupboard.

Good sign.

She carried the folded blanket and our floor mat.

The blanket was hers.

The mat was ours.

She handed Cala the mat.

"Room."

Cala nodded.

"Stair bad."

Neris nodded.

"Room good."

This was apparently the official report.

One of the carriers came behind her, rubbing his left forearm.

Not injured.

Tired.

He looked at me.

"Good hook."

I looked at my crutch.

"Years of specialized mat training."

He nodded solemnly.

"I could tell."

Neris snorted.

No coin appeared.

No one asked what I charged.

No one asked whether I moved cupboards professionally.

This was healthy.

Neris looked at Cala.

"I bring soup."

Cala said, "No need."

"I bring."

Cala shrugged.

Negotiations concluded.

Neris went upstairs again.

The carrier left for the courtyard.

I sat.

Not because I had done much.

Because standing in one place on crutches while other people moved heavy furniture was still standing in one place on crutches.

My palms had the faint compressed feeling of a longer hold.

No pain.

Right leg fine.

Residual limb fine.

I opened my hands.

Closed them.

Cala put the mat back by the basin.

Tavi began trying to move the stool.

"Stop," Cala said.

"I stopped," he said.

I laughed hard enough that my right hand slipped on the crutch grip.

Not enough to matter.

Cala looked at me.

"Your fault."

"Absolutely."

Tavi pushed the stool one inch.

"Stopped."

"Monster," I said.

He pushed it back.

*

I went out later.

Not for a job.

Not for Hessa.

Not for the cupboard.

The building had simply become loud enough that I wanted a quieter street.

The one copper and one bit stayed upstairs.

The fixed holdfast stayed wrapped.

My bag came with me because it usually did.

The narrow scrap inside had the marked-strip notes.

I did not take it out.

At the end of our lane, two delivery women were arguing over whether a stack of round baskets belonged to number eight or number eighteen.

A barber had hung white cloth from his awning to catch the afternoon light.

Someone was roasting peppers.

A little brass shop charm over a doorway clicked every time a customer entered and twice every time a customer left.

It was probably broken.

Nobody looked concerned.

I kept walking.

At a narrow crossing, a woman with a handcart waited for me to clear the curb cut.

I cleared it.

She passed.

No story.

A group of children had chalked a long crooked road across the paving and were making small clay animals follow it.

One animal had five legs.

Tavi would have respected the craft.

I sat for a while on a public bench under a patched shade cloth.

A man at the other end was asleep with his hat over his face.

Good bench etiquette.

I thought about the cupboard once.

Not the dry plate.

Not the stay bead.

The room.

Everyone had stared at the landing because the problem was on the landing.

The useful space had been somewhere else.

That thought tried to become magic.

I refused it.

Mostly.

Target specification.

Useful space.

Angles.

No.

Hessa would kill me by narrowing a sentence until it became a blade.

I let the cupboard remain a cupboard.

That was allowed.

The sleeping man snored once.

A cart rolled by with three blue glass jars suspended from a hanging rack.

Each jar swayed.

The rack did not.

Good suspension work.

I did not investigate.

Eventually I stood.

Hands fine again.

I went home.

*

Neris did bring soup.

Too much soup.

A deep clay bowl sat on our table when I returned.

Cala had already transferred half of it into the red pot.

"Payment?" I asked.

"No."

"Gift?"

"Soup."

Stronger category.

Lyssa was home.

The dark cloth from yesterday was gone.

Not explained.

Good.

She ate with one knee tucked under the bench and listened while Cala described the cupboard.

Cala's version made the entire event six sentences.

Mine would have been longer.

This was why she got invited places.

"Tall?" Lyssa asked.

Cala held one hand above her head.

Tavi stood on the bench and held both hands higher.

"Very," he said.

Lyssa looked at the threshold scrape.

"That new?"

"Surface," Cala said.

Lyssa rubbed it with her toe.

"Fine."

Consensus.

I ate soup.

It had white beans, greens, and too much pepper.

Good.

Neris had apparently decided gratitude should hurt slightly.

Tavi said, "Stop."

Nobody moved.

He looked around.

"I stopped."

Lyssa started laughing first.

Then Cala.

Then me.

Tavi had discovered power.

This would end badly.

Later, when the bowl was empty, Cala set it by the door.

Return tomorrow.

No mystery.

No network to investigate.

Just a bowl.

Upstairs, the fixed holdfast remained where I had left it.

The narrow scrap remained in the bag.

Three clean sheets remained clean.

One copper and one bit remained money instead of becoming opportunity.

I lay back for a while.

My hands were normal.

The house above us creaked once.

Then twice.

Something heavy moved across the third-floor room.

I listened.

No shout.

No crash.

No cupboard descending through the ceiling.

Success.

I thought about how much effort it had taken to move one ordinary piece of furniture up one ordinary building.

Two carriers.

Neris.

Cala.

A blanket.

A floor mat.

A doorway.

One excellent crutch hook.

No heroics.

No spell.

No breakthrough.

The cupboard was upstairs because enough small things had been useful at the same time.

That was a kind of strength I had not respected enough in my first life.

Maybe because I had spent too long being able to replace six people with force.

Maybe because force made every room look optional.

Below, Tavi shouted, "Stop."

Cala shouted back, "Sleep."

Silence.

A stronger magic.

I closed my eyes.

The cupboard stayed upstairs.
