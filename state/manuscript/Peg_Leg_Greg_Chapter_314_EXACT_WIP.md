# CHAPTER 314

## THE DRY HOUR

The water stopped while I was washing one spoon.

This was rude.

Not dangerous.

Not dramatic.

Just rude.

I had soap on the spoon.

I turned the little brass tap farther.

Nothing.

Turned it back.

Nothing.

Tapped the pipe once because humans had been doing that to uncooperative objects since before language.

Still nothing.

Cala looked over from the table.

"Dry?"

"Apparently."

She did not move.

That was suspicious.

"You knew."

"Bell."

"What bell?"

"The one you ignored."

I considered defending myself.

There had been a bell.

Probably.

Carrow had too many bells.

Meal bells.

Gate bells.

Weather bells.

Guild bells.

Fire bells.

Theatre bells.

People who owned one bell and had developed opinions.

"You could have said water."

"I did."

"When?"

"Before you washed spoon."

I looked down at the spoon.

The spoon had betrayed me.

Tavi leaned over the table.

"Dry."

"Thank you."

"Dry spoon."

"It is wet with soap."

"Bad."

"Correct."

Cala pointed toward the door.

"Lower court."

"What is in the lower court?"

"Water."

That improved the conversation.

I left the spoon in the basin.

Lyssa was not home.

The front room still showed no evidence that a cupboard had passed through it yesterday except for the dark scrape across the threshold, which Cala continued to regard as beneath human concern.

The old floor mat was back beside the basin.

Neris's soup bowl was gone.

Returned already, apparently.

No ceremony.

Good.

I took the small household jug from its hook.

Cala looked at it.

Then at me.

"No."

I stopped.

"Why?"

"Big jug."

She pointed under the table.

There was a larger clay jug with two loop handles.

I looked at it.

Then at my crutches.

Then at Cala.

"No."

She nodded.

Agreement.

Excellent household management.

She reached behind the door and pulled out the little shoulder-slung water sack instead.

Not mine.

Old household thing.

Waxed cloth.

Flat when empty.

A narrow strap.

"Two fills," she said.

"That sounds suspiciously like work."

"Water."

"Yes. I recognized the substance."

Tavi said, "Dry."

I put the sack over my right shoulder, opposite my regular brown bag because I did not need both straps fighting under my arm.

Then I reconsidered.

The regular bag stayed home.

No reason to carry charcoal and old magic notes to fetch water.

The water sack hung flat against my right hip.

Fine.

Cala said, "Lower court. Blue cart."

"Blue cart."

"Not red."

"What happens if I use the red cart?"

She looked at me for a long moment.

"I don't know."

That was less comforting than an answer.

I went downstairs.

The building courtyard was already louder than usual.

Three doors stood open.

A woman on the first floor had two buckets lined against the wall.

Somebody above shouted, "Still dry?"

Somebody below shouted, "Still asking?"

Community.

The lower street was worse.

Not bad.

Just occupied.

People had come out carrying every object in Carrow capable of holding water.

Buckets.

Jugs.

Lidded pots.

A narrow-necked bottle the size of Tavi.

One man had a cooking basin tucked under his arm and the expression of someone prepared to defend the decision.

The regular public spout at the corner was dead.

Its little green pressure square sat gray above the outlet.

A chalk board had been hung beside it.

LINE FLUSH
SECOND TO FOURTH BELL
LOWER COURT CART
HOUSEHOLD DRAW ONLY

Under that, in different handwriting:

STOP TURNING THE SPOUT

I looked at the brass handle.

It had been turned so many times it was wet with fingerprints despite producing no water.

People were optimists.

The lower court sat behind the dye lane, a broad paved square mostly used by handcarts when the market street became too crowded.

Today it had become a temporary well.

The blue cart was easy to find.

Mostly because it was blue.

Good system.

A squat tank filled the cart bed.

Four iron-banded barrels sat behind it.

Two city line workers stood beside a waist-high distribution frame with three taps.

Not Guild.

Not guards.

Gray work coats with blue shoulder tabs.

A little brass pressure plate had been clipped to the frame.

Its center glowed pale blue.

Water infrastructure.

Old Greg knew the category.

Portable head tank.

Pressure limiter.

Probably a self-closing draw valve.

Probably.

The later versions I remembered were larger and meaner and occasionally exploded when military quartermasters ignored cold-weather procedures.

This one looked content to distribute drinking water.

I did not ask it about war.

There were two lines.

One short.

One long.

I joined the short one.

A woman behind me said, "Household?"

I looked at her.

She pointed at my sack.

"Household line is there."

She pointed at the long line.

Of course.

"What is this one?"

"Shops."

I moved.

The short line had been too beautiful.

The household line curved around the edge of the court.

Maybe twenty people.

Not terrible.

I took my place behind an older man carrying two narrow copper cans on a shoulder yoke.

He looked at my water sack.

"One?"

"Two fills."

He nodded.

Apparently Cala had not invented that.

Ahead of us, a line worker called, "House mark or two-hand vessel. No casks."

A woman three places forward raised a bucket.

"What is a two-hand vessel?"

"If you need two hands to carry it empty, no."

She looked at the bucket.

Then at the worker.

"I can carry it one."

"Empty?"

"Yes."

"Then fine."

Rules were alive.

The line moved.

Slowly.

Not because the taps were slow.

Because everybody had questions.

"Can I fill the kettle after the jug?"

"Same household?"

"Yes."

"Together under the mark."

"What mark?"

"Your building mark."

"I don't have it."

"Then one vessel."

"What if my sister has it?"

"Then your sister is better prepared."

The other line worker said, "Marn."

"What?"

"Be kind."

"I am distributing free water during a line flush."

"That is not the same thing."

Marn considered this.

Then looked at the woman.

"One vessel now. Bring the mark for more."

"Thank you."

"See? Kind."

The second worker rolled her eyes.

I liked them.

A child near the front dropped a tin cup.

It bounced three times.

Nobody died.

His mother picked it up and put it inside her basket.

"You are not drinking from the ground."

"I wasn't."

"You were thinking."

This was a powerful accusation.

The older man ahead of me shifted his yoke.

The copper cans were empty but not light.

"You want ahead?"

I looked at him.

"No."

"You are standing on sticks."

"Crutches."

"Still sticks."

He had a point.

"I'm fine."

He looked at my right leg.

Then the left trouser leg pinned cleanly below the knee.

Then back at my face.

"Your funeral."

"Very small funeral."

He snorted.

The line moved another three people.

My palms were fine.

Shoulders fine.

Residual limb fine.

Standing in one place was sometimes worse than moving, but not after five minutes.

I shifted my right foot.

Changed the crutch angle.

Normal.

Across the court, the shop line moved faster.

A baker's boy filled two square lidded cans.

A woman in a leather apron argued over whether a dye rinse counted as household use.

It did not.

She knew it did not.

Her argument was mostly athletic.

The worker at that tap pointed toward a second chalk board.

TRADE DRAW: SOUTH TANK
TOKEN REQUIRED

The woman read it.

Then continued arguing.

Reading was not surrender.

Behind me, somebody said, "They flushed north line last month."

Another voice answered, "That was north."

"Water is water."

"Then drink north."

This continued.

I looked up.

A thin run of blue light moved along the exposed pipe strapped beneath the court wall.

Not mana.

Not mine.

Line charge.

The light traveled left to right, paused at a junction box, then vanished.

A moment later the brass pressure plate on the cart frame brightened.

The worker named Marn glanced at it.

"Hold."

All three taps closed.

The court complained as one organism.

Marn held up both hands.

"Pressure swap."

Nobody died of thirst in the next twelve seconds.

Barely.

The blue center of the plate dimmed.

Then brightened.

The other worker opened one tap into a shallow drain tray.

Water spat.

Cloudy for half a breath.

Then clear.

She let it run longer.

Clear.

She touched a small white test bead to the stream.

The bead stayed white.

"Good."

Marn reopened the household taps.

The line moved again.

I had seen larger versions of the same practice.

Army camps.

Field hospitals.

Siege towns where bad water killed more people than arrows and everybody insisted the arrows were more heroic.

Flush the line.

Bleed the dirty first run.

Test.

Then drink.

Nothing mysterious.

The local bead was neat.

I did not need to know how neat.

A young man two places ahead filled a tall ceramic jug.

When he lifted it, the bottom gave a small wet click.

He froze.

The line froze with him.

A hairline crack darkened along one side.

Not catastrophic.

Yet.

Marn pointed.

"Down."

The young man lowered it immediately.

Good.

Water began to bead through the crack.

"Oh, come on."

"Do not lift it."

"It's my good jug."

"It is becoming your broken jug."

The second worker slid a shallow wooden tray beneath it.

"Have another vessel?"

"No."

A woman beside him held up a squat pot.

"Use mine."

He looked at her.

"You need it."

"I have two."

"That one is soup."

"It was soup."

Marn said, "Transfer here. Slowly."

The young man looked miserable.

"I just filled it."

"Yes."

"That was the point."

"Still is."

They used a broad tin dipper from the cart kit.

No magic.

No clever rescue.

Just transfer the water before the crack opened farther.

The line waited.

Nobody shouted.

Well.

One person shouted.

"How long?"

Marn shouted back, "Longer now."

That solved nothing but improved my mood.

The older man with the yoke leaned slightly toward me.

"Good jug."

"Apparently."

"Was."

"Also apparently."

He smiled.

When the transfer was done, the cracked jug sat empty in the tray.

The young man hugged the borrowed pot to his chest.

"Thank you."

The woman waved him away.

"Bring it back."

"I will."

"You said that about my knife."

"I brought the knife."

"Three months."

"It came back."

There were relationships everywhere if you stood still long enough.

That was annoying.

Also useful.

My turn came.

Marn looked at the flat sack.

"House?"

"Second-floor Cala."

She pointed at a little board beside the tap.

Rows of scratched marks.

I found ours because Cala had shown me months ago after I had mistaken it for a carpenter's tally.

Two shallow cuts crossing one deeper diagonal.

Building draw mark.

Not family crest.

Not ownership.

Just municipal shorthand for a cluster of rooms tied to one regular line account.

I touched it.

Marn nodded.

"Two fills. One now, one after."

"After what?"

"Drink it."

I stared at her.

She stared back.

Then the second worker laughed.

"Marn."

Marn smiled.

"Fill. Empty into home storage. Come back."

"That was almost funny."

"It was very funny."

The sack had a stiff wooden mouth with a short cord loop.

I set one crutch against my right forearm, kept the other planted, and held the sack mouth beneath the tap.

Marn watched.

"Need hand?"

"No."

She did not reach.

Good.

She opened the valve.

Water hit harder than expected.

The sack jumped.

I tightened my grip.

"Violent water."

"Pressure plate is showing off."

The sack rounded against my hip.

Not huge.

Maybe enough for cooking and drinking until the regular line returned.

Marn shut the tap before it overfilled.

I tied the mouth.

Tested the knot.

The weight changed everything.

Not my balance exactly.

My right shoulder.

The sack pulled downward and inward.

Bad side for free swing.

I adjusted the strap shorter.

Moved the sack farther toward my back.

Better.

The older man with the yoke watched.

"Still fine?"

"Ask me upstairs."

"Funeral moved?"

"Third bell."

He laughed.

His two cans filled next.

I left the court carefully.

No heroics.

No racing.

Wet pavement near the drain tray.

Crowded lane.

A loaded water sack that could swing into a crutch if I let it.

So I did not let it.

Shorter steps.

Right foot planted clean.

Crutches slightly wider where space allowed.

The sack bumped my hip twice before I found the rhythm.

By the time I reached our building, I had decided water was heavier than its reputation deserved.

Cala opened the door before I knocked.

She looked at the sack.

"One."

"Yes."

"Two."

"I remember."

She took it only after I had both crutches planted and had loosened the strap myself.

Also good.

She poured the water into the covered household crock near the back wall.

Not all of it.

Some into the smaller kitchen jug.

Some into Tavi's cup because apparently he had been five minutes from becoming dust.

He drank.

"Water."

"Remarkable."

Cala handed me the empty sack.

"Again."

"You have become drunk with power."

"Water."

That word continued winning arguments.

I went back.

The second trip was easier because I knew the load.

The household line was shorter.

The cracked-jug man was gone.

The borrowed soup pot was gone.

The copper-can man was filling his second pair or perhaps somebody else's.

He saw me.

"Alive."

"Disappointing."

Marn lifted the tap.

"Second?"

"Second."

"See? System."

"I resent effective systems."

"You live in a city."

"Constant suffering."

The second fill went cleanly.

On the walk home, the regular corner spout coughed.

Everybody nearby turned.

It spat air.

Then brownish water.

Then stopped.

A woman reached for the handle.

Three people said, "Don't."

She withdrew her hand.

Good community.

A blue-coated line worker farther down the street shouted, "Not open yet. Wait for clear mark."

The green pressure square above the spout stayed gray.

Simple.

Visible.

A system that assumed people would still turn the handle anyway.

Realistic.

Back upstairs, Cala emptied the second sack.

That put enough water in the covered crock for the rest of the dry hour.

Maybe more.

I hung the sack where it had been.

My right shoulder felt used.

Not hurt.

Used.

Hands fine.

Residual limb fine.

Right leg fine.

I sat.

Tavi climbed onto the stool beside me.

"Dry?"

"Temporarily."

He thought about that.

Then pointed toward the basin.

"Spoon."

The soapy spoon still waited.

I had forgotten it.

Cala had not.

Of course.

I took the little kitchen jug.

Poured perhaps half a cup over the spoon.

Washed it.

Rinsed it into the basin.

Tavi watched with grave attention.

"Water."

"Yes."

"Good."

"Usually."

Outside, a bell rang.

Cala tilted her head.

"Line."

I waited.

Another bell.

Shorter.

Then from the street below came the unmistakable sound of twenty people testing taps at once.

Water hammered through the building pipe.

The brass tap above our basin shuddered.

Cala pointed at it.

"Wait."

"I have learned fear."

She counted under her breath.

Then opened the tap only a little.

Air hissed.

Cloudy water sputtered into the basin.

She let it run.

Clear.

A small green glass inset beside the pipe brightened.

Not a test bead.

Just the house line mark returning live.

Cala closed the tap.

"Good."

That was it.

No quest.

No broken infrastructure.

No hidden conspiracy in municipal water.

The line had been flushed.

The temporary cart had done its job.

The city remained hydrated despite several people's best efforts.

I looked at the clean spoon.

One spoon.

Two trips.

A courtyard full of vessels.

Three taps.

A cracked jug.

A pressure swap.

Old Greg had once crossed a poisoned valley because an army could not find water.

This Greg had carried two sacks downstairs and back.

The second task was smaller.

It still mattered before dinner.

I put the spoon away.

Then I drank a cup of water because spite was not hydration.
