# CHAPTER 315

## THE TRUE STORY

The first lie was about a chicken.

This made sense.

Chickens had been supporting human dishonesty for generations.

The man telling it sat on an overturned flour bucket outside a tea stall, one boot planted on the street and the other hooked around the bucket rim. He had a narrow face, a yellow scarf, and the solemn expression of someone reporting a military disaster.

"Three eggs," he said.

A woman beside him shook her head.

"Too many."

"Three. Every morning. Same hen."

"Already bad."

"Wait."

I had stopped because Cala had sent me for salt and because the salt seller was still arguing with a delivery boy over whether two sacks counted as late if one had technically arrived yesterday.

I had time.

The tea stall had six stools.

All occupied.

The flour bucket made seven seats if dignity was negotiable.

I stood at the edge with my crutches and listened.

The yellow-scarf man lowered his voice.

"Fourth egg came at noon."

Someone behind the tea counter gasped theatrically.

The woman beside him said, "Disqualified."

"You haven't heard the fourth egg."

"A chicken laying four eggs in one day is lazy lying."

"It was blue."

"Worse."

"And warm enough to cook the other three."

The whole group groaned.

I laughed.

That was my mistake.

The woman turned.

She was perhaps fifty, perhaps older, with gray braided through black hair and a narrow clay cup balanced on one knee.

"You laugh, you tell."

I looked behind me.

Nobody.

"That seems like a dangerous law."

"Tea law."

"I did not buy tea."

The man behind the counter lifted a cup.

"Water free. Law still applies."

Corruption.

I looked toward the salt seller.

Still arguing.

The delivery boy had now opened one sack as evidence of something.

I should have left.

Instead I said, "What are the rules?"

The woman smiled.

"Best lie wins."

"Wins what?"

"Nothing."

"Excellent prize."

"Has to sound true long enough."

Yellow Scarf pointed at her.

"That is not the rule."

"It is today."

"Yesterday was impossible lie."

"Yesterday you lost."

"Because nobody respects maritime knowledge."

The tea seller said, "You said the fish owned a boat."

"It did."

"See? Still lying."

I had lived fifty-nine years the first time.

I had crossed countries that no longer existed in the same shape I remembered.

I had fought things with too many mouths.

I had slept inside a dead titan's rib cage because the weather outside was worse.

I had once spent nine days trapped in a tower where every staircase returned to the floor below unless you walked backward while insulting the architect.

I had watched a priest negotiate with his own shadow.

I had eaten soup beside a dragon who claimed not to be a dragon and became offended when anyone agreed.

I was, objectively, overqualified.

"Fine," I said.

The woman pointed to an empty patch of wall beside the stall.

"Stand there."

"I am already standing."

"Then you are prepared."

I shifted one crutch slightly.

The group settled.

There were eight of them if I counted the tea seller.

Yellow Scarf.

Gray-Braid.

A narrow old man with a red nose.

Two women sharing a plate of something fried.

A boy maybe fifteen with ink on three fingers.

A broad man in a flour-dusted vest.

Tea Seller.

No one asked my name.

This was refreshing.

I considered inventing something.

That seemed wrong.

Not morally.

Structurally.

I had too much material.

So I chose a true story.

"Once," I said, "I spent three days being hunted by a door."

Silence.

Gray-Braid narrowed her eyes.

Yellow Scarf said, "Bad start."

"It was a very determined door."

"Worse."

I continued.

The door had belonged to a ruined watch post north of Seln.

Not this Seln.

The old one.

Or perhaps the name had changed twice since.

It had been bound to keep intruders out.

Then the wall collapsed.

The door remained.

The binding remained.

The definition of out became complicated.

I did not explain that part.

I told them the door followed our camp at night.

Not quickly.

Doors rarely had good pursuit speed.

But we would wake and find it standing twenty feet closer.

Next night, closer again.

We moved camp.

The door came.

The boy with ink fingers leaned forward.

Good.

I described how one of our scouts tied a bell to the handle.

The bell rang all night.

Not because the door moved constantly.

Because the wind existed.

This made everyone angry.

That part was also true.

"Then what?" one fried-food woman asked.

"We burned it."

Gray-Braid frowned.

"Too easy."

"Didn't work."

Better.

The binding had treated fire as an intruder.

The door shut on it.

This made no physical sense and had still happened.

I remembered Jalen staring at the smoking door and saying, with deep sincerity, that he hated architecture.

I almost said his name.

Did not.

"So what did you do?" Tea Seller asked.

"Opened it."

The group stared.

"That was the trick?" Yellow Scarf said.

"No. Opening it made things worse."

The door decided we were inside.

Then it began trying to keep everything else out.

Rain.

Birds.

A mule.

The mule won.

Barely.

The old man with the red nose started laughing before I reached the mule.

I smiled.

This was going well.

Then I told them how the door finally stopped following us when we rebuilt enough wall around it to give the binding a stable inside and outside again.

We left a freestanding rectangle of stone in the middle of a field.

Door closed.

Happy.

Problem solved.

I finished.

Nobody spoke.

Then Gray-Braid said, "True."

I blinked.

"What?"

"You told a true story."

"The game is lying."

"Exactly."

Yellow Scarf pointed at me.

"Disqualified."

"How do you know?"

Tea Seller shrugged.

"Too many bad details."

"Bad details?"

"Mule."

"There was a mule."

"See?"

The group laughed.

I felt personally attacked by accuracy.

Gray-Braid sipped her tea.

"A liar makes the door smarter."

"It was not smart."

"Exactly."

"It was a badly defined ward attached to a door."

Yellow Scarf slapped his knee.

"He keeps proving it."

I looked at the salt seller.

The delivery argument had ended.

I could leave.

I did not.

"Fine," I said. "Again."

Gray-Braid smiled.

"One each."

"That rule was not stated."

"Tea law."

"Your legal system is corrupt."

"True."

Apparently she could say that without being disqualified.

The boy with ink fingers went next.

His lie involved a clerk who accidentally stamped himself and became municipal property.

Strong opening.

He kept the details small.

The clerk had to sleep in a records room because the stamp said STORAGE.

His wife brought blankets.

The office charged her one bit for after-hours access.

I believed this completely.

That was the problem.

"True," I said when he finished.

Everyone booed me.

The boy grinned.

"Lie."

"That is absolutely something an office would do."

"Still lie."

I disliked the game.

The broad flour-dusted man told one about a baker who could smell tomorrow's rain in uncooked dough.

I knew three bakers who could do versions of that with pressure, humidity, yeast behavior, and low-grade weather sensitivity.

"True," I said.

Wrong again.

One fried-food woman told us her sister had married a man because his house shadow fell north at noon.

That sounded like a curse-selection custom from western hill country.

"True."

Wrong.

Yellow Scarf stared at me.

"Do you believe everything?"

"Apparently only lies."

Gray-Braid laughed into her cup.

The old man with the red nose finally introduced himself as Bel.

Only because Tea Seller called him Bel while accusing him of cheating.

Bel's lie was simple.

"I was handsome at nineteen."

Everyone immediately shouted, "Lie."

Bel won the round.

I objected.

"There was no evidence."

Gray-Braid said, "Look at him."

Bel bowed from his stool.

This was not a fair court.

The salt seller had begun serving people again.

I should have gone.

Instead Tea Seller pushed a cup of water toward the edge of the counter.

"Free."

I took it.

The cup was cool clay.

No charm.

No bead.

Just shade and water.

I drank.

A woman passing with a basket stopped long enough to hear Yellow Scarf begin another story.

This one involved a bridge toll collector and a ghost.

The ghost refused to pay because dead people were exempt from road maintenance.

The collector disagreed.

The argument lasted until sunrise.

Then the ghost vanished and the collector billed the cemetery.

I waited.

Everyone looked at me.

"What?"

Gray-Braid said, "True or lie?"

I considered.

"Lie."

The group cheered.

Yellow Scarf looked offended.

"True."

I stared at him.

"No."

"My uncle."

"Your uncle billed a cemetery for a ghost's toll?"

"No. My uncle was the ghost."

The group exploded.

Yellow Scarf could barely finish because he was laughing too.

"You are lying now."

"Yes."

"So the first part?"

"Also lie."

"Then I was right."

"But for the wrong lie."

I hated everyone.

It was excellent.

By the time I remembered the salt, the sun had moved half a building.

I bought the household packet with Cala's money.

The salt seller wrapped it in brown paper and tied it with thin reed fiber.

"You were over there a while."

"I was being professionally deceived."

"Tea stall?"

"Yes."

"Bel still claiming he was handsome?"

I looked at him.

"You know Bel?"

"Everybody knows Bel."

Of course.

I tucked the salt packet under my shirt because both hands belonged to crutches.

The walk home took longer than necessary.

Not because of the body.

Body fine.

Hands fine.

Residual limb fine.

Because I kept thinking about the door.

It had happened.

I remembered the field.

The wall stones.

Jalen's burned glove.

The mule refusing to go near the thing afterward.

I remembered being thirty-something and already strong enough that a wandering warded door had been an irritation rather than a threat.

I had told the story for years.

Usually to adventurers.

They believed it because they had seen worse.

Today a group of ordinary Carrow people had heard the same truth and rejected it because the details were too stupid to be invented well.

That felt fair.

My first life contained too much material that sounded badly written.

The dragon who denied being a dragon.

The staircase.

The priest's shadow.

The duke's shoe in the ceremonial basket.

A fortress whose west wall had been built six feet inside the property line because two survey charms disagreed and neither mason wanted to be legally responsible for choosing.

All true.

Terrible lies.

I reached home.

Cala took the salt.

"Long."

"Tea law."

She nodded immediately.

That was worse.

"You know them?"

"Who?"

"Tea stall. Bel. Gray braid. Yellow scarf."

"Some."

"Of course."

Tavi was on the floor arranging three wooden blocks and one spoon into what appeared to be a defensive position.

Lyssa sat near the window with dark cloth across her lap.

Not green vest.

Still not my work.

She looked up.

"What happened?"

"I lost a lying contest by telling the truth."

She stared at me.

Then nodded.

"Yes."

"You have too much confidence in this outcome."

"What truth?"

"Door hunted me for three days."

She stopped sewing.

"What?"

Finally.

I told it again.

Shorter.

Door.

Ward.

Wall gone.

Inside and outside confused.

Camp pursuit.

Fire failure.

Mule.

Rebuilt wall.

Lyssa listened with her needle held still.

When I finished, she said, "That is true."

"Why does everyone know?"

"Because you explain too much."

"The explanation is the interesting part."

"That is why."

I considered lying about being offended.

Probably would not work.

Cala said, "Bel handsome?"

"Apparently not."

She laughed.

Tavi looked up.

"Door red."

I pointed at him.

"Finally, a reliable witness."

He returned to his fortification.

Later, after the salt was in its jar and the household had moved on, I found myself trying to construct a proper lie.

Something small.

Believable.

No dragons.

No cursed architecture.

No S-class disasters.

I tried:

I once owned a blue hat.

Too boring.

I once stole a blue hat.

Better.

I once stole a blue hat from a priest.

Too much.

The priest stole it back.

Now it sounded true.

Damn.

Lyssa passed behind me.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You are making the face."

"What face?"

"Thinking wrong."

"I am trying to lie better."

"Why?"

"Professional development."

She kept walking.

"Start smaller."

"That is what Gray-Braid said."

"Who?"

"I don't know."

That was true too.

The next afternoon I passed the tea stall again.

I did not stop.

Not because I was avoiding them.

I had somewhere else to be.

No appointment.

No job.

Just bread to bring home before Cala decided I had become unreliable around errands.

Bel saw me from his bucket.

"Door boy!"

I kept walking.

"I have a better one!"

"I don't believe you!"

The whole stall laughed.

I laughed too.

Then I turned the corner and bought the bread.

Nobody paid me.

Nobody hired me.

Nobody asked what rank I had been.

For twenty minutes, a dead watch-post door had been more interesting than I was.

That felt healthy.

On the way home I decided I would return someday with a proper lie.

Not tomorrow.

Not because the story needed it.

Just because Bel was going to regret teaching me the rules.

I had fifty-nine years of truth to work around.

Surely I could invent one decent chicken.
