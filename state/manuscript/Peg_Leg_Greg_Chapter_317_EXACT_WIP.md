# CHAPTER 317

## THE SINGING BRIDGE

The bridge did not sing until somebody lied to it.

That was not what happened.

It was what Sevren told me at breakfast.

I had learned from the tea stall that my lie detection was unreliable.

So I considered it carefully.

"What kind of lie?"

Sevren tore hard bread in half.

"Any."

"That seems technically expensive."

"Bridge knows."

"Does it know omission?"

"Yes."

"Sarcasm?"

"Especially."

"What if I sincerely believe something false?"

He chewed.

"Throws you in river."

I looked at Daro.

Daro drank tea.

"Don't encourage him."

"Which one?"

"Either."

Good morning.

The relay house fed us porridge before sunrise.

Included.

Still included.

I had no bits left to discover hidden breakfast law.

One copper remained in Carrow.

The absence of money in my pocket was oddly clean.

Not comfortable.

Clean.

Nothing to decide about buying because there was nothing to spend.

This was probably not a sustainable financial philosophy.

We loaded before the road light faded.

My body had tolerated the floor better than expected.

Back stiff for the first few steps.

Right hip tight.

Residual limb fine.

Hands fine.

Skin fine.

I walked the stiffness out while Sevren helped Daro check lashings.

Not my freight.

I stayed out of the work lane.

The young man with the wrapped bundle was named Corren because Daro finally asked whether Corren intended to carry the thing all the way to Bell Ford without ever setting it down.

Corren said yes.

Daro said, "Then stop leaning it on my wheel."

Names arrived through irritation.

Reliable system.

Corren's bundle remained his business.

We rolled east.

The country changed slowly.

Orchards thinned.

The road rose onto darker stone.

Small streams cut under it through square culverts.

Several culverts carried pale blue line marks on their upstream faces.

Flood gauges.

Pressure marks.

Water wards.

Some combination.

I knew enough to know I did not need to know.

At midmorning we heard the bridge.

Not singing.

Humming.

Low.

Far ahead.

A note held beneath the sound of wheels.

I sat straighter.

Sevren smiled without looking at me.

"Lie detector."

"I hate you."

The note did not change.

"See? True."

Corren laughed.

Daro said, "It is wind."

"Only wind?" I asked.

"Wind and bridge."

That was better.

Bell Ford appeared around a bend.

Town first.

Bridge second.

The town occupied both banks of a broad shallow river, though most buildings sat on the west side where the road widened into a market strip.

Stone foundations.

Wood upper floors.

Blue-gray roofs.

A squat relay tower near the river.

Two mills downstream.

The bridge was older than the road around it.

I could tell from the stone.

Three broad arches.

Dark piers.

Newer parapet repairs in lighter blocks.

Along each side ran a row of narrow bronze tongues mounted beneath the handrail.

Wind caught them.

Not randomly.

The bridge hummed in layers.

Low from the stone cavities.

Higher from bronze.

A third faint tone came and went beneath cart wheels.

I knew the family.

Resonance bridges.

Not rare.

Not magical instruments for entertainment, though people inevitably used them that way.

Load feedback.

Structural warning.

Sometimes flood warning.

Sometimes ice.

Sometimes all three badly combined by committees.

The exact Bell Ford system was local.

Daro slowed before the bridge.

A woman in a blue-gray coat stood beside a waist-high post at the entrance.

Not guard armor.

Road worker.

She held up one hand.

Daro stopped.

"Freight?"

He gave weight class and destination.

She looked at the cart.

Then touched two fingers to the post.

A pale line climbed it.

The bridge's low note changed.

Not louder.

Clearer.

She listened.

So did Daro.

So did everyone else who knew enough to stop talking.

I shut up.

The note settled.

The worker nodded.

"Center track. Slow."

Daro clicked to the striders.

We moved.

The first wheel touched bridge stone.

The hum changed under us.

I felt it through the bench before I understood it with my ears.

A vibration in the wood.

Then the bronze tongues answered.

Not melody.

Information shaped enough like music that people had named it singing.

Corren leaned toward me.

"If it screams, jump."

Daro said, "If it screams, stay on the cart."

Corren sat back.

"He ruins everything."

I watched the road worker walk beside us for the first arch.

Her eyes stayed on the cart wheels and the bronze line, not on our faces.

The second arch gave a slightly rougher note.

She glanced down at the parapet base.

No alarm.

Third arch smoother.

Then we were across.

The worker stopped walking.

Daro lifted two fingers.

She returned the gesture.

Done.

No toll at that station.

No lie test.

No river throwing.

I looked at Sevren.

"You owe me breakfast."

"Included."

"Tomorrow's breakfast."

"Maybe included."

Bell Ford east side was smaller.

Stable yard.

Relay house.

A row of warehouses.

Two inns.

One smithy whose sign was a hammer with three badly painted sparks.

Daro pulled into the relay yard.

Sevren became busy.

Not theatrically.

Work busy.

He took his brass-edged token inside.

Came out with a clerk.

They checked three seals on the freight canvas.

Daro opened one side.

The cargo turned out to be bundled reed panels, sacks of dried beans, and six long wooden cases.

I did not ask what was in the cases.

Growth.

Corren finally carried his wrapped bundle into the relay office.

It was apparently a clock face.

I learned this because the wrapping slipped and revealed painted numbers.

He swore.

The clerk swore.

The clock face survived.

No Greg required.

Sevren returned after nearly an hour.

"Done."

"Your face says not done."

"Return token after noon."

"So not done."

"Courier done. Waiting not done."

This distinction mattered to him.

We had the rest of the morning.

I had no money.

This made Bell Ford inexpensive.

We walked.

Sevren knew the town enough to avoid pretending he knew it deeply.

"Bread there. Expensive."

"Useful."

"Cheap stew there. Bad."

"More useful."

"Bridge office there. Don't bother them."

"You know me too well."

"Yes."

We crossed a smaller footbridge downstream.

No resonance system.

Just wood and iron braces.

The main bridge sang above us whenever a heavy cart crossed.

Different loads made different notes.

I began noticing patterns.

Empty wagon.

High and quick.

Loaded grain cart.

Lower, longer.

A four-animal timber wagon produced a deep vibration that made people near the river glance up.

Not fear.

Attention.

The blue-gray road worker at the west entrance raised a flag.

The timber wagon stopped before entering.

Another worker crossed from the east.

They spoke.

One went beneath the near parapet through a narrow maintenance stair.

I watched from the footbridge.

Sevren watched me watching.

"No."

"I am standing."

"Your face is asking questions."

"My face has rights."

"Not here."

The worker returned.

Flag lowered.

Timber wagon crossed slowly.

Bridge note stayed deep but clean.

That was all.

No failure.

No mystery.

Inspection happened because the system gave them a reason to inspect.

Then traffic continued.

I liked that more than I wanted to admit.

First life had contained too many bridges that were either perfectly ignored or dramatically collapsing.

Most real bridges spent their lives between those states.

Maintained.

Listened to.

Argued about.

Paid for badly.

We ate at the relay kitchen because my seat included one Bell Ford midday meal.

I confirmed.

Sevren groaned.

Stew.

Not bad.

Not memorable.

Ideal travel food.

A courier at the next table recognized Sevren.

"East again?"

"For now."

"Maro still taking south?"

"Last I heard."

"He owes me a strap."

"Tell Maro."

"I am telling you."

"That is why you don't have your strap."

Independent lives continued around me.

I knew none of these people.

Good.

The courier looked at my crutches once.

Then at Sevren.

"Passenger?"

"Passenger."

Done.

No rank.

No story.

No explanation.

After noon Sevren collected a dark blue return token shaped like a flattened ring.

Different from the Three Wells proof token.

I noticed.

Did not ask.

He caught me noticing.

"You are suffering."

"Deeply."

"Ask."

"No."

"Why?"

"Personal growth."

He laughed.

Daro's return freight would not leave until morning.

We had one night in Bell Ford.

The passenger bunk was included if space existed.

Space existed.

Barely.

Six bunks in a narrow upstairs room above the stable office.

I got the lower one because the keeper looked at my crutches, looked at the ladder to the upper bunks, and possessed common sense.

No speech.

No pity.

"Lower."

"Thank you."

Done.

I left my bag and walked alone before dark.

Not far.

The bridge drew me back.

Of course it did.

I stood on the west bank below the first arch.

Water moved over pale stones.

The bridge hummed above.

A child on the bank threw pebbles into the river.

His father repaired a fishing net.

Two women washed long strips of cloth downstream where the current slowed.

The resonance note shifted as carts crossed.

No one stopped to admire it.

They lived here.

That mattered.

I had once crossed a singing bridge during a siege while three enemy mages tried to break its center span.

Different bridge.

Different country.

The resonance wards screamed so loudly blood came from one soldier's ears.

We held the crossing.

Later I learned the bridge had never been designed for war.

It had been built to warn shepherds when spring flood undermined the western pier.

War had borrowed the system and called itself important.

I looked at Bell Ford's bridge.

No siege.

No screaming.

A timber wagon made workers check a pier.

A grain cart crossed.

A woman pushed a handcart of cabbages.

The bridge sang for all of them.

That felt closer to what the old builders probably intended.

Sevren found me there near sunset.

"Knew it."

"What?"

"Bridge."

"I am not investigating."

"You're under it."

"Admiring."

"Worse."

He sat on a flat stone.

I remained standing until my hands told me sitting was smarter.

Then I lowered myself beside him, right leg doing the work, left knee clear.

Crutches laid beside me.

The river smelled cold.

"Worth one bit?" Sevren asked.

I thought about it.

The hard cart.

The relay floor.

Bell Ford stew.

The humming bridge.

One copper waiting at home.

"Yes."

"Good."

"Do not become smug."

"Too late."

We sat until the light went blue.

Then the bridge lamps woke.

Small amber points along the parapet.

Not all at once.

One.

Then the next.

Then the next.

A simple chained lighting run.

Common.

Pretty anyway.

The bronze tongues caught evening wind.

For a moment the bridge actually did sound like music.

Not because anyone lied.

Not because magic demanded attention.

Because wind crossed metal over moving water and people had been clever enough to build a structure that could listen to itself.

Sevren stood.

"Come on."

I reached for my crutches.

"Tomorrow home?"

"Tomorrow west. Three Wells night if Daro pushes. Carrow day after."

"Good."

"You miss Cala?"

"I miss a bed."

"Lyssa?"

"I miss a bed."

"Tavi?"

"I miss silence."

Sevren nodded.

"Home."

That was annoyingly accurate.

We climbed back toward the relay yard.

Behind us, another cart entered the bridge.

The note deepened.

Held.

Then settled clean.

Nobody cheered.

Nobody needed to.

The bridge was doing its job.
