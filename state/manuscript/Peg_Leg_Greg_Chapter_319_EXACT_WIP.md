# CHAPTER 319

## THE MISSED COIN

I came home poorer than I left and discovered I had missed a chance to become less poor.

This felt organized.

Carrow appeared near midday.

Not dramatically.

No skyline reveal.

No music.

One moment fields and low walls.

Then more carts.

Then workshops.

Then the east gate.

The gate witness bead flashed beside Daro's board mark.

The road striders walked through without caring that I had returned to the center of my current world.

Sevren looked at me.

"Home."

"Apparently."

"Disappointed?"

I considered.

"No."

True.

Bell Ford had been worth the bit.

Three Wells had been familiar enough to feel like a waypoint now.

The relay floor had been terrible.

The empty peg had stayed somebody else's work problem.

The bridge had sung.

I was ready for a bed that belonged to a room I knew.

Daro pulled into the east freight yard.

We unloaded ourselves before anyone touched freight.

Crutches out from the leather loop.

Both tips sound.

Right foot down.

Hand on frame.

Left knee clear.

I stood.

My body objected to standing after the last long cart stretch.

Right hip tight.

Back stiff.

Nothing sharp.

Nothing wrong.

I waited three breaths before moving.

Daro noticed.

Said nothing.

Good man.

Sevren handed me my water bottle.

"Next time?"

There it was.

Not a seat.

Not a schedule.

A question.

"Ask if one exists."

"Still?"

"Still."

He nodded.

No promise.

No handshake.

No dramatic road-brother pact.

Then he pointed at my hair.

"Worse."

"Five days of road improved it."

"That is what worries me."

I left him in the freight yard.

Not because the friendship ended.

Because he had work.

I had home.

The walk from the yard took longer than usual.

Cart body and walking body were different governments.

My right thigh had spent days bracing against vibration.

Now it wanted to walk normally and had forgotten the paperwork.

I shortened my stride.

Let the crutches carry rhythm rather than speed.

No rush.

The brown shoulder bag felt almost weightless after road travel.

No water sack.

No freight.

No holdfast.

One spare shirt now smelled like relay house.

The city smelled familiar.

Bread.

Drain water.

Hot metal.

Somebody frying onions.

Horse.

Soap.

Too many people.

Home.

I reached the building.

The front door opened cleanly.

Still repaired.

The rain shelf remained a shelf.

The water line worked.

The household sack from Ch314 was back on its hook where ordinary objects went after refusing to become plot.

Cala was at the table sorting beans.

Tavi was under the table.

Some systems did not change.

Lyssa was out.

Cala looked up.

"Long."

"Five days."

"Long."

"You knew the trip."

"Still long."

Tavi crawled out.

He stared at me.

Then at my bag.

"Horse?"

"No horse."

"Blue horse."

"Still no."

He looked betrayed.

I had apparently failed to bring back an animal from a trip that had never involved one.

Cala pushed a cup of water toward me.

I drank.

House water.

No relay inclusion question required.

Luxury.

"Lyssa?"

"Work."

Good.

"Anything happen?"

Cala shrugged.

"Normal."

This was the answer travelers claimed to want and never believed.

I believed it.

Then she pointed at the shelf near the door.

"Paper."

A folded note sat beneath the little clay weight Cala used to keep drafts from stealing messages.

My name was on the outside.

GREG.

Not Hessa's hand.

Not Sevren's.

Not theatre.

I recognized the Vale office hand from Antonius's earlier note.

Ah.

I picked it up.

The date mark was two days old.

That was not ideal.

I opened it.

The note was short.

VALE OFFICE.

GROUND FLOOR.

TWO HOURS.

SEATED.

CASH, ONE COPPER.

COMPARE SIXTEEN SHIPPING TALLIES TO SEALED RECEIPT COPIES.

MARK MATCH / MISMATCH / UNREADABLE ONLY.

NO CAUSE ASSIGNMENT.

MATERIALS READY.

TODAY, THIRD TO FIFTH BELL.

REPLY YES / NO BEFORE SECOND BELL.

A. VALE

I read it twice.

Then looked at the date again.

Two days ago.

One copper.

Cash.

Not debt credit.

Exactly the kind of work I had told Antonius to offer if it genuinely appeared.

Exactly the kind of work I had not been in Carrow to accept.

I laughed once.

Cala looked at me.

"Bad?"

"No."

That was true.

Annoying.

Not bad.

"Work?"

"Was."

She nodded.

No sympathy performance.

No accusation.

I folded the note again.

My private money state was now brutally simple.

One copper upstairs.

No bits.

One missed chance at another copper.

Two copper still sitting in the shape of a fixed holdfast I had not used.

And I had spent the last bit on Bell Ford.

The math could tell a moral story if I let it.

Bad purchase.

Bad trip.

Missed work.

Poor Greg punished for pleasure.

That story was available.

It was also stupid.

The holdfast might still be a bad purchase.

I did not know.

Bell Ford had been worth the bit.

I knew that.

The missed job had existed whether the trip was wise or not.

I had told Antonius I could refuse cash work.

Being absent was an especially efficient refusal.

Still hurt a little.

One copper was one copper.

I put the note beside my cup.

"When came?"

Cala said, "Boy. Morning. Two days."

"Did he wait?"

"No."

"Say anything?"

"Need answer before second. I said gone east."

"Then they knew."

"Yes."

Good.

No mystery about why I failed to answer.

No office believing I had ignored them.

No need to rush across Carrow and explain myself unless I wanted to confirm future terms still stood.

I wanted to.

Not today.

My body wanted bath water, food, and horizontal architecture.

I went upstairs first.

The room looked smaller than Bell Ford's bunk room and better than every relay floor.

The holdfast remained on the high shelf.

Wrapped.

Tag visible.

DO NOT TURN RING.

I looked at it.

"You cost me two copper."

It did not apologize.

"And now I have one."

Still nothing.

"Good talk."

I took out the narrow scrap from my bag.

Magic notes intact.

Road dust on the fold.

No need to write Bell Ford bridge mechanics.

No need to write the empty peg.

No need to write that one copper had passed me while I was gone.

The note itself existed.

I put the Vale cash-work note under the earlier debt-credit receipt.

Not as debt.

Not as obligation.

Just record.

Then I changed shirts.

The road shirt went into the wash pile.

I checked the left residual limb properly.

Warm.

A little more red where the trouser fabric had rubbed near the upper edge during the last cart day.

Not broken skin.

Not painful.

No swelling.

Wash.

Dry.

Rest.

I had learned enough not to negotiate with friction after five days of road.

Right foot fine.

Right shoe fine.

Hands fine.

Crutches fine.

I went downstairs.

Cala had put food out.

Beans.

Of course.

I stared.

"No."

"What?"

"Road fed me beans."

"Beans good."

"That is not the issue."

Tavi said, "Beans."

The household united against me.

I ate.

Lyssa came home near evening.

She opened the door.

Saw me.

Stopped.

Then continued inside.

"Back."

"Apparently."

She set down her work bag.

"Worth it?"

Same question as after Three Wells.

This time I had more answer.

"Yes."

She nodded.

Then noticed the Vale note beside the clay weight.

"Work?"

"Missed."

"Road?"

"Road."

"Bad?"

I thought.

"Annoying."

"Same?"

"Not same."

She waited.

I explained.

Two hours.

One copper cash.

Offer expired two days ago.

Cala had told messenger I was east.

No blame known.

Lyssa sat.

"You wish stayed?"

That was the question.

Not whether I wanted the copper.

I absolutely wanted the copper.

"No."

The answer came quickly enough to surprise me.

Lyssa looked at me.

"Then okay."

"I can want both."

"Yes."

"I want both."

"Yes."

This was infuriatingly simple.

I had spent years in my first life pretending choices stopped being choices if I was strong enough.

Take the contract and make the festival.

Save the town and reach the dinner.

Earn the money and keep the friendship.

Fight all night and travel all day.

S-class power made many physical limits negotiable.

It did not create more hours.

I had forgotten that often.

Current Greg had fewer ways to lie to himself.

One bit bought Bell Ford.

Five days away meant five days not here.

A one-copper job had passed through one of those days.

Both facts could be true without one proving the other wrong.

Lyssa reached across the table and touched the edge of my road-worn bag.

"Dirty."

"That is road authenticity."

"Dirty."

"Professional opinion?"

"Bag opinion."

Callback law remained strong.

I smiled.

"Tomorrow I will clean it."

"Tomorrow you go Vale?"

"Probably."

"Ask?"

"Tell them I was gone. Confirm future cash offers still welcome."

"Good."

"Not ask for replacement job."

She nodded.

That distinction mattered.

I was not owed a new coin because I had missed one.

The next morning my right hip had loosened.

Back normal enough.

Residual-limb redness faded after sleep.

No injury.

I walked to the Vale office after breakfast.

Not early enough to look desperate.

Not late enough to make the trip pointless.

Antonius was at the ground-floor desk with three ledgers open.

He looked up.

"East."

Not a question.

"Cala told your messenger."

"She did."

"I saw the note."

"Work is done."

"I assumed."

"Mera clerk took it."

Mera clerk could be a person named Mera or a clerk from somewhere called Mera or a category I did not understand.

I did not care enough to ask.

Growth.

"I came to confirm something," I said.

Antonius waited.

"If another suitable cash task genuinely appears, I still want the terms sent. Missing this one because I was away does not change that."

"Fine."

"No standing availability."

"I know."

"No assumption I accept."

"Greg."

"Yes?"

"I know."

Fair.

I nodded.

He looked back at the ledger.

Conversation over.

No replacement job appeared from beneath the desk.

No pity copper.

No consolation task.

No lecture about reliability.

I left with exactly as much money as I had entered.

One copper at home.

Zero bits.

The walk back felt lighter anyway.

Not because the money problem had improved.

It had not.

Because the terms still existed.

Cash work might appear.

I might be there.

I might not.

I could accept.

I could refuse.

And sometimes I could spend the last bit I had to hear a bridge sing, then come home and discover the world had not paused politely while I was gone.

That seemed expensive.

It also seemed like living.
