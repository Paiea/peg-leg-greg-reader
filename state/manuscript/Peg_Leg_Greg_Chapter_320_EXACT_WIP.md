# CHAPTER 320

## THE PORTRAIT

The woman drawing me had forgotten my left foot.

This was accurate.

I still objected.

"You're staring," she said.

"You've omitted something."

Her charcoal stopped.

She looked at the board on her knees.

Then at me.

Then down.

"Oh."

"Exactly."

"I thought the cloth hid it."

"The cloth hides the end of the trouser leg."

"Yes."

"There is normally a foot after that."

She looked at my crutches.

Then at the space below my left knee.

Then back at the board.

"Not in your case."

"Strong recovery."

The corner of her mouth moved.

I had not agreed to be drawn.

That part should be established.

I had gone out to clean my bag.

Not in the street.

I had enough dignity left to avoid publicly washing leather.

The brown shoulder bag had survived Bell Ford, relay floors, road dust, one damp bench, and Lyssa's professional diagnosis of dirty.

The household rag removed the worst of it.

Cala had produced a little tin of leather grease from somewhere in the cupboard.

I did not ask how old it was.

Old household tins were a separate branch of archaeology.

I worked a small amount into the leather upstairs, wiped off the excess, and discovered the bag now smelled strongly enough to make the room unpleasant.

So I hung it near the open window.

Then I remembered Tavi.

I moved it higher.

Then I remembered Tavi could climb.

I moved it to the outer sill where the strap could be looped twice around the shutter hook.

This created a bag that was clean, protected, and impossible to use until the smell stopped trying to occupy the building.

I went out without it.

That felt wrong.

The narrow scrap stayed home.

Charcoal stayed home.

One copper stayed upstairs.

No bits existed to feel lonely.

I carried only my crutches and the household errand Cala had given me.

Return a covered bowl to the woman two buildings down.

Not the bean bowl from before.

Different bowl.

Different woman.

Carrow had achieved enough density that pottery now required qualifiers.

The return took four minutes.

The woman took the bowl, said, "Good," and closed the door.

No soup.

No message.

No secret compartment.

A successful bowl transaction.

I should have gone home.

Instead I kept walking because five days on a cart had made walking around my own streets feel oddly luxurious.

My right hip had stopped complaining.

Back normal.

Left side normal.

The city was warm without being vicious.

Market traffic had thinned after the midday rush.

I reached the little paved triangle where three streets met near the old cooper's row.

There were two benches.

One occupied by an old man asleep with his hat over his face.

The other occupied by paper.

Not metaphorically.

Sheets had been spread across half the bench beneath flat stones.

Heads.

Hands.

Shoes.

A dog.

Three different noses.

A kettle.

A woman leaning over a counter.

Quick charcoal studies, most no larger than my hand.

A young woman sat on a low folding stool in front of them with a board across her knees.

Dark hair tied badly at the back.

Gray shirt.

Green skirt.

Black charcoal on three fingers and one cheek.

She was drawing a boy holding a basket.

The boy was not holding still.

This appeared to be part of the exercise.

"Stop moving," she said.

"I'm not."

"You have moved six times."

"I breathed."

"You moved before breathing."

The boy considered this accusation.

Then moved again.

I stopped because one of the pages showed a crutch.

Not mine.

Different shape.

Underarm frame rather than forearm support.

The drawing was only the lower half of a person crossing the square.

One foot.

Two crutch tips.

A long coat caught behind one brace.

The weight distribution was good.

Not beautiful.

True.

The artist glanced at me.

Then at my crutches.

Then back at the boy.

I continued looking.

The boy said, "Done?"

"No."

"You said quick."

"Quick is relative."

"To what?"

"People who pay."

He frowned.

"You're not paying me."

"Exactly."

The old man on the other bench laughed without removing the hat from his face.

The boy finally escaped three minutes later.

He took his basket and left without seeing the drawing.

The artist turned the board around.

It showed his shoulder, basket, and the expression of a person being personally oppressed by standing still.

Good.

She set it on the bench.

Then looked at me.

"Sit?"

"No."

"Excellent. Stand."

I laughed.

"No."

"Also useful."

She reached for another sheet.

"Did I agree?"

"No."

"Then don't hold still."

That was new.

"What?"

"If you hold still, I will draw you. If you leave, I will draw somebody else."

The old man said from under his hat, "She drew me sleeping."

"You were very cooperative," the woman said.

"I was unconscious."

"Best kind."

I should have left.

Instead I stood there long enough to become responsible for my own consequences.

Her charcoal moved.

Fast.

Not face first.

Crutch.

Right hand.

Shoulder.

Line of torso.

Right leg.

Left trouser leg.

I watched.

Then she forgot the foot.

Which returned us to the beginning.

After my complaint, she rubbed the lower line with her thumb.

Not erasing.

Softening.

Then she redrew the hanging trouser shape more clearly.

The left knee existed.

Below it, cloth folded and pinned back.

No invented foot.

No dramatic empty space either.

Just the body I had.

"Better?" she asked.

"Yes."

"You're difficult."

"You tried to give me a hidden foot."

"I did not give you one."

"You implied one."

"That is a very specific grievance."

"I have experience."

The old man laughed again.

The artist looked at my face now.

That was less comfortable.

People looked at faces constantly.

Being watched was ordinary.

Being measured by looking was different.

Hessa looked at movement.

Lyssa looked at fit.

Theatre people looked at whether I was about to miss an entrance, block a sightline, or fall into furniture.

This woman looked at shape.

I shifted my right foot.

She changed the line.

"Don't pose."

"I'm standing."

"You changed your shoulder."

"My shoulder became aware of itself."

"Tell it to stop."

"Unreliable employee."

Charcoal scratched.

A woman carrying onions slowed.

"Soldier?"

I looked at her.

"No."

She pointed with her chin toward the drawing.

"Looks like one."

The artist said, "It has no sword."

"Soldiers sit too."

"I'm not sitting."

The onion woman looked at me.

Then at the crutches.

Then apparently decided this conversation required more effort than onions deserved.

She left.

I looked at the artist.

"Do I look like a soldier?"

"No."

"That answer was fast."

"You look like a man standing in a square arguing about whether his missing foot was sufficiently represented."

"Good."

"Very martial."

She worked.

I had been a warrior.

That was the stupid part.

Not a soldier, exactly.

Different systems.

Different life.

Enough fighting that the distinction became academic to anyone being stabbed.

If she had drawn first-life me, there would have been armor.

Weapons.

More scars.

Both legs.

A face old enough to have learned when not to argue with charcoal artists.

Current me had a gray shirt, worn trousers, two crutches, one right shoe, and hair Sevren had recently judged worse.

The artist narrowed her eyes at the hair.

I became suspicious.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"That was a hair look."

"Your hair has direction."

"It has several."

"Yes."

"Road."

"Did the road attack it?"

"Repeatedly."

She smiled and kept drawing.

A little girl arrived with a woman who might have been her mother.

The girl went straight to the pages.

"Dog."

"Yes," the artist said.

"Whose?"

"Dog's."

The girl accepted this.

She picked up a page.

The artist immediately said, "Edges."

The girl held it by the edges.

Apparently not her first visit.

The mother looked at me.

Then at the drawing in progress.

"You're doing standing today?"

"I'm doing whoever stops."

"Bad business model."

"No business."

That explained the lack of price board.

The mother set a wrapped piece of bread beside the artist's stool.

"Eat."

"Later."

"Now."

"Later."

The mother looked at me.

I raised both hands as far as crutches allowed.

"Not my jurisdiction."

"Smart."

She left.

The artist stared at the bread.

Then at the charcoal.

Then put the charcoal down.

"Apparently break."

"Strong management."

"She doesn't manage me."

The old man said, "She brought the bread."

"Traitor."

She ate.

I sat on the free end of the bench because standing through somebody else's lunch was absurd.

The pages remained between us.

Up close, the drawings were less polished.

Some lines doubled.

Hands failed.

One face had been rubbed almost through the paper.

A shoe study had six attempts around it.

The good ones had survived selection bias.

That made me like them more.

"Learning?" I asked.

She swallowed.

"Always."

"Profession?"

"Sometimes signs. Sometimes shop cards. Sometimes copied borders. Portraits when people pay enough to discover they hate their own noses."

"Do they?"

"Usually."

"Why draw people free?"

"Hands."

I looked at the pages.

"Many heads."

"Heads are attached to hands if you wait."

Fair.

She took another bite.

"Also people move."

"Known problem."

"Paid portraits lie. Free people forget to."

I looked at the boy with the basket.

He had definitely forgotten.

"What's your name?"

"Senna."

"Greg."

"I know."

That got my attention.

She saw it.

"Not like that."

"Excellent clarification."

"Tea stall."

Of course.

"Bel?"

"Bel talks."

"Bel lies."

"Same mouth."

I considered the social consequences of losing a lying contest with a true story.

"How much did she tell you?"

"That a crutch man told the worst lie she'd heard all week and insisted it was true."

"I did not insist."

"Better."

"Did she mention the door?"

Senna looked at me.

"There was a door?"

I closed my eyes.

Good.

Networked reputation.

Accurate in the important ways.

"No."

"Now I want the door."

"You are not getting the door."

"Then I know exactly enough."

She finished the bread.

The old man sat up at last.

He had a white mustache and the face from one of the pages.

The drawing had improved him by making him look awake.

He pointed at my crutches.

"She'll make those crooked."

Senna said, "I drew yours straight."

"I don't have crutches."

"Exactly."

He considered.

Then put the hat back over his face.

Senna resumed.

This time I stayed seated.

She started a second drawing without asking.

I objected on principle.

"You already have one."

"Standing."

"I remain the same person."

"Debatable."

"Philosophy costs extra."

"No business."

The second drawing began with my hands.

Right hand resting over the crutch grip.

Left loose.

Then knees.

Right foot.

Crutch tips angled away.

My body when seated had a different geometry.

Obvious.

Still strange to see somebody else notice it without commentary.

She drew the residual limb only as the trouser shape showed it.

No emphasis.

No hiding.

Then face.

I looked away toward the street.

"Better," she said.

"Rude."

"Your face stops helping when you forget it exists."

"I was helping?"

"Badly."

A cart passed.

Two women argued over a bundle of reeds.

A man carrying three empty bird cages stopped to retie the top one.

Nobody cared that I was being drawn.

That helped.

Senna worked for maybe ten minutes.

Maybe fifteen.

I did not count.

When she turned the board, I recognized the crutches first.

Then the shoulders.

Then my right shoe.

Then me.

Not heroic.

Not damaged.

Not handsome enough to be suspicious.

Tired around the eyes.

Hair genuinely criminal.

One side of my mouth slightly higher because I had apparently been trying not to make a joke.

I stared.

"That is unfortunate."

"Your nose?"

"My hair."

"I told you."

"Sevren told me."

"Who?"

"Road friend."

"Then road friend has eyes."

I looked again.

There was something deeply offensive about being recognizable from the outside.

First-life portraits had been formal things.

Rank.

Armor.

Guild marks.

Weapons positioned to tell the viewer what kind of dangerous idiot had paid for the wall.

Later, after S-class, people had made images I never sat for.

Those were worse.

Broader shoulders.

Cleaner scars.

Jaw apparently forged from civic responsibility.

One had given me a sword I had never owned.

Another had removed a scar because the patron thought it made me look cruel.

Current Greg had no patron.

No rank worth painting.

No reason for a public image.

Senna had still caught the way I sat.

I pointed.

"Right shoulder is lower."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it is."

I checked myself.

Crutch habits.

Road stiffness gone, but not every asymmetry belonged to injury.

"Do I always?"

"I've known you twenty minutes."

Excellent.

No diagnosis.

No revelation.

Just a line.

The little girl returned.

Alone this time.

She looked at the drawing.

Then at me.

Then at the drawing.

"That's him."

Senna said, "Good."

The girl pointed at my left side.

"Where leg?"

"Not there," I said.

She looked.

"Why?"

There it was.

Children were efficient.

"Lost it."

"Where?"

I thought about giving her the truthful answer.

Which version?

First-life reincarnation complications were irrelevant.

Current-life injury had an answer.

The answer was not a square conversation.

"Not somewhere I can go get it."

She considered.

"Okay."

Then she pointed at the old man's portrait.

"He dead?"

The old man removed his hat.

"No."

She jumped.

Senna laughed hard enough to smudge the edge of my page.

The girl ran three steps, realized she had dignity, and walked the rest.

The old man said, "Worth waking up."

Senna wiped her thumb on a cloth.

"Now you're smudged."

"Adds mystery."

"It adds charcoal."

She set the drawing aside.

I expected her to hand it to me.

She did not.

Good.

I also wanted it.

Bad.

"Do you keep them?" I asked.

"Most."

"Why?"

"Practice."

"Right."

She looked at me.

"You want it."

"No."

"You looked at it three times after I stopped showing you."

"Evidence inflation."

"What?"

"Nothing."

She picked up the page.

"One copper."

I stared at her.

She stared back.

The old man began laughing before either of us spoke.

"Absolutely not."

"Then I keep it."

"You said no business."

"Portraits when people pay enough to hate their noses."

"That is extortion."

"You approached my bench."

"I approached paper."

"You stayed."

"You drafted me without consent."

"You could leave."

"You are using my own arguments against me."

"I met you twenty minutes ago. There aren't many yet."

I looked at the page.

One copper.

My entire liquid fortune.

For charcoal Greg.

No.

Not even close.

The fact that I wanted it did not improve the arithmetic.

"Keep it."

She nodded once.

No bargaining.

No reduced price.

No pity gift.

She slid it under a flat stone with the others.

That was somehow worse.

I could still see half my right shoe.

"Cruel."

"Poor."

"Also true."

She picked up the charcoal again.

"Tomorrow it may be two copper."

"Tomorrow I may steal it."

"Then I draw wanted notices."

"With the hair?"

"Especially the hair."

I stood.

Carefully.

No road stiffness now.

Crutches planted.

Right foot under me.

Balance set.

Senna watched the movement.

Not clinically.

Not politely looking away.

Just drawing again.

"You're doing another."

"Leaving."

"I object."

"Leave faster."

I did.

At the corner I looked back.

She was drawing the old man again.

He had fallen asleep.

My page stayed under the stone.

I went home.

The bag smell had improved.

Lyssa was at the table trimming thread ends from something dark that was not the green vest.

I did not ask.

She looked at me.

"Hair."

"What about it?"

"Bad."

"This is becoming coordinated."

Cala looked over from the stove.

"Cut."

"No."

"Cut."

"I have received enough public criticism."

Lyssa smiled.

"Public?"

I told them about Senna.

Not every detail.

Artist.

Square.

Free drawing.

One copper if I wanted the seated portrait.

I had refused.

Lyssa stopped trimming.

"One copper?"

"Exactly."

"All money."

"Exactly."

"Good."

"I know."

"Show?"

"I did not buy it."

"Tomorrow."

"No."

She looked at me.

I knew that look.

"Do not go buy my face with your money."

"Why?"

"Because it is my bad financial decision to make."

"Then make."

"No."

"Then I can."

"No."

Cala said, "Who Senna?"

I described her.

Gray shirt.

Green skirt.

Charcoal cheek.

Paper all over the bench.

Cala nodded.

"Sign girl."

Of course Cala knew.

"She does signs?"

"Fish shop. Blue letters."

Senna had said signs.

Still irritating when the city confirmed people without me.

Lyssa said, "Good signs?"

Cala shrugged.

"Blue."

A rigorous review.

I cleaned the rest of the grease from the bag strap.

Lyssa finished her thread ends.

Nobody bought the portrait.

The next morning I passed the triangle on purpose.

This was not the same as returning for the portrait.

Intent mattered.

Senna was there.

Different shirt.

Same skirt.

No charcoal on cheek yet.

The old man was absent.

The bench held new pages.

My seated portrait was gone.

I stopped.

Senna looked up.

"Morning."

"Morning."

I looked at the papers.

Not there.

Good.

Fine.

Completely reasonable.

"Sold?" I asked.

"No."

"Then where?"

She pointed behind her.

A cord had been strung between two posts.

Six studies hung from clips.

Boy with basket.

Sleeping old man.

Woman with onions.

Dog.

Me.

A hand holding a cup.

My portrait moved slightly in the breeze.

Public.

I stared.

Senna said, "Practice display."

"You have weaponized not buying it."

"No."

"People can see it."

"That is generally why drawings are displayed."

A man walking past glanced at the cord.

Then at me.

Then back at the drawing.

Recognition took one second.

He smiled.

"That's you."

I looked at Senna.

She was already drawing his hand.

"Apparently," I said.

He kept walking.

No soldier.

No hero.

No famous Greg.

Just a man who had seen a face on a cord and then found the same face standing beside it.

I looked at the portrait again.

The charcoal version of me looked like he had nowhere urgent to be.

That might have been the strangest part.

No job.

No test.

No debt hour.

No road seat.

No stage entrance.

No thing to solve before somebody died.

Just sitting in a square because I had stopped walking.

I almost explained that to myself.

Then I remembered I hated when stories did that.

So I stopped.

Senna looked up.

"You posing again?"

"No."

"Good."

I left before my face could become inventory.

At home, the bag no longer smelled like a tannery had died in it.

I put the narrow scrap and wrapped charcoal back inside.

The one copper remained upstairs.

The portrait remained in the square.

For once, both felt like they were exactly where they belonged.
