# CHAPTER 322

## THE BIDDER

The first expensive thing I wanted in Carrow belonged to a dead mason.

That was not why I wanted it.

It did improve the price.

I found the sale because half of Cooper Street had decided to stand in my way.

There were carts at both ends of the block, a chalk board tied to a post, and enough people under the awnings to make the middle of the street useless to anyone with crutches and standards.

I stopped before entering the crowd.

My shoulder bag sat high and close. Narrow scrap inside. Wrapped charcoal inside. One copper upstairs at home because carrying every coin I owned through a crowd was unnecessary, even when every coin I owned was currently one coin.

That fact had become less dramatic once I stopped narrating it to myself.

I could afford lunch.

I could not afford a building.

Civilization contained many intermediate states.

A man beside the chalk board said, "Viewing until second bell. First lots after."

I looked at the board.

HOUSE CLEARANCE.

TOOLS. FIXTURES. STONE GEAR. HOUSE GOODS.

Then, lower down in different chalk:

ONE REGISTERED FIELD PIECE.

That was why the street was crowded.

I had been heading nowhere important.

This was dangerous.

I moved closer.

The sale occupied the ground floor of a narrow stone-front house with its shutters propped open. Ordinary things filled the front room. Three planes. Two saws. A box of chisels. Lamp brackets. A folding work table. Kitchen pots. A cracked wash basin somebody had still bothered to number.

Nothing about the room suggested treasure.

That helped.

The people suggested treasure.

Two men in work coats stood near the back without touching anything. A woman with a leather account case kept checking the same folded paper. A broad man wearing a guild-blue cord had positioned himself where he could see both the rear table and everyone approaching it.

He looked at me once.

Then at my crutches.

Then away.

Good.

I preferred being underestimated for reasons that did not require effort.

The rear table held one object.

It was not impressive.

A dark metal sleeve lay on gray felt, about the length of my forearm and open along one side. Four narrow ribs ran from wrist end to elbow end. The inner surface was lined with old brown leather. A dull red stone sat in a shallow socket near the outside edge.

Beside it lay a paper tag.

REGISTERED IMPACT BRACE.

ESTATE OF ORREN VALEK, MASON.

FUNCTION DEMONSTRATED.

NO CURRENT SERVICE MARK.

SOLD AS SEEN.

I stopped three steps away.

Not because I recognized it.

Because I almost did.

That was worse.

The rib spacing bothered me.

Three narrow. One wide.

The red stone was too far toward the elbow for most common catch-braces I remembered.

And there was a tiny stamped mark under the second rib.

I could not read it from where I stood.

The guild-cord man noticed me looking.

"Don't touch."

"Wasn't going to."

"Everyone says that."

"Then your sign is working."

There was no sign.

He looked at the table.

Then at me again.

"Viewing only until demonstration."

"What does it do?"

He gave me the patient expression of a man answering the same question for the fiftieth time.

"Takes impact through the brace. Mason used it for bad stone and overhead break."

"How much impact?"

"Demonstration at second bell."

"That's not a number."

"Correct."

I liked him slightly more.

I shifted left so I was not blocking anyone.

A woman behind me stepped forward, read the tag, and said, "No service mark?"

The guild man said, "Last shop closed. Estate couldn't produce later papers. Registration is clean."

"Recharge?"

"Unknown."

"Repair?"

"Unknown."

She made a face and moved away.

Useful.

The market was not blind.

A field piece that did something valuable could still be a terrible purchase if nobody could keep it doing that valuable thing.

I looked again at the ribs.

Three narrow.

One wide.

Something in my first life moved.

Not memory exactly.

Recognition before memory.

I had seen that spacing on a wall.

No.

On armor.

No.

A broken door frame in Red Fen, maybe.

I hated this part.

People imagined living twice would make memory clean.

It mostly gave me twice as many places to misfile things.

A man with a gray beard leaned over the felt without touching it.

"Valek piece?"

The guild man nodded.

"His?"

"Estate says."

"He used it on North Cut."

"Estate says."

"Heard it saved his arm."

"Probably why he kept using it."

Gray Beard laughed.

Then lowered his voice.

"Heard it broke Jeren's hand."

The guild man did not laugh.

"I don't have that in the estate record."

"Wasn't his hand."

"Then I especially don't have it in the estate record."

Gray Beard moved on.

I stayed very still.

Saved his arm.

Broke somebody else's hand.

That could mean almost anything.

A mason wearing a brace might have punched a coworker.

People remained people even around magic.

But the rib spacing bothered me more now.

I stepped away from the table.

This was deliberate.

The broad man with the blue cord had already seen me stare too long.

I inspected chisels.

They were chisels.

One had a mushroomed head that should have been dressed down years ago.

I inspected a plane.

The sole was warped.

I inspected the cracked wash basin.

Still cracked.

My performance of indifference was becoming expensive in dignity.

Someone beside me said, "You buying the basin?"

"Strong possibility."

He looked at the crack.

"Why?"

"I have enemies."

He moved away.

Excellent.

Second bell sounded somewhere beyond the roofs.

The crowd tightened around the rear half of the room.

I stayed near the wall where I had space for both crutches.

A woman in a dark vest climbed onto the low front step.

"House goods first. Field piece after lot twenty-two. If you're here only for the brace, don't crush my pot buyers."

Several people shifted guiltily.

I liked her immediately.

The first lots went quickly.

Pots.

Two lamp brackets.

The warped plane sold, proving markets contained mysteries greater than magic.

I watched bidders more than goods.

Guild-blue cord bought the box of chisels.

Account-case woman bought nothing.

Gray Beard disappeared before lot twelve.

Two work-coat men remained near the back.

One whispered numbers to the other.

Not treasure hunters, probably.

Trade buyers.

That mattered.

By lot eighteen my right hand wanted a break. I shifted to the wall, rested one crutch against my hip, flexed my fingers, then changed hands carefully.

No heroics required.

The auctioneer sold the cracked basin.

I felt betrayed.

Then she said, "Twenty-three. Registered impact brace from the Valek estate. Demonstration before bidding. Give Rusk room."

The guild-cord man was Rusk.

Good. One fewer nameless person in the world.

He cleared the rear table and brought out a thick square of timber already scarred from previous abuse.

Then a wooden practice arm.

That was sensible.

The arm had a jointed elbow, a leather-wrapped forearm, and a heavy base.

Rusk fitted the metal sleeve over it.

The brace closed with two ordinary straps.

No flash.

No hum.

The red stone stayed dull.

He positioned the practice arm over the timber block.

"Brace was demonstrated for estate registration three days ago," he said. "Same procedure. Same striking hammer. No claim beyond demonstrated function."

The auctioneer lifted a long-handled hammer.

The crowd gave her more room.

Smart crowd.

She handed it to Rusk.

He checked the brace once.

Then struck the practice forearm.

Hard.

The impact made half the room flinch.

The timber block jumped.

The practice arm did not break.

The red stone flashed once.

Not bright.

Deep red, almost black at the center.

The four ribs tightened.

I heard it.

A short metal click from each rib, not at the moment of impact but just after.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

My stomach dropped.

I knew that sequence.

Not the brace.

The sequence.

I had heard it under a gate in the first life while a man named Teren screamed at everyone to stop hitting the door.

We had not stopped hitting the door.

We had been twenty-three and extremely committed to solving problems with force.

The door had opened.

The wall beside it had also opened.

Later, much later, somebody explained why.

Catch ribs.

Load taken in stages.

Then held.

Or transferred.

Or something adjacent to both.

I remembered the argument better than the explanation.

Teren had called us animals.

We had objected because one of us was technically an elf.

Not useful.

I watched the brace.

The red stone faded slowly.

Rusk waited.

Nobody moved.

Then he struck again, lighter.

The brace took it.

This time the ribs clicked almost together.

Different.

I did not know why.

That was important.

The auctioneer said, "Demonstrated against two direct strikes. Estate makes no claim for side load, blade load, repeated field use, remaining service life, recharge, or repair. Opening at six copper."

Six.

My private wealth was in another building and insufficient by a factor I did not need currency conversion to appreciate.

A work-coat man raised two fingers.

"Six."

Account Case said, "Seven."

Guild-blue cord did not bid.

Interesting.

The second work-coat man said, "Eight."

The auctioneer looked around.

I did nothing.

Not because I was cleverly hiding my interest.

Because I had one copper.

There were limits to theatre.

"Nine," Account Case said.

The first work-coat man leaned toward the second.

Whisper.

"Ten."

That was already more money than anything I had personally purchased in this life by enough margin to change the shape of the thought.

And I still wanted it.

Not at ten because I knew its value.

I did not.

I knew one sound.

One rib pattern.

One memory of a door and a wall and Teren swearing at us.

That was not appraisal.

It was a direction.

The market knew the brace could absorb a hammer strike.

That alone was worth real money to masons, guards, breakers, anyone who liked keeping forearms attached.

If I was right about the rib family, the visible function might be the least interesting part.

If I was wrong, it was an old impact brace with no service shop.

Both possibilities were expensive.

"Eleven," Account Case said.

One work-coat man shook his head.

The other stopped bidding.

The auctioneer waited.

"Eleven once."

Rusk's eyes moved over the room.

For half a second they stopped on me.

I looked at the cracked basin's former spot.

There was no basin.

My acting lacked props.

"Eleven twice."

A voice from the doorway said, "Twelve."

Everyone turned.

A thin older woman stood just inside the door wearing a rain-gray coat despite the dry weather.

She had not been there for the demonstration.

Account Case frowned.

"Thirteen."

The older woman said, "Fourteen."

Immediately.

No hesitation.

That changed the room.

Account Case closed her mouth.

The work-coat men looked at each other.

Rusk watched the older woman.

I watched everyone.

Fourteen copper.

For a field piece with no current service mark and uncertain repair.

The market was not treating it like junk.

Good.

That would have been stupid.

The question was why the woman thought fourteen was acceptable without seeing the demonstration.

Maybe she already knew the piece.

Maybe she knew Valek.

Maybe she was an idiot with money.

All remained possible.

"Fourteen once."

No bid.

"Twice."

Nothing.

"Sold."

The older woman did not smile.

That annoyed me more than if she had celebrated.

The crowd loosened.

People immediately began talking as though they had all predicted fourteen.

I stayed where I was.

The woman went to the front to settle the purchase.

Rusk removed the brace from the practice arm.

The red stone was dull again.

I watched his hands.

He did not touch the stone.

He loosened the upper strap first.

Then lower.

Then, before lifting the brace, he pressed the wide fourth rib with his thumb.

A tiny click.

Not activation.

Maybe release.

Maybe habit.

Maybe nothing.

I knew how to check a catch assembly once.

Or I had.

You loaded a thread of mana into the first rib and watched where the return came out.

No.

That was for bridge locks.

For braces you touched the housing and bled current across the seam.

Maybe.

I could picture my own older hand doing something with two fingers and a copper probe.

I could not picture what happened next.

And current me could barely produce enough clean external effect under Hessa's supervision to rotate a marked strip once out of three tries.

So even if I remembered perfectly, which I did not, I was missing the part where the person performing the test had mana worth mentioning.

Useful limitation.

I waited until the older woman had paid.

Then I approached Rusk.

Not the buyer.

"That fourth rib," I said.

He looked at me.

"What about it?"

"You pressed it before removal."

His expression changed slightly.

Not alarm.

Recalculation.

"Unload check."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning I check it before I take it off the frame."

"Because?"

"Because I like my fingers."

That was a warning.

A good one.

I nodded.

"Fair."

He waited for the next question.

I did not ask whether the brace could hold force.

If he knew, I learned nothing by revealing why I cared.

If he did not know, I had just improved somebody else's appraisal.

Instead I said, "Who would service something like that if the original shop is gone?"

Rusk glanced toward the buyer.

She was still at the front table.

"Depends what it is."

"Tag says impact brace."

"Tag says what estate can prove."

I liked him considerably more.

"And if someone wanted more than the tag?"

"Pay an assessor."

"Any assessor?"

He snorted.

"Any baker can also make shoes."

"Badly."

"Exactly."

He started folding the gray felt.

I shifted one crutch so a departing bidder could pass behind me.

"Who reads old field hardware?"

Rusk looked at me again.

"You buying old field hardware?"

"I own one bad tool."

"That wasn't my question."

"Then no."

He smiled once.

Small.

"Mara Quill reads old housings and maker work. Doesn't certify combat use. Doesn't promise function. Doesn't touch bonded pieces."

A name.

Not a solution.

Good.

"Where?"

"South registry row, three mornings a week if she's there."

"Expensive?"

"Compared to what?"

I considered this.

"A cracked basin."

He stared at me.

"More."

"Tragic."

I left before asking too much.

Outside, the street felt wider even though the crowd had spilled into it.

I moved toward home slowly.

Fourteen copper.

The number did not feel impossible.

That was new.

It felt far.

There was a difference.

Early in this life, two copper had been an event because almost everything was an event.

Now I had paid two copper for a holdfast because I wanted the tool, spent bits on food and travel, missed a one-copper job because I had chosen to be five days east, and stood in a room where fourteen copper had bought something I might have wanted very badly if I knew more.

I did not need fourteen copper tomorrow.

The brace was gone.

That mattered too.

Money after the sale could not buy the sold thing.

I had spent most of my first life thinking about equipment after I needed it.

Then, once I had money, I had thought about equipment before I needed it.

That transition had been quiet enough that I had forgotten it was a transition.

Current me had one copper upstairs.

More importantly, current me had no reserve for surprise.

That bothered me.

Not because I was poor.

Because I had just seen what being unready cost.

At home, Tavi was in the courtyard arguing about gates again.

I passed without jurisdiction.

Lyssa sat near the window with dark cloth across her lap.

Still not the green vest.

She looked up.

"You bought something."

"No."

"You look like bought."

"I look like failed to buy."

"Worse."

"Much."

I went upstairs.

The copper sat where I had left it.

I looked at it.

One copper remained one copper.

Useful.

Spendable.

Not sacred.

Also not fourteen.

I took out the narrow scrap and wrapped charcoal.

Under the holdfast notes, I wrote:

OLD FIELD HARDWARE

MARA QUILL

SOUTH REGISTRY ROW

Then, after a moment:

RIBS 3 NARROW / 1 WIDE

RED STONE

UNLOAD CHECK 4TH RIB

14 C SOLD

I stopped there.

I did not write what the brace did beyond what I had seen.

Two hammer strikes absorbed on a practice arm.

That was real.

The four clicks were real.

My memory was not nothing.

It was also not enough.

I put the charcoal away.

Across the room, high above my clothes, the wrapped holdfast sat on sailcloth with its ring sideways and its warning tag visible.

DO NOT TURN RING.

Two copper.

Bought because I had seen enough to think it worth owning and not enough to trust it.

Maybe that had been the beginning of something.

Not collecting.

That sounded too clean.

Preparedness, maybe.

Optionality.

A bag of bad decisions with good reasons.

I liked the last one better.

Downstairs, Tavi shouted that somebody had moved gate.

I ignored him.

For the first time in several days, I had something more expensive to think about.
