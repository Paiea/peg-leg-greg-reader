# CHAPTER 324

## THE PRICE

Mara Quill charged two copper to tell you she did not know.

I respected that immediately.

I could not afford it.

I respected that less.

South Registry Row was not really a row. It was three crooked streets pretending they had agreed on a direction, all of them crowded with narrow shops whose signs assumed the reader already knew why they were there.

SEALS.

WEIGHTS.

OLD MARKS.

BONDS COPIED.

STONE RECORDS.

NO CLAIMS AFTER THIRD BELL.

That last one seemed less like a service and more like accumulated trauma.

I had left the fixed holdfast at home.

That had been deliberate.

The black Kiln yard chit stayed in the inner fold of my shoulder bag. The narrow scrap and wrapped charcoal stayed where they belonged. My one copper stayed upstairs.

I had come to learn what a question cost before carrying a two-copper object across half of Carrow to ask it.

This was progress.

In my first life I had once carried a cracked ward plate six miles to a man who charged me for opening the wrapping.

Then he charged me again for closing it.

I had been twenty-three and furious.

At fifty-nine I would have called that a cheap lesson.

The shop with Mara Quill's name was easy to miss because it did not have her name on it.

The sign said:

OLD HOUSINGS

MARKS READ

REGISTRY COPIES

NO BONDED WORK

Underneath, in smaller letters:

IF YOU WANT A PROMISE, BUY A PRIEST.

I stopped.

Then I went inside.

The room was long and shallow. One counter. Three stools. Shelves behind wire screens. A square window high on the back wall letting in enough light to make dust visible and nothing else attractive.

An old woman sat behind the counter with a lens strapped over one eye.

Not old-old.

Older than Lyssa. Younger than my first corpse.

Gray threaded through black hair pulled tight behind her head. Brown vest. White shirt. Ink on two fingers.

She was holding a brass hinge in wooden tongs.

I waited.

She turned it once.

Twice.

Set it down.

"No."

The man on the stool across from her said, "You didn't even open it."

"Correct."

"Then how do you know?"

"Know what?"

"That it isn't Rellan."

"I don't."

He stared at her.

She stared back.

"You said no."

"I said no to opening it."

"Why?"

"Because the pin is newer than the housing, the left leaf was filed after the stamp, and you told me it came sealed from your grandfather's wall. One of those things may be true. I do not need to open the hinge to know I dislike the story."

The man looked offended on behalf of his grandfather.

"He didn't lie."

"Then somebody lied to him."

"Can you tell me if it's Rellan?"

"Two copper for a mark read. Four if I have to expose the seat. More if you make me regret agreeing."

"Four?"

"Five now."

He stood.

"You're impossible."

"Frequently."

He took the hinge.

She added, "Do not sell it as sealed grandfather provenance."

"I know what I own."

"Then why did you come here?"

He left.

I moved aside for him.

He did not thank me.

Mara Quill lifted the lens from her eye.

"Next."

I looked behind me.

Nobody.

"Apparently me."

"Apparently."

I sat on the nearest stool.

She looked at my empty hands.

Then my bag.

Then my face.

"What did you leave at home?"

That was annoyingly good.

"A question."

"Those travel well."

"The object attached to it doesn't."

"Better. What kind?"

"Old fixed holdfast. Maybe."

Her expression did not change.

"Maybe fixed?"

"Fixed. Maybe holdfast."

"Who called it that?"

"Seller called it a holdfast."

"Seller called himself handsome too, I assume."

"He did not."

"Missed opportunity."

I liked her less than Jessa.

Possibly because she was winning.

"What do you charge?" I asked.

She pointed at a board on the wall beside me.

I had walked past it.

Excellent start.

MARK READ: 2 C

HOUSING READ: 3 C

OPEN SEAT: 4 C + MATERIALS

REGISTRY COPY: BY PAGE

PROVENANCE SEARCH: BY HOUR

FUNCTION CLAIM: NO

COMBAT CLAIM: NO

BONDED PIECE: NO

PURCHASE OFFER: SOMETIMES

I read the last line twice.

Mara watched me do it.

"That line causes trouble," she said.

"I imagine that's why you kept it."

"It saves longer trouble."

"Two copper just to read a mark?"

"If it takes less than ten minutes, yes."

"And if you don't know it?"

"Still two copper."

"That's excellent work."

"It took thirty years to make not knowing useful."

Fair.

I leaned one crutch against the stool.

"What does two copper buy me?"

"A description of what I can actually support from the visible mark. Maker if I know it. Shop if I know it. Period if the mark changed over time. Whether the stamp looks cut, struck, copied, recut, or married to a different housing. Sometimes what trade used that form. Sometimes nothing useful at all."

"No function."

"Not from a mark."

"No condition."

"Not from a mark."

"No value."

"Not from a mark."

"You have built an impressive business around disappointment."

"People pay more for confidence. They should pay more for limits."

That sounded like something Hessa would say if Hessa had discovered invoices.

I looked at the board again.

Two copper.

My holdfast had cost two.

That did not make the assessment overpriced.

It made the object cheap enough that assessing it could double my money committed before I learned anything.

Different problem.

"Do people bring you things worth less than the read?"

"Every week."

"Why?"

"Because they think worth is hiding inside the object and I can pull it out with a lens."

"Can you?"

"Sometimes."

"That seems dangerous to admit."

"You don't have two copper."

I looked at her.

"Rude."

"Accurate?"

"Also rude."

She waited.

I said, "I have one."

"Then don't bring me the object."

That answer came too quickly.

"You don't even know what it is."

"You have one copper. The object is at home. You came here first to ask what information costs. That means spending your only copper matters to you and you are not stupid enough to spend it on half a service."

"There isn't a one-copper service?"

"There is."

I looked at the board.

"Not listed."

"Questions."

"You charge for those?"

"Good ones."

"How much?"

"Depends whether you ask one."

I laughed.

She did not.

Maybe a little around the eyes.

"Fine," I said. "What can I learn before paying you?"

"Better question."

She reached under the counter and put a thin wooden card in front of me.

It had six boxes burned into it.

MARK

MATERIAL

MOVING PARTS

STONE OR CORE

VISIBLE DAMAGE

KNOWN USE

"Write those down before you come back," she said. "Draw the mark. Don't improve it. Don't clean it. Don't polish it. Don't oil it. Don't turn anything because you think movement will help you describe movement."

My face did something.

Her eyes narrowed.

"You turned something."

"At the shop. Once."

"What?"

"Ring."

"And?"

"Base held to the bench."

"For how long?"

"Long enough to demonstrate."

"How long?"

"Not measured."

"What surface?"

"Wooden bench."

"Load?"

"Seller pulled against it."

"How hard?"

"Hard enough to show it held. Not enough for me to call it a limit."

She nodded once.

"Good."

"That's free?"

"No. That was me deciding whether you're dangerous."

"Result?"

"Undecided."

Fair.

She tapped the wooden card.

"Bring description first if money is tight. I can sometimes tell you the question is not worth buying."

That surprised me.

"You turn away paid work?"

"I turn away stupid work. There is more paid work."

First-life me had known three assessors who would have had her murdered for saying that aloud.

One had eventually been murdered.

Unrelated.

Probably.

I looked at the last line on the board again.

PURCHASE OFFER: SOMETIMES.

"When do you buy?"

"When I want something."

"Very technical."

"You haven't paid for technical."

"Do you buy before assessment?"

"Sometimes."

"Isn't that a conflict?"

"Yes."

I waited.

She waited better.

"You just admit that?"

"If I want to buy, I tell the owner before I price anything. Then they can leave, hire someone else, or ask what I will pay."

"And if they ask what it's worth?"

"I tell them I am no longer the person to answer."

I sat back.

That was irritatingly clean.

"You know Rusk?" I asked.

"Which Rusk?"

"Guild cord. Cooper Street clearance yesterday. Old field brace. Four ribs. Red stone."

"That Rusk."

Nothing else.

"He gave me your name."

"Then he wanted you to stop asking him questions."

"Probably."

"Did it work?"

"Temporarily."

"Good enough."

I pulled the narrow scrap from my bag.

Not to hand it to her.

Just to check my own notes.

Mara saw the writing upside down.

"Fourteen?"

I covered it with my thumb.

"You read upside down."

"Costs extra right-side up."

"Brace sold for fourteen."

"And?"

"I thought that was a lot."

"It is."

"Not impossible."

"Most prices aren't."

"Some are."

"Then they are not prices for you."

That landed harder than it deserved.

I had spent too much of my first life around people who treated money as proof of rank. Gold meant you had arrived. Silver meant you were climbing. Copper meant you were counting meals.

Then I had become S-class and discovered there were still doors with prices I could not casually pay.

Better doors.

More expensive mistakes.

Same arithmetic.

"I want to be able to move when something like that appears again," I said.

Mara's expression changed for the first time.

Not warmer.

More attentive.

"Then don't start by buying assessments."

I blinked.

"You are terrible at sales."

"You don't need sales. You need capital."

There it was.

Ugly word.

Useful word.

"How much?"

"For what?"

"Enough."

"Enough is not a number."

"Fourteen was."

"Yesterday."

"Six opened it."

"Yesterday."

"Eleven was serious trade money."

"Yesterday."

I frowned.

She tapped the counter.

"If you decide you need fourteen because one brace sold for fourteen, you will spend six months becoming ready for yesterday."

That was annoying.

Also correct.

"Then what do I become ready for?"

"Choice."

I hated answers like that.

She continued before I could complain.

"One copper lets you choose among one-copper things. Five lets you choose among some five-copper things and walk away from bad ones. Twenty does not mean you buy a twenty-copper thing. It means you can still have money after buying eight."

That was better.

Not profound.

Just structurally useful.

Reserve was not a target price.

It was room around a decision.

I looked down at the wooden card.

MARK.

MATERIAL.

MOVING PARTS.

STONE OR CORE.

VISIBLE DAMAGE.

KNOWN USE.

"Can I keep this?"

"One bit."

I stared at her.

She stared back.

Then she smiled.

Barely.

"Take it."

"Cruel."

"You have no bits."

"How do you know that?"

"You told me you had one copper with the expression of a man who would have mentioned smaller money if it existed."

I put the card in my bag.

"I dislike you."

"Two copper to confirm."

I stood.

My right hand settled onto the crutch grip.

"One more question."

"You haven't asked a paid one yet."

"What makes a question worth paying for?"

She looked at me for a long second.

Then she pointed toward the door.

"When the answer changes what you do."

I stopped.

That one was free too.

Probably because it was dangerous.

Outside, Registry Row had become busier. A courier pushed past carrying three sealed tubes. A woman argued with a weights clerk about whether a chipped brass standard could still be copied. Somewhere farther down the crooked street, a bell rang twice.

I moved into the flow.

The holdfast was still upstairs.

Untested.

Unassessed.

Still mine.

My one copper was still upstairs too.

Also mine.

Nothing had increased.

That was not the same as nothing changing.

At the next corner I stopped, took out the narrow scrap, and wrote beneath yesterday's Kiln notes:

MARA QUILL

MARK READ 2 C

HOUSING 3 C

OPEN 4 C + MAT

QUESTIONS FIRST

Then, after a moment:

DON'T SAVE FOR YESTERDAY

I looked at that.

Too dramatic.

I rubbed it out with my thumb until it became a gray smear.

Then I wrote:

RESERVE = CHOICE AFTER PRICE

Better.

Still annoying.

I put the scrap away.

Fourteen copper had looked like a wall yesterday.

Today it looked more like one door in a street full of doors.

Some cost two.

Some cost fourteen.

Some cost enough that nobody bothered painting the number where people like me could see it.

Fine.

I did not need every door.

I needed to stop arriving with exactly enough for none of them.
