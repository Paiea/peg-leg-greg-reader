# CHAPTER 326

## THE LOT

Three copper bought me the right to make a bad decision with confidence.

This was apparently progress.

The morning after West Glass, my shoulders felt as though someone had spent the night tightening them with a small wrench.

Not injured.

Just used.

There was a difference, and at nineteen I was finally old enough to appreciate it while still being young enough to complain.

I sat at the table with one crutch against my chair and rolled my right shoulder until something clicked.

Lyssa looked up from a strip of dark cloth.

"Good click?"

"Probably."

"Excellent medical system."

"I've survived worse."

"You say that about everything."

"It keeps being true."

She went back to the cloth.

I had not told her about the three copper yet.

Not because it was secret.

Because announcing money made it feel like money was about to leave.

This was irrational.

I had been rich enough in my first life to stop counting ordinary meals and still developed the habit of checking whether my purse existed every time I stood up.

Money changed.

Paranoia diversified.

Tavi came through the room carrying a wooden spoon like a sword.

"You working today?"

"Not yet."

"Then you're not working."

"That is a brutal interpretation of time."

He stabbed the air once and left.

Lyssa said, "He learned it from you."

"Impossible. I use more words."

I ate.

Then I checked the public board.

Not because I had decided to work.

Because three copper was enough to make the board interesting in a different way.

Yesterday I had looked for what paid best among things I could reasonably do.

Today I looked for what the money might let me do next.

There were two hauling notices, one copy job requiring clean script, a request for somebody who knew how to remove a goat from a tiled roof, and a card advertising six jars of preserved pears at a price that suggested the pears had political influence.

No Sel notice.

No West Glass trial.

Good.

Or bad.

Mostly not my problem.

I kept walking.

The lower market was already crowded. A fish seller was arguing with a customer over whether a fish with one eye cost less because it had seen less ocean. A woman had three copper kettles stacked on her head while carrying nothing in either hand, which seemed like showing off.

I passed the regular tool stalls.

Then the broken-tool stalls.

Then the stalls that called themselves old-tool stalls because broken sounded less profitable.

I was not hunting artifacts.

That sentence became less true the farther I walked.

Not because I expected to find one.

Meaningful old pieces did not sit in every basket waiting for a reincarnated idiot to recognize them.

Mostly baskets contained bent hinges, cracked lamp housings, worn kitchen stones, dead seals, obsolete fittings, and objects whose only hidden property was tetanus.

Still.

I had three copper.

Three copper was not much.

Three copper was enough that looking stopped being completely theoretical.

That mattered.

A stall near the end of Tool Lane had no sign.

Just a canvas roof, a narrow table, and an old man with both hands wrapped around a cup.

The table held six lots separated by chalk lines.

Not six objects.

Lots.

A box of brass fasteners.

Three cracked lamp cups.

A bundle of thin iron rods.

A tray of mixed stone fittings.

A small wooden case with no lid.

And a shallow basket containing what looked like the contents of a repairer's bad month.

I slowed.

The old man said, "Basket is one copper."

I had not asked.

"Good morning to you too."

"Morning is free. Basket is one copper."

"Why?"

"Because half copper doesn't exist in my patience."

I looked at the basket.

"What's in it?"

"Things."

"Useful."

"Somebody thought so once."

There were perhaps twenty pieces inside.

Two buckles.

A blackened hinge.

Four stone buttons.

A broken ring with three holes around its edge.

A flat copper plate bent at one corner.

Something that might have been a lock tongue.

Two pieces of cloudy glass.

A small iron hook.

A pale ceramic cylinder the length of my thumb.

And beneath them, partly hidden by a strip of leather, a dark oval no larger than a plum pit.

I stopped looking at it.

Not dramatically.

Just moved my eyes.

The old man drank from his cup.

I looked at the brass-fastener box instead.

"How much?"

"Two."

"The basket has more."

"Basket has worse."

Reasonable market.

I took Mara's wooden checklist card from my bag.

The old man noticed.

"You buying or praying?"

"Trying not to buy garbage."

"Then don't buy anything."

Excellent seller.

I crouched as far as my balance allowed, then decided dignity was cheaper than falling and pulled over an empty crate with my right foot.

"Can I sit?"

"If you don't break it."

I sat.

The basket stayed on the table.

I did not touch it.

MARK.

MATERIAL.

MOVING PARTS.

STONE OR CORE.

VISIBLE DAMAGE.

KNOWN USE.

The dark oval had no visible mark from this angle.

Material was harder.

Not stone.

Maybe fired glass.

Maybe old resin.

Maybe horn polished until the grain disappeared.

No moving parts visible.

No obvious core.

Damage unknown.

Known use absolutely none.

Excellent.

Mara would have been proud of how little I knew.

The reason I had stopped was not the oval itself.

It was the leather strip lying over it.

The strip had a burned line down its center and three tiny holes at one end.

Old mounting strap.

Maybe.

And the oval had a shallow groove around its edge.

Not enough to identify it.

Enough to remind me of something.

First life.

A road north of Teren Ford.

Rain.

A pack frame with one strap torn through.

A woman named Sera swearing because her left boot had filled with water.

Not useful.

Then later.

Same trip.

A night shelter with six wet people and a little black disk hanging from a nail beside the door.

Someone had touched it before sleeping.

Or after waking.

No.

That memory was wrong.

Maybe different trip.

I hated partial memories because they arrived with the confidence of complete ones.

I looked at the basket again.

"Where did this lot come from?"

"Repair sweep."

"Whose?"

"Mine now."

"Before yours."

"South warehouse clearance."

"Which warehouse?"

"The one that closed."

I waited.

He drank.

I was not going to win this through silence.

"Trade?"

"Mixed."

"How mixed?"

"Door fittings. Harness. Lamp work. Cart pieces. Two boxes marked household. One marked shrine and full of hinges."

"This basket from one box?"

"No."

That reduced provenance almost to weather.

Good to know.

"Anything in it work?"

"The buckle probably."

"Magically."

He looked at me for the first time with actual attention.

Then at the checklist card.

"If I knew, basket wouldn't be one copper."

There it was.

Rational seller.

Not stupid.

Not secretly blind.

He did not know because the lot was mixed low-value repair sweep and testing every piece cost more than dumping it.

That did not mean anything magical was inside.

It meant information had a price too.

I leaned closer without touching.

The dark oval caught light along one edge.

A thin pale line appeared in the groove.

Then vanished when a cloud moved.

Glass, maybe.

No.

Not enough.

"Can I turn the basket?"

"Basket, yes. Pieces, no."

I rotated the whole shallow basket a quarter turn.

The oval shifted slightly against the leather.

Its underside became visible.

There was a mark.

Barely.

Not a maker stamp.

Three short cuts forming an open triangle.

My stomach tightened.

Not because I knew it.

Because I knew the family of marks.

Maybe.

Road equipment.

Old field convenience pieces.

Things designed to be used by people too tired to read instructions.

Or I was inventing a pattern because I wanted the basket to matter.

Both were possible.

I took out charcoal.

"Can I draw the mark?"

"Draw whatever you like."

I copied the three cuts onto the narrow scrap.

Not improved.

Mara's rule.

The left cut was shorter.

The lower one might have been wear rather than intentional.

I wrote that too.

The old man watched.

"One copper," he reminded me.

"You said."

"You are making a copper face."

"What does that look like?"

"Like you're trying to make one copper become information."

Annoyingly precise.

I had three.

Buying the basket would leave two.

That was different from buying Mara's housing read and leaving zero.

Choice after price.

I could spend one and still have two.

That did not make the basket good.

It made the decision possible.

I inspected the rest.

The copper plate had a stamped number, probably inventory.

One stone button had a hairline crack.

The ceramic cylinder was hollow and smelled faintly of lamp oil.

The broken ring was ordinary iron.

One cloudy glass piece had a silver thread inside it, but the thread ended at the fracture. Could have been decorative. Could have been dead channel work. Could have been nothing.

The black oval remained the only piece that pulled at memory.

Not enough.

I stood.

"Half copper."

The old man lifted his cup.

"No."

"One copper and you keep the buckles."

"No."

"One copper and I get the basket too."

"You already get the basket."

"Actual basket?"

"Yes."

I looked at the shallow woven basket.

That improved the deal by an amount too embarrassing to calculate.

"Why not separate the pieces?"

"Because then I would have to sell twenty pieces."

There was the actual discount.

Labor.

Attention.

Table space.

He was selling the problem cheaply because sorting it was not his business today.

I reached into my bag.

Stopped.

"Anything dangerous you know about?"

The old man lowered his cup.

"Known dangerous? No."

"Unknown dangerous?"

"Everything is unknown dangerous if you work hard enough."

"Any piece from a death?"

"Not that I was told."

"Fire?"

"Warehouse had one old scorch wall. These lots weren't marked from it."

"Bonded?"

"Nothing sold to me as bonded."

"Restricted?"

"Not sold to me as restricted."

Careful wording.

Good.

"Returns?"

"No."

Of course.

I paid one copper.

The coin left my hand without pain.

That was new.

Not because one copper had become nothing.

Because I still had two.

The old man pushed the basket toward me.

"Congratulations."

"On what?"

"Owning my problem."

Fair.

I did not put the oval in my pocket.

I did not touch it at all.

I carried the basket level against my right hip with one forearm while using the crutch awkwardly on that side.

This lasted six steps.

Then I stopped.

Terrible plan.

I moved the shoulder bag across my body, tied the basket handle to the bag strap with the leather strip from inside the lot, tested the hang, hated it, untied it, and finally paid a nearby fruit seller one bit I did not possess in exchange for nothing because I did not possess a bit.

That plan had a flaw.

The fruit seller stared at me.

"You all right?"

"Financially inconsistent."

She pointed at a stack of old wrapping cloth.

"Take one."

"Free?"

"You bought nothing."

"That usually costs less."

"Take the cloth before I change my mind."

I took the cloth.

No money changed hands.

I wrapped the basket and tied it to the top of my shoulder bag tightly enough that it stopped swinging.

Better.

Still stupid-looking.

Functional won.

At home, Lyssa saw the basket before I got through the door.

"No."

"You don't know what it is."

"Neither do you."

"That's not the point."

"That is exactly the point."

Tavi appeared behind her.

"Treasure?"

"Repair garbage," Lyssa said.

"Potential repair garbage," I corrected.

Tavi reached.

I lifted the basket.

"No touching."

His hand stopped.

That was progress too.

"Why?"

"Because I don't know what some of it does."

"Then why did you buy it?"

Lyssa looked at me.

I looked at Tavi.

"I dislike this household."

"How much?" Lyssa asked.

"One copper."

She stared.

Not horrified.

Just recalculating me.

"For garbage."

"For the basket."

"Greg."

"And possible old field hardware."

"There it is."

I carried the lot upstairs.

The holdfast remained on the high shelf.

I cleared a patch of floor beneath the window, laid down one of my older scraps of sailcloth, and set the basket in the center.

Tavi stayed at the doorway because I told him to.

Lyssa leaned against the frame behind him.

"Are we exploding?"

"Not intentionally."

"Comforting."

I took Mara's checklist card out.

Then, one at a time, I moved the obviously mundane pieces out of the basket using a folded cloth around my fingers.

Not because cloth made unknown magic safe.

Because grime existed.

Buckles.

Hinge.

Buttons.

Copper plate.

Lock tongue.

Iron hook.

Ceramic lamp piece.

Broken ring.

Cloudy glass.

I did not activate anything.

I did not turn the oval.

I did not press the mark.

I did not scrape it clean.

I simply exposed it fully.

Dark oval.

About two inches long.

One inch wide.

Groove around edge.

Three-cut open triangle on underside.

One end had a tiny hole through it.

Not a stone.

Not ordinary glass either.

I could see faint depth under the surface, like smoke trapped under ice.

Tavi whispered, "Treasure."

"Still unknown."

Lyssa said, "You paid one copper for unknown."

"And nineteen pieces of garbage."

"Better."

I counted.

Eighteen.

"Eighteen pieces of garbage."

"Excellent investment."

I smiled despite myself.

Then the oval moved.

Not much.

A twitch.

I froze.

Tavi stepped backward without being told.

Good child.

Lyssa straightened.

The oval moved again.

One end rotated perhaps half an inch across the sailcloth.

Then stopped.

I had not touched it.

Nobody had.

The window was open.

Air moved through the room.

The sailcloth shifted slightly.

Possible explanation.

I closed the window.

Waited.

Nothing.

Good.

I opened it again.

Lyssa said, "That feels like testing."

"Environmental observation."

"That sounds like testing with extra syllables."

Fair.

I left the window open.

A breeze moved the curtain.

The sailcloth edge lifted.

The oval stayed still.

Then, when the breeze stopped, it rotated another fraction.

Wrong direction for the cloth.

I stared.

No glow.

No sound.

No mana sensation I trusted.

Just movement.

Small.

Slow.

The pointed end, if it had a pointed end, was now aimed roughly toward the doorway.

Tavi moved left.

The oval did not follow him.

Good.

Lyssa moved right.

Nothing.

I shifted my crutch.

Nothing.

Then somewhere downstairs the front door opened.

The oval turned.

Not toward the door.

Away from it.

A quarter inch.

Then stopped.

I felt the old memory catch.

Wet road.

Night shelter.

Black disk beside the door.

Someone touching it before sleep.

No.

Someone hanging it where it could move.

And in the morning it had pointed toward...

I could not get the rest.

Not yet.

"Do you know?" Lyssa asked.

"No."

That answer felt clean.

"Do you think you know?"

"Worse."

"Good."

I looked at Mara's checklist.

KNOWN USE.

Blank.

I wrote:

MOVES WITHOUT TOUCH

Then beneath it:

TWICE? THREE TIMES

Then:

DOOR OPENED -> TURNED AWAY? ONE OBSERVATION

I underlined the question mark, not the claim.

Hessa would have approved.

Probably while making me remove the word `away` until I established an angle.

I added:

NO ACTIVATION BY ME

NO MANA ATTEMPT

Lyssa read over my shoulder.

"You bought a thing that moves."

"Maybe."

"It moved."

"The question is why."

"You are becoming Mara."

"Cruel."

"Two copper to confirm."

I looked at her.

She smiled.

Apparently I had made the mistake of bringing jokes home.

I wrapped the oval separately in clean cloth.

Not one of the three unused gray household sheets.

I was not sacrificing good cloth to a mystery plum pit.

I put it in the shallow wooden case from the lot, because the case fit badly but adequately, then set the case on the floor beside the high shelf rather than on it.

Then reconsidered Tavi.

High shelf.

Different side from the holdfast.

No contact between them.

The remaining eighteen pieces stayed in the basket on the floor until I could decide whether any were worth keeping.

I counted my money.

Two copper.

Zero bits.

One copper gone.

A month ago I might have felt the loss first.

Today I felt the difference between buying a one-copper portrait and buying a one-copper basket of uncertain hardware.

Not because art was foolish.

Because I wanted this question more.

That was what money was for when it stopped being only survival.

Not virtue.

Preference.

I took out the narrow scrap.

Under `RESERVE = CHOICE AFTER PRICE`, I wrote:

TOOL LANE LOT 1 C

DARK OVAL / 3-CUT OPEN TRIANGLE

MOVES WITHOUT TOUCH

NO CLAIM YET

Then I added the thing that mattered most:

2 C LEFT

I almost crossed it out.

Too much purse counting.

Then left it.

This time the number was not fear.

It was runway.

On the shelf, the wrapped oval did nothing.

For nearly an hour.

Then Cala came home.

The front door closed downstairs.

A moment later, from inside the little wooden case, something made one soft click.

I looked up.

The holdfast did not move.

The case did not move.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing exploded.

One click.

That was all.

I smiled.

Not because I knew what I had bought.

Because for one copper, I had finally bought something worth finding out.
