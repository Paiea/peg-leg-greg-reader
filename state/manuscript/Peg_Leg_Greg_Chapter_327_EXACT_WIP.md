# CHAPTER 327

## THE LAST DOOR

The thing clicked every time I went through a door.

Not every door.

That would have been useful.

It clicked often enough to be suspicious and irregularly enough to make me hate it.

The first click came when I left the apartment.

I had put the dark oval in my shoulder bag because leaving it upstairs after buying it felt like admitting I had purchased decorative uncertainty.

I had not activated it.

I had not pressed the mark.

I had not threaded anything through the tiny hole.

I had wrapped it in the same clean cloth, placed it in the shallow wooden case, and tucked the case upright in the outer pocket of my bag where it could not touch the holdfast because the holdfast remained upstairs.

Separate problems.

Important principle.

I stepped through the front door.

Click.

Soft.

From the bag.

I stopped on the landing.

Lyssa, behind me, almost walked into my back.

"What?"

"It clicked."

"You click."

"Different click."

"Useful distinction."

I opened the bag.

The case had not opened.

The oval had not escaped.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing smelled like burning hair.

Good start.

I closed the bag.

Went back inside.

No click.

Stepped out again.

Nothing.

Lyssa folded her arms.

"Strong science."

"It did it once."

"So did Tavi yesterday with a spoon."

"He does that constantly."

"More reliable artifact."

I went down the stairs.

The building door opened.

I crossed onto the street.

Click.

I stopped again.

Lyssa was no longer behind me.

Good.

I turned around, went back through the building door, crossed the threshold, waited.

Nothing.

Out again.

Nothing.

"Excellent," I muttered.

A woman passing with a basket looked at me and decided not to ask.

Carrow had manners when it mattered.

I wrote on the narrow scrap:

FRONT DOOR OUT -> CLICK

BUILDING DOOR OUT -> CLICK

REPEAT SAME DOOR -> NO CLICK

Then I stared at that.

Too early.

I added:

SO FAR

Hessa would have approved of the insult.

I was supposed to be taking a receipt to Cala's dye man.

Not her dye man in the romantic sense.

Carrow was a difficult city to describe concisely.

A supplier two streets south of the old cooper row had sent the wrong shade cord with a previous bundle. Cala had already handled the actual correction. My job was to carry a folded receipt to a back counter because I was walking that direction anyway.

Small errand.

No money.

No mystery.

Perfect conditions for a mystery to become irritating.

I crossed into the narrow dye shop.

Click.

This time I heard it clearly.

I stopped halfway through the doorway.

The clerk behind the front counter said, "You coming in?"

"Apparently."

"That's usually how doors work."

I handed over the receipt.

"Back counter," he said.

"Cala's correction."

"Back counter."

I looked toward a narrow hallway beside hanging blue cloth.

"How far back?"

"Until someone looks annoyed."

Excellent directions.

I went through the hall curtain.

No click.

Through a wooden door.

Click.

Then down three steps into a workroom smelling strongly of wet fiber and vinegar.

A woman at a table looked up.

Not annoyed.

Wrong person.

"Receipt?" I asked.

She pointed farther back.

"Tally room."

Another door.

Click.

I stopped.

She watched me.

"You all right?"

"My bag has opinions."

"Leave it outside if it starts talking."

Reasonable policy.

The tally room contained one man, four shelves, three ledgers, and enough bundled cord to strangle a small army.

He took the receipt.

Read it.

Stamped it once.

"Done."

I waited.

"That's all?"

"Did you want an argument?"

"No."

"Then done."

I turned to leave.

The dark oval clicked before I reached the door.

Not crossing it.

Before.

I froze.

The tally man looked up.

"Now what?"

I opened my bag.

The shallow case had shifted.

Not much.

The oval inside was pressed against one side of the cloth wrapping.

I set the bag on an empty stool and opened the case without touching the oval directly.

The oval had rotated within the cloth.

Its narrow end pointed toward the door I had just used.

Maybe.

That was the first time the shape looked directional enough to call one end narrow.

I moved around the stool.

The oval did not follow me.

Good.

I turned the case ninety degrees.

The oval stayed where it was relative to the room.

Not the case.

My skin prickled.

The tally man leaned over.

"What is that?"

"Unknown."

"You carry unknown things into dye shops?"

"Only the small ones."

He stepped back.

Smart man.

I lifted the whole case and carried it three feet left.

The oval rotated slowly.

Still toward the door.

I stopped.

There it was.

Not proof of what the door meant.

But orientation.

Toward a doorway I had crossed.

Maybe the last one.

Maybe a particular one.

Maybe a material seam.

Maybe the receipt.

Maybe the tally man was secretly magnetic.

I looked at him.

He looked offended.

Probably not magnetic.

I covered the oval again.

"Done?" he asked.

"For now."

"Good."

I went out through the tally-room door.

Click.

I stopped immediately and reopened the case on a barrel top.

The oval had rotated.

Now it pointed back through the tally-room door.

I felt the first-life memory catch hard enough to hurt.

Wet boots.

Road shelter.

Six people asleep.

A black piece hanging from a nail beside the door.

One of the younger scouts asking why it mattered.

An older woman saying, "Because dark makes every door look honest."

Then laughter.

Then something else.

Not a compass.

A return piece.

Maybe.

No.

The word was close.

Backmark.

Door-mark.

Last mark.

The exact name would not come.

The function shape did.

You crossed a boundary.

It remembered one.

Then showed you back.

Useful in ruins.

Useful underground.

Useful in smoke.

Useful when every corridor looked the same.

And useless the moment you crossed the wrong doorway.

That last part arrived with the memory.

Important.

I wrote it down before confidence improved it:

AFTER TALLY DOOR -> POINTS BACK TO TALLY DOOR

CROSS DOOR -> CLICK + ORIENTATION CHANGES

POSSIBLE LAST-THRESHOLD RETURN PIECE

Then:

MEMORY SUPPORTS CATEGORY, NOT EXACT OBJECT

The dye woman at the worktable read upside down as I passed.

"You writing a book?"

"Trying not to."

I crossed the wooden door into the front hall.

Click.

I opened the case.

The oval now pointed back toward the wooden door.

Good.

Then I crossed the hanging cloth curtain.

No click.

The oval stayed pointed toward the wooden door.

Interesting.

Not every boundary.

Not cloth.

Maybe rigid frame.

Maybe enclosed threshold.

Maybe something magical in the door.

I stepped through the front shop door onto the street.

Click.

The oval rotated toward the shop entrance.

I smiled.

Then a cart nearly ran over my crutch tip because I was standing in a doorway staring at a small black object.

I moved.

Artifacts did not improve survival if you used them stupidly.

Good lesson.

I did not go home.

Not yet.

I walked two blocks with the oval case open inside my bag enough that I could glance down without touching it.

It continued to point roughly toward the dye-shop front door.

At the first alley I turned.

Still toward the shop.

At the second street I turned again.

Still.

No click.

Distance had not obviously erased it.

Useful.

Then I entered a bakery.

Click.

The oval rotated toward the bakery door.

I left immediately.

Click.

The oval rotated toward the same bakery door from outside.

I frowned.

That was different.

If it always pointed to the last crossed doorway, crossing back out should have made it point to the bakery door anyway.

Same physical threshold.

No contradiction.

I needed two doors.

The baker watched me through the window.

I bought nothing.

This was becoming socially expensive without costing money.

Across the street was a narrow public passage through an old counting house. One door on the south side, one on the north, corridor between. People used it as a shortcut.

Perfect.

I entered south.

Click.

Oval pointed south.

Crossed the north door.

Click.

Oval rotated north.

Good.

Then I stepped back through north.

Click.

Oval pointed north from inside.

Then exited south.

Click.

Oval pointed south.

Last physical threshold.

Strongly.

Maybe.

Enough to use carefully.

I was still writing when somebody said, "You going somewhere?"

I looked up.

A courier stood beside me with two sealed tubes under one arm.

"Eventually."

"Then stop standing in the passage."

Correct.

I moved.

That should have been the chapter.

Not literally.

Life did not know about chapters.

But I had learned a usable thing.

The object appeared to mark the most recent qualifying doorway crossed and orient back toward it.

That was enough for one day.

So naturally I got lost twenty minutes later.

Not dramatically lost.

Carrow did not suddenly become a maze.

I took a side route around a blocked drain repair near Bell Street and ended up at the rear entrance of an old storage court I had never used.

A chalk board at the corner said:

SOUTH WALK CLOSED

USE LOWER PASSAGE

The lower passage entrance was a stone arch descending beneath two connected warehouses.

I could have gone around another three streets.

My shoulders objected.

The lower passage had a handrail.

I chose the passage.

This was not artifact testing.

This was laziness with documentation.

I crossed the stone arch.

Click.

Oval pointed back toward daylight.

The passage descended twelve shallow steps, turned left, then split.

One branch had painted arrows.

The other had barrels.

I followed arrows.

Second arch.

Click.

The oval changed.

Third doorway, wooden frame without a door.

Click.

Changed again.

Then the arrows disappeared.

Of course.

A worker carrying rope came the other way.

"Bell Street?"

I asked.

"Up right, then left at white post."

"Thanks."

I went up right.

White post.

Left.

Then found two identical doors.

No signs.

One smelled faintly of grain.

The other smelled like wet stone.

I could have waited for someone.

Instead I looked at the oval.

It pointed behind me.

Toward the last wooden frame I had crossed.

Not toward either new door.

Good.

That was actually useful.

I retraced to the frame.

Click.

The oval now pointed back toward the room I had just left.

There was the limitation.

The second I crossed the frame, I had overwritten the previous mark.

If I kept using it to retreat one doorway at a time, fine.

If I crossed the wrong door while confused, the object would faithfully help me return to the wrong door.

Not a path recorder.

Not a route map.

One threshold.

One.

I remembered the old shelter woman again.

"Because dark makes every door look honest."

And someone else answering:

"It only remembers the last liar."

There.

Not the formal name.

But the joke.

I laughed in the underground passage by myself.

A man stacking crates looked at me.

"You all right?"

"Better than five minutes ago."

"Congratulations."

Carrow remained supportive.

I used the oval carefully after that.

Not to find Bell Street.

To preserve retreat.

Before crossing any uncertain doorway, I looked at where it pointed.

Then crossed.

If the next space was wrong, I could return one step.

This changed how I moved.

That was the first real proof that I had bought something other than curiosity.

Not power.

Procedure.

I stopped choosing doors casually.

Every doorway became a small decision because crossing it rewrote the only return marker I had.

The object made navigation safer and more annoying at the same time.

Good artifact.

Possibly.

I found Bell Street eight minutes later.

Outside, I sat on a low wall and wrote until my hand cramped.

OBSERVED QUALIFYING THRESHOLDS:

WOOD DOOR

STONE ARCH

OPEN WOOD FRAME

PUBLIC PASSAGE DOORS

NOT CLOTH CURTAIN

Then:

POINTS BACK TO MOST RECENT QUALIFYING THRESHOLD AFTER CROSSING

I stared at that sentence.

Too strong.

Changed it to:

SO FAR, CONSISTENT WITH MOST RECENT QUALIFYING THRESHOLD

Then:

CROSSING NEW QUALIFYING THRESHOLD OVERWRITES PRIOR ORIENTATION

That one had direct support.

At least four times.

Then:

USEFUL FOR ONE-STEP RETREAT

NOT ROUTE MEMORY

I underlined the second line.

That was the downside.

A good one.

Not deadly.

Potentially deadly if you trusted it beyond what it did.

Those were often the most dangerous tools.

I wrapped the oval again before heading home.

It clicked once when I entered the building.

I ignored it.

Then once at the apartment door.

I opened the case upstairs.

The oval pointed toward the apartment door.

Tavi was on the floor with wooden blocks.

"Treasure works?"

"A little."

He stood immediately.

"What does it do?"

"It remembers a door."

Lyssa looked over from the table.

"One door?"

"Apparently."

"Which door?"

"Last one."

"Useful."

"Sometimes."

"Bad?"

"Also sometimes."

She nodded.

That was all.

No applause.

Correct response.

I hung the oval from a short loop of spare cord through its tiny hole and suspended it from a wooden peg near my upstairs door.

Not touching the wall.

It rotated slowly until the narrow end aimed toward the doorway.

That felt closer to the first-life memory.

A black piece hanging beside a shelter door.

Not held flat.

Not boxed.

Free to turn.

I did not know whether hanging was required.

I only knew it made the behavior easier to see.

I wrote:

HANGING = EASIER TO READ

NOT PROVEN REQUIRED

Then I stopped.

The object had earned a name in my notes.

Not a real name.

A working one.

LAST-DOOR OVAL

Ugly.

Useful.

I could improve it later.

Or discover everybody else called it something obvious and feel stupid.

Both seemed likely.

I counted my money.

Two copper.

Still two.

The artifact had not made me richer.

It had made one kind of mistake cheaper.

That was better than I expected for one copper.

Then Tavi ran out of the room.

The oval clicked.

I looked at it.

He had not gone through my upstairs door.

He had crossed the open doorway into the hall.

The oval rotated toward it.

I frowned.

No physical door.

Just a frame.

Qualifying threshold.

Good.

Then Tavi came back through.

Click.

The oval still pointed toward the same frame.

Of course.

Same threshold.

I added one final note:

DOOR NOT REQUIRED

FRAME MAY BE ENOUGH

Then I put the charcoal down.

One layer.

Enough.

I had learned what the object could do today.

More importantly, I had learned what it could not do.

The first made it useful.

The second made it survivable.
