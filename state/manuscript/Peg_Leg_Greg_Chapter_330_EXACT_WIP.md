# CHAPTER 330

## THE BACK DOOR

For three days, our front door became decorative.

This improved it.

No one knocked on it.

No one stood in it talking while everyone else tried to pass.

No one opened it and discovered Tavi directly behind them for reasons known only to Tavi.

The municipal workers had boarded the damaged section of the front hall, chalked a red line across the floor, and declared the entire front route closed until the linewright finished deciding whose fault the wall was.

Cala had asked when that would be.

The worker had said, "When he knows."

She had stared at him.

He had added, "Probably tomorrow."

It was now the day after tomorrow.

Civilization.

We used the rear service route instead.

Kitchen passage.

Storage turn.

Rear stair.

Courtyard.

Then around the building to the street.

It added perhaps forty seconds for a person with two good legs.

For me it added more.

Not much more.

Enough that I noticed every single time.

The rear stair was narrower than the front one and had a turn halfway down where the inside crutch had to land closer to the wall than I liked.

The first morning I hated it.

The second morning I had a pattern.

Right crutch.

Right foot.

Left crutch down one.

Turn hip.

Do not catch shoulder bag on the peg beside the landing.

The third morning, Tavi moved the peg.

I stopped halfway down.

"Why?"

He looked at the peg in his hand.

"You hit it."

"Once."

"Twice."

"That is slander."

He put it on a lower hole where it no longer caught the bag.

Then continued downstairs.

I stood there for a moment.

Belonging was apparently people altering architecture because you were annoying.

I liked it.

The oval clicked when I crossed the rear service door into the courtyard.

I barely looked.

That was progress too.

It lived in the shoulder bag now.

Not because I trusted it.

Because an object that had been useful twice earned pocket space.

Case space.

Whatever.

I still kept it wrapped.

Still did not press the mark.

Still did not channel mana into it.

Still did not ask it what I should have for breakfast.

High standards.

The building changed socially before it changed physically.

People who had used the front stair for years suddenly met in the courtyard.

Neris met the old man from the upper rear unit and discovered they had both been living ten feet apart without knowing each other's names.

Cala learned that the woman across the courtyard had been borrowing our rear wall to judge whether her own kitchen was warm enough in winter.

Nobody understood what that meant.

The woman refused to explain.

Tavi found three children who normally entered from the street side and immediately turned the rear route into a race.

Cala ended that within four minutes.

"No running on the rear stair."

"We weren't running," Tavi said.

"Then whatever faster thing you invented is also banned."

Strong law.

The linewright finally arrived near noon.

I knew because Cala called upstairs.

"Greg."

I looked up from the narrow scrap.

"What?"

"The wall man is here."

"Linewright."

"Wall man."

"You are going to offend a profession."

"Good. Come listen so I don't have to remember it."

That was not an invitation to investigate.

It was household administration.

Different danger.

The linewright was a woman.

Cala did not apologize.

She was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, with gray hair braided close to her head and a dark red leather roll under one arm.

No uniform.

A small brass badge at her collar showed three nested squares around a dot.

Formal meaning unknown.

She stood behind the red chalk line looking at the opened wall.

Two municipal workers were with her.

One I recognized from the evacuation.

He recognized me enough to nod.

Good level of fame.

The linewright said, "Who owns the building?"

Cala named a man I had heard before and immediately forgotten.

The linewright wrote it down.

"Resident manager?"

"No."

"Rent collected where?"

Cala named a shop two streets away.

Written down.

"Any heat trouble before failure?"

Cala said, "Not in our unit."

Neris, from her doorway, said, "Mine clicked."

The linewright turned.

"What clicked?"

"Wall. Sometimes."

"When?"

Neris frowned.

"Cold mornings."

"This season?"

"Last season too."

"How often?"

"Not every day."

The linewright wrote.

No accusation.

No dramatic revelation.

Just information becoming expensive somewhere else.

She unrolled the dark red leather case on a crate.

Inside were four tools.

One looked like a flattened tuning fork.

One like a short black rod with a white ceramic tip.

One was a coil of thin copper-colored wire around a bone handle.

The last was a small square frame holding stretched translucent film.

Artifact-adjacent equipment.

Probably.

Specialist tools.

Definitely.

I stayed behind the chalk line.

Useful sentence.

The linewright held the square frame near the opened plaster.

The film tightened.

A pale orange line appeared across it.

She moved left.

The line bent.

Moved right.

Straightened.

One municipal worker said, "Seat?"

"Maybe."

Good answer.

She switched to the ceramic-tipped rod and touched exposed stone beside the failed joint.

Nothing happened.

Then touched the metal housing around the line.

The white ceramic went gray.

"Old load mark," she said.

"From failure?" the worker asked.

"Maybe older."

Even better answer.

I liked her.

Cala did not.

"Can we use the front door?"

The linewright looked at her.

"No."

"When?"

"After I know whether opening that wall farther makes the line worse."

"Today?"

"Probably."

Cala narrowed her eyes.

The linewright added, "Actually probably."

Improvement.

I looked at the oval inside my bag.

Not physically.

Mentally.

This was exactly the kind of moment where a stupid version of me would pull it out and say, `My mystery object pointed toward the rear before your wall exploded. Thoughts?`

Then I would become the interesting resident.

Never become the interesting resident during infrastructure repair.

I had learned things.

The linewright worked.

I went upstairs.

Two hours later, someone knocked on the rear service door.

That sentence felt wrong.

The rear door had not been a social door before.

It was for deliveries, rubbish, people carrying things that dripped, and household members avoiding the front.

Now it had become the only door.

I was closest.

I opened it.

Senna stood in the courtyard holding a flat board under one arm.

"This feels illegal," she said.

"It should."

"Your front door is boarded."

"We improved it."

She looked past me at the rear passage.

"Can I come in?"

The oval clicked inside my bag.

I had crossed nothing.

I froze.

Senna noticed.

"Was that you?"

"Unfortunately no."

The oval moved.

I could feel the case shift against my side.

Toward the courtyard behind Senna.

Or toward the rear service door.

Same line from where I stood.

Not useful yet.

I stepped backward into the kitchen passage without crossing another frame.

The oval stayed oriented toward the rear door.

Senna said, "Should I leave?"

"No. Why are you here?"

"Cala asked for a sign."

Of course she had.

Cala appeared behind me.

"Good."

Senna held up the board.

"You said rear entrance."

"Yes."

"You did not say how much rear entrance."

The board was perhaps two feet wide.

Painted in blue letters:

FRONT CLOSED

USE COURTYARD DOOR

Under that, smaller:

NO DELIVERIES THROUGH FRONT

Cala pointed at the last line.

"Good."

Senna looked at me.

"She pays better than you."

"Everyone does."

Cala took the board.

"How much?"

"One bit."

Cala paid her.

There.

A market price.

Not mine.

Senna saw my expression.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You made a money face."

Apparently this was visible across professions.

"I paid one copper for garbage recently."

"Bad garbage?"

"Promising garbage."

"Worse."

Cala carried the sign toward the courtyard.

Senna stayed.

"I brought the drawing."

I looked at the flat board under her arm.

"Which drawing?"

She gave me a look.

Right.

Me.

The portrait.

The one-copper portrait I had not bought.

She turned the board around.

It was not the original.

Smaller.

A copy.

Charcoal on cheap stiff paper, fixed lightly enough that it would still smudge if treated badly.

Me seated.

Crutches.

Bad hair.

Tired eyes.

That almost-joke in the mouth.

"Why?" I asked.

"The original sold."

I blinked.

"To who?"

"Woman from the square."

"Which woman?"

"The one who bought it."

Excellent provenance.

"For one copper?"

"No."

"How much?"

"Two bits."

I stared.

Senna smiled.

"You look offended."

"My face depreciated."

"Your face had no market."

"Cruel."

"She wanted the street studies together. Basket boy, old man, onion woman, you. I sold four for seven bits."

That was interesting.

Not because I was famous.

Because the portrait had value as part of a set that had nothing to do with me.

Good.

"So why copy mine?"

"Practice. Also you wanted it."

I looked at her.

"How much?"

"One bit."

I laughed.

She frowned.

"Too much?"

"I have zero bits."

"You have copper."

"Yes."

"Then change one."

I stopped.

That was embarrassingly obvious.

Not because conversion was obvious.

We still had not established one.

Because she was telling me to go get change somewhere, not that one copper equaled some invented number of bits.

"Where?"

"Any money table."

"At what rate?"

"Depends who is changing and how much."

There it was.

Useful and annoying.

"I am not changing a copper to buy my own face."

"Then don't."

"You could take copper and owe me."

"No."

"Strong business."

"I learned from the old man who sells garbage."

I stared.

"You know him?"

"No."

"Good."

She looked confused.

Better.

I looked at the copy again.

One bit.

I wanted it.

Still.

Maybe more now because the original had left the square.

But not enough to break a copper just to create small money today.

That was a choice.

Not inability.

"Keep it," I said.

Senna shrugged.

"Fine."

No tragedy.

No lesson.

She slid it back under her arm.

Then the oval clicked again.

Sharper.

Senna heard it this time.

"That definitely wasn't you."

"Correct."

The case moved against my hip.

I opened the bag.

Senna stepped back immediately.

Good instincts.

"Is that the garbage?"

"Promising garbage."

I took the shallow case out and opened it.

The oval pointed toward the rear service door.

Still.

"What does it do?"

"Sometimes it tells me where I should have gone."

"Useful."

"Sometimes."

Senna moved to the side.

The oval did not follow her.

Good.

Cala came back through the courtyard door carrying the sign's old wrapping cloth.

The oval clicked.

Turned.

Not toward Cala.

Toward the kitchen passage behind me.

I stared.

Cala stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing proven."

"Good."

Senna said, "Everyone says that to you?"

"I have cultivated a reputation."

The oval now pointed deeper into the apartment.

Not at the rear exit.

That seemed wrong if it was selecting retreat from danger.

Unless there was no danger.

Which there wasn't.

Probably.

So perhaps `retreat` was not emergency at all.

Maybe it was simply the most useful reversible boundary under current movement.

That sentence was terrible.

I did not write it.

Senna left through the rear door.

Click.

The oval did not update toward her.

Cala crossed into the kitchen.

Nothing.

I crossed the kitchen doorway.

Click.

The oval pointed back.

Normal.

Fine.

I put it away.

Not every click deserved a chapter.

That afternoon, the linewright opened more of the wall.

This produced dust, three new boards, and a phrase I immediately disliked.

"Building-side seat failure."

Cala said, "Meaning?"

The linewright sat back on her heels.

"Meaning the city feed is carrying correctly to the wall. The receiving seat in the building failed under load."

"Whose responsibility?"

"Probably owner."

There it was.

The important magic.

Liability.

Cala's shoulders dropped half an inch.

Not because she wanted the owner punished.

Because `probably owner` meant `probably not residents paying directly today.`

Probably.

I understood that feeling deeply.

"How long?" Cala asked.

"Temporary bypass by evening if the owner authorizes. Proper seat replacement after."

"If he doesn't authorize?"

The linewright looked at the opened wall.

"Then he can explain to his tenants why they don't have front heat service."

Cala smiled.

The owner authorized.

Amazing what concentrated inconvenience could accomplish.

By sunset, a temporary black conduit ran visibly along the upper front-hall wall instead of inside it.

Ugly.

Functional.

The red keep-clear line remained around the opened section.

Front door reopened with instructions not to lean anything against the temporary conduit and not to touch the sealed wall housing.

Tavi asked whether he could look.

The linewright said yes.

He asked whether he could touch.

She said no.

He asked why.

She crouched to his height and said, "Because it can burn you."

He nodded.

Best technical explanation of the day.

Then she stood and looked at my crutches.

Not rudely.

Practically.

"Rear stair working for you?"

"Less badly now."

"Front route will stay open unless the bypass heats. If it heats, don't wait for a worker. Use rear and pull the courtyard bell."

"What counts as heats?"

"Too warm to keep your palm near the wall for three breaths. Don't touch the conduit itself."

Specific.

Good.

"Any sound?"

"Fast ticking. Repeating. One click by itself means nothing."

I looked at my bag.

She noticed.

"What?"

"I own something that clicks."

"Then don't use it to diagnose my line."

I liked her even more.

"Wasn't planning to."

"Good."

She packed her tools.

No artifact conversation.

No appraisal.

No destiny.

Just a woman who knew her wall.

That night we used the front stairs again.

I had become accustomed to the rear route quickly enough that the front felt too wide.

Tavi ran halfway up before Cala said his name.

He stopped.

Walked.

Progress survived architecture.

At the upstairs doorway, the oval clicked.

Normal.

I hung it from the hook.

It pointed back toward the doorway.

Also normal.

Lyssa sat at the table with the dark cloth again.

"Front fixed?"

"Temporarily."

"Good."

"Senna came."

"I know."

"How?"

"Cala told me."

"Household espionage."

"Did you buy the drawing?"

"No."

"How much?"

"One bit."

"You have copper."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because you do."

I sat.

"I didn't want it enough to go change money."

Lyssa looked at me for a second.

"That's different."

"Yes."

No praise.

Good.

I took out the narrow scrap.

Under the house-line notes I wrote:

BUILDING-SIDE SEAT FAILURE

TEMP BYPASS / OWNER AUTHORIZED

FRONT OPEN AGAIN

Then:

SENNA COPY 1 BIT / DECLINED

I stared at that.

Not because it mattered economically.

Because it did.

A month ago, `I cannot buy this` and `I choose not to buy this` had lived too close together.

Now they were separating.

Two copper did not make me rich.

It made some decisions mine.

I liked that.

The oval turned on its cord.

I watched it without moving.

It rotated perhaps ten degrees.

Then stopped.

No click.

No scream.

No wall burst.

Nothing happened.

I waited.

Still nothing.

Then Tavi came through the doorway carrying the wooden spoon again.

Click.

The oval pointed back toward the door.

He looked at it.

"Treasure?"

"Useful garbage."

He nodded as if that made perfect sense.

Maybe it did.

Downstairs, someone opened the restored front door.

The temporary conduit gave one ordinary settling tick.

I heard both sounds.

Neither required me.

That might have been the best part of the repair.
