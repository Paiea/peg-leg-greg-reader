# CHAPTER 334

## HOUSE LEFT

Theatre looked different when nobody wanted me to move anything.

I distrusted it immediately.

The note had come that afternoon.

Not from Rinna.

Not from Nessa.

Marek.

That should have warned me.

It said:

GREG

TONIGHT

SECOND SHOW

HOUSE LEFT

COME WATCH SOMETHING YOU DID NOT BUILD

M

Under that, in smaller writing:

THIS IS NOT WORK

Then, squeezed into the bottom corner:

PROBABLY

I showed Lyssa.

She read it twice.

"You're going."

"I haven't decided."

"You're already looking for the clean shirt."

I looked at the chair.

The clean shirt was on it.

Traitorous furniture.

"That proves nothing."

"It proves you own one clean shirt."

"Cruel."

I went.

Not because Marek had written probably.

Despite that.

The public entrance had a line.

A real one.

Not West Glass chalk and poles.

People.

Couples.

Three children pretending not to push each other.

An old woman with a hat large enough to have its own weather.

Two men arguing quietly about whether the first show had been better last week.

I had not known the theatre had people who discussed it like that.

Of course it did.

That was the point.

I knew the rear hall.

The prop room.

The wardrobe turn.

The left-stage storage angle.

The cracked paint under one bench nobody fixed because the bench covered it.

I knew where Nessa kept dry cloth and where Marek absolutely did not return borrowed things.

I knew what the building looked like when the audience was not there.

Apparently that had tricked me into forgetting there was an audience.

A woman at the front table took bits from people ahead of me.

When I reached her, she looked down at a list.

"Greg?"

That was unsettling.

"Usually."

She found a line with her finger.

"House left, aisle."

"How much?"

"Marked."

"That is not a number."

She looked at me.

"Comp."

I hated that word.

"Why?"

"Ask whoever wrote your name."

Marek.

I hated the word more.

"Can I pay?"

"You can, but then I have to remove the comp, record the seat sale, and explain why the seat count changed after close."

"So no."

"I didn't say no."

"You made it expensive socially."

"That is theatre."

Good answer.

I accepted the seat.

House left aisle turned out to mean the outer seat in the third row from the back on the left side.

Not the best seat.

Not charity disguised as luxury.

A practical seat.

I could put both crutches against the wall-side end without making six people climb over them.

The aisle gave me room to shift my right leg.

Someone had thought about it.

That was worse than a free ticket.

I sat.

The oval clicked in my bag when I crossed the inner house door.

Normal.

Ignored.

The stage was smaller from the audience side.

That surprised me.

Backstage, everything had felt large because objects had to pass through it.

Frames.

Flats.

Bodies.

Crates.

Ropes.

People saying move while standing directly where movement needed to happen.

From the seat, all of that disappeared.

The stage became a rectangle of warm light.

The painted back wall looked like stone.

I knew it was canvas over a frame.

The window looked deep.

I knew there were six inches behind it before a brace.

A low table looked heavy.

Davin had complained about how light it was because the rear leg kicked sideways if you lifted the wrong end.

I had helped carry nothing in this exact set.

I still knew too much.

A boy two seats over whispered to his mother, "Is that a real wall?"

She whispered back, "Yes."

I nearly turned around.

Then stopped.

She was doing theatre correctly.

I was the problem.

The house lamps dimmed.

Not all at once.

Three rows of wall cups lowered in sequence.

A narrow brass strip beneath the stage lip took on a dull amber glow.

Not artifact in the dramatic sense.

Stage utility.

Probably a managed light rail or fed strip.

The audience did not care.

They were not supposed to.

The curtain opened.

Marek walked on carrying a basket.

The basket was empty.

I knew because he carried it one-handed by the wrong side and the rim did not pull.

Stop.

Audience.

Be audience.

He was playing a courier.

Of course he was.

His first line was:

"I have crossed three provinces, two bridges, and one marriage to bring you this letter."

The old woman with the enormous hat laughed before anyone else.

Good woman.

The scene moved quickly.

Marek was better than I expected.

That was unfair.

I had seen him backstage.

Backstage Marek borrowed nails and forgot where his belt was.

Onstage Marek knew exactly where his hands belonged.

He could lean against a table like he had been tired for ten years.

He could wait half a breath before a line and make the audience decide what he was thinking.

He could look at Hara across the room and make two hundred people understand that his character had just made a bad decision.

No explanation.

No note.

No tally.

Just timing.

I hated competence in new domains.

It was expensive to my opinions.

Hara entered through the painted wall.

Not literally.

A hidden door I had never noticed from the house.

I knew there was a side access behind the left flat.

I had walked behind it.

Still, when the wall opened with no visible seam and she stepped through wearing a dark blue coat, the audience made one collective sound.

Small.

Not a gasp.

Attention becoming physical.

That was interesting.

The scene was about a letter.

Then not about a letter.

Then apparently about whether Hara's character had betrayed someone named Els.

I had missed the first show in the run.

This mattered.

A lot.

The boy two seats over whispered, "She did."

His mother said, "Shh."

He was probably right.

Halfway through the first act, the stage changed.

Not scenery.

Sound.

Marek crossed to the window.

Hara stayed by the table.

He spoke quietly.

Too quietly for the back rows.

Except I heard him perfectly.

Not louder.

Closer.

His voice seemed to arrive from the air directly in front of us.

There.

Artifact work.

Or embedded theatre equipment.

Something.

I looked at the stage lip.

The amber brass strip was darker now.

Along the left wall, three small black disks had opened like flowers.

Sound cups.

Echo mouths.

Whisper plates.

Different places called them different things.

Old Greg had seen versions in council halls, military command tents, one extremely wealthy bathhouse, and a courtroom where the judge liked hearing himself too much.

Theatre use made sense.

Obvious value.

The audience paid to hear lines.

Of course a theatre with enough money would spend some of it making speech reach the cheap seats.

No hidden bargain.

No mystery.

Just magic doing work.

I smiled.

The boy's mother looked at me suspiciously.

I faced forward.

Marek said, quietly enough to sound private,

"I did not come back because I forgave you."

The room heard every word.

Hara did not answer.

That was better.

Then the amber strip brightened again.

The black disks closed.

The next line belonged to the room rather than the mechanism.

I knew, suddenly, why Nessa cared about where objects sat.

Not intellectually.

I had always known that.

But the work had an end product.

The exact angle of the table controlled whether Hara could cross behind Marek without blocking the audience's view.

The left-stage passage mattered because the hidden wall entrance had to stay invisible.

The flower frame route mattered because tonight, later in the act, the frame arrived onstage looking effortless.

Jori's complaint about a shelf corner became three seconds of clean motion nobody applauded because nobody noticed.

That might have been the purest form of competence.

Work disappearing into experience.

I had spent a lot of my second life thinking about jobs as money.

Fair.

Money had been urgent.

Then jobs as access.

Also fair.

Now I watched a hundred invisible bits of work become one visible thing.

That was different.

The first act ended with Hara tearing the letter in half.

The audience clapped.

I clapped.

The boy beside me said, "I knew she did it."

His mother said, "You said that before we knew what she did."

"I still knew."

Strong method.

During the interval, people stood.

I did not.

Not because I could not.

Because everyone else had decided the aisle belonged to standing bodies, and I had no desire to negotiate two crutches through a temporary human forest for no reason.

The old woman with the hat moved into the aisle and looked down at me.

"You know them?"

I looked at the stage.

"Some."

"You've been watching the side walls more than the actors."

Caught.

"I know some of the backstage."

"Dangerous."

"Apparently."

She nodded toward the black disks.

"New this season."

That got my attention.

"The sound cups?"

"Is that what they're called?"

"Maybe. Depends where they're from."

"They used to lose half the quiet lines back here."

"Better now?"

"Much."

There it was.

Market visible utility.

No mystery.

The woman adjusted her hat.

"Still hate Marek."

I blinked.

"Personally?"

"His character."

"Good clarification."

"He always plays men who need slapping."

"That may be typecasting."

She looked at me.

Then laughed.

"You do know him."

She went to find something to drink.

I stayed seated.

A minute later Nessa appeared at the side of the house.

Not onstage.

In the narrow wall passage beside the audience.

She saw me.

Pointed two fingers at her eyes.

Then at me.

I pointed at the stage.

She shook her head once.

No idea what that meant.

Then she vanished through a curtain.

The second act began.

This one had more movement.

A public room scene.

Six actors.

Two tables.

People entering and leaving from three directions.

A tray of cups.

The flower frame.

The hidden wall door.

A narrow raised platform at stage right.

And a small suspended glass lantern above center stage that I had not seen before.

The lantern held no flame.

It held a pale point of blue-white light.

Not bright.

Steady.

Artifact or stage device.

Again, obvious purpose.

When characters stood beneath it, their shadows sharpened.

When they moved beyond its center, the shadows softened.

That let the stage create a private-feeling pool inside a crowded room.

Clever.

Not powerful.

Useful.

Then I noticed the problem.

Not magical.

Human.

Marek entered too early.

Half a breath.

Maybe one full breath.

I knew because Hara had not cleared the flower frame yet.

From backstage, that would have been obvious.

From the audience, it looked like he had chosen to stop beside her.

Hara looked at him.

Marek looked at the frame.

The actor carrying it froze for less than a second.

Then Hara turned her line toward Marek instead of the man seated at the table.

"If you insist on arriving before you're wanted, at least make yourself useful."

The audience laughed.

Marek took the near side of the flower frame.

The other actor released it without looking surprised.

Together they moved it three steps.

Marek set it down.

Then answered with his actual line.

"I've never made that mistake successfully."

Bigger laugh.

The scene continued.

I sat very still.

There.

That was live recovery.

Not hiding error by pretending nothing happened.

Using the error as material.

Hara had not fixed Marek.

Marek had not apologized.

The frame carrier had not fought for the original blocking.

The whole thing had bent around the mistake and returned to shape.

I thought of Hessa.

Late correction.

My habit of feeling control thin and shoving harder.

Do not chase clean result.

Different domain.

Same temptation.

Not same rule.

Careful.

I was not going to turn theatre into magic homework.

Still.

There was something familiar in letting a bad moment remain small.

Marek had arrived early.

The scene had not died.

Nobody had dragged him backward to the mark and restarted reality.

Useful.

The rest of the second act went cleanly enough that I stopped tracking clean.

That was probably the point.

At the final scene, the blue-white lantern dimmed.

The sound cups opened again.

Hara stood alone by the fake window.

The set behind her went dark except for the painted window frame.

Then the glass panes showed rain.

Not water.

Light.

Thin silver lines falling over the glass from nowhere.

Illusion work.

Simple compared with combat phantasms.

Still beautiful.

The audience went silent.

Hara looked through fake rain at a street that did not exist.

Then said one quiet line.

"I kept the door because I thought leaving was the same as losing."

The sound cups carried it to the back.

The oval clicked in my bag.

I felt it.

My hand moved halfway toward the flap.

Stopped.

No.

Not now.

Not every click deserved me.

Onstage, Hara put her hand against the fake window.

The silver rain slowed.

Stopped.

Blackout.

The audience stood so fast I almost got hit by a coat.

Applause filled the room.

Real applause.

Not polite.

The old woman with the hat shouted, "Slap him next time!"

Marek, bowing onstage, heard her.

He pointed at Hara.

Hara slapped him.

Not hard.

Perfectly timed.

The house lost its mind.

I laughed until my right side hurt.

Not injured.

Just laughing.

Good problem.

After the bows, people filed out slowly.

I waited.

Crutches plus crowd.

No reason to race.

The boy beside me told his mother the rain was real.

She said, "Yes."

I approved this time.

When the aisle cleared, I stood.

The oval clicked again when I crossed the inner house door.

Normal.

Whatever the earlier click had been, it had not mattered enough to interrupt the play.

Good.

I was learning not to let objects train me.

Marek found me in the lobby still wearing half his costume.

Blue waistcoat.

No coat.

One boot unlaced.

There he was.

Backstage Marek restored.

"You came."

"Against my judgment."

"How was I?"

"Early."

His face changed.

"You saw that?"

"Flower frame."

He pointed at me accusingly.

"You are not allowed to watch theatre anymore."

Hara appeared behind him.

"He saw it?"

"Immediately."

She looked pleased.

"Good."

"Why good?" Marek asked.

"Because you were early."

"I recovered."

"You were still early."

"The audience laughed."

"At me fixing you."

"At us."

"No."

They were still arguing when Nessa arrived carrying the empty basket from Act One.

I pointed at it.

"Empty."

She looked down.

"Yes."

"Marek carried it wrong."

Marek said, "I carried a basket."

"Like it weighed nothing."

"It did weigh nothing."

Nessa closed her eyes.

Hara laughed.

"He's right."

Marek looked betrayed.

"Whose side is this?"

Nessa handed him the basket.

"Carry it like there are six bottles in it tomorrow."

"There are no bottles."

"Then act."

I liked Nessa.

Rinna came through the lobby with a small slate in one hand.

She saw me.

"Seat worked?"

There it was.

Not Marek.

Rinna had chosen the seat.

"Yes."

"Crutches clear of aisle?"

"Yes."

She marked something on the slate.

Not charity.

Operations.

Even better.

"Thank you," I said.

She looked up.

I meant the seat.

She understood.

"You moved three unpaid routes this month."

"I did not move three routes."

"You checked three routes."

"That is different."

"Then you checked enough for one seat."

"Is that accounting?"

"No. If it were accounting, you'd hate the number."

Probably true.

She moved on.

Marek said, "See? Not work."

I looked at him.

"You wrote probably."

"I believe in accuracy."

Hessa had infected the city.

I left before anyone gave me a task.

Outside, the night had gone cool.

People spilled from the theatre into the street still talking about the show.

Not about the hidden wall seam.

Not about the sound cups.

Not about the flower frame route.

They argued about whether Hara's character should have burned the letter.

Whether Marek's character deserved the slap.

Whether the rain at the end meant she was leaving.

The machinery had disappeared.

The work had disappeared.

Even the magic had mostly disappeared.

What remained was what people thought happened.

That felt important.

Not in a lesson way.

In a craft way.

Maybe that was why the theatre kept pulling me back.

It was a place where hundreds of practical decisions existed solely so nobody would think about them when they succeeded.

My whole life had been the opposite lately.

I noticed every coin.

Every threshold.

Every load.

Every failed draw.

Every fraction of movement.

Necessary.

But exhausting.

For two hours, I had watched a fake room become more real than the real room around it.

That was worth something.

Apparently exactly one comp seat.

I walked home with two copper still in my purse.

No new job.

No new artifact.

No magical revelation.

The oval clicked at my building door.

Normal.

Upstairs, Tavi was asleep.

Cala was still awake at the table.

She looked up.

"Good?"

I put my crutches against the wall.

"Marek got slapped."

"Good."

"You don't know why."

"Still good."

Strong audience instinct.

I went upstairs.

Lyssa was awake too.

"How was it?"

I sat on the edge of the bed.

Thought about Hara's fake rain.

The hidden wall.

Marek's early entrance.

Nessa's basket.

Rinna's aisle seat.

The sound cups opening in the dark.

"Better from the front," I said.

Lyssa smiled.

"That's usually where they put the audience."

"Apparently for a reason."

I took the oval from my bag and hung it on the hook.

It turned toward the doorway.

Normal.

I did not write anything down.

That might have been the strangest part.
