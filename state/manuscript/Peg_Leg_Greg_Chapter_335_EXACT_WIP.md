# CHAPTER 335

## THE BLUE HARNESS

The first thing I wanted badly enough to become ambitious about cost twenty-six copper.

This was inconvenient.

I had two.

The difference between those numbers was not subtle.

It began with a piece of paper nailed crookedly to a post outside the lower market.

Not a work notice.

I checked.

Growth.

The heading read:

SOUTH QUAY FREIGHT SALE

PUBLIC VIEWING TODAY

SALE TOMORROW AFTER THIRD BELL

WAREHOUSE GOODS / CLAIMED LOTS / TOOLS / FITTINGS / THREE REGISTERED WORKING PIECES

NO CHILDREN PAST BLUE LINE

I stopped at `three registered working pieces.`

Of course I did.

Theatre the night before had apparently cured me of writing things down for almost twelve hours.

A personal record.

I read the rest.

Viewing free.

Entry to sale free.

Registered pieces subject to buyer name and transfer fee.

Transfer fee not listed.

Annoying.

South Quay was farther than I had planned to go that morning.

I had planned to buy nothing, do nothing useful, and possibly sit somewhere that sold tea cheaply enough that I could resent other people's purchases.

Instead I turned toward the river.

Ambition began with terrible scheduling.

The lower streets changed as I went.

Less cloth hanging from windows.

More rope.

More carts with wheels thick enough to survive people who thought roads were suggestions.

The smell changed too.

Fish first.

Then wet wood.

Then pitch.

Then river mud underneath everything.

Carrow's south quay sat lower than most of the market district, broad stone ramps descending toward a line of warehouses and loading yards.

I had been near it before.

Not here.

Not this row.

That mattered.

First-life cities had taught me that docks were all the same right until they killed you differently.

Local rules mattered.

Blue paint marked walking lanes between cart routes.

I stayed inside it.

A bell rang twice from somewhere upriver.

Nobody around me reacted.

Not my bell.

Excellent.

Warehouse Twelve had both doors open.

A painted board beside them repeated the sale notice.

A woman at a narrow table looked at my crutches, then at the blue floor line, then at me.

"Viewing?"

"Yes."

"Working pieces are behind rope. Don't touch anything with a red tag. Yellow tag means damaged or incomplete. White means ordinary lot."

"Registered pieces?"

"Blue cards."

"Can I read them?"

"That's why they're there."

Wonderful institution.

"Transfer fee?"

She pointed to a smaller board I had not seen.

REGISTERED TRANSFER: 1 C PER PIECE

There went another copper I did not have.

Not today.

Tomorrow, hypothetically.

Still no.

"Thank you."

"Stay out of cart lane."

"Planning to."

"Everyone is."

Fair.

Inside, the warehouse was colder than the street.

Stone floor.

High rafters.

Light through dusty upper windows.

Rows of sale lots separated by chalk and rope.

Most were exactly what the notice promised.

Tools.

Fittings.

Crates of nails.

Two broken pulley assemblies.

A stack of shutters.

A copper wash basin with a dent large enough to become personality.

Bundles of old sailcloth.

A carpenter's bench.

Three narrow carts.

Nothing magical about most of it.

Good.

Meaningful artifacts were supposed to be rare.

If every warehouse clearance contained six hidden miracles, nobody would clear warehouses.

They would hire more Mara.

The first blue card stood beside a red-painted iron box.

REGISTERED WORKING PIECE 1

HEAT-SAFE STORAGE CHEST

VISIBLE FUNCTION VERIFIED AT INTAKE

INTERIOR COOLING / PASSIVE WHEN LATCHED

HINGE DAMAGE / RIGHT REAR

NO TEMPERATURE CERTIFICATION

OPENING 8 C

Useful.

Obvious.

Eight copper.

I moved on.

The second was a pair of brass clamps mounted on a board.

REGISTERED WORKING PIECE 2

TWIN LOAD CLAMPS

MATCHED PAIR

HOLD UNDER DEMONSTRATED STATIC PULL

RELEASE UNEVEN

NO RATED LOAD

OPENING 11 C

Also useful.

Also expensive.

Also not mine.

Then I saw the blue harness.

It was not blue.

That was the first problem.

Mostly brown leather.

Wide shoulder straps.

A waist belt.

Two narrow metal rails running from shoulder to hip.

Four dark blue thread bundles stitched through the leather in parallel lines.

At the center of the back sat a palm-sized silver housing with a cloudy blue stone under a protective cage.

The whole thing hung from a wooden frame behind rope.

It looked like work equipment.

Old work equipment.

Good work equipment.

The blue card read:

REGISTERED WORKING PIECE 3

ROAD CARRIER'S LOAD HARNESS

MARK: VENN ARLO / SOUTH WORKS

REGISTERED 47 YEARS PRIOR

VISIBLE FUNCTION VERIFIED AT INTAKE

REDUCES CARRIED LOAD DURING CONTINUOUS WALKING

EFFECT FALLS WHEN USER STOPS

TESTED WITH 40-LB SACK / WALKING LOAD SUBJECTIVELY REDUCED

NO RUNNING TEST

NO STAIR TEST

LEFT SHOULDER STRAP REPLACED

CORE CLOUDING PRESENT

OPENING 26 C

I read it twice.

Then a third time because apparently poverty improved literacy.

Venn Arlo.

South Works.

Forty-seven years prior.

That was wrong.

Not the card.

My reaction.

I knew that name.

Not famous now, apparently.

Or famous enough that nobody around me was making noise about it.

In my first life, Venn Arlo had been a name attached to military road gear.

Later road gear.

Much later.

Field bridges.

March frames.

Load-transfer harnesses that officers loved because they could make exhausted people carry more things while calling it efficiency.

I had hated them.

Not because they were bad.

Because they were good enough to encourage bad decisions.

The best ones did not simply make weight lighter.

They changed where load lived while the wearer moved.

Feet.

Ground contact.

Stride timing.

Body rhythm.

Some models could make a pack feel almost polite until you stopped.

Then the world remembered physics.

I stared at the harness.

Forty-seven years prior.

How old had Arlo been then?

I did not know.

That was important.

I knew the later name.

Not this object.

Not this period.

Not whether this was even the same Venn Arlo.

Names repeated.

Workshops reused names.

Sons inherited stamps.

People lied.

Registries helped but did not perform miracles.

Still.

Venn Arlo.

South Works.

My memory pulled a wet military road.

Not a battle.

Worse.

Logistics.

Men complaining under packs.

A quartermaster telling me the new Arlo frames had cut mule demand on the ridge route.

Someone else saying they had doubled knee injuries because commanders immediately increased standard loads.

Humanity.

Give us efficiency and we would use it to invent more suffering.

I looked back at the card.

REDUCES CARRIED LOAD DURING CONTINUOUS WALKING.

EFFECT FALLS WHEN USER STOPS.

That alone justified money.

Twenty-six copper opening money?

Maybe.

For freight work, absolutely maybe.

A porter who could carry more per trip had direct earning value.

A courier.

A caravan hand.

A survey crew.

Anyone moving tools over distance.

The market did not need my future knowledge to see that.

Good.

Then I looked at my crutches.

That was the second problem.

Walking for me was not symmetrical.

Right foot.

Crutches.

Left side absent below knee.

The harness might interpret movement through footfalls.

Or ground contacts.

Or hip translation.

Or repeated displacement.

Or something else entirely.

If it required alternating feet, useless.

If it used total ground-contact rhythm, maybe not.

If it transferred load into every contact point, crutches might count.

That could be incredible.

Or terrible.

A device pushing redistributed load through my shoulders and crutch tips could turn lighter carrying into joint damage.

The card said no stair test.

Nothing about crutches.

Of course not.

I leaned closer without touching the rope.

The replaced left shoulder strap was newer leather.

Different stitching.

Functional-looking.

The blue thread bundles crossed beneath it and disappeared into the silver back housing.

One bundle had been restitched around the replacement.

Not cut.

Rerouted.

Interesting.

"You planning to buy it with your eyes?"

I looked right.

A man stood two spaces away.

Forties maybe.

Shorter than me.

Heavy gray coat despite the warehouse not being that cold.

Hands with rope scars.

Not decorative hands.

He held a folded sale sheet.

"Working on it," I said.

He looked at my crutches.

Then the harness.

Then back at me.

Not pity.

Calculation.

"Wouldn't."

"Why?"

"Stops heavy."

"Card says effect falls when user stops."

"Card is polite."

That got my attention.

"You've used one?"

"Not that one."

Good distinction.

"Same family?"

He shrugged.

"Carrier harness. Old dock crew had two. You walk, load goes friendly. You stop wrong, pack remembers what it weighs."

That matched my first-life category memory.

Not proof about this unit.

"Dangerous?"

"Everything is dangerous if you put a dockhand inside it."

Fair.

"What happened to the two?"

"One sold. One cracked a core."

"From stopping?"

"From being dropped off a cart."

Also humanity.

He looked at the blue card.

"Twenty-six is stupid."

I kept my face neutral.

"Too high?"

"Opening? Yes."

Interesting.

"What would you pay?"

"Eighteen if I needed it. Twenty if the core was clear."

"It's cloudy."

"Exactly."

"You bidding?"

He smiled.

"You?"

"I have two copper."

He laughed once.

Not cruelly.

"Then no."

"Strong analysis."

He moved on.

I watched him go.

No name.

No automatic rival.

Just a man who knew carrier gear and thought the opening price was high.

That mattered too.

Future fame did not erase present condition.

Clouded core.

Replaced strap.

Unknown interaction with my body.

No stair test.

No running test.

Twenty-six opening plus one-copper transfer.

At least twenty-seven just to leave with it if nobody bid higher.

I had two.

This was not a bargain I had failed to notice.

It was an expensive object that might be more important than the current market understood for reasons the current market could not reasonably price.

Different thing.

I wanted it anyway.

That surprised me.

Not because it was magical.

I had seen magical equipment.

Not because it was powerful.

It was a carrying harness.

The thing made sacks less awful while moving.

Very heroic.

I wanted it because I could see my life bending around it if it worked with me.

The shoulder bag became easier.

The holdfast became easier to transport.

Longer walks changed.

Field jobs changed.

Travel changed.

Buying awkward things changed.

Maybe stairs became worse.

Maybe stopping became dangerous.

Maybe my crutches made the entire device useless.

That uncertainty made me want information almost as much as the object.

I found the viewing-table woman.

"Can registered pieces be demonstrated before sale?"

"Intake verification only."

"Can bidder request fit test?"

"No."

"Can buyer inspect after purchase before transfer?"

"Visual only until transfer recorded."

"Can I see intake notes?"

She pointed to another table.

"One bit per copied page. Read here free."

Excellent.

I went there.

The intake sheet for Piece Three was two pages.

I read both.

No copying.

The harness had come from a dissolved freight partnership called Marrow & Pell South Carry.

No relation to marker Pell established.

Probably.

Do not invent relatives because names repeat.

It had been stored six years according to claimant paperwork.

Intake verification used one adult worker, forty-pound sack, level warehouse floor, twenty paces out, twenty back.

Worker reported load reduction after fourth pace.

On stopping, full perceived load returned over approximately one breath.

No fall.

No stumble.

No visible core flare.

Housing warmed slightly.

Second pass not performed because intake clerk considered one verified function enough for sale classification.

Reasonable.

Condition notes:

Left strap replaced before storage.

Blue transfer threads continuous through replacement path.

Core cloudy before test and unchanged after.

Silver housing shows two shallow dents.

Waist clasp stiff.

No registry hold.

No bonded-user notation.

No known injury claim attached to lot.

That was it.

No secret.

No free answer about crutches.

No line saying THIS BECOMES LEGENDARY LATER.

Rude paperwork.

I went back to the harness.

Twenty-six.

Plus transfer.

Maybe more in bidding.

The rope-scarred man was gone.

A woman with a freight company's yellow shoulder stripe was now reading the card.

She had a clerk with her.

The clerk wrote something.

There.

Real buyer.

Of course.

A freight company could value this directly.

I had no special right to it because I remembered a future surname.

I also could not outbid a company with two copper and personality.

Probably.

I left the warehouse.

Outside, the quay was louder.

A cart rolled past carrying bundled poles.

Two porters moved a chest between them.

A boy balanced three empty baskets on his head and looked more stable than I felt emotionally.

I sat on a low stone wall inside the blue pedestrian line.

Twenty-six copper.

My first thought was stupid.

I need twenty-six copper.

No.

Mara had already cured me of saving for yesterday.

If I somehow found twenty-six by tomorrow, I would still need transfer money.

And then I would have almost nothing.

And I would be buying a clouded-core artifact without knowing whether it worked with my gait.

Bad plan.

The useful thought was larger.

I wanted enough money that a twenty-six-copper object could become a decision.

Not today.

Not this harness necessarily.

This category of opportunity.

Twenty-six should be painful.

It should not be fictional.

That was different from wanting twenty-six coins in a pile.

Reserve.

Income.

Access.

Information.

The ability to lose an auction and still eat without composing a tragedy about bread.

I took out the narrow scrap.

Then stopped.

Last night I had not written anything down.

That did not mean notes were bad now.

Growth was not a religion.

There.

Ugly.

Useful.

A freight bell rang once.

People moved.

I did not know what the bell meant, so I watched before moving.

Also growth.

A line of carts began crossing the broad ramp.

I waited.

Then headed home by a different street because the quay road was now clogged.

The oval clicked normally at the warehouse door when I had left.

It clicked normally at a stone arch near the upper market.

It did nothing interesting.

Good.

My shoulders were tired by the time I reached home.

The irony was aggressive.

Tavi was on the floor building something from the remaining repair-sweep junk.

Not the oval.

Not the holdfast.

The ordinary pieces.

Cala had apparently decided the obviously mundane buckles and iron hook were safe enough under supervision.

He had made a machine that did nothing.

"Carrier."

"For what?"

"Spoon."

Of course.

"Five copper."

"Four."

"He started it."

Two copper away from a simple bench observation if I wanted to become broke.

I did not feel poor.

That was the strange part.

Much more dignified problem.
