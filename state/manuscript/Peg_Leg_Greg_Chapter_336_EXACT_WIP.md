# CHAPTER 336

## THE BID LINE

I arrived at Warehouse Twelve with two copper and the emotional confidence of a man attending an auction he could not influence.

Strong position.

Nobody could accuse me of overbidding.

The blue pedestrian lanes were busier than yesterday.

Carts queued outside the freight doors.

Men and women with warehouse tags stood in clusters pretending not to inspect each other.

The public entrance had a board now.

SALE ORDER

ORDINARY LOTS FIRST

REGISTERED WORKING PIECES AFTER THIRD BELL

TRANSFER FEE 1 C PER REGISTERED PIECE

NO CREDIT WITHOUT PRIOR ACCOUNT

That last line destroyed several fantasies I had not been having.

I took my place near the back.

Not hidden.

Just poor.

The oval clicked when I crossed the warehouse threshold.

Normal.

Ignored.

Yesterday's display tables had been moved.

The ordinary lots now sat in rows with chalk numbers.

Crates.

Hooks.

Used fittings.

One dented handcart.

Three bundles of rope that looked better from ten feet away than from five.

The registered pieces stayed against the east wall behind a yellow line.

Heat-safe chest.

Twin load clamps.

Blue harness.

The harness looked exactly as expensive as yesterday.

Rude object.

A clerk stood beside it with the blue card.

Another clerk held a ledger.

The freight-company woman from yesterday was there.

Yellow shoulder stripe.

Today she had two other people with her.

One wore the same stripe.

The other did not.

No company name visible.

The gray-coated dock man was also there.

He saw me.

Nodded.

I nodded back.

We had reached the deepest level of male friendship.

Mutual artifact skepticism.

Ordinary lots took longer than expected.

Everything did.

The auctioneer was a square man with a square voice.

He stood on a low platform and sold thirty pounds of bent brackets with more authority than some kings had shown over borders.

"Five bits."

Silence.

"Four."

Hand raised.

"Four bits. Any five?"

No.

"Sold."

Efficient.

Then a box of mismatched hinge plates went for one copper and three bits.

No conversion established.

Excellent.

I learned nothing useful from the arithmetic except that people paid real money for boring things when they needed them.

Which was probably the most useful lesson available.

A cooper bought two iron bands.

A woman with green sleeves bought the dented handcart after arguing with the auctioneer about one wheel.

The auctioneer did not care about the wheel.

The woman cared enough for both of them.

By third bell the ordinary lots were mostly gone.

People shifted.

Different posture.

Different attention.

The registered pieces changed the room.

Not dramatically.

Just enough.

More ledgers came out.

Fewer casual bidders remained near the front.

The auctioneer looked at the first blue card.

"Registered working piece one. Heat-safe storage chest. Verified passive interior cooling when latched. Right rear hinge damaged. No temperature certification. Opening eight copper. Transfer one additional copper."

Eight.

Silence.

Then a hand.

"Eight."

Another.

"Nine."

The first bidder waited.

"Ten."

A woman near the wall said, "Eleven."

No drama.

No gasps.

People were buying equipment.

The chest sold for twelve copper.

Plus transfer.

Thirteen out the door.

I wrote nothing.

I remembered.

Second piece.

Twin brass load clamps.

Opening eleven.

Uneven release.

No rated load.

That one stalled at twelve.

The auctioneer pushed.

"Verified static hold. Matched pair. Registered transfer. Twelve once."

A man in a blue coat raised two fingers.

"Thirteen."

Nobody answered.

Sold thirteen.

Plus transfer.

Fourteen total.

Interesting.

The harness was next.

I knew because the room reorganized again.

The yellow-stripe freight group stepped closer.

Gray coat did not.

He stayed beside me.

"There," he said.

"You bidding?"

"No."

"Thought you said maybe eighteen."

"I said if I needed it."

"You don't."

"I need knees more."

Fair.

The clerk lifted the blue card.

The auctioneer read slowly.

Not because the card was difficult.

Because price liked ceremony.

"Registered working piece three. Road carrier's load harness. Venn Arlo, South Works. Registered forty-seven years. Verified intake function: reduced carried load during continuous walking. Effect falls on stop. Intake test forty-pound sack, level floor, twenty paces out and return. Reduction reported after fourth pace. Full perceived load returned over approximately one breath after stop."

People listened.

"No running test. No stair test. No crutch test. Left shoulder strap replaced before storage. Transfer threads continuous through replacement. Core cloudy before and after intake test. Silver housing two shallow dents. Waist clasp stiff. No registry hold. No known injury claim attached. Opening twenty-six copper. Transfer one copper."

There it was.

Twenty-six.

Silence.

Longer than for the other pieces.

Not because nobody wanted it.

Because twenty-six was a different kind of decision.

The yellow-stripe woman raised her hand.

"Twenty-six."

Immediate.

The auctioneer nodded.

"Twenty-six."

No one else moved.

He waited.

"Twenty-seven?"

A man near the front said, "Twenty-seven."

I had not noticed him yesterday.

Dark vest.

Shaved head.

Ink-stained fingers.

Not dock hands.

Not obvious freight either.

Yellow stripe did not look at him.

"Twenty-eight," she said.

Dark vest said, "Twenty-nine."

Gray coat beside me made a small sound through his nose.

"Still high?" I asked.

"Higher."

Good analysis.

Yellow stripe said, "Thirty."

Now people started watching the bidders instead of the harness.

Human nature.

The auctioneer said, "Thirty copper. Thirty-one?"

Dark vest paused.

Then raised one finger.

"Thirty-one."

The freight woman finally looked at him.

Not angry.

Assessing.

One of her companions leaned close.

Whispered.

She shook her head once.

"Thirty-two."

There.

This was already above the twenty-six opening by enough to matter.

Not absurdly.

Not war-chest money.

But clear demand.

Dark vest looked toward the harness.

Not at the card.

At the cloudy core.

Then at the replaced shoulder strap.

Then at the silver housing dents.

He said, "Thirty-three."

The freight woman said, "Thirty-five."

The room went quieter.

Not shocked.

Interested.

Gray coat said, "Company wants it."

"Apparently."

"Or wants him not to."

That was more interesting.

I looked at dark vest.

No insignia.

No clerk beside him.

But he had a folded document in one hand.

Registry copy maybe.

Inspection note.

Provenance paper.

Something.

He did not bid immediately.

The auctioneer waited.

"Thirty-five copper. Thirty-six?"

Dark vest unfolded the paper.

Read one line.

Then said, "Thirty-six."

Yellow stripe's second companion spoke quietly.

I caught only one word.

"Repair."

The woman asked the auctioneer, "Can registered condition note be reread?"

The auctioneer looked at the clerk.

The clerk said, "Yes."

No one complained.

Useful market.

The clerk read the condition lines again.

Cloudy core.

Two shallow housing dents.

Replaced left shoulder strap.

Stiff waist clasp.

No running, stair, or crutch test.

Then added something I had not heard yesterday.

"Claimant paperwork includes one prior service notation, nineteen years ago. Housing alignment checked after cart fall. No part replacement recorded."

I stared.

Gray coat said, "There it is."

"What?"

"Dents got history."

That mattered.

Not because cart fall meant broken.

Because the dents were no longer generic wear.

They had provenance.

A known event.

Nineteen years ago.

No part replacement recorded.

Could be fine.

Could matter.

The yellow-stripe woman asked, "Any later alignment notation?"

Clerk checked.

"None attached."

Dark vest asked, "Any later bonded inspection?"

"None attached."

The auctioneer said, "Condition as stated. No additional certification. Thirty-six copper standing."

The freight woman did not bid.

Interesting.

The auctioneer waited.

"Thirty-six once."

No movement.

"Thirty-six twice."

Yellow stripe's companion whispered again.

She shook her head.

"Sold. Thirty-six copper. Registered transfer one copper."

Thirty-seven total.

Dark vest had it.

Not mine.

Never plausibly mine.

Still hurt.

Annoying.

The buyer stepped forward.

The clerk took his name too quietly for me to hear.

Good.

No automatic identity gift.

He paid a deposit at the desk.

Not full visible coin.

Prior account maybe.

Or registered bidder terms.

The board had said no credit without prior account.

So he had one.

Another layer.

Artifact access was not just cash.

It was accounts.

Registry.

Transport.

Inspection.

Repair tolerance.

People who could bid thirty-six without carrying thirty-six loose copper in a pocket like a lunatic.

I hated civilization again.

Gray coat said, "Company stopped at thirty-five."

"Because of the cart-fall notation?"

"Maybe."

"You think thirty-six is bad?"

He shrugged.

"Depends what he knows."

There it was.

Perfect answer.

"You'd still stop at twenty?"

"For me? Yes."

"Even after thirty-six sale?"

"Sale price tells me what somebody paid. Not what I should pay."

I looked at him.

"That's annoyingly sensible."

"I work docks."

Apparently that explained philosophy.

The auction moved on to ordinary closing announcements.

People dispersed.

The yellow-stripe group stayed near the clerk.

Not arguing.

Asking something about another lot list.

So losing the harness had not ended their day.

Also useful.

The dark-vest buyer stood near the harness while a warehouse worker adjusted the waist clasp.

He did not put it on.

Good.

No spontaneous demonstration.

No destiny.

I stayed back.

Watching.

The buyer pointed at the replaced strap.

Asked something.

The clerk answered.

He pointed at the silver housing.

More talking.

Then a warehouse porter brought a rigid transport frame.

The harness was tied into it before removal.

That caught me.

Not because transport frames were exotic.

Because yesterday I had been thinking about wearing the thing home.

Of course a thirty-six-copper registered working piece left in a protected frame.

Money changed logistics before function.

I thought of carrying the holdfast against my back in a basket with scrap slats.

Different scale.

Same problem.

Access required infrastructure.

I walked outside.

South Quay smelled like salt, oil, wet rope, and other people's capital.

I sat on a low stone edge beside the pedestrian lane.

Not long.

Enough to rest shoulders.

Two copper in my purse.

Thirty-six sale price in my head.

Thirty-seven with transfer.

The target line I had written yesterday suddenly looked small.

Twenty to thirty copper opportunity.

The harness had opened at twenty-six and left at thirty-six.

That did not mean all such opportunities would.

It meant reserve had to include bidding uncertainty too.

Not just asking price.

If I had somehow arrived with twenty-seven exactly, I would have learned nothing except how fast twenty-seven stopped mattering.

I took out the narrow scrap.

I had said yesterday I would not write `NEED 26 C.`

Good.

Still correct.

Under the reserve line I added:

OPEN 26 / SOLD 36 / +1 TRANSFER

Then:

PRICE CAN MOVE ~10 C BEFORE DECISION ENDS

I stared at that.

Too specific.

One auction.

One object.

I crossed out the ten.

Wrote:

OPENING PRICE != BUYING PRICE

Then:

ACCESS = CASH + ACCOUNT + INSPECTION + TRANSPORT + REPAIR ROOM

That was uglier.

Also better.

I underlined REPAIR ROOM once.

The cart-fall notation had not destroyed the harness.

It had changed who stayed in.

The freight company stopped at thirty-five.

The private buyer went thirty-six.

Maybe he had cheaper repair access.

Maybe he valued the maker.

Maybe he knew Venn Arlo.

Maybe he was simply wrong.

Markets were allowed to contain confident idiots too.

Rational did not mean omniscient.

Useful distinction.

I did not write that.

I was becoming selective.

Dangerous.

On the walk home, the oval clicked at South Quay gate.

Normal.

At the building door.

Normal.

At the upstairs doorway.

Normal.

The holdfast sat on its shelf.

Two copper between me and Mara's simple bench observation.

Still not doing it.

That surprised me less now.

The harness sale had made the reason clearer.

Zero was not just no money.

Zero was no ability to react when the next useful thing appeared.

Even if I could not afford the next useful thing yet.

Reserve was practice for larger reserve.

That sounded unbearably mature.

I hated it.

Lyssa came in while I was looking at the scrap.

"How much?"

"Thirty-six."

She stopped.

"Copper?"

"Yes. Plus one transfer."

"You were not close."

"Thank you."

"You knew that."

"I preferred my version."

She sat across from me.

"Who bought it?"

"Man in dark vest. Didn't hear name. Freight company stopped at thirty-five."

"Why?"

"Condition note. Old cart fall, maybe. Or price. Or repair."

"You don't know."

"Correct."

She nodded.

Then looked at the scrap.

"What did you learn?"

I slid it over.

She read:

OPENING PRICE != BUYING PRICE

ACCESS = CASH + ACCOUNT + INSPECTION + TRANSPORT + REPAIR ROOM

"That's more expensive than yesterday."

"Everything becomes more expensive when understood."

"Not everything."

"Name one thing."

She looked toward the kitchen.

"Tavi."

From downstairs Tavi shouted, "I HEARD THAT."

Lyssa smiled.

"Cheaper every day."

Good household economics.

I took the scrap back.

Thirty-six copper.

I still wanted the harness.

That was the inconvenient part.

Not that harness specifically.

Maybe that one.

Yes, that one.

But more than that, I wanted to live in a financial shape where seeing something like it did not immediately place me outside the room.

Not rich.

Ready.

Different ambition.

I looked at the two copper.

Then at the holdfast.

Then at the oval hanging quietly.

One had cost two.

One had cost one.

Both had taught me more than their prices.

The harness had cost me nothing.

It had still changed what money meant.

Annoying object.

I hoped the dark-vest man enjoyed it.

Not too much.
