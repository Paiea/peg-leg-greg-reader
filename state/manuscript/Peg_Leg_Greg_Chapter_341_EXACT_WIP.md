# CHAPTER 341

## THE LONG MILE

Cala's cousin arrived with a cart, a cracked wheel rim, and a problem that lived eight miles outside Carrow.

I knew this because he announced all three before breakfast.

"Cart's fine," he said.

The cart outside leaned visibly to the left.

"Wheel's cracked."

"You just said the cart was fine."

"Cart is. Wheel isn't."

I looked at Cala.

She was stirring something in a pot and refusing to help me.

The man was called Beren.

I had met him once.

Maybe twice.

Wide face, short beard, shoulders built by carrying things he should have asked other people to carry.

He lived north of Carrow near a place called Moss Ford, which was less a village than several farms, a mill, a shrine, and enough houses to make arguments communal.

His cart carried three sacks of grain, a wrapped iron fitting, two empty baskets, and a wooden cage containing a bird that hated civilization.

The bird screamed at me.

"Good morning," I said.

It screamed again.

"Strong position."

Beren ignored the bird.

"Need a second pair of eyes."

I looked behind me.

"Cala?"

"You."

"I have one pair."

"You know field marks."

That was dangerous language.

"I have looked at some field marks."

"Same thing."

"Very much not."

Cala finally turned.

"Tell him the whole thing."

Beren sighed like the whole thing had personally inconvenienced him.

"North path's moved."

"Paths do that."

"Not that one."

"Then what moved?"

"Don't know."

Excellent beginning.

He explained.

There was an old public foot-and-cart route between Moss Ford and Carrow's north road.

Not the main road.

A secondary route.

Used by farm carts, walkers, mill traffic when the lower road flooded, and people avoiding toll weight on larger loads.

Three days ago, the path began bending east around a low meadow.

Not people choosing a new track.

The actual packed road.

"Bending how?"

"You walk straight. End up east."

"That is usually how turns work."

"No turn."

I stopped being funny.

"No visible turn?"

"No."

"Landmarks?"

"Wrong."

"How wrong?"

"Mill stack looks closer. Shrine stones look farther. Creek crossing comes early."

There.

Interesting.

"Anyone hurt?"

"No."

"Missing?"

"No."

"Carts getting stuck?"

"One went into wet ground yesterday because driver followed the fence instead of road."

"Why would he do that?"

"Because road put him east of his own fence."

That was better.

Or worse.

"Who has checked it?"

"Moss shrine keeper. Mill people. Two road men came yesterday, put stakes, argued, left."

"What did they say?"

"Don't use after dark."

"Useful."

"Need to bring this fitting to my brother anyway. Thought you might come look."

I looked at Cala.

"Why me?"

She shrugged.

"You like things that are wrong."

That was unfairly accurate.

Beren added, "And you know the old field stuff."

"Some."

"And you're cheap."

"Ah."

There it was.

"How cheap?"

"Lunch. Ride there and back."

"That's not pay."

"No."

"Good. I wanted the insult clearly labeled."

He grinned.

I considered saying no.

That was new enough to notice.

Eight miles out.

Ride both ways.

No pay.

Unknown road effect.

No one missing or injured.

Road workers already aware.

I had no obligation.

I could stay home.

I could go to the market.

I could stare responsibly at my boxed dangerous stone.

I could do nothing.

Then the bird screamed.

"Fine," I said.

Cala said, "Because of the bird?"

"It made a compelling argument."

I packed water, narrow scrap, charcoal, and nothing magical except the oval.

I almost left that too.

Then reconsidered.

A route behaving incorrectly was one of the few contexts where the oval's known behavior might actually be relevant.

Not authoritative.

Relevant.

The holdfast stayed home.

The rest stone stayed boxed.

No reason to bring either.

Beren's cart wheel had a split iron rim brace, not a cracked wheel rim exactly.

The wood itself was fine.

He had lied by simplification.

Normal human behavior.

I sat on the left side of the cart bed with my crutches tied beside me where I could reach them.

Not comfortable.

Better than walking eight miles before investigating a road that might not respect distance.

We left through North Gate.

The oval clicked.

Normal.

Carrow thinned slowly.

Stone buildings became timber.

Timber became yards.

Yards became fields.

The road widened first, then narrowed.

We passed two freight carts, a woman driving six goats, and a boy carrying a bundle of reeds taller than he was.

Nothing magical happened.

This was disappointing in the healthy way.

Beren talked.

Mostly about grain prices without numbers.

Bad rain.

Good rain.

Wrong rain.

Mill timing.

His brother's inability to repair anything before it became expensive.

The bird screamed whenever he paused.

I learned its name was Hush.

"You named the loud bird Hush."

"Daughter did."

"Good child."

Two miles out, the land began to roll.

Not hills.

Long shallow rises.

Fields separated by stone walls and drainage cuts.

Magic lived here differently than in Carrow.

Less brass.

Less glass.

More things tied to posts.

Small white stones hung from orchard branches on red cord.

A field gate had three copper nails driven into the top beam in a triangle.

At one drainage crossing, a flat black slab sat half submerged with water bending around it too cleanly.

I knew categories.

Weather wards.

Boundary discouragement.

Flow shaping.

Local implementations varied.

I did not ask about every one.

This was also growth.

At the fourth mile marker, Beren pointed.

"Road men."

Two fresh stakes stood beside the road.

White tops.

Black numbers.

One on each side.

A thin cord had been stretched between them earlier, judging by the cut grass, but removed now.

"Yesterday?"

"Yes."

"Where does the problem start?"

"Past next rise."

We continued.

The road looked ordinary.

Packed dirt.

Wheel ruts.

Grass shoulders.

Fence to the west.

Low meadow east.

Mill stack ahead-left.

A line of dark trees farther north.

Then Beren said, "There."

I felt nothing.

No mana pressure.

No vertigo.

No dramatic shimmer.

The road continued straight.

The cart continued straight.

The fence continued straight.

The mill stack moved right.

I frowned.

Not physically.

Relative position.

We had not turned.

The stack had shifted from ahead-left toward directly ahead.

I looked back.

The rise behind us seemed slightly farther west than it should have been.

"Stop."

Beren stopped.

The full cart weight settled.

Hush screamed.

I untied my crutches and climbed down.

Ground ordinary.

Packed road beneath right foot and crutch tips.

I turned slowly.

Fence west.

Meadow east.

Road straight.

Except the wheel ruts ahead curved very slightly east.

No.

Not curved.

They were straight.

The fence and ruts were no longer parallel.

That was the wrongness.

"Was fence always like that?"

Beren said, "Fence is old."

"Not my question."

"I don't know."

Good answer eventually.

I walked ten yards ahead.

Oval in bag.

No click.

Another ten.

No click.

At a pair of old stone posts with no gate between them, click.

Normal qualifying threshold.

The oval turned back toward the posts.

Good.

I kept walking.

Twenty yards.

The mill stack shifted farther right.

I looked back at the stone posts.

They appeared behind-left.

The oval pointed behind me.

Toward the posts.

As expected.

I turned my body without moving.

Oval still toward posts.

Good.

Beren called, "Anything?"

"Road is rude."

"Knew that."

I returned to the cart.

Crossed posts.

Click.

Oval updated normally.

No strange behavior.

That was information.

Not much.

"Take me to the shrine stones."

"They're ahead."

"Apparently everything is."

We continued slowly.

The road did something I disliked.

Distance accumulated normally under the wheels.

Landmarks did not.

A drainage ditch that should have crossed after a long meadow arrived within perhaps half the expected travel.

Beren swore.

"Early."

"You said that."

"Worse today."

I looked at him.

"You sure?"

He hesitated.

"No."

Good.

We crossed the ditch.

The water flowed west to east.

Normal enough.

The road beyond remained dry.

Then the meadow grass changed.

Not color.

Direction.

Every blade leaned north despite wind moving south.

I got down again.

Beren said, "Don't wander."

"Wasn't planning to."

I approached the shoulder but stayed on packed ground.

Grass leaned north.

Wind touched my face from north to south.

The grass moved against it.

Not all grass.

A band perhaps six feet wide running diagonally across the meadow.

I followed the band with my eyes.

It crossed the road fifty yards ahead.

There.

Something physical enough to see once I knew where to look.

"You see that?"

Beren squinted.

"Grass?"

"The strip."

He stared longer.

"Oh."

"Was that there yesterday?"

"Didn't see it."

Again, not same as no.

We did not cross it yet.

I took out the narrow scrap.

Drew rough road.

Fence.

Meadow.

Ditch.

Grass band.

No conclusions.

Then a cart approached from the north.

Empty flatbed.

Older woman driving.

She slowed when she saw us stopped.

"Road open?"

Beren called, "Supposedly."

She snorted.

"Supposedly isn't a road condition."

I liked her.

"You came through the strange section?" I asked.

"If you mean the long mile, yes."

There.

A name.

"Long mile?"

"That's what my father called it when it woke up."

Beren turned toward her.

"Woke up?"

She looked at him like he was young.

He was at least forty.

"You Moss Ford people forgot everything useful."

"I'm Moss Ford people."

"Exactly."

I stepped closer but stayed clear of her horse.

"How often?"

She shrugged.

"Not in my adult life. Father said twice when he was young. Grandmother said more."

"What happens?"

"Road goes long."

"It feels shorter in places."

"Depends which way."

Interesting.

"Dangerous?"

"If you're stupid."

Universal condition.

"Specific stupid?"

She pointed toward the meadow.

"Leave the packed road because your eyes tell you the fence is closer than the road, you get wet. Follow a landmark instead of the ruts, you get lost. Travel after dark, maybe worse."

"Maybe?"

"I wasn't there."

Good.

"What causes it?"

"Road."

"Very helpful."

"You're welcome."

Beren asked, "Why didn't shrine keeper say this?"

"Which keeper?"

He named someone I did not know.

She made a face.

"His mother's from south."

Apparently magical road knowledge was hereditary through local gossip.

Plausible.

I asked, "Grass strip?"

She looked.

"Don't cross off-road there."

"Why?"

"Because I said so."

"Excellent evidence."

She smiled.

"You can learn without obeying. Once."

Fair.

"Does the road itself cross it safely?"

"Usually."

"Usually today or usually historically?"

That made her pause.

"Good question."

She looked at the ruts.

Then the grass.

"I crossed. Horse didn't care."

Better evidence.

"How long ago?"

"Quarter hour."

Useful.

She drove on.

No name exchanged.

No destiny.

Just somebody who knew a road.

Beren watched her leave.

"My father never mentioned long mile."

"Maybe he didn't like you."

"He didn't."

That took the joke away.

"Sorry."

"Why? He was an ass."

Good.

We approached the grass band on the road.

Slow.

I kept the oval hanging loose inside my open bag where I could see orientation.

Not touching it.

Not obeying it.

At the edge of the band, no click.

We crossed.

The world did not shimmer.

The cart did not vanish.

Hush screamed.

Normal.

Then the oval rotated.

Not back toward the grass band.

Not toward the last stone posts.

It turned ninety degrees toward the meadow.

I stared.

No click.

Beren said, "What?"

"Stop."

He stopped.

The oval held east.

Toward open meadow.

I stepped down.

Did not leave road.

Turned the bag.

Oval remained room-relative.

East.

Not toward any visible door or frame.

No click.

That was new.

Or not new exactly.

It had oriented without crossings before.

Theatre.

Building fire.

Window.

But here there was no obvious threshold nearby.

Unless the grass band counted.

Except it had not clicked.

And it pointed along the band, not back across it.

Maybe.

I checked alignment.

The leaning grass strip ran northeast-southwest.

Oval pointed almost due east.

Not clean.

Good.

Messy evidence was safer.

"What is it?" Beren asked.

"Object I don't understand."

"Why bring it?"

"Because I make good decisions."

He accepted that for some reason.

I watched for a full minute.

Oval slowly drifted.

Not much.

East toward southeast.

Then stopped.

The mill stack, visible beyond meadow, seemed to slide left while we stood still.

I blinked.

Still left.

"Beren."

"Yeah."

"Is the mill moving?"

"No."

"Good."

"Looks like it."

"Less good."

We remained on the road.

After perhaps another minute, the apparent mill position stabilized.

The oval turned back.

Not toward our last threshold.

Toward the road behind us.

Then clicked.

Once.

I did not like that.

"We're continuing," I said.

Beren looked surprised.

"Thought you'd want to poke it."

"I'm becoming boring."

"Shame."

We continued.

The next half mile took long enough that I understood the old woman's name for it.

Not physically long.

Maybe.

My body said we traveled normal distance.

Cart wheels turned.

Horse walked.

Time passed.

But the shrine stones ahead refused to get closer for several minutes.

Then they arrived almost all at once.

Three waist-high stones beside the road.

Each capped with a little iron roof.

Blue thread tied between them.

A man waited there.

Gray robe.

Bare head despite drizzle.

Probably shrine keeper.

He looked annoyed before we spoke.

"You came from Carrow?"

Beren said, "Yes."

"Road men sent word they're closing this section by evening."

"Why?"

"Because people keep leaving the road."

There it was.

Not because the road itself was killing anyone.

Because people responded badly to distorted geometry.

The keeper pointed to fresh white stakes along both shoulders.

"Stay between marks. Moss Ford side open until dusk. Carrow side may close earlier if they get enough cord."

I asked, "Long mile?"

He looked at me.

"Who told you that?"

"Woman with empty flatbed."

"Old Nera."

A name now.

Fine.

"She says it woke before."

He sighed.

"Everyone says something now."

"Do records?"

That got a better look.

"Shrine book has two entries. Maybe three. Last clear one fifty-eight years ago."

There.

"What do they say?"

"Not enough."

"Can I read them?"

"No."

Healthy boundary.

"Summary?"

"Road-length distortion. Landmark disagreement. Do not leave marked path. Stronger after sunset. Settled within six days first entry, eleven second. Third might be same event copied twice."

Excellent.

"Cause?"

"No agreed cause in book."

Even better.

Beren said, "Why didn't you tell us yesterday?"

Keeper looked at him.

"Yesterday I was looking for the book."

Reasonable.

"Road men know now?"

"They have the summary."

Good institution eventually.

I looked at the shrine stones.

Blue thread between them trembled despite almost no wind here.

"Those monitoring?"

"Boundary witness."

"For the road?"

"For shrine ground."

Not everything was about current mystery.

Useful correction.

"Any change?"

"No."

So whatever the long mile was doing, shrine boundary had not visibly shifted according to its local witness system.

Maybe.

I did not know how that system worked.

I asked no more.

Beren delivered his wrapped fitting to a man at the mill who looked exactly enough like him to be his brother and differently enough to explain family arguments.

We ate lunch there.

Bread.

Hard cheese.

Something pickled.

Hush got grain and became quiet for the first time all day.

"Dead?" I asked.

Beren checked.

"Eating."

"Same appearance."

His brother asked why I had come.

Beren said, "He likes wrong things."

Apparently that was becoming my profession.

We returned before dusk.

Road workers had added cord between the white stakes.

Not magical cord as far as I could tell.

Bright enough to see.

A worker at the Moss Ford side told us not to stop inside the marked section unless necessary and absolutely not leave the packed surface.

We followed instructions.

The return felt different.

The shrine stones vanished behind us too quickly.

The drainage ditch took too long to arrive.

The mill stack appeared on the wrong side of my shoulder for a while.

The oval stayed wrapped in my bag.

I did not watch it.

That was deliberate.

If the road was safe enough under marked procedure, I wanted to know what the road felt like without turning the artifact into a compass I did not understand.

We crossed the grass band.

Nothing happened that I could verify.

We passed the old stone posts.

Click.

Normal.

Then the fourth-mile stakes.

Road ordinary again.

Not suddenly.

Just gradually less rude.

By North Gate, I was tired despite riding most of the day.

Watching distance misbehave was exhausting.

Carrow looked offensively stable.

Buildings stayed where architecture had put them.

I appreciated this.

Beren dropped me at home.

"Thanks."

"For what?"

"Coming."

"I solved nothing."

"Didn't ask you to."

That was surprisingly nice.

He handed me a small sack.

I looked inside.

Onions.

I stared at him.

"Cala said you bought good ones."

"This is payment?"

"No."

"Good."

"My brother grows them."

"Still suspicious."

He left.

Cala was delighted by the onions.

Of course.

I had traveled sixteen miles to return with produce.

Life had structure.

AT GRASS BAND CROSSING: NO CLICK / ORIENTED EAST THEN SE / LATER TURNED BACK + CLICK WHILE STATIONARY
