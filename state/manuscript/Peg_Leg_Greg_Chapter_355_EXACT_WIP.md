# CHAPTER 355

## THE BORROWED LAMP

The lamp found me before I found the lamp.

This was convenient enough that I distrusted it immediately.

Two days after we locked three questionable decisions in a cedar chest, Nessa knocked on the front door carrying a wooden case under one arm.

Not a small case.

Long enough that she had to turn sideways through the doorway.

Cala looked at the case.

Then at me.

"No."

"I haven't done anything."

"You are standing near it."

"That standard is impossible."

Nessa said, "Good morning."

Cala pointed at the table.

"Put it there."

Nessa did.

The case landed with the careful weight of something expensive enough to have rules.

I knew that kind of weight.

"What is it?" I asked.

"A lamp."

I looked at her.

She looked at me.

"No," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

This was becoming a problem.

Lyssa came in from the back room carrying folded cloth.

Saw Nessa.

Saw case.

Saw me.

"Absolutely not."

"Again, I have done nothing."

"You're interested."

"That is not an offense."

"It becomes one around equipment."

Nessa opened the case.

Inside lay a narrow stage lamp.

Not one of the big front housings.

Portable.

Brass spine.

Flat round base.

White ceramic light cup protected by three dark ribs.

A small core housing at rear.

Two sliding shutters on either side.

And, stamped into the inside of the case lid, a maker-service plate.

I read it before I meant to.

LANTERN WORKS / HOUSE SERVICE 4

RATED TABLE / WALL / CARRIED LAMP

"Why is that here?"

Nessa pulled a folded note from coat pocket.

"Because Jori wants the house-left aisle lamp checked before tomorrow night, and because I told him you bought something stupid."

I looked at Lyssa.

Lyssa looked innocent.

Too innocent.

"How did you know?"

Nessa said, "Lyssa told Hara."

Lyssa said, "Hara came for the gray cloth."

"And you told her I spent five copper on a bad lantern."

"I told her you spent five copper on something that does not make light."

"That phrasing is hostile."

"It is accurate."

Nessa continued, "Hara told Jori because she thought it was funny. Jori stopped thinking it was funny when she said Three Reeds."

There.

Not coincidence.

Social residue.

People carried versions of me around when I wasn't present.

Apparently they also carried my bad purchases.

"What did Jori say?"

Nessa unfolded note.

"He said, and I quote, 'If the idiot actually has an old Three Reeds shade, do not let him put it on anything until I see the card.'"

"He called me idiot?"

"I may have improved punctuation."

"That was not punctuation."

"He wants the card."

I looked at the stage lamp.

Then the service plate.

Rated table, wall, carried lamp.

The family card upstairs said:

FOR TABLE / WALL / CARRIED SERVICE.

PAIR ONLY WITH RATED LAMP ASSEMBLY.

Close enough to become interesting.

Not close enough to become safe.

"Is this lamp rated for Three Reeds?"

"No idea," Nessa said.

Good.

"Jori?"

"Also no idea. That's why he wants the card and the unit before anyone connects anything."

Better.

"Why bring lamp here?"

"I didn't. I was taking it to service court. I stopped here because your house is on the way and because Rinna wants the aisle lamp back by second bell tomorrow."

That made the world less convenient.

The lamp had not arrived for me.

I had merely become a side errand attached to an existing equipment route.

Excellent.

"Where is service court?"

"Theatre rear yard. Jori has the bench open until midday."

I looked toward the back room.

Cala's key ring hung from her belt.

New friction.

I liked it less when it worked.

"Can I bring the shade unit?"

Nessa shrugged.

"That's the request."

"Test it?"

"That's not the request."

Right.

I looked at Cala.

"Key?"

She studied me.

Not suspicious.

Checking whether I had actually planned.

"Where?"

"Theatre rear service court. Jori. Nessa. Existing rated lamp. No home activation."

"Back when?"

"Before midday if he has time."

Nessa said, "I am leaving in about one minute whether he comes or not."

Cala handed me the key.

"Bring it back."

"The key?"

"All of it."

Fair.

I opened the cedar chest.

The shade unit sat where I had left it.

Inactive.

Upright.

Family card above.

The holdfast remained wrapped.

Rest stone remained boxed.

I took only the shade unit and card.

Closed chest.

Locked it.

Returned key to Cala before leaving.

That was satisfying in a way I refused to examine.

The shade unit went into shoulder bag wrapped loosely, handle out.

Nessa watched.

"Five copper?"

"Yes."

"For that?"

"You work in theatre."

"That is not a defense."

"You own painted walls."

"The theatre owns painted walls."

"Collectively worse."

She considered.

"Fair."

We left.

The rear service court smelled like warm wood, dust, lamp oil that was not actually lamp oil anymore, and somebody's lunch.

Jori had claimed one long bench under the awning.

The stage lamp case sat open at one end by the time we arrived.

Another lamp housing was clamped upright farther down.

Jori wore the same leather-and-metal support at his left wrist. It clicked once when he tightened a small screw.

He looked up.

Looked at me.

Looked at bag handle.

"You actually bought it."

"That sounds judgmental."

"It is diagnostic."

Nessa put the borrowed lamp case beside him.

"House left. Rinna wants it tomorrow."

"I know."

She left without ceremony.

No audience.

No event.

Just work.

Jori held out hand.

"Card first."

I gave him card.

He read slowly.

Not because reading was difficult.

Because equipment language mattered.

He turned it over.

Blank back.

"General family card," he said.

"Yes."

"No model line."

"Yes."

"No rated pair list."

"Water damage maybe."

"Maybe. Or this card never had one."

He handed it back.

"Show me unit. Inactive."

I did.

Set it on clear section of bench.

Jori did not touch rear stud.

He turned unit by base.

Looked underneath.

Three Reeds mark.

Looked at vents.

Used a thin wood strip to check that each opening was physically clear.

Not inside.

Just mouth.

"Core warm now?"

"No."

He put two fingers near rear plate without touching stone.

Then inspected front shutter.

"You moved this?"

"Seller did. Open, half, closed. No visible light."

"Because it isn't a lamp."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Card says shade unit."

"Card says family shade unit. Your object has glass."

"Later combined housings sometimes did."

He looked at me.

There it was.

Too much knowledge.

Not impossible.

Just enough to require source.

"Where'd you hear that?"

"Old equipment stories."

"From who?"

I shrugged.

"People."

He stared another second.

Then let it go.

People was a terrible source.

Fortunately most of civilization used it.

"This theatre lamp is current-rated carried service," he said. "That does not mean rated pair."

"Agreed."

"Connector?"

He turned shade unit sideways.

There was no obvious connector.

I had noticed.

He found something I had not.

Under the rear handle mount, hidden by black cord wrap, was a shallow brass tongue with two slots.

Not electrical.

Mechanical mounting interface.

He did not unwrap cord.

Just moved it enough to expose edge.

Then checked theatre lamp case.

Inside lid, beneath service plate, two spare mounting brackets sat in narrow grooves.

One was a flat U.

One was a bent double hook.

Jori held double hook near Three Reeds tongue.

Did not insert.

The spacing looked close.

Very close.

"Compatible?" I asked.

"Spacing compatible."

Good correction.

"Rated?"

"No."

"Safe?"

"Not established."

"Can we establish it?"

"Maybe."

He looked at card again.

Then lamp plate.

"The problem is pair only with rated lamp assembly. This lamp is rated. Your shade unit is from a family that used carried service. Bracket spacing matches one service bracket. None of that proves the pair."

"What would?"

"Exact model paper. Pair table. Service ledger. Or controlled low-output trial with enough distance and no idiots."

"How many idiots allowed?"

"One."

"Which one?"

"I haven't decided."

He checked time by light through awning.

"I need house-left lamp checked anyway. Low output is part of that check. We can observe your unit beside it first. No mounting. No connection. If nothing strange happens, maybe bracketed position with lamp at minimum. If I don't like it, we stop."

"Cost?"

He looked at me.

"For me doing my scheduled lamp check while your object sits nearby? Nothing. If we start adapting hardware, opening housings, or doing work for you, then money."

Fair.

Very fair.

"And if it damages theatre lamp?"

"Then we don't do the part likely to damage theatre lamp."

I liked Jori.

Not emotionally.

Procedurally.

He moved us to the dark test passage behind the service court.

Not fully dark.

A narrow stone corridor with one shuttered opening at far end.

The theatre used it to check portable lights without blacking out a room.

Useful infrastructure existed because other people had jobs before I arrived.

Jori set the house-left lamp on a marked waist-high stand.

Shade unit stayed on a separate wood block two feet to its left.

No contact.

"Your unit off," he said.

"Yes."

He activated theatre lamp at minimum.

Soft white light filled near corridor.

Not bright.

Enough to see floor joints and far shutter.

Jori slid side shutters until light formed a broad forward wedge.

Spill still washed both walls.

"Normal minimum," he said.

He marked two charcoal lines on wall where obvious edge faded.

Then nodded at my unit.

"Activate. Don't touch shutter after."

I pressed rear stud.

Cloudy stone warmed under plate.

No light came from shade unit.

For two breaths, nothing obvious happened.

Then the left wall changed.

Not dark.

That would have been dramatic.

The wash from theatre lamp narrowed.

The brightest part stayed almost the same.

But the soft useless light beyond left edge thinned.

Jori leaned forward.

I did not move.

The right wall looked nearly unchanged.

Left wall, nearest shade unit face, lost a band of spill perhaps hand-width at first charcoal line.

Not vanished.

Reduced.

Enough that old stone texture became less obvious.

"Off," Jori said.

I pressed stud.

Within a breath or two, spill returned.

Jori made new mark.

"Again."

On.

Same direction.

Same broad reduction.

Not exactly same boundary.

Close.

"Off."

Off.

He touched shade-unit rear plate carefully with back of two fingers.

"Warm."

"Seller demo did that."

"How long was it active?"

"Short."

"Keep it short."

We waited until plate cooled.

Then he rotated shade unit ninety degrees on its own block.

No mounting.

No lamp movement.

"Again."

On.

This time the far end of corridor changed.

Subtly.

The lamp's main forward wedge remained.

But beyond a line angled toward shutter, the diffuse glow around it tightened.

Not enough to hide the lamp.

Not enough to make corridor dark.

Enough to make its light less socially expansive.

That was the phrase my brain produced.

Light with worse manners.

No.

Better manners.

It stayed where told.

Mostly.

Jori said, "Off."

I turned it off.

He looked at unit.

Then card.

Then corridor.

"Well."

That was all.

I smiled.

"Don't."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing victory face."

"It worked."

"It produced a repeatable visible effect twice in one lamp context."

"That is longer for worked."

"Longer is safer."

Tavi would have hated him.

Or loved him.

Possibly both.

"Can we try shutter positions?"

"Not yet."

"Why?"

"Because we already changed orientation. That's one variable enough."

"You changed orientation."

"And now I'm refusing your second variable."

Fine.

"Bracket?"

"Not today."

"You said maybe."

"I saw enough."

"Enough for what?"

"Enough to know your five copper was not entirely stupid."

High praise.

He took the theatre lamp back to service court.

Continued its actual check.

My shade unit sat inactive on wood block cooling.

I watched him inspect lamp shutters, base, core seat, and one loose side screw.

The world had not stopped because my artifact worked.

That was good.

After several minutes he said, without looking up, "If you use that near somebody else's light, ask first."

"Obviously."

He looked at me.

"You bought it before you had a lamp."

"Fair."

"It changes spill. That can change sightlines. Warnings. Signals. Who can see what. Don't treat reduced stray light like harmless darkness."

That was better than a safety lecture because it was true in several directions.

"And heat?"

"Rear plate warmed fast. Maybe normal. Maybe old core. You don't know duration. Short activations until you know more. Vents open. No wrapping active."

"Agreed."

"No mounting to this lamp until pair rating is established or I decide a controlled bracket trial is worth risk."

"Agreed."

He finally looked at me.

"You agree too easily when you get what you wanted."

"I am pleasant in victory."

"You're unbearable in victory."

"Still victory."

He returned the family card.

"Write down what you actually saw."

"I do that."

"I know. That's why I said it."

Interesting.

Another version of me carried around when absent.

I packed shade unit inactive after it cooled.

No fee.

No theatre job.

No promise of future testing.

Jori's work had existed without me.

I had simply arrived with an object that fit one useful question at the edge of his day.

On the way home, I kept thinking about the corridor.

Not because effect was enormous.

It wasn't.

That was why I liked it.

A lamp still lit what it lit.

The shade unit changed the waste around that light.

The announcement.

The glow around corners.

The soft evidence that somebody nearby had decided to see.

At nineteen, with three copper and twenty-six owed, I owned an object that could make light less talkative.

That was useful.

Specific.

And dangerous in ways that were not immediately about explosions.

Better.

At home Cala asked for report before key.

"It works."

She held out hand.

"Longer."

Jori had infected the household.

"In one controlled context, beside a current-rated theatre lamp at minimum output, unmounted and physically separate, it repeatedly reduced visible stray light beyond one side of its face. Rotating the unit changed where the reduction appeared."

Cala gave me the key.

"Better."

Lyssa was at table sewing.

"Five copper?"

"Less stupid."

"How much less?"

"Enough."

"That is not a number."

"Neither is joy."

"You have three copper."

"Joy is economically independent."

She snorted.

I put shade unit back in cedar chest.

Inactive.

Cool.

Upright.

Card above it.

Locked chest.

Returned key.

Then wrote:

THREE REEDS / CONTROLLED THEATRE LAMP CONTEXT

CURRENT-RATED CARRIED LAMP / MIN OUTPUT

NO MOUNT / NO CONTACT

UNIT ON -> REPEATABLE REDUCTION OF STRAY LIGHT BEYOND ONE FACE

MAIN LIT AREA MOSTLY PRESERVED IN OBSERVED TEST

ROTATE UNIT -> REDUCTION MOVES WITH ORIENTATION

NOT DARKNESS

NOT CONCEALMENT

NOT FULL LIGHT REMOVAL

REAR PLATE WARMS QUICKLY

SHORT USE ONLY FOR NOW

NO BRACKET TEST

NO SHUTTER-POSITION TEST

ASK BEFORE USING NEAR OTHER PEOPLE'S LIGHT

Then:

3 C CASH

26 C VALE

I stared at the page.

Five copper.

A real effect.

No new money.

No new job.

No secret fortune.

Just one old object that changed a small rule of the world around me.

I added one final line.

LIGHT CAN BE QUIETER.

That sounded stupid.

I left it.
