# CHAPTER 357

## THE GOOD COAT

Lyssa wanted a coat.

This became my problem because I made the mistake of having an opinion.

"That one," I said.

She looked at the coat.

Then at me.

"No."

"You haven't touched it."

"I can see it."

"That's generally how coats begin."

"It looks like a clerk."

The coat did look like a clerk.

Dark brown wool.

Clean collar.

Straight front.

Four plain buttons.

No decoration except a narrow stitched line at the cuffs.

Respectable.

Warm.

Probably durable.

Deeply uninteresting.

"Clerks are dry," I said.

"Clerks are boring."

"Those properties can coexist."

The seller, an older man with a measuring cord around his neck, said, "That one is six copper."

Lyssa looked at me.

"See?"

"I don't understand what price has to do with clerks."

"Six copper to look boring."

"You were about to spend five on the green one."

"The green one is nice."

The green one was nice.

Dark green with a fitted waist, angled pockets, brass-colored fasteners, and a shoulder line that made Lyssa look like she intended to collect debts personally.

It was also thinner.

The seller had already told us that.

"Lined through body," he said, touching the brown coat. "Half-lined sleeve. Green is fashion weight. Good for dry cold. Not long rain."

Lyssa said, "I don't need long rain."

I looked outside.

A gutter still dripped from yesterday.

"Carrow appears to disagree."

"I have survived rain before."

"Without a coat."

"Exactly."

There was no winning against evidence used badly.

I leaned on both crutches and considered leaving.

I had not come to buy anything.

Important distinction.

This was Lyssa's purchase.

Her money.

Her work.

Her body.

Her coat.

I was there because she had asked if I wanted to walk with her after breakfast, and because apparently the previous day's blue-hour separation had not convinced either of us that walking together was dangerous.

The fog was gone.

Carrow had returned to ordinary gray weather and ordinary people making expensive decisions about cloth.

Lyssa lifted the green coat from hook.

"Try it," I said.

"I was going to."

"Then why are you arguing with me?"

"Because you started helping."

Fair.

The seller had a curtained fitting corner.

Lyssa disappeared behind it.

I stayed outside and became furniture.

The shop was narrow.

Coats along one wall.

Work jackets along other.

Hooks with capes, rain shells, heavy aprons.

A small rack near counter held secondhand gloves.

I looked at none of it.

This required effort.

There was an old leather shoulder guard with a metal hinge near the gloves.

I did not look at that either.

Growth.

"Greg."

I turned.

Lyssa came out wearing green coat.

It looked good.

Very good.

Which was inconvenient because I had been correct about brown.

The green fit her shoulders closely.

Waist pulled slightly when she reached forward.

She raised both arms.

Fabric tightened across back.

Seller saw it too.

"One size up."

"Then it'll be loose here," Lyssa said, touching waist.

"Can take waist."

"How much?"

"One copper alteration."

"No."

Immediate.

Not poor.

Preference.

I liked that.

She moved arms again.

"I need to carry."

Seller nodded.

"Then not this cut."

Also good.

No pressure.

No pretending fashion coat was work coat.

Lyssa took it off.

"Still nice," she said.

"Very."

"You sound happy it's wrong."

"I am never happy when reality agrees with me."

"Liar."

"Constantly delighted."

The brown clerk coat came next.

She put it on.

Looked at mirror.

Made face.

"No."

"Move first."

"I can move while ugly."

"Excellent. Test conditions."

She raised arms.

Reached forward.

Crossed them.

Turned.

Bent.

Pockets stayed accessible.

Shoulders did not bind.

Seller pulled lightly at back hem.

"Enough room for vest under."

Lyssa froze.

Not visibly to seller.

To me, yes.

Vest.

Her load vest.

The unresolved one.

Not my project.

I said nothing.

She looked at mirror again.

Brown coat still looked like a clerk.

A competent clerk now.

Possibly one who could lift boxes.

"How rainproof?" she asked.

"Not proof. Treated wool. Sheds ordinary rain. Soaks in long rain. Dry before storing."

"Repair?"

"Any cloth shop can do seams. Treatment can be renewed."

"Buttons?"

"Standard."

"Six?"

"Six."

She took it off.

"Five."

Seller shook head.

"Six."

"Five and I don't make you pretend the cuff stitching is decorative."

The seller looked offended.

"It is decorative."

"It is reinforcing the fold."

"It can be two things."

I liked him.

Lyssa said, "Five and one bit."

"Six."

"You haven't moved."

"Neither have you."

She looked at me.

I looked at ceiling.

Not my negotiation.

This was harder than artifact appraisal.

At least artifacts did not ask me whether brown made them look like clerks.

Lyssa put brown coat back on.

Looked at mirror.

Then green coat.

Then brown.

"I hate this one."

Seller said, "Then don't buy it."

Perfect.

She laughed.

Actually laughed.

"You are terrible at selling."

"I sell coats. I don't sell liking coats."

She took brown off again.

"Do you have something between these?"

"Between useful and pretty?"

"Yes."

"Expensive."

"Of course."

He went to back.

Returned with charcoal coat.

Longer than green.

Shorter than brown.

Soft black-gray wool.

Simple front.

Slight shape at waist without pulling tight.

Deep angled pockets.

Dark horn buttons.

No bright metal.

The collar could fold flat or button high.

Lyssa touched it.

Her face changed.

There.

Want.

I knew that face now.

Not because she made it often.

Because I had recently made my own version over a bad lantern.

"How much?" she asked.

"Nine."

She let go.

Immediate.

Interesting.

Seller did not chase.

"Why nine?" I asked.

Lyssa looked at me sharply.

I held up hand.

"Information. Not buying."

Seller answered anyway.

"Denser wool. Better shoulder cut. Double pocket facing. High collar. Treatment included."

"Alteration?"

"Minor waist or sleeve, included if done now."

Lyssa said, "No."

Seller nodded.

Hung it back.

And that should have been end.

We left.

We made it half a street.

Lyssa was quiet.

I did not ask.

This was deliberate.

One street.

Two.

At third, she said, "Nine is stupid."

"For a coat?"

"For me."

Different sentence.

I looked at her.

She kept walking.

"Why?"

"Because I don't need it."

"You need a coat."

"I need something dry. I don't need nine copper."

"Nine copper is not an object."

"You know what I mean."

I did.

We walked another few steps.

Her hands were empty.

Mine had crutches.

The street smelled like wet stone and bread.

Someone was arguing over fish farther down.

Normal day.

"You wanted it," I said.

"So?"

"Nothing."

She looked at me.

"You spent five on something you couldn't even use."

"Correct."

"And now you're going to tell me to spend nine on a coat."

"No."

"You were about to."

"I was about to ask whether nine changes anything important."

She frowned.

"What?"

"If you spend nine, what can't you do?"

"Have nine copper."

"Excellent financial analysis."

"Greg."

"Do you have something else you need the money for?"

She was quiet.

That was not my invitation to inventory her finances.

I waited.

"Maybe," she said.

"Then that's real."

"Maybe isn't real."

"It is if you're keeping room for it."

She looked at me again.

"You sound like Mara."

"Cruel."

"You like Mara."

"Procedurally."

"That isn't a way to like people."

"It is one of many."

We stopped near a bakery awning because rain began lightly.

Not blue fog.

Just rain.

Lyssa stood under awning without coat.

This improved argument materially.

"I have twelve," she said.

I did not react.

Not because twelve was huge.

Because she had chosen to tell me.

"Okay."

"Twelve copper. Some bits."

"Okay."

"Nine leaves three."

There it was.

My number.

I almost laughed.

Did not.

"Three is survivable," I said.

"For you."

"For me now. Maybe not for you."

"Exactly."

Good.

Her money had different obligations.

Different uncertainty.

Different work.

Different appetite for being stupid.

"What are you keeping room for?" I asked.

She leaned against wall.

"Materials. Maybe. If Marra's thing turns into more work. Or if I need to replace the good shears. Or if the vest needs something. Or if Cala needs house money. Or if nothing happens and I just want money."

All valid.

"Then nine is expensive."

"Yes."

"But not because you're not worth nine."

She looked at me.

I regretted sentence immediately.

Not because wrong.

Because it sounded like I had prepared it.

I had not.

"Don't," she said.

"I know."

"Don't make it a thing."

"Already stopped."

She stared at rain.

Then said, "The brown one is ugly."

"Deeply."

"Six is also a lot."

"Less than nine."

"Thank you."

"I contribute."

Rain strengthened.

We remained under awning.

A woman ran past with a basket over head.

Someone across street shouted that fish were cheaper because weather was ruining everybody's mood.

Lyssa said, "What would you do?"

Dangerous.

"With your money?"

"Yes."

"Nothing."

"Useless."

"I mean I wouldn't decide for you."

"I asked."

"Then I'd buy neither today."

She turned.

"Why?"

"Because you hate the six-copper coat and the nine-copper coat makes you nervous enough that you're trying to turn wanting it into a moral failure."

Her face went still.

I continued because survival instincts had left.

"So leave. If you still want charcoal after a few days, then you're choosing the coat, not escaping the shop. If it's gone, it's gone."

"That's easy for you to say after the load lock."

There.

She knew that story too.

Of course she did.

"Yes," I said.

"You hated losing it."

"Yes."

"And you still think walking was right."

"Yes."

She looked back at rain.

"Annoying."

"I have developed principles through suffering."

"You bought a bad lantern two days later."

"And it works."

"Partly."

"Tavi says that distinction matters."

She laughed again.

Rain eased.

We walked home without a coat.

This should not have felt like an outcome.

It did.

At home Cala looked at Lyssa's empty hands.

"Nothing?"

"Nothing."

"Didn't like them?"

Lyssa took off damp outer wrap.

"Liked one too much."

Cala nodded as if this were a complete category of problem.

Maybe it was.

"Price?"

"Nine."

Cala winced.

"Good coat?"

"Very."

"Then maybe."

Lyssa looked betrayed.

"You too?"

"I said maybe."

Tavi appeared.

"What color?"

"Charcoal."

"That's not a color."

"It is."

"That's burned wood."

"Also a color."

He considered this suspiciously.

I went upstairs.

Did not open cedar chest.

Did not look at artifacts.

I sat and took out scrap.

Almost wrote:

LYSSA / COAT / 9 C

Then stopped.

Absolutely not.

Her money was not my ledger.

I crossed out the blank line before it existed.

Instead I wrote my own:

3 C CASH

26 C VALE

Then underneath:

WANTING SOMETHING DOES NOT REQUIRE BUYING IT TODAY.

I stared at that.

It sounded annoyingly mature.

I added:

UNLESS IT IS VERY GOOD.

Better.

Downstairs Lyssa called, "Greg."

"What?"

"If I go back tomorrow, don't say anything."

I smiled.

"About the coat?"

"About anything."

"Impossible."

"Try."

"For how long?"

"The whole shop."

"That's unreasonable."

"Then don't come."

I considered.

"Can I make faces?"

A pause.

"No."

Cruel.

"Then I won't come."

"Good."

Another pause.

"Maybe come."

There it was.

Not a decision.

Not yet.

Just room for one.
