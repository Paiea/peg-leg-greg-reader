# CHAPTER 358

## THE HANGING ROAD

The bridge lifted while I was on it.

Not collapsed.

That would have been simpler.

It lifted.

I had one crutch planted, one moving, right foot between them, and a basket of black-root under my left arm when the stone under me rose three inches and the entire bridge made a sound like somebody dragging a chair across the sky.

Everyone stopped.

Good instinct.

Then the bridge rose another inch.

Worse.

"Nobody move!"

That came from ahead.

A woman in a yellow work coat stood near the middle span with both arms out.

Not dramatic.

Not frightened.

Angry in the specific way people became when infrastructure misbehaved during their shift.

I liked her immediately.

The Hanging Road crossed the south cut above a freight canal.

Not actually hanging.

Usually.

It was an old city bridge made from six broad stone spans laid over iron ribs, with pale tension plates set beneath the rail posts every twenty feet.

The plates compensated for load changes.

Broad category familiar.

Local design not.

I had crossed it plenty of times without caring.

This was what infrastructure wanted.

Nobody thinking about it.

Today it had become needy.

The stone beneath my crutch shifted sideways a finger-width.

I froze harder.

A man behind me said, "Is it breaking?"

The yellow-coat worker shouted, "No!"

Then, after a beat:

"Probably not!"

Less comforting.

Someone laughed once.

Wrong laugh.

Stress laugh.

The bridge gave another long scrape.

Below us, canal water looked much farther away than it had one minute earlier.

Because we were rising.

Not quickly.

Maybe another foot total now.

The west end remained connected to street.

The east end did too.

But the middle spans were bowing upward.

A stone bridge turning into a shallow hill while people stood on it.

That was new enough to earn my attention.

I did not analyze the plates.

This was because the woman ahead was already doing that and because dying from professional curiosity remained embarrassing.

"Stay where your feet are!" she called. "Do not run downhill. Do not bunch up. Rail only if you're already touching it."

Important.

I was not touching rail.

I stayed not touching rail.

My basket slipped slightly under arm.

Black-root rolled against wicker.

Ridiculous thing to notice.

I noticed it anyway.

There were maybe twenty people on bridge.

Not crowded.

Enough.

Two handcarts.

One small delivery mule attached to a narrow cart near west end.

The mule did not understand structural restraint.

It stepped backward.

Cart wheel rolled.

The bridge answered.

One pale tension plate under nearest rail flashed white.

The worker swore.

"Hold the mule!"

Three people near it did.

Not by grabbing animal randomly.

One took bridle.

One blocked wheel with boot.

One set cart brake.

Distributed competence.

Good.

I looked down at my own position.

Right foot flat.

Left leg absent below knee as usual.

Both crutch tips on separate stones.

My stance occupied more width than most people.

Normally useful.

Now I had three load points instead of two.

Could matter.

Maybe not.

Do not invent problem.

The yellow-coat worker moved one careful step.

Bridge twitched.

She stopped.

Looked down at tension plate beside her.

"Nobody copies me," she said.

Excellent instruction.

A second worker appeared at east end.

Orange vest.

Carrying a long forked pole with a blue ceramic jaw.

He did not step onto bridge.

He set pole against a square socket in first rail post.

Twisted.

Nothing happened.

"West lock?" he shouted.

A voice from behind me answered, "Not seated!"

"Seat it!"

"Can't under live lift!"

"Then don't!"

Again.

Competence often sounded like people refusing to do the thing they were just told to do.

The bridge rose another two inches.

Now the slope was obvious.

A woman farther ahead began sliding.

Not falling.

Her leather soles lost purchase on wet stone.

She dropped to one knee.

The person beside her caught sleeve.

The yellow-coat worker said, "Stay down. Don't stand until I tell you."

The woman stayed.

Good.

I adjusted nothing.

My right thigh had begun to burn from holding still.

Shoulders too.

Crutches were designed for movement.

Stillness on a slope used them differently.

One tip squeaked against stone.

I hated that sound.

"You with crutches," the worker called.

I looked up.

"Yes?"

"Can you hold there?"

"For now."

"If not, say before you move."

"Yes."

That was all.

No special accommodation speech.

Just useful information.

A child near east side started crying.

His father crouched but did not move feet.

"It's okay."

The child said, "It's not."

Fair.

The bridge made another scraping groan.

Then stopped rising.

Everyone waited.

Nothing.

No one cheered.

Smart crowd.

The orange-vest worker at east end shouted, "Holding!"

Yellow coat shouted back, "Temporary or caught?"

"Don't know!"

"Wonderful!"

I liked her more.

She looked at us.

"We are going to unload people in order. East side first. One person at a time from the end. Nobody passes anybody. Handcarts stay until people are off unless I say otherwise."

A man behind me said, "Why east first?"

She looked at him.

"Because that's the end I'm looking at."

He shut up.

Maybe there was a technical reason too.

Maybe not.

Sometimes a controlled plan was better than the perfect plan nobody had yet.

First person stepped off east end.

Bridge dropped half an inch.

Everyone felt it.

The crying child screamed.

Yellow coat raised hand.

"Stop."

Nobody else moved.

The bridge settled.

"Again."

Second person off.

Tiny drop.

Settle.

Third.

The process continued.

Slow.

Boring.

Wonderful.

The hill under us reduced by fractions.

Each removed body changed load enough that tension plates pulsed faintly.

Not synchronized.

Different plates responded differently.

I did not study pattern.

Mostly.

By the time east half cleared, I had become near front of remaining group without moving.

Yellow coat came closer.

Careful step.

Pause.

Careful step.

She stopped two body lengths away.

"Crutches."

"Greg."

"Greg. When I tell you, move both crutches first or foot first?"

Good question.

"Depends slope."

"This slope."

I looked.

Down toward east now.

Moderate.

Wet.

"One crutch, other crutch, right foot. Short."

"Can you stop mid-cycle?"

"Yes, but worse."

"Then complete one cycle at a time. Don't shorten after you start."

"Understood."

She pointed to stone joint three feet ahead.

"To that line. Stop."

Specific.

Good.

"Ready?"

"Yes."

"Go."

Left crutch.

Right crutch.

Right foot.

Bridge shifted slightly under third contact.

I completed cycle.

Stopped at line.

Basket pressed against ribs.

Black-root remained deeply unhelpful.

"Hold."

Held.

Plate near us dimmed.

"Again. Same length."

I moved.

Not fast.

Not elegant.

The bridge seemed more dangerous when I was allowed to move because now every sound became possibly mine.

Crutch tip.

Stone grit.

Leather.

My own breathing.

The worker did not touch me.

Did not grab crutch.

Did not try to help by changing rhythm.

Excellent.

At next line she said, "Basket?"

"Light."

"Keep it."

I appreciated not surrendering vegetables during civic crisis.

Three more cycles.

Then bridge dipped unexpectedly under right foot.

Not much.

Enough.

My balance went backward.

I drove both crutches wider.

Stopped.

Basket fell.

Black-root scattered.

Of course.

One root rolled downhill past yellow coat.

She watched it go.

Then looked at me.

"You stable?"

"Yes."

"Leave the roots."

"I was going to."

"You looked at them."

"They were expensive emotionally."

"Leave them."

"Yes."

We resumed.

At east end, stone transition between lifted bridge and street had become a four-inch step down.

Normally nothing.

With crutches on unstable span, more interesting.

Orange vest put a broad wooden wedge against edge.

Not for bridge.

For me.

"Can use this?"

I looked at wedge.

Gentler transition.

Stable on street side.

"Yes."

He planted boot behind it.

Yellow coat said, "One cycle through. Don't stop on seam."

I moved.

Crutch.

Crutch.

Foot.

Street.

The bridge behind me rose one inch.

I was off.

My body knew before mind.

Shoulders released.

Right leg shook once.

I leaned harder on crutches.

Orange vest said, "Move five steps clear."

I did.

Then turned.

People still remained.

No triumph.

No chapter-ending applause because Greg had successfully crossed a bridge he had not broken.

I stood clear and watched.

Yellow coat sent next person.

Then next.

Eventually only workers, handcarts, mule cart remained.

The handcarts came off one at a time with wheel chocks and ropes from street.

Mule cart last.

The animal had become suspicious of civilization.

Correctly.

Once people were clear, workers closed both ends with red boards.

Not yellow rope.

Red board.

Different severity.

A city clerk arrived carrying writing case.

Another worker brought two squat iron braces on a shoulder yoke.

A woman in gray inspected canal side from below.

Suddenly there were specialists everywhere.

Because this was their problem.

I sat on a low wall.

My black-root remained on bridge.

Three roots visible.

One had rolled farther.

Maybe four total.

I could wait.

The yellow-coat woman eventually came off.

She looked tired now.

Not dramatic.

Work tired.

She saw me.

"You stayed."

"My vegetables are hostages."

She looked at bridge.

"We'll get them if bridge comes down."

"If?"

"When. Probably."

"You use probably professionally."

"Frequently."

She sat two arm lengths away.

Took off one glove.

Her hand had a pale pressure mark across palm.

"What happened?" I asked.

She looked at me.

I corrected.

"What are you allowed to say happened?"

That earned half a smile.

"Center equalizer overcorrected. Or one side underreleased. Or one sensor lied. Those are guesses."

"So bridge lifted itself because it thought load changed?"

"Maybe."

"Broadly."

"Broadly, bridge is supposed to stay boring. Today it failed."

Good enough.

"Danger of falling?"

"Always."

"Helpful."

"Current danger? We don't know yet. Main ribs held. Stone joints shifted within more than normal travel but not enough that I saw a break. That's not inspection result. That's me telling you why you're alive."

"Appreciated."

A clerk approached.

"Name?"

I gave Greg.

"Injury?"

"No."

"Property lost?"

I looked at bridge.

"Black-root."

Clerk looked up.

"How much?"

"Four roots."

"Value?"

I had not counted exact bit price because civilization had progressed.

Now civilization wanted receipt.

"Ordinary market. Under a bit each."

"Claim under one copper total?"

"Much under."

"If unrecovered, household loss claim at east desk tomorrow."

"I am not filing a claim for four roots."

"Then no property claim."

He wrote.

Simple.

No encouragement either way.

"Were you instructed before moving?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

I pointed yellow coat.

"Name?"

She said, "Sella."

There.

Name.

Not full identity.

Clerk wrote.

"Did you observe anyone running, passing, or moving a cart after stop order?"

"Mule stepped backward before cart was held."

"Anything else?"

"No."

"Thank you."

He moved on.

Sella said, "You didn't mention bridge talking."

"Bridge didn't talk."

"Good."

"It screamed structurally."

"Don't tell clerk that."

"I didn't."

We waited.

After maybe twenty minutes, workers installed both iron braces under side rail sockets.

A technician on canal bank called numbers I did not understand.

Someone at west end turned a locking wheel one notch.

The bridge lowered.

Slowly.

Stone hill relaxed toward flat.

Not all the way.

Stopped with center maybe six inches high.

Then another adjustment.

Lowered again.

This time nearly level.

No one reopened it.

Good.

A worker wearing rope harness crossed alone from west end while clipped to an overhead temporary line strung between posts.

He collected debris.

Including my roots.

One had cracked.

Three survived.

He brought basket off.

Sella handed it to me.

"One casualty."

I looked at split black-root.

"Died in service."

"Go home."

"You don't know where I live."

"Then go somewhere else."

I stood.

My right leg had stiffened while sitting.

Shoulders still tired.

Nothing injured.

I shifted basket under arm.

Three whole roots.

One cracked.

Probably edible.

Maybe more urgently edible now.

At home Cala looked at basket.

"Why is that broken?"

"Bridge tried to become a hill."

She stopped cutting cabbage.

"What?"

Tavi appeared instantly.

Of course.

"Which bridge?"

"Hanging Road."

"It hangs?"

"Apparently more than advertised."

Lyssa came in from back room.

No charcoal coat.

I did not look at that fact long enough to make it a question.

She looked at me.

"You were gone a while."

"Bridge lifted."

"Which bridge?"

"Hanging Road."

"You were on it?"

"Yes."

That changed her face.

Small.

Real.

"You okay?"

"Yes."

"Fall?"

"No."

"Anybody?"

"Not while I was there. One woman went to knee. No injury I saw."

She nodded.

Then:

"Marked door?"

I stared at her.

She smiled.

Not helpful.

"Wrong emergency."

"Still worked yesterday."

"Bridge had no door."

"Poor planning."

Tavi said, "Did it go all the way up?"

"No."

"Could boats go under it?"

"Boats could already go under it."

"Could a bigger boat go under when it goes up?"

"That is not why it goes up."

"Possibly."

"Then maybe it's for boats."

"The workers will be relieved you solved it."

"I didn't solve it. I said maybe."

Fair.

Cala took broken black-root.

Cut around crack.

"Still good."

There.

Infrastructure incident reduced to dinner.

As most things should eventually be.

Not bridge doctrine.

IF INFRASTRUCTURE STARTS MOVING, LET PEOPLE WHO MAINTAIN IT OWN THE PROBLEM.

Knowing and doing were separate skills.

No magic notes.

No money gained.

One root cracked.

I could live with that.

Still no coat.

"You really didn't ask."

"Ask what?"

She looked at me.

I smiled.

"Growth."

"Don't ruin it."

"Stopping."

The bridge had lifted while I was on it.

The city had gotten me off.

No one had paid me.
