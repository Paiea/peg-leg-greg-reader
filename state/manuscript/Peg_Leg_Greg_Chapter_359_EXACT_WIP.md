# CHAPTER 359

## THE REFUSAL

The first real offer I got from South Quay was worse than no offer.

Not morally.

That would have been easier.

It was good work.

That was the problem.

The message came after breakfast on a folded blue slip with my name written correctly.

Already suspicious.

Cala handed it to me.

"Boy left it."

"Which boy?"

"A boy."

"Useful."

"He had hair."

"Narrowing rapidly."

She went back to cutting onions.

I opened slip.

SOUTH QUAY RETURN WALL

TEMP COUNT / THREE DAYS

SECOND BELL TO LATE CART

3 C PER DAY + MEAL

REPORT BLUE SHED / MERREN

ANSWER BY MIDDAY

Nine copper.

Three days.

Meal each day.

Not guaranteed beyond that.

I read it again.

Lyssa was at table repairing a seam on one of Cala's work wraps.

She looked up.

"Work?"

"Three days."

"Good?"

"Nine copper."

Her eyebrows moved.

Not dramatically.

Enough.

"That's good."

"Yes."

I hated it immediately.

Not the work.

The timing.

There was a second note at bottom.

THIRD DAY MAY RUN PAST DARK IF WEST RETURNS ARRIVE LATE.

I had no scheduled theatre work.

No Hessa appointment written anywhere.

No immediate Vale payment.

No reason not to take it.

Which meant I needed to find one.

This was a healthy instinct.

"You're making the face," Lyssa said.

"What face?"

"The one where something is good and you start looking for why it isn't."

"I don't have that face."

Cala said, "You do."

Betrayal everywhere.

I sat.

Nine copper would take me from three to twelve.

Twelve did not buy the load lock because that was gone.

Good.

Stop thinking about load lock.

Twelve did not buy the South Quay harness either.

Also gone.

Stop shopping with hypothetical future money.

Twelve did something better.

Reserve.

It put me back above the five I had spent on Three Reeds.

It made two-copper specialist work feel different.

It made one-copper mistakes survivable.

It made Vale cash payment possible without becoming mandatory.

It made wanting another object less stupid.

Nine copper was not wealth.

It was room.

"Take it," Cala said.

I looked at her.

"You didn't even ask what it is."

"Counting boxes."

"How do you know?"

"You counted boxes last time."

"Returns."

"Boxes returned."

Close enough.

Lyssa said, "Why wouldn't you?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then that's not a reason."

Also betrayal.

I folded slip.

"I need to answer by midday."

"Then answer by midday," Cala said.

"I appreciate the procedural clarity."

"Eat first."

That was the end of family financial council.

I ate.

Then left house without answering.

Not to investigate South Quay.

Not to inspect anything.

I needed to walk.

Thinking while stationary made money feel like arithmetic.

Thinking while moving made it feel like time.

Three days.

Second bell to late cart.

Three days of leaving before midmorning and returning near dark.

Fine.

I had done worse.

First life had included months where sunrise and sunset were things other people experienced.

Second life had included theatre nights, yard work, Hessa, hauling, travel, random disasters, and one bridge trying to become a hill under me.

Three days was nothing.

So why did I dislike it?

I reached East Steps before answer arrived.

Not mystical answer.

A person.

Senna was sitting on low wall drawing a dog.

The dog was not cooperating.

It kept turning around to look at her.

"Stop being aware of art," she told it.

Dog wagged.

"Hopeless," I said.

She looked up.

"You're worse."

"I hold poses beautifully."

"You complain with your shoulders."

"That's anatomical slander."

She moved charcoal.

"Sit if you're staying."

"I'm not staying."

I stayed.

Not because Senna had plot relevance.

Because I knew her.

This was increasingly how city worked.

She drew dog.

I watched people pass.

A porter with two baskets.

A woman carrying a rolled rug.

Three children arguing whether rainwater counted as clean if it had not touched ground yet.

Reasonable question.

A man selling hot bean cakes from brass pan.

I bought nothing.

Three copper remained three copper.

Senna said, "You look employed."

"That's insulting."

"You have paper."

I had forgotten slip in hand.

"Three-day count work."

"Good?"

"Nine copper total."

She stopped drawing.

"That's good."

Everyone had same line.

"Yes."

"You don't want it?"

"I didn't say that."

"You made the face."

Unacceptable.

"What face?"

"The one where you want something and are angry at it."

Different diagnosis.

Closer.

"I want the money."

"Then take it."

"Apparently this city has one financial adviser."

She went back to dog.

"Maybe you don't want three days."

There.

Simple.

I looked at slip.

"Three days isn't much."

"Then take it."

"You are not helping."

"I am drawing a dog."

Correct.

The dog sneezed.

Senna erased its nose.

"I have other things," I said.

"Scheduled?"

"No."

"Promised?"

"No."

"Then you have imaginary things."

Rude.

"Potential things."

"Same."

I almost argued.

Then stopped.

Because she was wrong in an interesting way.

Potential things mattered.

That was reserve.

Not only money.

Time had reserve too.

If I filled three consecutive days because offer was good, I lost ability to say yes to anything else during those days.

But that alone was not reason to refuse.

Opportunity cost did not mean never commit.

It meant compare.

Compare to what?

Nothing scheduled.

Nothing promised.

Nothing urgent.

So take it.

I hated when logic ended early.

Senna said, "You can leave now."

"I was never staying."

"You have been here ten minutes."

"Temporary residence."

I stood.

Dog immediately put head in Senna's lap.

"Traitor," she told it.

I walked toward South Quay.

Not because I needed to see job.

Because answer went there.

At North Grain crossing, another person found me.

This time Saret.

He was carrying two short iron tags threaded on cord and looked annoyed enough that they were probably work.

"Greg."

"Saret."

"You get Merren's slip?"

"Yes."

"You taking it?"

"Probably."

"Good."

He started past.

Then stopped.

"Actually."

Dangerous word.

"What?"

"You free tomorrow morning?"

There it was.

Of course.

The universe waited until I had nearly made decision.

"Maybe."

"North Secondary. Not gate work."

"Then what?"

"Tag walk."

"Explain."

He shifted iron tags.

"After closure work they need independent physical read against replacement list before day route stays open. Two people. I have one. Need another who reads tags and doesn't fix numbers to be helpful."

That reputation again.

Boring correctly.

"Pay?"

"Four copper. Morning only if clean. Could run midday if not."

Four copper.

One morning.

South Quay day one was three copper plus meal.

This was more money for less time.

Obvious.

Except South Quay offered three days.

Nine total.

North Secondary offered four once.

No guarantee after.

"When do you need answer?"

"Now."

Of course.

"Why me?"

"Because I gave Merren your name and now she stole you."

"That seems like your fault."

"Yes."

He said it without shame.

"What exactly am I reading?"

"Replacement tags. Marker, location, stamped line, physical part present. You call mismatch. I call list. Neither of us signs repair."

Bounded.

Good.

"Any climbing?"

"No. Ground route. Some stairs."

"Any closed-route entry?"

"Marked work lane. Escort."

"Four copper cash?"

"Yes."

"Morning only if clean?"

"Yes."

"If not clean?"

"Same four unless supervisor expands scope. If expands, you can refuse or agree new rate."

Also good.

I looked at South Quay slip.

Three days.

Nine copper.

Reliable enough to plan.

North Secondary.

Four copper.

One morning.

Less total.

More per time.

More uncertainty after.

And it touched older thread without making me investigator.

Not that I thought in chapters.

Usually.

"Can I do North tomorrow and start South Quay day after?"

Saret shrugged.

"Ask Merren."

Right.

Adults negotiated schedules.

I had somehow forgotten this.

South Quay was not a divine command written on blue paper.

"I'm going there now."

"Tell her I stole you back."

"You are both using me incorrectly."

"Probably."

He left.

I continued to South Quay.

Now choice had shape.

Take guaranteed three days and decline North.

Take North and lose South if Merren needed exact start.

Ask.

Possibly get both.

This felt almost disappointingly functional.

Merren was at blue shed when I arrived.

She had three slips in one hand and a pencil behind ear.

"You got it."

"Yes."

"Taking it?"

"I have a conflict tomorrow morning."

She looked at me.

Not angry.

Not pleased.

Just waiting.

"Saret offered four copper for North Secondary tag walk. Morning only if clean."

"He did?"

"He says you stole me."

Merren closed eyes briefly.

"I gave him your name."

"He mentioned."

"Idiot."

"Which one?"

"Yes."

Good.

She looked at blue slips.

"I need three consecutive days because west returns are stacking. Tomorrow through third day."

There.

No both.

"Can't start day after?"

"I can put someone else on tomorrow, but then I need them all three days. Training the count rhythm twice costs me more than keeping one person."

Rational.

"So if I take North, I lose this."

"Yes."

"No offense?"

"Why would I be offended?"

"People enjoy being offended by scheduling."

"I enjoy having counters."

Fair.

"When do you need answer?"

"Now is good. Midday latest."

I looked around.

Return wall active.

Carts.

Blue shed.

Workers who already knew jobs.

Three days here would be useful.

Not exciting.

Good.

Nine copper.

Meal.

Potential future temp reliability.

North Secondary was four.

One morning.

A better rate.

More free time after.

Also older route residue I had some reason to care about.

But careful.

Interest was not compensation.

I did not owe North Secondary curiosity.

I owed myself decision.

"If I take this," I asked, "am I more likely to get future temp count work?"

Merren considered.

"If you do it well, yes."

"How much more likely?"

"I don't know."

Good answer.

"If I refuse?"

"I call next name."

"Does refusing hurt list?"

"No. Not unless you keep saying yes and then don't show."

Important.

"So declining cleanly is neutral."

"Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"If I need somebody ten times and you're never available, eventually I stop asking first. That's not punishment. That's scheduling."

Excellent.

Real consequence without morality.

"North Secondary?" she asked.

"Four copper. Morning."

"Take it."

I blinked.

"You are telling me to refuse your job?"

"I'm telling you four for a morning is better than three for a day if you don't need my three-day certainty."

"You need a counter."

"I have other names."

Again.

The world did not collapse if Greg said no.

This remained emotionally suspicious.

"Why offer me first?"

"You were good last time."

That landed harder than it should have.

Not praise.

Assessment.

Useful.

"And if I say no?"

"Then you were still good last time."

There.

I folded slip.

"I'm saying no."

"Fine."

Merren took slip back.

Crossed my name.

Wrote another.

That was it.

Nine copper disappeared without drama.

Not lost.

Declined.

Different.

My stomach still disliked distinction.

"Tomorrow," she said, "if Saret's thing falls through, don't come here expecting slot back."

"I wouldn't."

"Good."

"You think I should take North?"

"I think you already did."

She was right.

I had.

I left South Quay with same three copper I arrived with.

No job there.

No resentment.

No guarantee.

And one four-copper morning accepted verbally with Saret, assuming I could still find him.

I could.

He was where people with iron tags eventually ended up: near North Secondary office.

"I told Merren no," I said.

"Good."

"You owe me four copper tomorrow."

"Supervisor owes you."

"Less personal."

"Second bell. North office. Eat first."

"Ground route?"

"Ground route."

"Marked work lane?"

"Yes."

"If scope expands?"

"We stop and ask."

Good.

"Accepted."

He nodded.

Then handed me nothing.

No token.

No dramatic contract.

"My name is on list?"

"Now it is."

"You just wrote it?"

"Before you got here."

"What if I said no?"

"I'd cross it out."

Everyone was disturbingly comfortable with refusal today.

I went home.

Lyssa was there.

Still no charcoal coat.

I did not mention it.

She saw my face.

"You took the three days."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Got offered four copper for one morning instead."

She stopped sewing.

"You gave up nine for four?"

"Yes."

"That sounds stupid when you say it like that."

"I know."

Cala looked over from stove.

"Why?"

I explained.

Three days versus one morning.

Nine total versus four.

South Quay future probability versus North route one-off.

No punishment for clean refusal.

No guarantee after either.

Lyssa listened.

Then said, "So you bought time."

I looked at her.

"With five copper I never had."

"Basically."

That was annoyingly good.

"Maybe."

Cala said, "Four copper is still four copper."

"Tomorrow."

"If you show up."

"I intend to."

"Then eat breakfast."

Household economics remained brutally practical.

Upstairs I wrote:

SOUTH QUAY OFFER

3 DAYS / 3 C EACH + MEAL

DECLINED CLEANLY

NO LIST PENALTY STATED

Then:

NORTH SECONDARY

TAG WALK / TOMORROW / SECOND BELL

4 C / MORNING IF CLEAN

GROUND ROUTE / MARKED WORK LANE

SCOPE EXPANSION REQUIRES NEW AGREEMENT

Then my money:

3 C CASH

26 C VALE

Potential tomorrow:

+4 C

I stared at plus sign.

Crossed it out.

Not money yet.

Good.

I wrote instead:

TIME IS ALSO RESERVE.

Then immediately disliked it.

Too neat.

Too wise.

I added:

BUT RENT DOES NOT ACCEPT AFTERNOONS.

Better.

Downstairs Lyssa called, "Greg."

"What?"

"I might go back to coat shop after lunch."

I froze.

"Okay."

"That's all?"

"You told me not to say anything."

"I said in the shop."

"I'm practicing early."

Silence.

Then:

"Do you want to come?"

Danger.

I looked at notes.

Tomorrow had work.

Today did not.

Time was reserve.

Reserve could be spent.

"Yes," I said.

"Don't make faces."

"No promises."

"Then stay home."

"I can control some faces."

"Which ones?"

"None reliably."

"Greg."

"Coming."

I put paper away.

Three copper.

Four maybe tomorrow.

Nine refused.

An afternoon still mine.

Not every good offer had to become my life just because it was good.
