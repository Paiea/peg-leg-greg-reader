# CHAPTER 371

## THE WITNESS

The dead man was arguing with a fishmonger.

Loudly.

This would have been more concerning if he were actually dead.

He was painted.

Mostly.

His face occupied a whitewashed wall across from East Fish Row, twelve feet tall and deeply offended by the price of silverfin.

The fishmonger painted beside him was equally offended by existence.

I stopped in middle of street.

Someone behind me said, "Move."

Reasonable.

I moved.

Then stopped again under awning.

The wall had not been like that yesterday.

I was almost certain.

Carrow changed quickly, but twelve-foot dead merchants usually left some procedural residue.

A woman beside me carrying two baskets said, "That's Pell."

"Which one?"

"Dead one."

Useful.

"How long?"

"Dead?"

"Painted."

"Morning."

She kept walking.

No explanation.

Carrow had standards.

I looked again.

The painted man had one hand raised with two fingers extended.

The painted fishmonger held a silverfin by tail.

Between them, in narrow black letters:

TWO BITS IS THEFT IF FISH STILL HAS EYES

Below that, in different hand:

PELL SAID THIS EVERY MARKET DAY FOR NINETEEN YEARS

Then:

HE WAS WRONG FOR EIGHTEEN OF THEM

People were laughing.

Not crowd exactly.

Current.

They slowed.

Read.

Pointed.

Moved on.

An old man touched wall with two fingers and kept walking.

A girl maybe ten asked mother, "Who was Pell?"

Mother said, "Annoying."

Then after three steps:

"Your grandfather liked him."

That was all.

I had somewhere to be.

Not urgently.

No guaranteed work.

No Hessa.

No auction.

No cloth to carry.

Nine copper in purse.

Twenty-three with Vale.

Apparently enough economic stability to become vulnerable to murals.

I stayed.

A boy on ladder was adding blue shadow beneath silverfin.

Not boy.

Young man.

Hard to tell from below.

He wore paint on both elbows and one cheek.

A woman on ground held ladder and shouted, "Too dark."

"It's shade."

"It's fish."

"Fish have shade."

"Not blue."

"This one does."

"Pell would complain."

"Pell complained about everything."

"Then honor him."

The painter sighed and wiped some blue away.

I liked city immediately more.

Then a bell rang farther down row.

Not alarm.

One clean strike.

People near wall turned.

A cart rolled slowly into street.

No goods.

Flowers.

Bad flowers.

Not ugly.

Old.

Stems tied in uneven bundles, some already browning.

Three children pulled cart with rope while two adults walked behind.

No coffin.

No body.

Just flowers, a folded coat, one wooden market scale, and a black board with white letters.

PELL ARVEN

FISH ROW / EAST GATE / EVERYWHERE HE WASN'T ASKED

DIED FOUR DAYS AGO

WALL WALK TODAY

SAY WHAT YOU REMEMBER

DO NOT IMPROVE HIM

That last line did work.

People laughed softer.

The cart stopped at mural.

Someone put folded coat beneath painted feet.

Not permanently, probably.

One of adults was fishmonger from mural.

Older now.

Real.

Same long nose.

Same narrow shoulders.

He looked at painted version of himself.

"Too handsome," he said.

Ladder painter looked down.

"Which one?"

"Both."

People laughed.

Real fishmonger touched painted Pell's raised fingers.

Then said, "Two bits wasn't theft."

Someone behind him shouted, "It was that day."

"Shut up, Laro."

"Dead man can't save you."

"Never did alive."

More laughter.

Then quiet.

Not commanded.

Arrived.

Fishmonger looked at wall.

"He bought from me every fifth day unless tide was bad. Complained about price before asking price. If I lowered it, complained fish was too small. If fish was big, complained bones were greedy. Once brought own knife and tried to show me how to cut my own fish."

A woman said, "That sounds like him."

"I threw knife in gutter."

"Also sounds like you."

"Yes."

He rubbed mouth.

"When my wife was sick, he brought soup every night and told everyone it was because I couldn't cook. Which was true. He never once said it was for her."

Silence again.

Then fishmonger added, "Soup was bad."

Relief.

People laughed.

He stepped aside.

Someone wrote on wall beneath black text.

SOUP BAD

No signature.

I understood now.

Wall walk.

Not funeral exactly.

Memory route.

Maybe temporary.

Maybe old Carrow custom I had somehow missed.

First life had taught me dozens of death customs.

Military names read from rolls.

River stones.

Ash lanterns.

Weapons broken or passed down.

Three kingdoms where you never spoke dead person's name for a year.

One where family hired professional liar to praise them so badly everyone could safely complain.

I had thought that one stupid until now.

This was better.

DO NOT IMPROVE HIM.

Hard instruction.

People loved improving dead.

Made them easier to carry.

Cart moved after maybe ten minutes.

So did crowd.

Not all.

Some stayed.

Some joined.

I joined.

No reason.

That was reason enough.

We went down East Fish Row.

Children pulled cart badly.

One rope longer than other, so cart kept drifting right.

Adult corrected twice.

Third time let them figure it out.

They did eventually.

At first stop, old cooper door.

Another painting waited.

Smaller.

Pell sitting on barrel with boot up, apparently telling cooper something unbearable.

Text:

HE KNEW BARRELS BECAUSE HE ONCE OWNED ONE

Someone had added:

IT LEAKED

Cooper came out.

Not old.

Middle-aged woman with shaved left side of head and sawdust on apron.

"That barrel did not leak," she said.

Three people shouted variations of yes it did.

She pointed at crowd.

"The floor leaked."

"Floors don't leak," fishmonger said.

"His did."

"Because barrel leaked."

"No evidence."

This city had infected everyone.

She looked at folded coat on cart.

Face changed.

"He owed my father four copper for six years."

A man behind me said, "Seven."

"Six."

"He borrowed before flood year."

"After."

"Before."

Cooper looked at him.

"You want speak or count?"

He shut up.

"Four copper," she continued. "Then one morning he paid five and said interest was for making him feel guilty every time he walked past. My father said guilt was free. Pell said not if done professionally."

Fishmonger snorted.

Cooper touched cart scale.

"He was a pain. He fixed east gate tally when clerk shorted my father three deliveries. Wouldn't stop until they checked old board. Took two weeks. Nobody asked him."

She stepped back.

Someone wrote:

DEBT PAID LATE

Another hand added:

WITH INTEREST

Cooper immediately crossed out WITH INTEREST and wrote:

WITH ONE EXTRA COPPER AND A SPEECH

Better.

Accuracy mattered even in grief.

Maybe especially.

I followed.

Third stop was tiny shrine court between bakery and cobbler.

No mural.

Only blank gray board and chalk.

A priest with bad knees sat on stool.

He looked at cart.

"Late."

One child said, "Rope problem."

Priest nodded like this explained death.

"Pell never came inside," he said.

Someone laughed.

"He stood at door every first rain and argued about roof fund."

"Did he pay?" fishmonger asked.

"Eventually."

"How eventually?"

"After explaining how I should run shrine accounts."

"Did he know shrine accounts?"

"No."

"Did that stop him?"

Priest stared.

Crowd laughed.

He smiled.

"He carried my stool home during flood because I could not walk far. Then complained stool was heavier than faith."

Someone wrote it.

STOOL HEAVIER THAN FAITH

Priest said, "Don't write that."

Too late.

He sighed.

"Fine."

We moved.

I began noticing people who did not speak.

Woman with baskets from first wall followed three stops, never said anything.

A dock worker touched folded coat once.

Two boys joined because crowd was interesting, then left when memory required patience.

A man at spice stall watched from doorway and did not come out.

At fourth stop, he was already there.

Nobody explained.

Pell had routes.

That was what I understood.

Not importance exactly.

Not rank.

Routes.

He had irritated same fishmonger nineteen years.

Owed cooper family money.

Argued shrine roof.

Bought bread somewhere.

Apparently cheated at tiles.

Knew whose daughter moved north.

Forgot birthdays.

Remembered injuries.

Told same story too often.

Walked East Gate even when South Gate shorter because he hated one paving stone.

People knew him unevenly.

That was the shape.

No one had whole Pell.

Good.

Whole people were suspicious.

At bakery stop, a woman with flour to elbows said, "He stole rolls."

Fishmonger said, "Bought?"

"Stole."

"Pell had money."

"Still stole."

"Why?"

"Said stolen bread tasted better."

"Did it?"

"He paid after."

"Then not stolen."

Baker slammed palm on counter.

"That is what I told him."

Crowd lost itself for while.

I laughed too.

Harder than deserved.

Maybe because I had spent morning having Hessa reduce magic into ratios.

Maybe because yesterday I carried cloth and watched work become money that wasn't mine.

Maybe because dead man had apparently spent decades turning minor commerce into argument and people missed him for it.

I did not know Pell.

That became important.

At fifth stop, someone handed me chalk.

I looked at it.

"No."

Woman holding chalk frowned.

"You walking."

"Didn't know him."

"Write what you saw today."

"That's not memory."

"You saw wall."

"Of him painted."

"Still saw."

I shook head.

"No."

She shrugged and gave chalk to someone else.

No pressure.

Good.

I stayed.

Witness did not mean contributor.

Sometimes it meant not pretending.

That should have been obvious.

It wasn't always.

We turned onto Narrow Bell.

Here street tightened between old three-story houses.

Laundry overhead.

Cart barely fit around vegetable stand.

One child pulling rope said, "Left."

Other said, "I am left."

"Your other left."

"I only have one."

Adult closed eyes.

I considered helping.

Did not.

They solved it by moving vegetable basket six inches.

Civilization again.

At Narrow Bell, mural was different.

No Pell face.

Only pair of shoes painted beside door.

One upright.

One on side.

Text:

HE WAITED HERE THREE HOURS IN RAIN

Below:

BECAUSE MIRA SAID SHE WOULD COME BACK

No joke.

Crowd quiet before cart stopped.

A very old woman sat just inside doorway.

Blanket over knees despite warm day.

Mira, presumably.

She looked at painted shoes.

"Four hours," she said.

Someone near wall changed THREE to FOUR.

No ceremony.

Accuracy.

Mira said, "I was giving birth."

That changed context substantially.

"Not his," she added.

Crowd laughed, including her.

"My husband was upriver. Pell was supposed to fetch midwife. He fetched midwife, then stayed outside because I told him if he came in I'd kill him."

Fishmonger said, "Reasonable."

"He kept asking through door if I needed water."

"Did you?"

"Yes."

"Did you tell him?"

"No."

"Why?"

Mira looked at fishmonger.

"I was busy."

Fair.

She rubbed blanket edge.

"He came back next day with pears. Baby couldn't eat pears."

Someone laughed softly.

"I ate them."

A child pulling cart asked, "Where's baby?"

Mira pointed into crowd.

A woman maybe forty raised hand.

Oh.

That got me.

Not dramatically.

Small internal shift.

The daughter had been there whole time.

She looked nothing like story's baby because babies were temporary.

Annoying fact.

Mira said, "He taught her cards when she was six."

Daughter said, "Five."

"Six."

"Five."

"You couldn't count at five."

"I could count cards."

"Because Pell cheated."

"Exactly."

Someone wrote:

PEARS

Then underneath:

BAD AT TIMING

Mira pointed.

"Keep pears. Remove timing."

They did.

I stood near back.

No one knew me.

A few recognized face maybe from Senna portrait or theatre or simply city accumulation.

Nobody asked why I was there.

That felt right.

Procession continued toward East Gate.

By then maybe fifty people moved with it in pieces.

Never fifty together.

Some joined one block.

Some peeled away.

A butcher came out, shouted "He hated my sausage because he had no taste," then went back inside before anyone answered.

Someone wrote NO TASTE on cart board.

One of children crossed it out because instruction was say what you remember, not editorialize.

Good child.

At East Gate there was no wall painting.

There was old paving stone.

Cracked diagonal.

Apparently the one Pell hated.

Of course.

Cart stopped beside it.

Gate clerk came down from booth.

Different clerk than any I knew.

He looked at stone.

"He filed eleven complaints."

Fishmonger said, "Only eleven?"

"Written."

"Ah."

"First three said trip hazard. Next four said drainage. Then two said wheel catch. One said civic embarrassment. Last said previous ten complaints were evidence of institutional stubbornness."

I liked Pell more every minute.

"Was stone dangerous?" someone asked.

Clerk looked down.

"Not really."

Crowd laughed.

"It catches narrow wheels if you hit wrong angle," clerk admitted.

More laughter.

"We're replacing it next resurfacing."

Fishmonger shouted, "Don't tell him now."

Quiet came again.

Because there was no him to tell.

That was grief's cheap trick.

Every joke eventually found edge.

Gate clerk took folded coat from cart.

Held it up.

Brown.

Patched elbows.

One button missing.

"Who gets this?"

Nobody answered immediately.

Then Mira's daughter said, "Ralo."

Fishmonger said, "Too small."

"He'll wear it open."

"Ralo hates brown."

From back someone shouted, "I do."

Apparently Ralo.

"Then sell it," daughter said.

"Pell would haunt me."

Priest said, "He is not authorized."

Crowd laughed.

Coat went to Ralo anyway.

Scale went to fishmonger.

He protested.

Everyone ignored him.

Flowers got divided.

Not by family.

By route.

One bundle to fish stall.

One shrine.

One cooper.

One bakery.

One Narrow Bell door.

Children each took one.

Remaining browning stems went into East Gate water trough because nobody had plan and apparently Pell would have complained about waste.

Then it was over.

No final speech.

No priest blessing.

No bell beyond one strike.

People left.

I stayed near cracked stone.

Fishmonger stood beside me with Pell's scale under arm.

"You knew him?" he asked.

"No."

"Then why walk?"

Good question.

"I wanted to see."

He nodded.

Not offended.

"Now you did."

"Yes."

He looked at scale.

"Thing lies by half ounce."

"Why keep it?"

"Because he accused mine of lying for nineteen years."

"Does yours?"

He looked at me.

"Of course."

I laughed.

He left.

I walked home alone.

No chalk note from me.

No Pell story.

I had none.

But city had changed slightly.

Not because important man died.

Maybe Pell had been important to someone.

Everyone was.

That wasn't it.

A route had lost one repeated body.

Fish Row would still sell fish.

Cooper would make barrels.

Shrine roof fund would survive without argument.

East Gate stone would eventually be replaced and nobody would get to tell Pell he had technically won.

People would keep saying things he said.

Wrongly, probably.

Then someone would correct them.

Four hours, not three.

Five years old, not six.

One extra copper, not interest.

Soup bad.

That was life after someone too.

Correction.

I had died once.

This was not new information.

Still, first death had been different.

I had not attended aftermath.

Obviously.

I knew what I remembered.

Not what other people did.

Maybe someone improved me.

Probably.

Maybe someone made me worse.

Also probably.

Maybe there had been one stupid story about boots or soup or a door I always used.

I would never know.

That thought did not hurt exactly.

It had nowhere useful to go.

So I let it walk beside me.

At home Lyssa sat by window moving wrap loops.

Eight wraps stacked left.

Three finished on right.

Separate one-copper rework.

Her work.

She looked up.

"Where were you?"

"Funeral."

Needle stopped.

"Who died?"

"Pell."

"Pell who?"

"Exactly."

She stared.

"That was terrible answer."

"I didn't know him."

"Then why funeral?"

"Wall walk."

Recognition.

"Oh."

"You know it?"

"I've seen one. Not since I was little."

There.

Local life continuing to exist without me.

"Is it always like that?"

"Like what?"

"People correcting dead man."

She smiled.

"If people liked him."

"What if they didn't?"

"Probably shorter walk."

Reasonable.

She returned to stitching.

"What did he do?"

"Complained about fish. Owed cooper money. Hated paving stone. Brought pears to newborn."

"Sounds exhausting."

"Apparently."

"You would've liked him."

"Probably not."

"Exactly."

I sat.

Tavi came in carrying piece of charcoal.

"Why funeral?"

He had heard one word from hallway and arrived immediately.

"Man died."

"Old?"

"I don't know."

"How?"

"I don't know."

"Did you see body?"

"No."

"Then how know?"

"People told me."

He frowned.

"Could be trick."

Lyssa said, "Why would whole street trick Greg about dead man?"

Tavi thought seriously.

"Surprise party."

I looked at Lyssa.

She put needle down because laughing with needle was apparently poor craft.

"For Pell?" I asked.

"Maybe he's not dead and they surprise him because he thinks funeral is for someone else."

"That is terrible surprise."

"Big surprise."

Technically.

Cala entered carrying folded laundry.

"Pell Arven?"

I turned.

"You knew him?"

"No. Saw board this morning."

"You didn't mention it."

"You didn't ask."

Cala remained undefeated.

"Did you know wall walk?"

"Yes."

"Why does nobody tell me city customs before I trip over them?"

She put laundry down.

"Too many."

Fair.

Lyssa resumed loop.

Tavi asked if we could paint him on wall when he died.

Cala said, "Not soon."

"Later."

"Later."

"Big?"

"Depends what people remember."

He looked at me.

"What would they paint you?"

Cruel question from child.

I considered crutches.

Artifacts.

Theatre.

Vale.

Bad decisions.

Good decisions delayed until they became bad differently.

"Probably arguing with sign."

Lyssa said, "Accurate."

Tavi said, "With sword."

"No sword."

"Big spoon."

"Absolutely not."

"Then no one remembers you."

Harsh.

He left.

I watched Lyssa stitch.

Three finished wraps became four.

Nothing dramatic.

One copper when done and accepted.

Second lot waiting after.

Life continuing around death because it had terrible manners.

Later upstairs I opened notes.

Nine copper.

Twenty-three Vale.

Magic fifty-nine, fifty-three, one twenty-nine.

No changes.

I almost wrote WALL WALK.

Stopped.

Not because it didn't matter.

Because not everything that mattered needed to become system.

I had not known Pell.

I had not helped.

I had not fixed anything.

I had not earned or spent.

Witness.
