# CHAPTER 402

## THE SHOPPER

Westbank had a market.

This was dangerous.

Not because of thieves.

Probably.

Because Mella Torr mentioned it while I was eating my second potato.

"Boat yard row sets tables after noon," she said. "Mostly hooks, cord, smoked things, old river junk. Since the second sun started, everyone's selling anything shiny for twice what it's worth."

I stopped chewing.

Sori saw.

"No."

"I haven't said anything."

"Your face did."

"My face is private."

"Not that face."

"You the artifact boy?"

"No."

"Dog boy?"

"Also no."

"Crutch boy?"

I looked down.

"Unfortunately evidence supports that one."

Mella nodded.

"There's a woman two doors past cooper who sells retired ferry hardware. Don't buy anything she calls lucky."

"Why?"

"Because she calls everything lucky."

Sori closed eyes.

"Mella."

"What?"

"You just gave him directions."

"He looked sad."

I had not.

Probably.

Vessa finished bread.

"We have the afternoon. Second observation before sunrise. Greg can look at junk if he remains available."

There.

Authorization.

"You're worse."

"I'm not his mother."

"Neither am I."

"Then stop parenting his purse."

I liked Vessa more every day.

Maro drank tea.

"Fourteen copper."

I looked at him.

"Why do you know?"

"You talk."

"Apparently too much."

Dask had already unfolded yesterday's observation sheet beside his plate.

Vessa tapped it once.

"Eat first."

He folded it again.

Progress remained terrifying.

We spent the first part of afternoon doing actual work.

Unfortunate.

Sori compared five mornings of timing.

Maro walked Westbank shore with Toma Grell to ask about current, wind, insects, fish, ferry behavior, and every other thing fishermen could lie about with confidence.

Kel checked our cases and arranged pre-dawn mule hold.

Vessa spoke with Westbank road clerk about what happened after second outside-witness morning.

I got the clean-copy witness ledger.

My job was to turn six people's observations into something a stranger could read without accidentally inheriting our guesses.

Harder than fighting dogs.

Less biting.

Mostly.

I wrote:

**OBSERVED: FALSE WEST-FACING SUNRISE REFLECTION PRESENT WHILE PHYSICAL SUN VISIBLE EAST.**

Then:

**OBSERVED: PHENOMENON APPEARS TO REQUIRE CONTINUOUS RIVER-SURFACE CONTEXT UNDER CURRENT SIMPLE TESTS. COLLECTED BUCKET WATER DID NOT RETAIN FALSE REFLECTION.**

I stared at second sentence.

Too strong.

Crossed out **require**.

Changed to:

**OBSERVED: PHENOMENON WAS NOT RETAINED IN COLLECTED BUCKET WATER UNDER CURRENT SIMPLE TEST.**

Better.

Dask leaned over shoulder.

"Continuous surface context is still likely."

"Likely isn't observed."

"We could write probable."

"Could."

"Will we?"

"No."

He considered.

"Okay."

There.

I had broken him.

Or improved him.

Same silhouette from distance.

By midday my shoulder had gone from ache to low complaint.

Good.

Hand still tender where hound bite-force had traveled through wood into palm.

Also good.

Pain shrinking into memory.

Not gone.

I tested crutch wrap once outside.

Solid.

Then Mella yelled from inn door.

"Tables are up!"

Sori looked at ceiling.

"Why are you helping him?"

"Commerce."

Excellent woman.

It was twelve tables, three blankets, two carts, and one man selling eels from a barrel while loudly denying they were yesterday's eels.

"Fresh!"

Toma Grell, passing behind him, said, "They know your name."

The eel man ignored her.

"Did it stay?"

"No."

"Then what am I buying?"

"Memory."

"I have one."

No universal bit conversion.

Also bottle.

Two victories.

"Vessa says stay in village."

"I am."

"She said specifically because you were going to ask if the road counted."

I was.

"Oppressive leadership."

"You want coffee?"

"Yes."

He handed me cup.

Free.

Best price.

The retired ferry-hardware woman sat exactly where Mella said.

Two doors past cooper.

Table under blue cloth.

Woman maybe sixty, maybe forty after river weather. Hair white at temples, arms brown and ropey, left little finger missing first joint.

Sign:

**RIVA SORN**

**FERRY HARDWARE / OLD SIGNAL / RIVER SALVAGE**

Under it, smaller:

**NOT RESPONSIBLE FOR PREVIOUS OWNERS**

Promising.

Riva watched me approach.

"Dog boy."

"No."

"Twelve dogs."

"Zero."

"Less impressive."

"Safer for dogs."

She looked at crutches.

"Those kill twelve?"

"They participated in zero deaths."

"Shame."

Kel drank coffee beside me and refused to help.

Riva's table was better than shiny-bottle man.

Not because things looked magical.

Because they looked used.

A brass ferry bell clapper with one side flattened.

Three ceramic current tags.

A spool of black waxed cord.

Two palm-sized slate plates etched with parallel grooves.

An old route token with center hole worn oval.

A little silvered mirror in leather frame, cracked corner.

A copper cup with no handle.

A fist-sized cage made from six thin brass ribs around empty center.

That one caught eye.

Of course.

Riva saw.

"Lucky."

Mella had warned me.

"What does it do?"

"Keeps ferry lantern from going out in bad crosswind."

"How?"

"By being lucky."

"Actual answer."

Riva smiled.

"Old wick cage. Air enters low, leaves high. Brass warms, keeps draft from biting flame directly. Mundane. Two copper."

There.

Good seller.

"Then why lucky?"

"Because people pay more when scared."

Kel coughed into coffee.

I picked up one grooved slate plate.

"This?"

"Current reader."

Interesting.

"Meaning?"

"Old ferry type. Set flat at waterline. Wet it. Grooves show cross-current by how film pulls."

"Magic?"

"Barely, depending who you ask. Slate's treated. River men call everything magic when they don't want to explain surface tension."

"Does it work?"

"Yes."

"Why retired?"

"Because painted floats are easier to see from boat."

I turned plate.

Back had stamped marks.

**WS-F/9**

White Span Ferry maybe.

Riva said, "One copper."

One copper.

I had fourteen.

I did not immediately buy it.

Growth.

"Can you demonstrate?"

She looked offended.

"Yes."

Better.

She took shallow tray from under table.

Poured water from bucket.

Set plate at one edge.

Then dragged spoon through water sideways.

Film across grooves pulled visibly toward spoon wake, leaving darker wet lines on downstream groove edges.

Simple.

Useful.

Not magical enough to ruin day.

"Treatment wears?"

"Eventually."

"How know?"

"Water stops beading clean."

"Can renew?"

"River shops. Wax-mineral rub. Cheap."

"Any false readings?"

Riva looked at me properly now.

"Rain. Oil. Soap. Mud. Bad angle. Person who already decided what current should do."

Excellent.

"Last one expensive."

"Always."

I wanted it.

Problem.

Not because one copper hurt.

Because wanting cheap useful thing was how bags became heavy.

First-life me had owned rooms full of equipment.

Some useful.

Some theoretically useful.

Some purchased because I had once thought, **I could imagine a situation.**

A situation was not a reason.

I looked toward river.

Tomorrow we were literally observing surface behavior.

That was a reason.

Maybe.

But local crew already had current ribbons and floats.

That weakened it.

"White Span has these?"

Riva snorted.

"Had. That's from White Span."

"Why'd they retire them?"

"New float boards eight years ago. Easier from bridge. Plates still good close-bank."

"Why are you selling station property?"

Kel finally became interested.

Riva pointed at stamped slash beside code.

Tiny diagonal punch.

"Retirement mark. Bought lot legal. Ask Fenner if you enjoy paperwork."

I did not.

Sori would.

"One copper?"

"One."

"With renewal rub?"

"No."

"How much rub?"

"Two bits."

There was bits again.

I still didn't know conversion universally because world hated accounting.

"One copper with enough rub for one renewal."

Riva stared.

"You bargain badly."

"Why?"

"Because I would have given two renewals."

I stared back.

Kel laughed so hard he spilled coffee.

"Fuck you."

Riva smiled.

"One copper. Two renewals."

"Fine."

I paid.

Fourteen to thirteen.

There.

New object.

Small.

Useful.

Not destiny.

Riva wrapped plate in old cloth and added thumb-sized tin of gray wax-mineral paste.

"Don't use paste until beading fails. Too much makes grooves sluggish."

"How much?"

"Rice grain each edge. Rub thin. Warm with palm."

"Good."

She tapped cracked mirror.

"Lucky river mirror. Three copper. Shows drowned men before they drown."

"No."

"Two."

"No."

"One."

"You said three because scared people pay more."

"You're learning."

I left.

Kel said, "You bought one thing."

"Yes."

"I'm proud."

"Don't make it weird."

"Could've bought dead-man mirror."

"It was cracked."

"That the problem?"

"Among several."

Sori inspected retirement punch before I even asked.

"Legal enough."

"Thank you, mother."

She looked at me.

"I will throw it in river."

"Then we can test current."

"I hate that answer."

"Personal hardware."

"Yes."

"You don't add it to official test tomorrow unless Fenner agrees."

"Yes."

"You don't use it as excuse to stand somewhere stupid."

"Define stupid."

"No."

Fair.

"Is that treated slate?"

"No."

"I can see it."

"Then why ask?"

"May I inspect it?"

Progress again.

"Riva said."

"These are good."
