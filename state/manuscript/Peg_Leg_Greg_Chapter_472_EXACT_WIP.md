# CHAPTER 472

## THE BROKER

Seven copper was not enough.

That was the thought I woke up with.

Not because seven copper was bad money.

Yesterday two silver for half a day had felt like proof that trusted work could finally move above the one-copper, two-copper sludge I'd been treating as normal.

Seven was better.

Eight was better.

Ten would be better still.

And all of them were the wrong scale.

I lay on the bunkhouse floor staring at the underside of the cot above me and remembered silver.

Actual silver.

Not as a mythical currency rich people whispered about while I scrubbed ferry tags.

I'd had silver before.

I'd wasted silver before.

I'd won silver before.

At one point I'd handled an object I had confidently described as worth forty gold.

Forty.

Gold.

Maybe I'd been right. Maybe I'd been catastrophically full of shit. The important part was that my brain had once been willing to think at that scale.

Now I was excited because a board might let me earn seven copper if two clerks liked my paperwork.

Healthy was not the same thing as ambitious.

That landed harder than I wanted.

The last months had been healthy.

Learn the city.

Pay what I owed.

Don't gamble food money.

Take work I could actually do.

Build relationships.

Don't pretend I was still S-class.

Don't buy every shiny thing.

Recover from mistakes.

Travel.

See the world.

All good.

All necessary.

Also autopilot.

A very responsible autopilot.

Still autopilot.

I sat up.

My shoulder objected mildly.

"Yeah. You too."

The man on the cot above me shifted.

I decided not to explain.

At the district board, the Bonded Lot Discrepancy Review had my application tag moved from **PENDING** to **APPROVED WITH SCOPE LIMIT**.

Seven copper.

One day.

No valuation.

No opening sealed goods.

No ownership determination.

I stared at it.

Then took my application tag down.

The clerk looked over.

"You're declining?"

"Yes."

"You applied yesterday."

"I was younger."

He waited.

"By one day."

"Transformative period."

He did not laugh.

Good.

"Reason?"

I looked at the board.

Seven copper was a perfectly rational next rung.

Which was exactly the problem.

"I'm trying to figure out whether I'm climbing the wrong ladder."

That got his attention.

"Seven copper is more than five."

"I did notice."

"Then what ladder do you want?"

"The one where I stop selling days."

He leaned back.

There.

That was the sentence.

I hadn't known it until I said it.

The clerk glanced toward the private notices at far end of room.

"Then you're looking at trade, commission, brokerage, appraisal, ownership, or management."

"Which one requires the least money to start?"

"Brokerage if somebody trusts you. Commission if somebody needs you. Management if somebody is foolish."

"Appraisal?"

"Training, registration, liability."

"Trade?"

"Capital."

"Ownership?"

"More capital."

Good.

There was the actual map.

"Brokerage."

He shrugged.

"People hear that word and imagine standing between two idiots while money falls out. Usually you're standing between two people who each know their own business better than you and both think your fee is too high."

"Sounds social."

"It's mostly finding mismatch. Somebody has the wrong thing. Somebody else needs it. Or somebody knows something in one market that another market hasn't priced yet. Then you make the introduction, carry the risk, or buy the spread."

Arbitrage.

There it was.

Not the word.

The shape.

I knew that shape.

Very well.

Support had always been full of it.

Not financial arbitrage necessarily.

Resource arbitrage.

One person had time.

Another had force.

Someone had information nobody else understood.

A healer had three minutes before overload.

A striker had one opening.

A retreat route had capacity nobody was using.

A useless artifact in one fight became decisive in another.

My entire old profession had been identifying mismatches and moving capability to where it mattered most.

I had been treating that instinct like a personality quirk.

It was probably my most expensive skill.

"Where are private commission notices?"

The clerk pointed.

I went.

The board was smaller.

Less standardized.

Worse handwriting.

Better numbers.

**SEEKING BULK DRY REED GLASS / LAKEWARD OR NORTH SHORE / 1 SILVER CEILING / COMMISSION BY AGREEMENT**

**BUYER SEEKS MATCHED HARBOR TENSION TILES / CONDITION SECONDARY / QUOTE REQUESTED**

**CINDER CROSS TOOL HOUSE SEEKS RETIRED ROUTE HARDWARE / NO SUNROAD PROVENANCE PREMIUM WITHOUT RECORD**

**PRIVATE HOUSE REQUIRES THREE SEASONAL FIELD CREWS / SOUTH ROAD / ORGANIZER FEE AVAILABLE / REFERENCES REQUIRED**

**WANTED: TWO VERIFIED QUIETENERS FOR COPPER LINE WORK / FUNCTION TEST REQUIRED**

I stopped.

I owned a retired line quietener.

One.

Not selling it automatically.

But now the object in my pack had a market context I hadn't asked about.

Then the Harbor Tension tile.

I owned one of those too.

HT-LP-4.

Bought for two copper.

Retired.

Last-peak load tile.

Useful enough that I'd kept carrying it.

Was somebody buying matched ones?

Maybe mine was worth three.

Maybe twelve.

Maybe nothing without its pair.

The point was I did not know.

And I had been congratulating myself for knowing the price of bunkhouse floors.

I copied every private notice that touched something I understood.

Then went to market.

Not artifact market first.

Freight market.

Lakeward had three adjacent lanes of sheds where goods changed from lake boats to road carts.

Reed glass.

Dried fish.

Wool.

Oil.

Salt substitutes from inland mineral beds.

Tool parts.

Ceramic pipe.

Fog-wick material.

Things I'd been walking past for chapters because they were scenery.

Now I looked at prices.

Not all posted.

Enough.

Dry reed glass strips sold retail by bundle.

North-shore bundles were cheaper when stained or mismatched.

Fog Bowl House used wick glass that had to meet narrower visual consistency.

Cinder Cross tool dealers paid more for retired route hardware if function could be demonstrated, but explicitly refused folklore premiums.

Different places valued different attributes.

Of course they did.

I sat on a public bench and felt stupid in a productive way.

The world had been screaming arbitrage at me.

Dorrin kelp.

Black Reed drying.

High Shelf resonance work.

Mere Ridge wool.

Lakeward glass.

Sunroad collector hardware.

Carrow theatre fabrication.

Harbor salvage.

Different specialists.

Different constraints.

Different markets.

And I had been earning two copper to wash the table they negotiated over.

That was harsh.

Also fair.

I had lacked capital.

I had lacked confidence.

I had lacked route knowledge.

I had lacked proof.

I had lacked mobility.

Some of those were still true.

But not all.

I knew more people now.

A lot more.

And I'd been underpricing that too.

Not because they owed me favors.

Because I knew who knew what.

That was different.

Sori understood commercial artifact judgment.

Hark & Venn understood work limbs and mechanical fitting.

Hessa understood field behavior and magic better than almost anybody I had met in this life.

Mara Fen knew old signal infrastructure.

Mera Tonn had repaired Lot Sixty-Seven correctly when I couldn't.

Della Orr knew resonance moth handling.

Lakeward archive staff knew Three Rays records.

Bracken ridge office knew Sunroad.

Morrow growers knew water pressure in ways district paper didn't.

Theatre people knew fabrication, labor, audiences, and how to make something convincing without making it real.

I knew people who did not know each other.

That mattered.

A lot.

I had spent my old life managing specialists stronger than me in their own lanes.

God-tier support did not mean I personally performed every function.

It meant I knew who should be doing what, what they needed, what they were missing, and when the plan needed to change.

Apparently I had decided reincarnation meant I should become a one-legged day laborer with excellent note-taking.

Interesting choice.

I opened notebook.

Yesterday:

**LEVER: EARNING POWER**

Today I crossed out the second line underneath it.

Not the lever.

The method.

**STOP SELLING DAYS.**

Then:

**EARN ON JUDGMENT, CONNECTION, SPREAD, OWNERSHIP.**

That was closer.

I did not have enough capital for ownership at scale.

So start with judgment and connection.

Brokerage.

Commission.

Arbitrage where risk was bounded.

I went back to the private board.

The dry reed glass notice had a contact desk number.

I found it in a merchant hall two streets over.

The buyer was a woman in a dark green vest with ink on three fingers.

No name needed yet.

"You have glass?"

"No."

Her face closed immediately.

"Then why are you here?"

"I know a dry house at Black Reed that sorts wick glass and pays careful hands to grade it. I know Lakeward Fog Bowl House uses narrower consistency. I don't know whether Black Reed has the grade you want, whether your ceiling is sane, or whether transport kills the spread. I'm asking what specification you're actually buying."

Her face reopened slightly.

"Not wick glass. Reed glass. Different product."

Good.

Wrong first assumption.

"Then I don't know enough."

"Apparently."

"What makes a useful introduction worth paying for?"

That changed conversation.

She tapped notice.

"I need six matched dry bundles, low warp, no salt bloom, delivered Lakeward inside twelve days. North shore has stock. North shore also has three buyers ahead of me and ferry delays. If someone finds equivalent inland stock or gets me ahead of delay without paying stupidity prices, I pay commission."

"How much?"

"Depends what you solve."

"Range."

"One to six copper for information or introduction. More if you take purchase risk yourself."

Still copper.

But not for a day.

For a connection.

And the ceiling on the purchase was one silver.

There.

Scale.

"What counts as equivalent?"

She gave me dimensions, acceptable warp, edge clouding limits, bundle count, bloom rejection.

I wrote them exactly.

"Can I take a copy?"

"One bit."

I paid.

Then I asked the important question.

"If I find six qualifying bundles at a seller you don't know, and you buy them directly, what's the introduction fee?"

"Three copper if they pass inspection. Five if landed price beats my current north-shore quote by ten percent."

"And if I negotiate seller down?"

"If I'm buying directly, your fee is still five. If you buy and resell, your margin is your problem."

Good.

Clear.

"No exclusivity?"

"Not for five copper."

Fair.

I left with specification.

No job.

A hunt.

Different feeling.

I did not know where equivalent reed glass sat.

But I knew something else.

The Fog Bowl House.

They sorted wick glass, not reed glass.

Still glass infrastructure.

People who handled one material often knew who handled adjacent material.

I went there.

The foreman from the board wasn't working front bench. Another sorter was.

I showed specification.

"Not ours," he said.

"I know. Who sells it?"

He read again.

"Low warp dry bundles? Lakeward buyers usually pull north."

"Usually."

"There's a kiln yard at Tollen that does reed backing for window shutters. Not same grade."

"Could they make it?"

"Maybe. They hate small orders."

"Six bundles."

He laughed.

"They'll hate you personally."

"Excellent. Who else?"

He thought.

"Old marsh works east of Cinder Cross used to dry it for signal houses. Don't know if they still do."

Cinder Cross.

The next road east.

There it was.

Adventure and leverage touching.

Not because quest marker.

Because markets had geography.

"Name of works?"

"Don't know current. Used to be called East Fen Glassyard."

I wrote it.

"Anything else?"

"If your buyer needs no salt bloom, inland makes sense. But inland reed tends thicker. Depends why she wants it."

I had not asked why.

Good catch.

Back to merchant hall.

The buyer looked annoyed to see me again.

"What is it for?"

"You don't need to know."

"Then I need functional constraints instead. Flex? Heat? Optical? Structural?"

She stared.

Then gave me a better answer.

"Lamp-house shutters. Needs to flex in frame without edge powdering. Optical quality irrelevant. Low warp because bundles are cut into paired strips."

Not fog bowls.

Not collector glass.

Functional.

"Thicker inland stock acceptable if it flexes?"

"Within gauge."

She added gauge tolerance.

Now I had something.

Not money yet.

Something.

I checked road board.

Cinder Cross.

Five copper.

That would take me from fifteen to ten.

Exactly floor.

Bad if I arrived and found nothing.

Unless the trip also had other value.

Private board already had:

**CINDER CROSS TOOL HOUSE SEEKS RETIRED ROUTE HARDWARE / NO SUNROAD PROVENANCE PREMIUM WITHOUT RECORD**

I had Sunroad records.

Not hardware to sell.

But I knew Lakeward market had an eighteen-copper orphan disk nobody should buy alone.

Could Cinder Cross Tool House want it as a component?

Maybe.

Could broker that too.

Now one route had two possible spreads.

Better.

Then I noticed the private crew notice again.

**THREE SEASONAL FIELD CREWS / SOUTH ROAD / ORGANIZER FEE AVAILABLE**

Managing people.

I almost ignored it because organizer sounded like somebody with a clipboard and a louder voice than mine.

Then I remembered first life.

Rooms full of specialists.

People stronger than me.

People smarter than me in narrow domains.

People with egos large enough to affect weather.

I had coordinated them while things tried to kill us.

Maybe I could manage three seasonal field crews.

Maybe not.

But the idea was not absurd.

That was new.

I copied notice.

Not applying yet.

Need scope.

Need references.

Need to know whether organizer meant hiring, scheduling, provisioning, field supervision, or being blamed when forty people hated lunch.

But I stopped dismissing it.

People leverage.

Not someday.

Potentially now.

I returned to bunkhouse with no additional copper.

Fifteen.

Same as morning.

Seven-copper job declined.

By ordinary accounting, I had made negative progress.

I disagreed.

I had found the scale transition.

Copper labor was not wrong.

It had kept me alive.

It had taught me roads.

It had introduced me to systems.

It had built proof.

But it was not where I should stay.

The next level was not simply bigger wages.

It was leverage.

Information before other people had it.

Knowing two markets.

Knowing two specialists.

Seeing when an object was mispriced because one buyer valued history and another valued function.

Taking commission for solving mismatches.

Eventually taking ownership risk when I had enough capital to survive being wrong.

And managing people when the value came from coordination rather than my hands.

Silver stopped feeling like fantasy currency.

It became scale target.

I wrote:

**NEXT ECONOMIC TIER: THINK IN SILVER.**

Not `have one silver tomorrow.`

Not `refuse copper.`

Think in silver.

Seek transactions, contracts, commissions, lots, or coordinated work where total value moved was silver-scale even if my first cut was still copper.

That was how you learned the market above you.

Then:

**FIRST ARBITRAGE TEST: CINDER CROSS.**

Potential lanes:

Reed glass introduction.

Route-hardware buyer.

Tool-house market information.

New inland suppliers.

Maybe nothing.

Five-copper travel cost.

Current cash fifteen.

Would hit ten-copper floor exactly.

Too tight unless I secured something else first or negotiated travel/work together.

Good.

Constraint.

Not stop sign.

I added:

**Find way east without paying full fare from capital.**

Work passage.

Cargo escort.

Commission advance, unlikely.

Shared cart.

Route task that paid in same direction.

There.

Highest leverage did not mean abandon adventure.

It meant make the road do more than one job.

I closed notebook.

For months I'd been proud that I could survive on copper.

I was still proud of it.

I just wasn't interested in proving it forever.
