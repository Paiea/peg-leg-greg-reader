# CHAPTER 474

## THE SOURCER

East Fen Glassyard existed.

That was the first good news.

It had not become a goat shed.

It had become three sheds, two kilns, one leaning drying house, and a yard full of cut reed frames that looked like somebody had tried to build a fence out of bad decisions.

I stood at the west gate with fourteen copper, eight silver, Vale still open, one Lakeward specification folded inside notebook, and exactly enough confidence to be irritating.

A woman in a leather apron looked at me through the gate.

"What do you want?"

"Dry reed glass."

"Everybody wants dry reed glass."

"Six matched bundles. Low warp. No salt bloom. Lamp-house shutter gauge."

That changed her face.

Not friendlier.

More specific.

"Who sent you?"

"Nobody. I have a buyer."

"Then you have paperwork."

I took out the copied specification.

She opened the gate.

Good start.

East Fen sat maybe half a mile off the Cinder road where the land flattened into pale marsh grass and low drainage cuts. No salt smell. That mattered, maybe. The buyer had rejected bloom, and the Fog Bowl sorter had said inland made sense for that reason.

The yard itself was old enough that newer work had grown around older bones.

Brick kiln mouths.

Timber drying racks.

A narrow water channel running under two sheds.

Glass bundles stacked on slatted shelves with cloth between them.

Most of it was not the translucent sheet glass I had expected the first time somebody said reed glass months ago.

These were long narrow strips made from fused reed-silica composite, cloudy amber to pale green, flexible enough to bow slightly without cracking.

Fantasy world.

There you are.

I'd spent three days talking about money until the world had started sounding suspiciously like accounting with magic nearby.

The apron woman handed my specification back.

"Six matched is the problem."

"Why?"

"We can make six."

"That wasn't the question."

She looked at me again.

"You're annoying."

"I've been told it scales well."

A voice from inside the nearest shed said, "Lena, if he's buying, let him be annoying."

There.

Name.

Lena.

Not necessarily owner.

I did not need to assign her a biography because somebody had used one syllable.

A man came out carrying a bundle gauge under one arm. Older than Lena, gray at beard, left thumb wrapped in linen.

He held out hand for the paper.

"Lakeward lamp shutters?"

"Yes."

He read.

"Whose order?"

"Buyer in Lakeward merchant hall. I have introduction terms if qualifying stock passes."

"Name?"

I hesitated.

I had not written the buyer's name because she had not given it in a way I trusted myself to remember from state.

Better not invent one.

"Desk contact is on the copy."

It was.

He looked at the mark.

"Knew that house."

Not the person.

Good.

He read gauge tolerance twice.

"We have four that pass this now."

"Four bundles?"

"Four matched dry."

"Buyer needs six."

"Then buyer can need things somewhere else."

Lena smiled.

Apparently that was the customer-service department.

"How far are the next two from passing?"

The man tapped the paper against his leg.

"Depends what you mean."

"Gauge, warp, bloom, edge powdering. Which thing fails?"

That earned me entry into the shed.

Not trust.

Entry.

Four bundles sat on center racks with slate tags.

Two more sat separately near a wall where warm air moved through slatted vents.

The man took a strip from first four.

Bent it against a curved wooden form.

Not much.

Enough.

No white edge dust.

He checked thickness at three points with a split gauge.

Then laid it against a straight board.

Low warp.

The fifth bundle looked similar until he put it to the board.

One end lifted.

Not terrible.

Enough.

"Drying pulled it," he said.

"Can it settle?"

"Maybe."

"How long?"

"Properly? Five or six days."

Buyer had twelve days from yesterday.

Travel back to Lakeward.

Inspection.

Margin.

Still possible.

"What about sixth?"

He showed me.

Gauge good.

Warp good.

Then rubbed one edge with thumb.

Fine pale powder came off.

"Bloom?"

"No. Wrong failure."

Good correction.

"What is it?"

"Outer reed binder ran hot. Edge skin too brittle."

"Can it be recut?"

"Bundle width would fall under spec."

"Can it be re-faced?"

He gave me the look people gave when a non-specialist used a specialist verb.

"Not without making it worse."

So four.

One maybe in six days.

One dead.

Not a deal.

Unless there was more stock.

"Anything not dry yet?"

Lena laughed.

The man did not.

"Plenty."

"Anything already within gauge?"

"Some."

"Enough for one bundle?"

"Probably."

"Could you dry it inside six days?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because wet glass doesn't care about your buyer."

Fair.

"What controls drying time?"

That was the useful question.

Not "can you hurry."

Not "what if we pay more."

Process first.

Airflow.

Rack spacing.

Humidity.

Initial water content.

Binder composition.

Thickness.

Temperature, but not too much because uneven heat warped strips or hardened edges.

The old drying house used passive cross-draft through high vents.

Six to nine days for this gauge depending weather.

Current weather was damp.

Worse.

I walked outside and looked at the roofline.

Two black iron vanes sat above the drying house.

One moved.

One did not.

"What's that?"

The man looked up.

"Draft pair."

"Artifact?"

"Old yard tool."

There it was.

He did not sound impressed.

Good sign.

"What does it do?"

"Pulls air through when the channel runs."

"One isn't moving."

"Yes."

"Broken?"

"Not exactly."

Excellent answer.

"What does not exactly mean?"

Lena said, "It means don't touch it."

Also excellent.

The man led me around back.

A bronze spindle ran from the water channel into a boxed housing under the drying wall. The channel turned a small paddle wheel. Inside the box, something hummed faintly enough that I felt it more in the crutch tips than heard it.

One roof vane rotated in short steady turns.

The other remained angled east.

"Draft pair used to alternate," he said. "One pulls upper vents, one pulls lower. Keeps the room from drying one face harder than other."

"And now?"

"Upper works. Lower locks after three or four turns."

"Why?"

"If I knew, I'd have fixed it."

"Who services it?"

"Used to be Fenwright shop."

"Used to?"

"Closed."

"How long has lower been bad?"

"Two years."

"Then how do you dry?"

"Slower. Rearranged racks. More hand turning. Works."

There.

The glassyard was not failing.

It had adapted.

Important distinction.

"Would a working lower draft shorten six-day window?"

The man crossed arms.

"For wet stock? Maybe. If it worked correctly."

"Not enough confidence."

"No."

"What about the warped fifth bundle?"

Pause.

"That one might."

Because it was already dry enough.

The problem was not make new glass in impossible time.

The problem was relax and finish one nearly qualifying bundle, then find one more from current semi-dry stock close enough to finish.

Maybe.

Still one short.

I asked to see semi-dry inventory.

Lena said, "You planning to buy the yard?"

"No. I currently have sixteen copper."

"Then definitely not."

The man laughed once.

We went to second drying shed.

The semi-dry stock was less orderly.

Different orders.

Different gauges.

Different buyers.

And, crucially, different ownership status.

Some bundles were already committed.

Some were production overage.

Some had been rejected for cosmetic color variation that did not matter to my buyer because optical quality was irrelevant.

That last category caught me.

"These why rejected?"

He lifted one.

Pale green with darker streak down center.

"Window backing order. Visible through outer lattice. Customer wanted even color."

"Structure?"

"Fine."

"Gauge?"

He checked.

Inside tolerance.

"Warp?"

Inside.

"Edge?"

Clean.

"How dry?"

"Another two days at least."

"How many?"

"Two bundles."

There.

Not matched by appearance.

Buyer did not care about optical quality.

Matched meant functional dimensions and performance, not color.

I reread specification.

Low warp.

Gauge tolerance.

No salt bloom.

Flex without edge powder.

Six bundles.

It did not say color matched.

I had assumed matched meant visual matching because glass.

Stupid.

Useful stupid.

"Does 'matched' on this trade usually mean color too?"

The man read contact mark again.

"For lamp-house shutter work? Usually gauge and batch behavior. Depends buyer."

"Then I need to ask."

I did not pretend.

Cinder Cross had a message counter.

Not instant.

Still better than riding two days because I was shy about clarification.

I could send a paid road message back toward Lakeward merchant hall.

Cost?

One copper for next-run commercial note, according to Cinder posting I had passed.

A copper mattered.

Not crisis.

This was what capital was for.

Information before movement.

I went back to Cinder Cross that afternoon.

Shoulder tolerated the road because I hired the cheap local cart seat from the fen junction instead of proving I could crutch six miles for free.

Bits.

Fine.

At message counter I wrote:

**EAST FEN HAS 4 PASSING DRY BUNDLES. POSSIBLE 2 ADDITIONAL COSMETICALLY MISMATCHED BUNDLES WITHIN APPROX 2 DAYS, GAUGE/WARP/EDGE EXPECTED TO SPEC. DOES MATCH REQUIRE COLOR/OPTICAL CONSISTENCY OR FUNCTIONAL GAUGE/BATCH ONLY? ALSO ONE DRY WARPED BUNDLE MAY BE RECOVERABLE WITH CONTROLLED DRAFT. PLEASE CONFIRM ACCEPTABLE MIX BEFORE I NEGOTIATE.**

Then paid one copper.

Sixteen to fifteen.

Still five above floor.

I disliked that less than I would have a month ago.

Not because fifteen was more money.

Because the copper was buying decision quality on a silver-scale transaction.

That was a different species of expense.

I stayed in Cinder because there was nothing useful to do at the yard while waiting.

Tool House buyer saw me pass.

"More illegal metal?"

"Glass."

"Worse."

"Why?"

"Breaks."

Strong expertise.

I asked whether he knew anyone who serviced Fenwright draft pairs.

He stopped tapping a hinge.

"East Fen's?"

"Probably."

"Lower vane still sticking?"

There.

"Yes."

"Then no."

"You know the problem?"

"I know everybody asks after they decide a dead shop owes them service forever."

"Anyone local?"

"There's a mill tuner south edge who works water-drive governors. Draft pair isn't governor."

"Close enough to inspect?"

"Close enough to charge you for being wrong."

Good.

"What does inspection cost?"

"Ask her."

Her.

I went.

The mill tuner operated from a shed beside a grain wheel, mostly belts, bronze teeth, regulator arms, and three small stones that floated in a vertical cage to show load.

Her name was Tessa because a customer called it while I waited.

Again, no biography required.

I described the draft pair without pretending I understood mechanism.

One upper vane worked.

Lower locked after three or four turns.

Water drive active.

Two-year adaptation by rack turning.

Could she inspect without promising repair?

She said, "Two copper plus ride."

"Ride from Cinder to East Fen?"

"You provide."

"One copper inspection if you're already going that direction?"

"I'm not."

"Then two."

Reasonable.

Too much for me to speculate casually.

Could ask yard to split.

Better.

I returned East Fen next morning after one-copper message had not yet returned because roads remained roads.

The gray-bearded man was turning racks by hand with Lena.

I asked his name because if I was going to propose money, "the man" was becoming stupid.

"Orin."

Good.

"Orin, local mill tuner will inspect the draft pair for two copper plus ride."

"I know Tessa."

"Then why haven't you?"

"Because she told me two years ago she doesn't service Fenwright draft."

"Inspection, not service."

"What good is inspection if she won't fix it?"

"Could tell you whether the failure is mechanical enough for somebody else to fabricate."

He gave me a look.

"Somebody else who?"

"Don't know yet."

Correct answer.

"You paying?"

"No."

"Then no."

Also correct.

I sat on a low beam.

That was the commercial problem.

Everybody wanted uncertainty removed.

Nobody wanted to fund removing it because the immediate value belonged partly to somebody else.

Lakeward buyer wanted six bundles.

East Fen could maybe produce them.

Tessa could maybe identify a fault.

Some fabricator could maybe repair it.

I could spend my own copper and become a philanthropist with excellent notes.

No.

Broker.

Move value, not absorb everyone's risk.

"What if inspection cost comes out of sale only if buyer takes six?"

Orin stopped.

"If no sale?"

"No inspection."

"Tessa won't agree."

"Then yard pays one copper, I pay one, and if buyer takes six you reimburse my one from price."

Now I was taking bounded risk.

One copper.

Potential two-silver commission if landed terms beat north quote materially.

Also market relationship.

Potential future yard output.

Worth considering.

Orin said, "Why would I reimburse your copper?"

"Because if her inspection gets you one qualifying bundle inside window, you get a sale you do not currently have."

"And if she says useless old junk?"

"I lose one too."

"That supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to make me less annoying."

Lena said, "Didn't work."

Progress.

Orin thought.

Then shook head.

"Not yet. Wait for buyer answer."

Good.

No need to solve a machine before knowing whether we needed it.

That was Hessa's influence and basic adulthood working together.

The reply came late that day.

Commercial message slip.

Buyer had written only:

**MATCH = GAUGE / FLEX / DRYNESS / BUNDLE LENGTH. COLOR IRRELEVANT. MIXED COLOR ACCEPTABLE. SIX MUST PASS INSPECTION. SEND ASK + EARLIEST READY + TRANSPORT TERMS.**

Beautiful.

No need for draft repair.

Four dry.

Two cosmetic rejects semi-dry, two days expected.

Six.

Potentially.

I took the reply to Orin.

He checked those two again.

One had dried enough overnight to show slight edge pull on three strips.

Not failure yet.

Risk.

The other looked better.

"So five?" I asked.

"Four certain. One likely. One maybe."

Not six.

I hated honest tradespeople.

They made optimism expensive.

"What makes maybe become likely?"

"Turn twice today. Keep lower rack off warm wall. Dry slower."

"That costs time."

"One extra day."

Buyer had time if transport worked.

"What is earliest honest ready?"

"Three days from now."

"Not two?"

"Three."

I wrote three.

"Ask?"

He named a price for six bundles that I did not have a useful denomination conversion framework to compare against Lakeward's north-shore quote.

It was below one silver ceiling.

That was enough to continue.

"Does that include road to Lakeward?"

"No."

"To Cinder?"

"Yes if our cart is free."

"Which day?"

He looked at Lena.

She answered without checking paper.

"Fourth morning."

Different expertise.

"Can six be at Cinder fourth morning if they pass third-day check?"

"Yes."

"Then Lakeward transport from Cinder is my problem?"

"Your buyer's."

"Could be mine if solving it increases fee."

Orin shrugged.

Correct.

I went back to Cinder freight board.

Lakeward backhaul existed daily, but prices varied.

One carrier had space fourth morning.

Reed glass required covered slat rack, no top stacking.

He had that.

He wanted payment from shipper on delivery.

Good.

I gave him bundle dimensions.

He quoted.

I wrote it, did not bargain yet.

Then I did the actual broker work.

I sent one more commercial message to Lakeward.

Another copper.

Fifteen to fourteen.

Four above floor.

The old me would have started getting twitchy.

Current me noticed the floor.

Then asked what the copper bought.

It bought a live quote with source, readiness, and transport.

Worth it.

Message:

**EAST FEN CAN OFFER 6 FUNCTIONALLY MATCHED BUNDLES SUBJECT FINAL THIRD-DAY PASS. 4 DRY PASS NOW. 2 COLOR-REJECT OVERAGE CURRENTLY DRYING, COLOR ONLY OUTSIDE PRIOR CUSTOMER PREFERENCE. EARLIEST CINDER FOURTH MORNING. COVERED SLAT BACKHAUL AVAILABLE SAME MORNING. SELLER ASK + CARRIER QUOTE ATTACHED. I HAVE NOT COMMITTED BUYER OR SELLER. CONFIRM WHETHER LANDED TERMS QUALIFY 1S OR 2S INTRODUCTION CONDITION IF FINAL INSPECTION PASSES.**

Then I waited.

That was the worst part of brokerage.

Not bargaining.

Waiting while other people remained independent organisms.

I spent the afternoon at Tool House, not working for them, just watching a buyer reject a crate of old latch springs because half had been annealed badly.

He saw me watching.

"You're learning to enjoy people saying no."

"Useful market function."

"Expensive hobby."

"Have you ever bought from East Fen?"

"Glass? No."

"Why not?"

"I sell tools."

"Strong boundary."

"Try one."

"What?"

"Why?"

**IF FINAL SIX PASS SPEC AND SELLER ASK REMAINS AS ATTACHED, LANDED QUOTE BEATS CURRENT NORTH-SHORE PURCHASE ENOUGH FOR 5C INTRODUCTION FEE. BUYER WILL PURCHASE DIRECT FROM EAST FEN AFTER RECEIVING FINAL PASS MARKS. CARRIER MAY BILL BUYER ON DELIVERY IF SEALS / BUNDLE TAGS MATCH. NO PURCHASE RISK REQUESTED FROM BROKER.
