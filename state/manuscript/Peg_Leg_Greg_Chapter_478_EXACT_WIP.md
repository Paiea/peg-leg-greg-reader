# CHAPTER 478

## THE CORRESPONDENT

The letter was waiting under my name.

That was inconvenient.

Not because I did not want it.

Because I wanted it enough to become stupid.

Lakeward post opened before the breakfast stall beside it, which felt like an institutional mistake. Nobody should have to receive emotional information without fried dough.

I went anyway.

Eighteen copper.

Twenty-three with Vale.

Bits unspecified.

Fifty-nine.

Fifty-three.

One hundred thirty-nine.

All the numbers still where I had left them.

The clerk had three bundles of route notices, two merchant receipts, one narrow parcel wrapped in gray cloth, and a folded cream sheet with **GREG** written on the outside in handwriting I knew immediately.

Lyssa wrote my name like she was annoyed the letters had to take up space.

I stared at it.

The clerk stared at me.

"Taking it?"

"Considering whether that's wise."

"It's yours."

"That does not answer the question."

He pushed it closer.

"Take your problems off my counter."

Good public service.

I took it.

There was no seal beyond a dab of ordinary wax pressed flat with the back of something round. No family mark. No merchant stamp. No dramatic blood.

Just Lyssa.

I went outside.

Then did not open it.

That was stupid too.

Across from the post, Lakeward was already becoming itself.

A fish cart came through too fast.

Two dock women argued about baskets while loading the same baskets.

A boy swept water off a stone step directly toward another boy, who looked down at the water, looked up at him, and said, "I know where you sleep."

The first boy kept sweeping.

Strong relationship.

I bought breakfast with bits.

Ordinary.

Sat on a low wall.

Letter in my coat.

Ate half the bread.

Still did not open it.

This was ridiculous.

Old Greg had opened battlefield casualty lists with steadier hands.

That was not useful perspective.

Casualty lists had rules.

Lyssa did not.

I finished chewing.

Opened the letter.

Her first line was:

**You are terrible at telling me where you are.**

Fair.

Second:

**Your last note said Lakeward, then maybe east, then "probably back before this reaches you," which is not an address and also turned out to be wrong.**

Also fair.

I kept reading.

**I sent this to Lakeward because the post woman said if you were still wandering somebody might know where to send it, which sounds fake, but apparently this is how your life works now.**

I smiled.

That annoyed me.

**The room is fine. I am fine. Before you start doing that thing where you turn "fine" into a diagnostic category, I mean I am busy, I am sleeping enough, I ate yesterday, and nobody has threatened me.**

There.

Relationship language.

Specific enough to stop me.

Not sentimental.

Better.

**Work has been stupid. Two people wanted the same green thread after I told both of them there was only enough for one. One of them said she had asked first. She had not. The other one said she did not care which green. She absolutely cared which green. I solved this by making both of them leave and come back tomorrow.**

I could see it.

Not the customers.

Lyssa's face when somebody said they did not care about color while visibly caring about color.

**Jessa says I should start charging people for changing their minds. Marra says then I would become rich and unbearable. I told her Greg is already working on the unbearable part for the household.**

I stopped.

Read that again.

There were people in the room with her.

Work continuing.

Jessa.

Marra.

Nothing waiting for Greg to activate it.

Good.

Also rude.

**Your cup is still here. The bad one. I almost threw it away because the crack is worse. Then I remembered you get weird about objects when they have survived too long. It is on the high shelf. Do not tell me this is proof of love. It is proof I did not want to listen to you complain.**

I looked out at Lakeward water.

The cup.

I had forgotten the cup.

That hurt more than I expected.

Not because it was important.

Because it had become unimportant in a place where I lived.

Those were different things.

A gull landed three feet away and stared at my breakfast paper.

"No."

It took one step closer.

"Absolutely not."

It took another.

I moved the paper.

The gull looked offended.

Good.

Lyssa continued.

**Cala stopped by. She borrowed the small chair again. She brought it back this time without me asking. One leg is still shorter. I am not fixing it. If you fix it, I will know you were bored.**

I laughed.

The gull left.

Maybe judgment.

**The theatre sent somebody by for you once. I do not know what for. I told him you were east. He said, "Again?" like this was my fault. I told him no. He left.**

That was all.

No urgent theatre job.

No plot summons.

Just someone expecting me enough to knock.

I liked that too much.

Then:

**I got one note from Hark & Venn addressed to you. I did not open it. It is with your papers. Before you ask, no, I do not know what it says, and yes, I know you are going to make a face when you read this. Make it there.**

I made the face there.

Good.

There was work-limb information at home.

Not here.

Not conveniently copied into the letter.

Excellent.

I resisted the urge to build six theories.

Barely.

**If you are coming back, come back. If you are not, send a useful note. I am not asking for an itinerary. I am asking for a city. Maybe two if you are feeling ambitious.**

Then one blank line.

Then:

**I miss you. This is not permission to become dramatic.**

I sat very still.

Lakeward remained rude enough to keep operating.

Cart wheels.

Water.

Somebody shouting about eels.

Hammering from dock repair.

A woman behind me saying, "No, the blue rope. The blue one. That is gray. Do you have eyes?"

Useful world.

I read the last line again.

Then the one above it.

Then the whole letter.

Not because I had missed anything.

Because paper did not get annoyed when you repeated a conversation.

I folded it carefully.

Unfolded it.

There was more on the back.

Of course there was.

**Also your other shirt is missing a button. I put it on your pillow because apparently I enjoy creating future work for myself. Do not wear it like that.**

I closed my eyes.

There.

Lyssa.

Entire person.

I put the letter inside notebook behind the Bracken reference.

Then moved it.

Bracken reference was official and stiff.

Lyssa's letter did not belong behind a road closure.

I put it inside coat.

Better.

The next question was obvious.

Go home.

My mind immediately tried to improve it.

Check road board.

Compare fares.

See if another freight seat moves west.

Ask merchant hall whether anyone needed a document carried.

Maybe Hark & Venn had Lakeward routing.

Maybe theatre note meant work.

Maybe I could stop somewhere and turn travel into another commission.

No.

I had just learned this.

Apparently lessons did not become permanent merely because I wrote them in notebooks.

I stood.

Shoulder fine.

Right leg fine.

Crutch palms ordinary.

No trial limb.

No magic.

Lakeward westbound board was two streets over.

I went there.

Three departures listed.

One toward Carrow district next morning.

One late freight route that did not name Carrow but passed the south branch.

One water packet with final stop west basin.

Prices posted.

I read them.

Then stopped myself before turning the board into a thesis.

The Carrow route left at first bell.

That was enough.

I asked the board clerk, "Seats?"

"Two."

"Can I hold one?"

"Until dusk."

"Cost to hold?"

"One bit."

Fine.

I paid the bit.

He wrote **GREG / FIRST BELL** on slate.

No copper moved.

The fare would be due on boarding.

I did not ask him to explain the entire route economy.

Growth continued.

"How long?"

He gave me the normal answer.

Long enough that if everything behaved, I would be home after one more road day and part of another.

Good.

Physical.

Not teleportation.

I nodded.

"Hold it."

"Already did."

Right.

I left.

Now I had most of a day in Lakeward.

This created a dangerous amount of unscheduled time.

Old Greg would have used it to inspect equipment.

Current Greg was also tempted to inspect equipment.

At least I was consistent.

Instead I went to the public washhouse.

Not because of character development.

Because I had slept in Cinder bunks, freight clothes, road dust, auction rooms, and one Lakeward cot, and my shirt had reached the point where it deserved independent legal representation.

Washhouse attendant took bits.

I rented a basin, soap, drying rail.

Crutches leaned within reach.

Coat stayed with me because Lyssa's letter was inside and I had apparently become twelve.

I washed shirt.

Then undershirt.

Then myself as much as public decency allowed.

A man two basins down was trying to remove black resin from his sleeves and losing.

He had the defeated posture of someone who had already been told the correct method and ignored it.

The attendant walked past.

"Cold first."

"I did cold."

"You did warm."

"It felt cold."

"Water disagrees."

He stared into basin.

"Can water disagree?"

"Yours is."

She kept walking.

Different people.

Good.

I scrubbed road grit from cuff.

One seam near elbow had loosened.

Nothing dramatic.

I could wear it.

Lyssa would see it.

That changed classification.

I found a needle seller outside washhouse.

Bits.

Ordinary repair thread.

I bought enough to close seam myself badly or bring home and let Lyssa tell me I had done it badly.

I considered fixing it.

Did not.

Not because helplessness was romantic.

Because sometimes the person who knew how to do the thing was also the person whose work I respected.

And because I wanted to see her.

That was becoming inconveniently central.

I spent midday near water.

No deals.

No investigations.

I watched a dock crew use a pulley frame with a blue-glass brake bead that darkened whenever load swung too fast.

Magic.

Ordinary.

Not mine.

A boy carried tally boards.

One woman ate half a pear while shouting weights.

A man on barge roof sang three lines of same song until someone threw a rag at him.

He caught rag.

Kept singing.

The brake bead darkened twice.

Crew adjusted.

No accident.

No Greg.

Good.

I bought lunch.

Bits.

Sat with letter again.

This time I did not read entire thing.

Only:

**If you are coming back, come back.**

And:

**I miss you.**

Then I put it away before language became syrup.

I needed to write back.

This was almost pointless because I might beat the letter home.

That was not the point.

She had asked for a useful note.

So she got one.

Lakeward post sold folded sheets.

I bought one.

Sat at high writing counter.

The first version began:

**Lakeward. Going home tomorrow.**

Good.

Useful.

Then I stared.

What else?

I wanted to tell her Cinder had been strange.

That I had moved illegal freight.

No.

Not in a casual letter through public post.

I wanted to tell her I had found East Fen glass.

Fine.

I wrote:

**Lakeward. Going home tomorrow. I found the glass source and the buyer accepted all six bundles, so that worked. I got paid.**

Then:

**I also lost an auction for a broken artifact I wanted because someone else had more money than I could safely spend. This was correct and I hated it.**

That sounded like me.

Then:

**The road from Cinder had a charge-ground failure and the wardens fixed it while we waited. Nobody died. I did nothing useful. I enjoyed this.**

Also good.

Then I stopped.

I had written her a field report.

Lyssa had written me about thread, a chair, a cup, work, and missing me.

I had answered with transactions and infrastructure.

Excellent boyfriend.

I scratched none of it out.

Instead added:

**I miss you too.**

Looked wrong.

Not false.

Just naked.

I added:

**Do not become dramatic about this.**

Better.

Then:

**Tell Marra I am already rich in unbearable. Tell Jessa charging for changed minds is correct and dangerous. Do not throw away the cup yet. I need to inspect whether the crack has achieved historical significance.**

I smiled.

Then:

**Leave Hark & Venn note sealed. I will read it when I get there.**

There.

No theory.

No request to transcribe.

No making Lyssa become my administrative assistant because she happened to share a room with my paper.

Then I added one last line.

**First bell route tomorrow. Carrow. That is a city. I did what you asked.**

I folded it.

Addressed home.

Paid postage in bits.

The post clerk took it.

"Could arrive after you."

"I know."

"You still sending?"

"Yes."

He shrugged.

Good institution.

I walked away lighter by one letter and no copper.

Eighteen stayed eighteen.

That mattered less than it used to.

By late afternoon Lakeward had turned gold around water edges.

I did one useful thing.

Not profitable.

Useful.

I copied the morning westbound seat mark into notebook and checked that my hold was still on slate.

It was.

Then I bought dinner.

No purse recount.

That was getting easier.

At bunkhouse, I cleaned crutch tips.

One had collected black grit in rubber edge.

Picked it out with blunt needle back.

Checked shafts.

Fine.

Hands.

Fine.

Shoulder.

Better.

Residual limb.

Ordinary.

Right leg.

Tired enough to know I had walked city all day, not enough to create a problem.

Protocol stayed fifty-nine, fifty-three, one hundred thirty-nine.

No magic.

No artifact.

No work.

No income.

No debt payment.

Good.

I put Lyssa's letter under pillow.

Then immediately moved it because that was sentimental and also because somebody could steal it.

Notebook pocket.

Safer.

I lay down.

The man in next bunk turned over and said, "You snore?"

"Not according to me."

"That is useless."

"Ask me while I'm asleep."

He thought about that longer than necessary.

"I don't think that works."

"Then we're trapped."

He grunted.

Different roommate.

Different city.

Tomorrow home.

That word had changed quietly.

Home used to mean where my things were.

Then where Lyssa was.

Then where work could find me.

Then where people got annoyed when I left without saying exactly where.

Now all of those were true at once.

I did not write that down.

The scene had already made the point.

Good.

Before sleep, I checked the seat hold slip.

First bell.

Carrow.

Then Lyssa's letter one more time.

Not whole thing.

Just the end.

**I miss you. This is not permission to become dramatic.**

I smiled in dark.

"Terrible woman."

The man beside me said, "What?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

Lakeward creaked around us.

Tomorrow I would go home.

Not because a quest ended.

Not because I had failed abroad.

Not because the city had run out of things to teach me.

Because someone had written:

**If you are coming back, come back.**

So I would.
