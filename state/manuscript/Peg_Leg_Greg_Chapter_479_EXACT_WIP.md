# CHAPTER 479

## THE HOMECOMER

The fare home was six copper.

I paid it.

That was becoming a habit.

Not the six copper part.

Paying for the thing I wanted instead of demanding that every inconvenience prove my cleverness.

The Lakeward board clerk took my hold mark, checked my name, and said, "Six."

I gave him six copper.

Eighteen became twelve.

Ten stayed floor.

Two remained above it.

That was narrow.

It was also fine.

I was going home.

The cart left at first bell with no magical weather, no broken grounding post, no illegal freight, no glass bundles, and no artifact auction happening within conversational distance.

I considered this suspicious.

The driver did not.

He had gray hair, a green coat, and the settled expression of a man whose professional ambition was to arrive where the route slate said he would.

I respected him immediately.

The Carrow cart was covered but open at the sides. Two passengers slept before Lakeward had fully disappeared behind us. One woman shelled peas into a cloth bag for almost an hour. A boy across from me kept trying to balance a copper washer on his knuckles until his father finally took it away and said, "You have demonstrated gravity."

The boy said, "I can do it."

"You have demonstrated gravity six times."

"Seven."

"Thank you."

Different family.

Different problem.

Good.

I rested.

That was most of the first day.

The road did ordinary road things.

It narrowed.

It widened.

It became bad near a drainage cut.

We stopped once because a freight wagon ahead needed room to turn.

The driver swore at a milestone because someone had painted the west face blue.

Nobody knew why.

At midday I bought food with bits and did not calculate whether hunger had positive return on capital.

Growth.

My shoulder stayed quiet.

Right leg got stiff from sitting.

Hands appreciated not doing ten miles of work.

The residual limb had ordinary end-of-day pressure from the socket liner I wore for shape and protection, not from a trial limb.

No new skin problem.

No magic.

Fifty-nine.

Fifty-three.

One hundred thirty-nine.

The numbers remained boring.

I wanted boring.

By evening we stopped at a road house where the fare included floor space but not dinner.

Fine.

I ate.

Slept badly.

Someone near the door talked in his sleep about onions.

Not metaphorically.

He had strong opinions.

Morning came.

We moved again.

The second day felt shorter because Carrow began appearing before Carrow appeared.

That was how home worked.

First the road markers changed.

Then the carts.

Then people used names I knew without explaining them.

A produce wagon had Marrow Lane chalked across its side.

A courier complained about South Steps.

A woman getting off at a junction told the driver she could cut through Cooper Row if the paving crew had finally moved.

I knew all three places.

Not as map labels.

As distances in my body.

South Steps meant one route if tired and another if carrying something awkward.

Cooper Row meant uneven stones near the drain.

Marrow Lane meant the bakery two corners over sold out earlier than it claimed.

That was home too.

Accumulated inconvenience.

By late morning the city stopped being ahead of us and became around us.

Carrow.

I had expected something.

Not a revelation.

Maybe recognition.

Maybe the old strange feeling that I had crossed from temporary road-life back into the place where my current life actually happened.

Instead my first thought was:

That gutter still leaks.

Excellent.

The cart dropped us two districts from home.

I climbed down slowly, took my crutches from the driver, and adjusted bag.

Twelve copper.

Twenty-three Vale.

Bits unspecified.

One sealed Hark & Venn note somewhere in my room.

One girlfriend who had written that she missed me and then immediately prohibited emotional consequences.

I started home.

Carrow did not care.

A delivery man almost backed into me with cloth rolls.

A dog barked at one crutch and ignored the other.

Two theatre handbills had been pasted over an older soap advertisement.

A woman I recognized from somewhere nodded at me, then looked uncertain about why she recognized me.

I nodded back.

Partial Greg.

Good.

I passed the little paved triangle where Senna had once drawn me.

She was not there.

The bench was.

Somebody else sat on it eating an apple.

Also good.

Not every piece of my life needed to remain staged for my return.

Closer to home, the streets changed from familiar to specific.

The corner where Lyssa had once made me wait because she needed thread and came out with buttons instead.

The doorway where Cala had argued with a vegetable seller about whether bruised tomatoes were cheaper or merely sadder.

The wall I had leaned against the first time my right palm split from too much crutch pressure.

Nobody had put plaques up.

Civilization survived.

I reached our building around midday.

Then stopped outside like an idiot.

Window open.

Our window.

A strip of pale cloth hung over sill.

Not mine.

Voices upstairs.

One Lyssa.

One woman I did not recognize.

Work voice.

Not intimate.

Not angry.

Fast, material, occupied.

I could have gone up.

Obviously.

I lived there.

Instead I stood on street for ten seconds because I had been away long enough that entering felt like interrupting.

That was stupid.

Then the upstairs window opened wider.

Lyssa leaned out.

She looked down.

Saw me.

Her face changed.

Not dramatically.

First surprise.

Then relief.

Then annoyance arrived quickly enough to preserve structural integrity.

"You."

I looked up.

"Me."

"You said tomorrow."

"It is tomorrow."

"You said first bell."

"I left at first bell."

"That was not the complaint."

The woman inside said something I couldn't hear.

Lyssa looked back.

"One minute."

Then at me:

"Come upstairs."

Excellent.

Permission to enter my own home.

I went.

The stairs were still stairs.

I hated them with renewed affection.

By the time I reached landing, door was open.

Lyssa stood there with three pins in her mouth.

She took them out.

"Don't hug me."

I stopped.

She held up both hands.

Blue chalk on one.

Needle in the other.

"Customer."

"Right."

Then she stepped forward and hugged me anyway, carefully, one arm around shoulders and the other held out to side so she did not stab me.

I put one arm around her.

Crutch trapped awkwardly between us.

Perfect.

She smelled like soap, cloth dust, and home.

I closed my eyes.

She said into my shoulder, "Do not."

"I didn't do anything."

"You were about to."

"I was breathing."

"Dramatically."

"That seems medically necessary."

She let go first.

Looked at me.

Really looked.

Face.

Shoulder.

Coat.

Crutches.

Hands.

Bag.

The quick inventory she would deny was an inventory.

"You look tired."

"I traveled."

"You look less terrible than last time."

"Thank you."

"Not compliment."

"I took it anyway."

From inside, the customer said, "Can I move?"

Lyssa turned.

"No."

"Still?"

"Especially now."

I looked past her.

A woman stood on our little fitting platform wearing a half-pinned brown overskirt and the expression of someone who had been warned repeatedly about movement and was building a legal case against the restriction.

Lyssa pointed at the chair.

"Sit."

I sat.

Apparently home had hierarchy.

The customer looked at me.

"You're Greg?"

I looked at Lyssa.

Lyssa said, "Unfortunately."

Good.

I said, "I've been called worse."

Customer said, "She talks about you."

Lyssa, without looking up: "No, I complain about him. Different service."

The woman nodded like this distinction made sense.

Lyssa went back to pinning hem.

And that was my homecoming.

I sat in my chair while my girlfriend worked on somebody else's skirt.

No music.

No reunion audience.

No immediate sex.

No emotional confession.

She had a customer.

I had been gone.

Both facts survived contact.

The room looked almost exactly the same.

Which meant it was completely different.

The small chair Cala borrowed had returned beside wall, one leg still shorter.

My cracked cup sat high on shelf.

Actually high.

Lyssa had not exaggerated.

My other shirt lay folded on pillow with one missing button beside it in a tiny dish.

Papers were stacked under window.

A sealed note sat on top.

HARK & VENN.

There.

I looked at it.

Then away.

Lyssa saw anyway.

"Don't."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

"I looked."

"You just got here."

"Correct."

"Then be here."

That stopped me.

Not because it was profound.

Because she said it while holding three pins between lips and trying to make a brown skirt stop fighting gravity.

I leaned back.

"Fine."

Customer said, "Is this where you tell him you missed him?"

Lyssa looked at her.

"No."

The woman smiled.

"I would."

"Then write him."

"I don't know him."

"Excellent boundary."

I laughed.

Customer moved.

Lyssa slapped side of her own thigh.

"Stop moving."

"Sorry."

"You said sorry last time."

"I mean it every time."

"That is not helping."

The fitting took another twenty minutes.

I watched.

Not like inspector.

Like man sitting in his own room while someone else's work happened.

Lyssa had changed something while I was gone.

A narrow hook rail had been screwed under shelf.

Three bundles of thread hung there.

The table had shifted half a foot toward window.

A second lamp stood near fitting platform.

Not dramatic.

Work accumulating.

The room adapting to her because she used it more than I did.

Good.

The customer finally escaped with instructions I did not understand and a warning not to pull one seam until tomorrow.

At door she looked at me.

"Welcome home."

"Thank you."

Lyssa said, "Leave."

The woman laughed and left.

Door shut.

Silence.

Then Lyssa turned.

No pins.

No needle.

No customer.

I stood.

She crossed room fast and hit me in chest with both hands.

Not hard.

"Your notes are terrible."

"I improved."

"You sent me a letter from Lakeward that may arrive after you."

"That is technically useful location information."

She stared.

I smiled.

She kissed me.

Hard enough that one crutch slipped half an inch.

I caught it.

She pulled back.

"Careful."

"Mixed signals."

"Sit down."

"I just sat for two days."

"Then stand responsibly."

She kissed me again.

Slower.

My hand found her waist.

Hers went behind my neck.

For a little while there was no economy.

No leverage.

No road.

No Vale.

No artifacts.

No protocol.

Just Lyssa warm against me and the small stupid relief of touching someone who had been paper yesterday.

She broke kiss first.

"You smell like road house."

"I used washhouse."

"Then road house fought back."

"I had a roommate with onion dreams."

She paused.

"What?"

"I don't know."

"Did you ask?"

"He was asleep."

"That has not stopped you before."

Rude.

Accurate.

She touched side of my face.

Then softer:

"I missed you."

"I know."

"You don't get to say that like a victory."

"I missed you too."

"I know."

There.

Enough.

She looked down at bag.

"Did you bring me anything?"

I froze.

She watched my face.

Then started laughing.

"Oh, no."

"I brought thread."

"For me?"

"No."

Worse.

"What is wrong with you?"

"My seam is loose."

She laughed harder.

I took the little thread bundle out.

She held out hand.

I gave it to her.

She inspected it.

"This is bad thread."

"It was ordinary repair thread."

"It is ordinary bad repair thread."

"I paid bits."

"You overpaid."

"How much?"

"I don't know. I wasn't there."

"Then your appraisal lacks evidence."

She threw thread at me.

I caught it against chest.

"Fix your own seam."

"There it is."

"What?"

"Home."

She almost smiled.

Then did.

"Idiot."

I looked at high shelf.

"My cup."

"Still cracked."

"Historical significance?"

"Trash with tenure."

"Strong classification."

"You are not drinking from it."

"We'll see."

"We will not."

I crutched over to shelf.

Could not reach comfortably without leaning.

Lyssa watched.

"Do not fall for cup."

"I'm evaluating access."

"Evaluate from floor."

I left it.

Growth.

She began clearing pins from table.

I took off coat.

Bag down.

Crutches against wall in familiar angle.

The room accepted my stuff without ceremony.

That might have been best part.

I did not need to decide where bag belonged.

I knew.

Lyssa knew.

There was already a hook.

She said, "Eat?"

"Yes."

"I have bread. And lentils."

"Marry me."

"No."

"Harsh."

"You say that whenever food is already cooked."

"Pattern recognition."

She put pot on low heat.

I sat again.

The sealed Hark & Venn note remained on papers.

I could see it from chair.

White fold.

Workshop mark.

My name.

Lyssa followed my eyes.

"You can open it."

"I know."

"But?"

I looked at pot.

At her.

At cup.

At shirt on pillow.

At one-short-leg chair.

At the little new lamp.

At my bag back on hook.

"Later."

She nodded.

No praise.

Good.

We ate.

She told me the green-thread story with more detail than letter had included and got angry again at exact same point.

Apparently one customer had claimed "green is green" while holding two shades against daylight for ten minutes.

Lyssa considered this a personal attack on language.

I told her about East Fen.

Not the whole transaction ledger.

Orin's yard.

Reed-glass with ugly color that passed because color didn't matter.

She liked that part.

"See? Sometimes people actually mean they don't care which color."

"That was glass."

"Still color."

"Different profession."

I told her about stillhook.

She listened while tearing bread.

When I finished with eleven-copper sale, she said, "Good."

I frowned.

"You don't know what it did."

"You didn't spend eleven."

"I couldn't safely."

"Exactly."

"I wanted it."

"I gathered."

"It might have been very useful."

"It is currently very useful to somebody else."

Cruel.

I loved her.

Then I told her about High Cinder road ground.

She asked if I had touched anything.

"No."

"Good."

"You and Hessa would get along."

"I don't want that curse."

Fair.

I did not tell her about illegal freight.

Not yet.

Not because I planned to lie forever.

Because warm lentils after returning home did not need a customs confession stapled to them.

Some conversations deserved chosen timing.

After food, she took my coat to inspect loose seam.

I said, "That's shirt."

"This one is also loose."

"What?"

"Here."

She showed me a thread near pocket.

I stared.

"Road is predatory."

"Cloth experiences use."

"Less poetic."

"More true."

She handed coat back.

"Tomorrow."

I nodded.

Tomorrow.

That word felt good.

Not because anything was solved.

Because there was one.

I checked body before bed mostly from habit.

Hands fine.

Shoulder better.

Residual limb ordinary.

Right leg stiff from travel but settling.

No skin break.

No magic.

Fifty-nine.

Fifty-three.

One hundred thirty-nine.

Cash twelve copper after fare.

Vale twenty-three.

Bits unspecified.

Ten floor.

Two flexible copper.

No panic.

No debt payment.

No artifact.

No income.

Home.

The Hark & Venn note stayed sealed on papers.

I moved it once so cup of water would not spill on it.

That was all.

Lyssa got into bed beside me and immediately stole more blanket than physics allowed.

I said, "I was gone for over a week."

"And?"

"I thought absence might reset territorial claims."

"No."

"Unreasonable."

"You can leave again."

I pulled blanket back.

She pulled harder.

We negotiated through violence.

Eventually I had enough.

Barely.

She turned away from me.

I put arm around her.

She backed into me without comment.

Outside, someone dropped something metal in alley and swore.

A cart passed late.

Somewhere farther away, somebody laughed too loudly.

Carrow.

No fanfare.

No quest completion.

No return reward.

Just the city continuing around a room that had kept my cup, my shirt, my papers, and one person who was irritated I had been difficult to locate.

I had spent six copper to come home.

Worth it.
