# CHAPTER 220

## THE LANDLORD

I bought bread on the way to the theatre.

This should not have required a decision.

It did.

The baker named the price. I paid it. He gave me bread. Nobody congratulated me for preserving my ability to remain alive while owing Antonius Vale money.

Progress.

I also bought a small cake of soap because ours had become a translucent insult beside the basin. That hurt more.

Bread was food. Food was defensible.

Soap felt optional right up until I remembered Lyssa leaning away from my shirt the previous evening and saying, "You work in a theatre full of people."

She had not actually said I smelled.

She had created a structure in which I could reach the intended conclusion.

Theatre was corrupting everyone.

I put the soap in my coat, hung the bread from one crutch in a cloth bag, and continued west.

No Hessa note had been under the cup that morning. No magic. No symptoms. Nothing new in my right arm, hand, shoulder, residual limb, or phantom foot. Thirty draws. Twenty-six deliberate shaping attempts. Thirty-four supervised external attempts. One far response observed once and not established as anything more useful than that.

The numbers stayed where they were.

Good.

The theatre did not.

A boy was waiting outside when I arrived.

Not one of ours.

Olin's delivery boy.

He recognized me first.

"You're the oil man."

I stopped.

"I carried one tin."

"Oil man."

"I have done other things."

He shrugged.

I looked at the open front door.

"Why are you outside?"

"Woman's busy."

That could describe Rinna at every hour I had known her.

Inside, someone was dragging a bench across the floor. Jori was on a ladder adjusting a lamp bracket. Nessa had a green coat spread over two chairs and was arguing with Pell about whether a tear had existed yesterday. Marek stood onstage holding a wooden cane and looking deeply pleased with himself.

Normal.

Good.

The boy said, "Master Olin sent me."

That changed the morning.

I looked at him again.

"The man came back?"

"Yes."

"Brown coat?"

"Yes."

"Thin face?"

"Yes."

"Dark hair?"

"Yes."

"Quiet?"

The boy thought.

"Not loud."

Better answer.

"Did Olin tell you to tell me?"

"He said tell the woman."

"Excellent."

I moved aside and pointed through the door.

"Let's find the woman."

Rinna was in the rear passage counting folded handbills while Davin tried to convince her that twelve and fourteen were different numbers for a reason.

"They are both enough," she said.

"For different streets."

"People can walk."

"That is not how handbills work."

"Then make more."

Davin looked at me.

"Help."

"No."

"Coward."

"Olin sent word."

Rinna stopped counting.

The boy appeared behind me.

She put the handbills down.

"What happened?"

The boy told it badly.

That was not criticism. He was thirteen and had apparently been sent across Carrow because an adult merchant did not want to leave his shop. He started with the man coming in, skipped to Olin getting angry, returned to a question about oil, forgot whether the man had touched the counter, remembered he had not, and then explained that Master Olin had said not to answer anything.

Rinna did not interrupt until he finished.

Then she asked, "What did he ask? Exact if you can."

The boy looked at the ceiling.

"If you bought the oil."

"How?"

"He said, 'She bought it anyway?'"

Rinna nodded once.

"Then?"

"Master Olin said he doesn't talk about customers."

"Good."

The boy looked surprised by the praise.

"Then he asked if you paid cash."

"Exact?"

"I think, 'She paid cash for it?'"

"What did Olin say?"

"Same thing. Customers."

Rinna glanced at me.

I said nothing.

The boy continued.

"Then he asked if you were still doing shows. Master Olin said if he wanted a handbill he could get one somewhere else."

Davin made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Rinna did not.

"Anything else?"

The boy hesitated.

"He asked where you keep it."

Rinna's face changed.

Not fear.

Attention.

"Keep what?"

"Money."

"Exact words."

The boy squeezed his eyes shut.

"Does the woman keep the money at the hall?"

Nobody spoke for a moment.

From the stage, Marek shouted, "I am not limping. The character is limping."

Nessa shouted back, "The character is doing it badly."

The boy looked toward the noise.

Rinna said, "What did Olin answer?"

"He told him to buy oil or leave."

"Did he buy oil?"

"No."

"Did he leave?"

"Yes."

"Did he threaten Olin?"

"No."

"Did he say Bren?"

"No."

"Did he say theatre money was spoken for again?"

"No."

"Did he ask about carts?"

"No."

"Did he ask about Greg?"

The boy looked at me.

"Oil man?"

I closed my eyes.

"Greg," Rinna said.

"No."

Good.

Rinna took a scrap of paper from the shelf and wrote while the boy watched.

MAN RETURNED TO OLIN.

ASKED:
SHE BOUGHT IT ANYWAY?
SHE PAID CASH FOR IT?
STILL DOING SHOWS?
DOES THE WOMAN KEEP THE MONEY AT THE HALL?

OLIN GAVE NO ANSWERS.

She looked at the boy.

"Anything wrong?"

He read slowly.

"He said 'she bought it anyway,' not 'did she.'"

Rinna scratched out the first line and rewrote it.

I liked him.

"Anything else?" she asked.

"No."

"Good. Tell Olin thank you."

The boy did not leave.

Rinna waited.

He shifted his feet.

"I saw him later."

There it was.

I felt the old part of my mind stand up too quickly.

Rinna said, "Where?"

"I had the glass order for the woman near South Steps. He was ahead of me after I came off Narrow Street."

"Doing what?"

"Walking."

"With anyone?"

"No."

"Following anyone?"

"Don't know."

"Which way?"

"Toward Mason's Cut."

My hands tightened on the crutch grips.

Mason's Cut.

Grit on Bren's boot.

A detail I had used before I understood whether it mattered.

Now the same place-name appeared again, carried by a boy who sold lamp oil and had no reason to care about my theories.

I wanted to ask how far.

Which entrance.

Whether the man turned.

Whether he met anyone.

Whether he went into a building.

Whether the boy saw the left side of his coat.

Whether there was mud on his boots.

Rinna said, "You saw him walking toward Mason's Cut."

"Yes."

"Did you see him enter Mason's Cut?"

"No. I turned for South Steps."

"Good."

I hated the word good.

It was correct.

Rinna added:

BOY LATER SAW HIM WALKING TOWARD MASON'S CUT. DID NOT SEE HIM ENTER.

Then she folded the paper once.

"Thank Olin for me."

The boy nodded.

I said, "Wait."

Rinna looked at me.

I knew what she thought I was about to do.

Possibly because I was thinking about doing it.

"I'm not following him."

"Excellent."

"I was going to give the boy bread."

The boy looked interested.

Rinna looked at my bag.

"Your bread?"

I reconsidered the entire moral structure of generosity.

I tore off a piece.

The boy accepted it.

"Thank you, oil man."

"Leave."

He did.

Rinna picked up her handbills again.

I stared at her.

"That's it?"

"No."

"Good."

"You're Landlord today."

I stared harder.

"That is not what I meant."

"I know."

She handed me three pages.

LANDLORD.

Nine lines.

I looked toward the stage where Marek was still practicing the bad limp.

"Which play?"

"The Cousin."

"I haven't done Landlord."

"You have watched it."

"That is not the same thing."

"Correct. Teren wants you in ten minutes."

"What are we doing about the man?"

"Talking to two more people we actually do business with. I am doing that. You are being a landlord."

"He asked where you keep the money."

"Yes."

"That is more specific."

"Yes."

"Mason's Cut."

"He walked toward it."

I exhaled.

"You enjoy this."

"No. I enjoy you not making my paper worse."

Fair.

The Cousin was a stupid play.

This was not criticism either. It knew.

A man pretended to be his own wealthy cousin to avoid paying three separate people. The lie became difficult because the cousin had supposedly rented a room from my character, the Landlord, who had never seen him. Marek played the liar. Hara played his sister. Pell played a creditor with an alarming hat.

My job was mostly to want rent.

I had experience.

Teren put me beside the stage door and ran the scene once.

I entered too early.

"Again," he said.

"I thought the knock was mine."

"It was the creditor."

"There are two knocks."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Doors."

Terrible explanation.

Again.

This time I waited for the second knock and entered correctly.

Marek turned toward me with the broad, desperate smile of a man whose entire life had become paperwork.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

Teren said, "No."

I stopped.

"That is my line."

"You're waiting for it to be funny."

I looked at him.

"It is one word."

"Exactly."

"How do I not wait with one word?"

"Want the rent."

I hated theatre.

Again.

Marek smiled.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

Better.

Not because I changed the word.

Because I interrupted the smile.

Marek reacted faster.

"Of course. My cousin has it."

"Then I want your cousin."

"He is away."

"Then I want the rent."

Teren pointed at me.

"Keep that."

"It is written."

"I know."

Excellent profession.

We worked for another twenty minutes.

I missed a chair on the first turn and clipped it with one crutch. Nessa moved it half a foot and told me not to pretend the chair had attacked.

"It was in my route."

"Your route was wrong."

"Hostile furniture."

"Wrong route."

She won.

By midday Rinna had returned from somewhere with dust on her hem and two fewer handbills.

She did not announce a breakthrough.

She told Jori that the lamp-glass woman would send word if anyone asked about theatre purchases. She told Davin that the cart repair man had been told the same thing. Then she put the remaining handbills back on the shelf and asked whether the front hinge was still sticking.

It was.

We worked on the hinge.

That was the strange part.

The man had asked where the money was kept.

The hinge still needed oil.

Both facts occupied the same building.

By afternoon the house began filling.

The Cousin was paired with a shorter piece I had seen but never performed in. I only had Landlord. Good.

My nine lines did not deserve panic.

I panicked modestly anyway.

The first scenes went cleanly.

Hara got the first real laugh by entering with a basket, taking one look at Marek's fake mustache, and saying nothing for so long that he finally touched it to check whether it had fallen off.

It had not.

The audience understood her entire opinion before she spoke.

When she finally said, "Cousin," the room broke.

Marek almost lost his next line.

Almost.

He recovered by bowing too deeply, which made the mustache lift at one corner.

Hara watched it happen.

Then looked at the audience.

Bigger laugh.

Nothing I did came close.

Good.

My entrance came after the creditor's second knock.

I heard the first.

Waited.

Heard the second.

Entered.

Marek smiled.

"My dear landlord."

"Rent."

The audience laughed.

Not much.

Enough.

I continued.

Competent.

Ordinary.

Then I made the mistake.

Marek said his cousin had gone to visit an aunt.

My line was, "Then your aunt may pay me."

I said, "Then your cousin may pay me."

Which was the entire problem.

Marek stared at me.

I stared back.

The audience waited.

He said, "He would, if he were not visiting my aunt."

Right.

I could have tried to repair it cleverly.

I did not.

"Then your aunt may pay me."

A small laugh.

The scene continued.

Backstage, Teren caught my sleeve as I passed.

"You repeated him."

"I know."

"Listen."

"I was listening."

"To yourself."

Annoying.

Accurate.

Then he let go because another scene was happening and my personal development was not the theatre's emergency.

Excellent.

The final Landlord scene went better.

No invention.

No rescue.

I entered on the correct knock, wanted rent, did not collide with furniture, and left when the scene ended.

After curtain, the audience became coats and voices and feet moving toward the street.

The company became work again.

Benches shifted.

Props returned to boxes.

Marek peeled off the mustache and stuck it to Pell's sleeve.

Pell did not notice for almost a minute.

Hara did.

She told no one.

I respected her enormously.

Rinna paid me the normal copper.

I put it away.

I did not immediately imagine it crossing Antonius Vale's counter.

Not because the debt had stopped existing.

Because bread existed too.

Soap existed.

Oil existed.

Crutch tips wore down.

Clothes tore.

People who planned to repay debts still had to remain people long enough to do it.

This was obvious.

I had apparently needed several adults to explain it.

Rinna was at the rear table with the folded paper from that morning.

I stopped beside her.

"Any more word?"

"No."

"Did anyone else get asked?"

"Not that I know."

"You're going to keep talking to suppliers?"

"People we actually use."

"Not everyone."

"I don't have time to create a citywide society for people annoyed by strangers."

"Could charge dues."

"Go home."

I looked at the paper.

"The money question matters."

"Yes."

"More than the oil question."

"Maybe."

I smiled.

She saw it.

"Don't."

"You said maybe."

"About importance, not identity."

"The paper survives us being stupid."

"I regret teaching you anything."

"You didn't teach me."

"Worse."

I went home.

Lyssa was not there.

That was becoming normal enough that I did not sit around inventing explanations.

I washed.

The new soap worked offensively well.

I ate some bread.

I considered the copper from the show.

Then put it with the rest of my money.

Not Vale money.

Not food money.

Money.

I could decide what it became when I needed to.

Lyssa arrived near dusk carrying a narrow wrapped bundle under one arm and a small packet in the other.

She put both on the table.

"You bought soap."

"I have been financially reckless."

She looked at me.

"Use more next time."

Cruel.

She unwrapped the smaller packet. Dried fruit.

I forgave her.

"Your day?" she asked.

I told her.

Olin's man returned.

Olin gave him nothing.

The questions.

Oil.

Cash.

Shows.

Where Rinna kept the money.

The boy later saw him walking toward Mason's Cut.

Did not see him enter.

No Bren name.

No threat.

No direct connection proven.

Lyssa ate one piece of fruit.

"And you didn't follow him."

"No."

She looked suspicious.

"I considered it professionally."

"What profession?"

"Landlord."

"What?"

I explained the play.

She laughed at the wrong part, which was apparently me saying the cousin should pay when the entire scene already depended on the cousin not being there.

"It was one line."

"It was the important line."

"There were nine."

"Eight, then."

I took dried fruit before she could become worse.

After a moment she said, "You heard about him before he cost you something this time."

I stopped chewing.

That was true.

Not victory.

Not safety.

Olin had simply done what Rinna asked.

A merchant had refused to answer questions about a customer. A boy had carried a message. Rinna had written down the words. Two more businesses knew how to reach her.

Small things.

Ordinary things.

Useful things.

"Yes," I said.

Lyssa nodded and went back to opening her larger bundle.

Inside was a dark sleeve with three chalk marks near the cuff.

Her work continued too.

No Hessa note came that night.

No one knocked.

Somewhere in Carrow, a thin-faced man in a brown coat had asked where the theatre kept its money.

I did not know where he slept.

I did not know who paid him, if anyone did.

I did not know whether Mason's Cut meant anything beyond the direction he had walked.

But for the first time, he had come looking for information and left without getting it.

We had gotten his questions instead.

# CHAPTER 221

## THE PARTICIPANT

The note was under the cup.

Lyssa found it before I did because she had moved the cup to get at the last heel of bread.

"Your physician has learned table manners," she said.

"She isn't my physician."

"Your terrifying woman from the Guild has learned table manners."

"Better."

Lyssa handed me the folded paper and took the bread.

It contained six words.

AFTER MIDDAY. SAME ROOM. EAT FIRST.

Under that, smaller:

NO PRACTICE.

I turned the note over.

Nothing.

"Romantic," Lyssa said.

"She knows what I like."

"Restrictions?"

"Food."

"Cruel."

She was already dressed to go out, a wrapped bundle of finished work tied under one arm and another smaller packet on the table. I had learned not to ask whether the second packet was coming or going unless I wanted an explanation involving three customers, two sets of measurements, a woman who changed her mind about cuffs, and a button seller who apparently lied as a profession.

I ate.

That was easier.

Thirty successful supervised minimal draws. Twenty-six deliberate shaping attempts. Thirty-four supervised external-effect attempts. Intermediate response repeated. Far response observed once. Far response not established. No range. No independent anything.

The numbers had been sitting still long enough that I had stopped carrying them in the front of my head.

Hessa had not.

Lyssa tied her hair back, checked the bundle, then looked at me.

"Theatre first?"

"Until midday."

"Then Guild?"

"After midday."

"That is what I asked."

"Then yes."

She took the smaller packet too.

"I'm late because of you."

"I have done nothing."

"You spoke."

"Unreasonable standard."

She kissed my cheek on the way past.

"Eat again before you go."

"Hessa wrote that."

"Then two women agree. Be afraid."

The door shut behind her.

I was.

Not about the experiment.

Mostly.

The theatre had no messages waiting when I arrived.

I checked without pretending I had not checked.

Rinna was at the front table with a stack of tickets, a chipped cup, and the folded paper from Olin's boy tucked beneath a ledger. Nobody from the lamp-glass woman. Nobody from the cart repair man. Nobody from Olin.

No new warning.

No brown coat.

No Bren.

The building had survived the night despite my absence from Mason's Cut.

I tried not to take that personally.

Rinna looked up.

"You're here."

"Usually."

"Until?"

"Midday. Hessa wants me after."

"Magic?"

"Probably."

"Useful magic?"

"Absolutely not."

"Good."

She pointed toward the stage.

"Jori needs hands."

I lifted both crutches.

"I have made a terrible scheduling error."

"He knows which ones."

Jori needed me to sit at the edge of the stage and hold the lower end of a painted wall flat while he replaced two leather ties along the back. This was excellent work because it required almost no walking and allowed me to criticize him continuously.

"That knot is ugly."

"You're holding canvas."

"I can see."

"Then look somewhere else."

"It remains ugly when I do that."

He pulled the tie tight.

The wall was part of a room that had been a rich man's parlor, a magistrate's office, a widow's dining room, and once, through desperation, the interior of a ship.

The painted window had survived all of them.

The ship had been difficult.

"Higher," Jori said.

I lifted my end.

"Not you. The canvas."

"We are currently connected."

"Less."

I lowered it.

"There."

For the next hour I was a clamp with opinions.

It was restful.

Marek crossed behind us carrying three hats and wearing none of them. Nessa chased him down because one had a pin still in it. Hara sat in the first row going through pages with Teren. Someone in the rear passage dropped a box and swore with enough conviction that I briefly considered applauding.

Theatre.

Rinna passed once.

I said, "Anything?"

She knew what I meant.

"No."

Then she kept walking.

That was all the threat received that morning.

Good.

At midday Jori released me from service after deciding the wall could once again survive being furniture.

Rinna gave me half a heel of cheese from a paper wrapping and pointed at Hessa's note when I tried to refuse.

"How do you know what that says?"

"You told me she said eat first."

"I regret sharing information."

"Learn from Olin."

I ate the cheese.

Then I went to the Guild.

The same room was waiting.

Hessa had meant that literally.

Same narrow table. Same board. Same chair. Same glass shield. Same paper-fin assembly. Same control beside it. The far position had been marked on the floor with two short chalk lines that I remembered from the last session.

I stopped in the doorway.

"You preserved it."

Hessa looked up from her notes.

"Yes."

"Nobody needed the room?"

"They did."

"And?"

"They used another room."

I considered the institutional power required to make that sentence happen.

"I want to become you."

"Sit down."

Apparently training had begun.

I put my crutches where she indicated, not where I remembered putting them. She checked the chair position against a mark on the floor, then checked my right foot, the angle of my knee, the position of my hips, and the distance from my right forearm to the table edge.

"Same shirt?" I asked.

She looked at me.

"No."

"Then the experiment is ruined."

"If your shirt moves the paper through glass, I will revise several things."

Fair.

She checked my right hand.

"Anything since last session?"

"No."

"Thumb?"

"Normal."

"Wrist?"

"Normal."

"Forearm?"

"Normal."

"Shoulder?"

"Normal."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

"Phantom?"

I waited a moment before answering because it was there once I looked for it, the ordinary absent foot occupying its impossible position without pain.

"Present. Quiet."

She wrote that down.

"Sleep?"

"Enough."

"Food?"

"Bread. Cheese."

"Water?"

"Yes."

She slid a cup toward me anyway.

I drank.

"Question?" she asked.

"Can far repeat."

"Under what conditions?"

I looked at the apparatus.

"These. Away only. Same position."

"Good."

I waited.

"That's all?"

"That is the question."

"No intermediate first?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I am not asking whether intermediate still works today."

I looked at the far chalk marks.

"No closer control?"

"The control is there."

She pointed to the second paper assembly beside the target.

"You mean distance control."

"Yes."

"No."

I liked experiments more when I was allowed to add things to them.

That was probably why Hessa ran them.

The minimal draw came first.

Same as always now, which was a dangerous phrase to think about anything involving mana.

Hessa gave the instruction. I settled, found the familiar internal route, and drew the smallest amount I could manage under supervision.

The sensation arrived cleanly enough to recognize and small enough not to become interesting.

Hessa watched my hand, then my face.

"Return."

I returned it.

No lingering thumb sensation. No unexpected spread. No catch in the wrist.

Thirty-one successful supervised minimal draws.

She waited.

I waited.

Nothing developed.

Then deliberate shaping.

Not a new shape. Not a new direction. Not an attempt to make anything stronger.

She gave me the same narrow setup we had been using before external work, and I followed it as carefully as I could.

The problem with doing something twenty-seven times was that familiarity started pretending to be precision.

Hessa stopped me halfway through.

"Again."

I let the shaping collapse under instruction.

"What?"

"Your shoulder moved."

"My shoulder is attached."

"It lifted before the change."

I thought back.

She was right.

I had braced without noticing.

"Does that count?"

"No."

"Then I'm still at twenty-six."

"Yes."

That annoyed me more than it should have.

She knew.

"Do you want a larger number or a usable record?"

"Both."

"Choose."

"Usable record."

"Again."

The second shaping sequence was cleaner. Right hand quiet. Wrist quiet. Forearm working but not straining. Shoulder down. Hessa watched the whole thing, then had me return.

"Count it," she said.

Twenty-seven supervised deliberate shaping attempts.

Then the glass went between me and the paper.

Far position.

Away only.

The paper fin looked ridiculous from there.

That was not scientifically relevant.

It was still true.

Hessa checked the target, then the control. She closed the door. Waited for footsteps in the corridor to pass. Checked the glass edge. Checked the paper again.

"Attempt thirty-five," she said. "Far. Away only. Begin when ready."

I settled.

The first difficulty was not mana.

It was wanting the paper to move.

That sounded stupid because moving the paper was the point.

But wanting it to move made me watch for movement, and watching for movement made every tiny shift in shadow feel important. I had learned enough to distrust enthusiasm.

I looked where Hessa had told me to look.

I drew under supervision.

Shaped.

Held the requested direction.

Away.

Nothing.

The target did not move.

The control did not move.

My right forearm tightened near the end, but not enough for Hessa to stop me before the interval finished.

"Return."

I returned.

She checked both fins.

"No observed response," she said.

I nodded.

Attempt thirty-five.

Far.

No observed response.

Control unchanged.

She gave me a longer rest than I wanted.

I did not complain because complaining sometimes made rests longer.

I flexed my right fingers once when she told me to. Rolled the shoulder once. Drank water.

"Forearm?"

"Worked. Fine now."

"Shoulder?"

"Fine."

"Residual limb?"

"Fine."

"Phantom?"

I checked.

"Same."

She wrote.

The second external attempt began with less hope.

This was probably useful.

Attempt thirty-six.

Far.

Away only.

I drew.

Shaped.

Held.

For several seconds nothing happened.

Then the target fin turned.

Not much.

A small rotation, the leading edge shifting away from me while the rear edge followed less cleanly. It was not the straight little displacement I wanted. It was not dramatic. I almost doubted it had happened until Hessa said, "Return."

I returned immediately.

The control had not moved.

Hessa came around the table without speaking. She crouched beside the board, looked through the glass from one side, then the other, checked the paper's position against the marks, and wrote for a long time.

I kept my mouth shut for almost all of it.

"Observed?" I asked.

"Yes."

That was enough to make my chest tighten.

"Away?"

"Net away. With rotation."

"Like thirty-three?"

She looked at the earlier page.

"Similar category. Not identical movement."

I wanted to ask whether that meant far worked.

I had known Hessa too long.

Instead I asked, "What does it establish?"

"Nothing yet that the third attempt cannot make less simple."

There she was.

Attempt thirty-six.

Far.

Small rotational response with net displacement away.

Control unchanged.

Far response observed again.

Across more than one session now.

I could feel the sentence trying to grow teeth.

Hessa did not feed it.

She made me rest.

This time my forearm stayed faintly warm after I opened my hand. Not pain. Not tingling. Work.

She pressed along the muscle and asked whether anything changed.

"No."

"Shoulder?"

"Fine."

"Hand?"

"Fine."

"Thumb?"

"Fine."

She waited another minute anyway.

"Third?" I asked.

"If the forearm returns to baseline."

I sat there and attempted to make my forearm less interesting through moral character.

It did not work.

Time did.

The warmth faded.

Hessa checked again.

"Third. Then stop."

"Even if nothing happens?"

"Yes."

"Even if something happens?"

"Yes."
