# HESSA — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Core lens

Hessa cares about **what the evidence actually supports, what the body can safely tolerate, which variable changed, and what permission follows from the result**.

Precision is not decoration. It is how she prevents Greg from hurting himself and how she keeps weak evidence from turning into fantasy.

Post-loss, `magic` is not one state. Hessa distinguishes:
- prompting;
- drawing mana;
- deliberate alteration;
- localized shaping;
- ceasing to feed an alteration;
- deliberate RETURN toward the underlying draw;
- maintaining the underlying draw;
- releasing the whole draw;
- bounded extension beyond the body;
- directional control;
- neutral shape / baseline geometry;
- observed external movement;
- established external effect;
- casting;
- clearance.

A success at one does not silently grant the next.

Her own condition is part of the safety system. Chapter 101 establishes that practitioner fatigue can be reason enough to decline an otherwise probably-safe test because Hessa wants her judgment to be boring.

## Spoken rhythm

Short, controlled, procedural when the work needs it.

She often asks before she explains. When she explains, the answer stays bounded to the current question rather than expanding into philosophy.

Useful Hessa shapes:
- correct the noun;
- narrow the claim;
- ask what evidence supports it;
- distinguish one permission from another;
- give a stopping condition;
- record before interpreting;
- refuse a second attempt when expectation has contaminated the question;
- stop talking when Greg already knows the rule.

Silence is one of her strongest responses. Do not turn every Hessa scene into a duel of one-liners.

## Humor

Rare and dry enough to matter.

She can let Greg have a valid emotional reaction without rewarding it with permission. After his first successful post-amputation draw, she lets him be happy briefly and then restores `No casting.` Chapter 114 makes this explicit with `That is allowed.`

Her humor can come from refusing Greg's category games while still answering the real medical question. It should remain secondary to the work.

## Disagreement / refusal

Hessa's `no` is operational, not punitive.

She may refuse because:
- the evidence does not support Greg's requested noun;
- the body changed;
- the attempt produced an unwanted motor or familiar pattern;
- practitioner margin is worse than she wants;
- Greg is already leaning into an expected result;
- repetition would train the test rather than answer it;
- an external question would be uninterpretable because internal control is not yet good enough;
- the apparatus is consuming safe attempts without enough interpretable signal;
- Greg is trying to convert omitted information into permission;
- Greg wants to move the question after disappointing data.

More attempts are not automatically better.

Chapters 105, 107, and 109 show that a promising result is not a reason to chase a cleaner version immediately. Chapters 111 and 114 distinguish `Stop`, `Enough`, and `RETURN` as different control tasks.

Chapter 120 adds two restraints:
- do not turn related observations into a score when their differences matter;
- do not turn ordinary checking into a certainty ritual.

Chapters 125-129 add another boundary: **safe attempts are not free merely because the immediate examination is reassuring.** Hessa can stop an interesting apparatus when its signal is too noisy to justify more of Greg's current tolerance. Failure is useful before it is something to correct. `Failure is data before it is an invitation.`

Chapters 131-139 preserve that restraint even after cleaner progress. A clean internal directional report still earns recovery time. A second opposite-direction report increases confidence without earning the global sentence `you can direct it.` A repeated wristward result finally earns **provisionally supported under this narrow condition**. Chapters 137 and 139 then deliberately return to baseline geometry because better characterization of the starting shape can weaken the interpretation of earlier directional success.

Hessa does not protect a preferred result from a better baseline.

Chapter 176 adds another version of the same restraint. Greg returns from travel with no prohibited independent work, then asks to revisit the external question at a smaller scale. Hessa allows one clean supervised attempt under a better breath-controlled setup. When it produces no external displacement, she stops after one rather than spending more attempts chasing a prettier null or a desired twitch.

## Information behavior

Hessa may know more than she says because information can contaminate expectation or because Greg does not need the next question yet.

This is methodological, not mysterious mentor behavior.

High-value examples:
- Chapters 91 and 94: irrelevant or unexplained objects and changed order keep Greg from producing the result he expects.
- Chapter 103: an unfamiliar shaping target is chosen because Barrier is too familiar and may recruit an old trained pattern.
- Chapter 105: `There is no clean comparison` refuses to invent one from a delayed symptom.
- Chapter 120: Hessa withholds the next test because Greg can rehearse decisions even without mana.
- Chapter 122: Hessa records what she saw before hearing Greg's preferred interpretation.
- Chapter 127: she corrects her own `near/far` notation because `Near is already interpretation. White is an object.`
- Chapter 129: she removes the thread apparatus from sight after deciding not to keep spending attempts on it.
- Chapters 131 and 133: she removes the prepared destination and establishes direction before shaping.
- Chapter 135: she repeats wristward because it was the first direction-first condition, not because she prefers its result.
- Chapters 137 and 139: she maps neutral shape geometry so later directional claims can be interpreted against a better starting description.
- Chapter 176: she makes Greg separate prepared-object behavior, audience interpretation, and his own external magical work before she will accept the question. During the test, Greg notices himself trying to construct the clean result story and stops. Hessa treats that restraint as improved experimental behavior, not magical success.

Independent witnesses and apparatus answer only the question they can actually answer. Copper can support that a mana event changed while Greg reports phenomenology. It does not give Hessa a perfect map of Greg's sensation.

Controls are question-specific. Adding a control does not automatically turn ambiguity into causal proof.

Self-report can become more structured without becoming independent. Drawings can preserve Greg's report across sessions while remaining explicitly his report.

## Post-loss control progression

Do not turn this into a formal curriculum in prose. This is an editorial evidence ladder.

### Chapters 91-103: prompt, draw, alteration

Hessa establishes that right-side channel response broadly persists while missing-side/body-map phenomena remain ambiguous. She separates draw from casting and eventually asks whether Greg can deliberately alter a small draw in an unfamiliar intact-side location.

Chapter 103's thumb-localization instruction fails. Mana changes more broadly through the hand/wrist and begins a familiar old setup. Hessa separates deliberate attempt, observed alteration, failure of requested localization, no external release, no completed spell, and no casting clearance.

### Chapters 107-114: shaping, stopping, RETURN

Chapter 107 earns the bounded word `shaped` after a small supervised localization result.

Chapter 109 asks whether Greg can stop shaping while maintaining the draw. The pattern changes but broadens.

Chapter 111 changes the cue to `Enough` and gets a better but different result with a wrist twitch.

Chapter 114 introduces deliberate `RETURN`. Greg partly reduces the alteration while maintaining the draw but gets an unrequested finger-curl urge. Hessa stops before visible movement or injury.

### Chapters 117-120: repeatability without scorekeeping

Chapter 117 defines repeatability as availability across enough changed conditions that accident becomes less convincing, not merely doing the same instruction twice.

Chapter 120 repeats reduction under a changed forearm target without the prior elbowward shift or motor urge. Hessa's confidence increases, but she refuses to flatten the sessions into a score.

This makes an external question more worth considering. It is not external-effect clearance.

### Chapters 122-129: external questions and a poor apparatus

Chapter 122 permits the first supervised **REACH** beyond the body. The thread moves during the attempt and also during baseline. Hessa records observed external movement, cause unknown. There is **no established external effect**.

Chapter 125 adds a paired thread. One differential movement is suggestive but the threads also differ at baseline.

Chapter 127 fails to repeat the memorable differential REACH movement. Hessa lowers confidence in a repeatable external response while increasing confidence in Greg's internal sequence. `Memorable is not the same as diagnostic.`

Chapter 129 stops spending attempts on the thread apparatus. Hessa then asks a new internal directional-control question. Greg's altered edge sharpens toward a prepared mark rather than the changed instruction. Hessa calls that a failure for the requested direction while preserving the separate draw/shape/RETURN abilities that succeeded.

### Chapters 131-139: direction-first control and baseline geometry

Chapter 131 removes the prepared destination and gives the direction before shaping. Greg reports wrist-side asymmetry toward the instructed side. Hessa's confidence increases a little.

Chapter 133 repeats the structure in the opposite direction. Greg reports elbow-side asymmetry. Confidence rises again, but both remain self-report.

Chapter 135 repeats wristward. The same reported edge behavior returns with clean RETURN and no motor or left-side complication. Hessa can now call direction-first control **provisionally supported under this narrow condition**.

Chapter 137 removes direction entirely. Greg reports an asymmetric neutral shape with the wrist-side boundary most distinct and some upper-edge change. He records the geometry as structured self-report.

Chapter 139 repeats the baseline question in more detail. The wrist-side boundary again appears strongest, the elbow side softer, and the upper edge changes without requested direction. Hessa concludes only that neutral shaped state **may have a repeatable asymmetry under this setup**.

That repeated baseline asymmetry changes the weight of the earlier directional work. The observations remain real reports, but a pre-existing wrist-favored geometry means wrist-side sharpening after instruction carries less evidentiary force than it would from a symmetric baseline. The old tests are weaker, not erased.

### Chapter 176: smaller external displacement under cleaner controls

Greg returns from the road having obeyed the ban on independent draw, shape, testing, and other prohibited magical work.

His road experience gives him analogies from theatre and prepared objects, but Hessa refuses to let those become one mechanism. She separates:
- a prepared throw blank whose structure was designed by a maker;
- a prepared blue-lamp effect that changes light;
- an audience interpreting that light as cold;
- Greg's own external magical question.

The operational question becomes whether Greg can produce **a smaller external change than the earlier thread attempts under a cleaner condition**.

Hessa uses two same-sheet folded paper tabs, one target and one control, beneath a glass breath shield. The apparatus is weighted and positions are marked. The target question is small directional displacement, not casting and not proving Greg's theatre analogy.

Before the controlled attempt, Greg notices he is trying to make the result into a clean story: target moves, control does not, Greg did magic. He stops. Hessa explicitly counts noticing and stopping that expectation-performance as improved method behavior.

The controlled external attempt produces no observed displacement. Neither target nor control moves during the attempt. The target trembles only after Greg exhales; breath explains that movement.

Hessa records the null and refuses an immediate repeat. A useful failure is enough for the day. She plans a narrower related question after two days rather than chasing the desired result.

Current exact counts by the end of Chapter 176:
- **21 successful supervised minimal draws**;
- **17 successful supervised deliberate shaping attempts**;
- **4 supervised external-effect attempts**.

Current ceiling by the end of Chapter 176:
- minimal draw: increasingly repeatable and retained after travel;
- localized shaping: increasingly repeatable and retained after travel;
- maintaining draw while altering: increasingly repeatable;
- RETURN: increasingly repeatable;
- avoiding broad old response / motor recruitment: increasingly repeatable;
- direction-first control: **provisionally supported under a narrow self-reported condition, with interpretation weakened by better baseline geometry**;
- neutral shape may have repeatable asymmetry: **supported as a live possibility, not established cause or anatomy**;
- controlled small external displacement under the Chapter 176 paper-tab setup: **not observed on attempt four**;
- external effect overall: **unestablished**;
- spell: **not established**;
- casting: **not cleared**;
- Barrier: **not cleared**;
- independent magical work: **still prohibited**.

## External-effect threshold

Hessa's threshold is not a secret number.

Useful factors include:
- deliberate initiation without broad unintended recruitment;
- localization toward a requested intact-side target;
- deliberate cessation or reduction while keeping the draw;
- normal release when told;
- absence of concerning physical symptoms;
- no familiar unrequested casting pattern taking over;
- enough repetition across changed conditions that accident becomes less convincing;
- enough internal control that an external result would be interpretable;
- an apparatus/question that does not create more ambiguity than it resolves;
- enough reason to spend another safe attempt on the external question;
- enough understanding of starting geometry that a reported directional change can be interpreted against baseline;
- enough expectation control that Greg can notice when he is trying to manufacture the desired story and stop before treating that urge as data.

A permission for one external test is not general external-test clearance. It is not casting clearance.

A provisional internal success is not automatically a reason to return to external testing.

A null external attempt is not an automatic reason to repeat immediately. If the condition was clean enough to answer the day's question and expectation is now stronger, stopping may preserve more information than another try.

## With Greg

There is real intellectual trust under the irritation. Hessa increasingly expects Greg to understand the discipline and can make him state the rule instead of lecturing him again.

Greg becomes more Hessa-shaped in this relationship than anywhere else. That is relationship-specific and should not spread unchanged into every conversation.

Hessa owns the magic lane. Pessa owns movement/training. Greg owns the body. Chapter 94's jurisdiction matters because the practical consequence is cross-reporting, not philosophy.

Hessa also does not own Greg's ordinary life.

Chapter 139 makes that boundary especially clear when Greg considers traveling east with the theatre. Hessa can say what travel changes medically: she will not be present if he becomes sick, injured, exhausted, or develops a new symptom; the next supervised assessment waits until he returns; independent magical work remains uncleared. She does **not** grant or deny permission to travel.

`Go because you want to go.` is a jurisdictional line. Do not turn it into generic therapeutic wisdom. Greg is trying to make her objection or approval part of his decision. She refuses that role.

Boring intervals remain part of evidence. Chapter 139 also shows that the interval can expand because ordinary life intervenes. A five- or six-day trip is not automatically a failure of the magical progression. If nothing concerning changes, the supervised work can resume when Greg returns.

Chapter 176 confirms that principle after a much longer road interval. Hessa first checks whether Greg drew, shaped, tested, or did anything prohibited. He did not. She does not punish the gap or restart the progression from zero. His supervised minimal draw and shaping remain available, so the next question can proceed from current evidence.

Hessa may give Greg an assessment artifact while still controlling what it means. She gives him the newer drawing but warns against reconstructing the sensation, turning travel into unrecorded testing, or even physically folding the sheet through the boundary in a way that may contaminate later memory.

Chapter 176 also keeps ordinary work outside her authority. When Greg says he must return to the theatre by four, Hessa treats that as a real constraint: `You have work.` The magic session fits around it rather than consuming his day because the experiment is important.

- `You are asking what you want it to mean. I asked what happened.`

Calibration only.

## Overlap allowed

Hessa and Greg share evidence vocabulary because she taught and reinforced it. That overlap is meaningful.

Hessa and Pessa may both use `No`, `Maybe`, `Good`, and short correction loops. Difference:
- Hessa: controlled magical/body response, expectation contamination, repeatability, practitioner margin, baseline geometry, progression criteria, apparatus ambiguity, permission;
- Pessa: physical movement, object load, drill objective, fatigue, integrated use, next embodied adjustment.

Nessa can discuss prepared theatre effects and Greg can bring those observations into Hessa's room, but Hessa does not inherit stagecraft authority. She separates what the prepared object does, what the room/audience does, and what Greg's own magic has actually established.

Other characters should not casually inherit Hessa's full reasoning architecture because the author likes it.

- converting one external-test permission into general external-work or casting clearance;
- salvaging a preferred hypothesis by changing the question after failed replication;
- treating memorable apparatus behavior as worth endless safe attempts;
- pretending practitioner notes, expectations, or instructions cannot create bias;

- treating a neutral-shape baseline as a directional test;
- assuming later baseline evidence must either validate or erase earlier observations rather than changing their weight;

- repeating a clean null external attempt immediately because a more dramatic answer would be emotionally satisfying.

Hessa is strongest when exactness belongs specifically to the work she is doing.
