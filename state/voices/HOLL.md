# HOLL — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Core lens

Holl thinks from **commercial sorting and throughput** outward.

He cares about:
- which pile a worker should choose;
- whether a cheap piece is usable enough for the customer;
- how much expert recheck time the process consumes;
- what a wrong band costs compared with a false reject;
- whether a worker can make the decision without Holl standing beside them;
- whether the customer actually needs the same tolerance as the next customer;
- whether the worker is actually the thing that needs changing.

His precision is commercial and workflow-bound. He does not need a complete theory of the material to know which decision his shop must make.

Chapter 96 sharpens the last point. Worker behavior, bench condition, tray behavior, reference setup, and Holl's own recheck load can all contribute to the result. Holl does not need to model all of them forever, but he does need enough distinction to avoid changing the wrong thing.

## Spoken rhythm

Short to medium, blunt, and comfortable with trade shorthand.

He often gives Greg exactly the operating answer he asked for and stops there. If Greg turns the problem into a larger measurement system, Holl is willing to follow only while the abstraction still helps the shop.

He can explain more fully when the question reaches price, customer consequence, expert time, or whether a worker is actually causing the problem.

When Greg asks what decision Holl needs now, Holl can answer at the level that matters: whether to change how Iris works. That current decision is a stronger organizing principle for him than collecting every imaginable variable.

## Vocabulary and register

Natural domains:
- weak / usable / upper bands;
- piles;
- lots;
- returns;
- rejects;
- set-asides;
- rechecks;
- customers;
- price;
- throughput;
- worker time;
- his own close-piece judgment;
- benches, trays, fixtures, and other shop conditions when they actually alter sorting work.

Do not make him sound like a statistics lecturer merely because Greg can describe the problem statistically.

## Humor

Dry and work-annoyed.

Holl is often funniest when Greg supplies a grand label and Holl rejects the label while keeping the useful procedure.

He can also answer Greg's qualification with the physical/commercial fact he already knows, as when `Maybe` meets `It was bad stone.`

His humor should not make him immune to being annoyed by his own shop. Chapter 96's sticky tray is funny because the workers treat a persistent fixture problem as ordinary while Holl discovers his own fields never asked for it.

## Information behavior

Holl knows the shop from repeated handling, customer consequences, and what reaches his table for recheck.

He may know confidently that a lot is bad without having instrument-grade evidence for every reason Greg wants separated.

He also knows the limits of his records. He will not pretend he tracked every piece merely because Greg can imagine a cleaner dataset.

A major recurring boundary is **transferability**: a useful system should let an ordinary worker make the needed sorting decision without requiring Holl on every piece.

Chapter 96 adds a second boundary: **the record only knows what the shop bothered to ask**. Iris can truthfully say the right tray sticks while also never having written that fact down because Holl asked her to record ambiguous pieces, not tray behavior. Holl is allowed to discover a blind spot in his own process.

He does not automatically respond by rebuilding everything. If the current decision is whether to change a worker, a few more days tracking bench condition may be enough. The commercial question sets the scope.

## With Greg

Holl values Greg when Greg helps him make a real business decision, not when Greg merely produces more interesting questions.

Their useful difference:
- Greg expands the model and worries about denominators, false rejects, hidden customer differences, bench effects, and what the records fail to show;
- Holl asks what he should actually change, whether the worker is too loose, what the change costs, how much of his own time the process consumes, and whether the shop can keep moving.

Holl is willing to pay Greg for useful judgment. He does not need Greg to own the process afterward.

He can accept a bounded comparison or sample while refusing Greg's language for it. `Good experimental design` becoming `business annoyance` is representative.

Chapter 96 also shows that Holl can tolerate an answer that postpones action. If the evidence does not yet justify changing Iris's work, `then don't` is usable because changing the wrong part of the shop also costs money.

## Canon lines / fingerprints

- **CANON LINE:** `I sort and certify cheap suppression stock for people who don't want to pay for better stone.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Which pile.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Something a worker can use without me.`
- **CANON LINE:** `It was bad stone.`
- **CANON LINE:** `And sometimes the worker is the problem.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Whether to change how Iris works.`
- **CANON LINE:** `And my time?`
- **CANON PATTERN:** when Greg calls a bounded comparison `experimental design`, Holl tells him not to call it that and accepts `business annoyance` instead.
- **CANON PATTERN:** samples clear calls as well as ambiguous calls, then treats one worker's higher ambiguity rate separately from sampled clear-call error and Holl's own recheck time.
- **CANON PATTERN:** discovers the sticky right tray only because Iris says it during the test; his original record fields did not ask for that process condition.

These are evidence, not catchphrases.

## Overlap allowed

Holl can be concise and empirical like Hessa, Merra, Antonius, or Greg.

The distinction is what closes the question:
- Hessa closes on safe/repeatable evidence;
- Merra closes on what records support;
- Antonius closes on what the operation is willing to buy or risk;
- Holl closes on whether the shop can sort, sell, recheck, or change the worker/process without wasting more than the mistake costs.

## Watch list

Avoid turning Holl into:
- Hessa with rocks;
- a generic data analyst;
- a man who wants measurement for its own sake;
- a shop owner who automatically blames or automatically excuses workers;
- a theory-heavy materials expert when the manuscript gives him practical commercial authority;
- Greg's permanent experiment partner;
- an omniscient process engineer who already knows every bench and fixture problem;
- somebody who expands data collection past the decision the shop actually needs to make.
