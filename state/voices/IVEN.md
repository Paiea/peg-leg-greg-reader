# IVEN — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Identity

Iven is the recurring guard/debtor actor explicitly named in Chapter 143.

He is a working actor and recurring scene partner for Greg, especially in Shopkeeper material.

## Core lens

Iven notices **whether the current scene still has a usable shape, which facts hold that shape up, and whether his partner is actually answering what is happening now**.

He is less interested than Greg in diagnosing performance as a system. He cares whether Greg is waiting for a dead version, stepping on him, forcing a line, missing the current cue, preserving the wrong detail, or leaving enough room for the exchange to continue.

Chapters 156-158 add an emergency-substitution layer: Iven can distinguish literal wording from the information the next beat actually depends on. Under severe time pressure, he is willing to cut language, prompt by functional keyword, accept a paraphrase, and protect the few facts that cannot safely move.

His competence can look lazy offstage because he sleeps constantly. Do not confuse low visible effort between scenes with low professional usefulness inside them.

## Spoken rhythm

Dry, economical, and often flatter than Marek.

He can answer with:
- one word;
- a short correction;
- a deliberately incomplete explanation;
- an altered line that forces Greg to answer rather than reproduce yesterday;
- a keyword that retrieves one load-bearing scene fact without feeding the whole sentence.

He does not need to soften a useful correction or turn it into coaching language.

Useful Iven shapes include:
- `Words.` after Greg misunderstands `You're stepping on me` physically;
- `Waiting for ghosts.`
- `Ghost.`
- `Go.`
- `It is now.` when the preceding live scene has changed the expected cue;
- `Seal.` / `Wagons.` / `King refuses.` / `From.` when Greg needs the functional skeleton of an emergency replacement role.

## Scene-partner cognition

Iven's strongest knowledge is practical scene shape.

Chapter 150 names it directly. If an actor knows the `shape` of the scene, some missing material can be filled under pressure. The relevant shape includes who wants what, who enters, who leaves, and which line somebody actually needs.

Iven's own warning is equally important:

`Don't invent too much.`

Emergency flexibility is not permission to rewrite the play for self-amusement.

He has personal evidence for this. He once learned a large replacement part during intermission and says simply that he was `Very bad.` The show still had a second half. Survival and quality are separate facts.

### Emergency substitution

Chapter 156 turns scene shape into actual page triage.

Greg inherits the sick Advocate's roughly forty-three lines with almost no preparation time. Iven immediately starts cutting.

He does not preserve or discard lines by literary preference alone. He keeps what the show needs:
- the forged/seized order;
- the seal;
- the three wagons;
- the King's refusal;
- the bridge consequence;
- the magistrate entrance that must still have a reason to happen.

When Greg produces a correct functional paraphrase and objects that it is not the actual line, Iven answers `Good.` Exact prose is not always the current success condition.

But his tolerance is not careless. A wrong north/south gate can be corrected and then dismissed as locally nonfatal. `FROM` versus `TO` is different. Reversing that word reverses the bridge action and therefore changes what later characters are responding to.

The useful distinction is:

**protect downstream load, not surface fidelity for its own sake.**

Do not turn this into a general Iven manifesto against written text. This is emergency replacement work under brutal time pressure.

### Retrieval under pressure

Iven often prompts Greg with the smallest cue that retrieves the next necessary structure:

- `Seal.`
- `Wagons.`
- `King refuses.`
- `From.`

The keyword is enough because Greg already has the surrounding role in memory. This is different from Teren's formal prompting lane. Iven is helping a scene partner carry a specific replacement role through the current show.

He also knows when checking has become counterproductive. In Chapter 158 Greg keeps drilling `FROM` outside its actual scene position. Iven tells him `Stop checking.` The fact matters. Rehearsing it until it contaminates sequence does not help.

## With Greg

Iven is an increasingly useful peer/rival scene partner.

He can identify Greg's recurring performance failures without becoming a mentor:
- visible thinking that removes him from the exchange;
- waiting for the successful laugh or pause from a prior run;
- speaking too quickly to avoid that waiting;
- stepping on Iven's line;
- trying to make Iven say the setup Greg wants;
- changing tempo merely because Iven mentioned fatigue;
- preserving exact words while losing the event the words were supposed to cause;
- checking one dangerous detail so hard that it enters at the wrong moment.

Chapter 147 gives the cleanest earlier sequence:
- Greg waits for the old successful timing;
- Iven calls it `Waiting for ghosts`;
- Greg overcorrects and rushes;
- Iven says `You're stepping on me.`
- Greg mistakes that as physical distance;
- Iven answers `Words.`

Later, when Greg tries to force a known consultation exchange, Iven simply refuses to give him the expected setup.

Chapter 149 repeats the lesson. A coat improvisation works once. Greg reaches for a version of it on the next run and the scene worsens. Iven calls `Ghost.`

Chapter 152 makes the anti-repetition behavior explicit before the second show: do not do the dead uncle merely because Greg wants the thing that worked.

Chapters 156-158 add a different pressure. Greg is no longer merely waiting for yesterday. He is trying to hold too much new material at once. Iven responds by reducing the retrieval problem to the few facts the scene actually needs.

## Cue behavior

Iven treats the cue as part of the live scene, not a sacred remembered sentence.

If the prior scene runs long, skips lines, or changes shape, Greg's expected cue can cease to exist. In Chapter 151 Iven tells him `Go.` Greg says that is not his cue. Iven answers `It is now.`

This does not make Iven the prompter. Teren still owns formal page/cue/reset authority. Iven is telling his current scene partner that the live entrance has arrived.

Chapter 156 adds that he can also test readiness mischievously. Before Shopkeeper he says `Go` before the cue, Greg correctly refuses, and Iven says `Correct.` This should remain occasional partner pressure, not a constant trick.

## Improvisation

Iven can alter lines to keep Greg responsive rather than rehearsed.

He is willing to:
- vary the debtor's explanation;
- change the uncle/aunt/coat/purse material;
- use Greg's unexpected line if it gives the scene something playable;
- recover a late or wrong constable without stopping the show;
- accept an emergency-role paraphrase when it preserves the necessary event.

He also lets dead lines die. Not every bad Greg answer requires rescue or commentary.

His humor is often partner pressure rather than standalone joke construction.

Chapter 158 gives a clean boring-success example. After Sword produces no notable disaster, Iven says `Good.` Greg says nothing happened. Iven answers `Exactly.` A live role that does not become a problem can be a successful result.

## Offstage rhythm

Iven sleeps whenever the work allows it, eats readily, and does not make preparedness look ceremonial.

This should not become a narcolepsy gimmick. The useful contrast is that he can look half asleep offstage and still know exactly when Greg is stepping on him once the scene starts.

His tiredness language can be practical. Before open rehearsal he asks whether Greg is tired, then warns him not to make the scene slow because he is tired and not to make it fast merely because Iven said that.

During emergency substitution, he can wake, cut pages, run the functional skeleton, disappear to sleep again, and return when the work needs him. Do not convert this into mystical effortless competence. He is simply experienced enough to know which part of the current problem deserves his attention.

## Overlap allowed

Iven and Marek both understand live scene shape.

Difference:
- Marek is more socially expansive, performative, shameless, and likely to turn embarrassment into visible momentum;
- Iven is drier and more partner-local, often ending the correction after one usable sentence.

Iven and Serra can both correct Greg as scene partners.

Difference:
- Serra often speaks from what she physically or relationally has to answer as the partner in the scene;
- Iven often speaks from timing, line space, current cue, functional information load, and whether Greg is forcing an old exchange.

Iven and Teren can both say `Go`, `Again`, `No`, or participate in cuts, but Iven does not own the formal rehearsal reset or company page state. Chapter 156 shows Teren accepting Iven's emergency cuts, not Iven replacing Teren's authority.
