# IVEN — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Identity

Iven is the recurring guard/debtor actor explicitly named in Chapter 143.

He is a working actor and recurring scene partner for Greg, especially in Shopkeeper material.

## Core lens

Iven notices **whether the current scene still has a usable shape and whether his partner is actually answering what is happening now**.

He is less interested than Greg in diagnosing performance as a system. He cares whether Greg is waiting for a dead version, stepping on him, forcing a line, missing the current cue, or leaving enough room for the exchange to continue.

His competence can look lazy offstage because he sleeps constantly. Do not confuse low visible effort between scenes with low professional usefulness inside them.

## Spoken rhythm

Dry, economical, and often flatter than Marek.

He can answer with:
- one word;
- a short correction;
- a deliberately incomplete explanation;
- an altered line that forces Greg to answer rather than reproduce yesterday.

He does not need to soften a useful correction or turn it into coaching language.

Useful Iven shapes include:
- `Words.` after Greg misunderstands `You're stepping on me` physically;
- `Waiting for ghosts.`
- `Ghost.`
- `Go.`
- `It is now.` when the preceding live scene has changed the expected cue.

## Scene-partner cognition

Iven's strongest knowledge is practical scene shape.

Chapter 150 names it directly. If an actor knows the `shape` of the scene, some missing material can be filled under pressure. The relevant shape includes who wants what, who enters, who leaves, and which line somebody actually needs.

Iven's own warning is equally important:

`Don't invent too much.`

Emergency flexibility is not permission to rewrite the play for self-amusement.

He has personal evidence for this. He once learned a large replacement part during intermission and says simply that he was `Very bad.` The show still had a second half. Survival and quality are separate facts.

## With Greg

Iven is an increasingly useful peer/rival scene partner.

He can identify Greg's recurring performance failures without becoming a mentor:
- visible thinking that removes him from the exchange;
- waiting for the successful laugh or pause from a prior run;
- speaking too quickly to avoid that waiting;
- stepping on Iven's line;
- trying to make Iven say the setup Greg wants;
- changing tempo merely because Iven mentioned fatigue.

Chapter 147 gives the cleanest sequence:
- Greg waits for the old successful timing;
- Iven calls it `Waiting for ghosts`;
- Greg overcorrects and rushes;
- Iven says `You're stepping on me.`
- Greg mistakes that as physical distance;
- Iven answers `Words.`

Later, when Greg tries to force a known consultation exchange, Iven simply refuses to give him the expected setup.

Chapter 149 repeats the lesson. A coat improvisation works once. Greg reaches for a version of it on the next run and the scene worsens. Iven calls `Ghost.`

Chapter 152 makes the anti-repetition behavior explicit before the second show: do not do the dead uncle merely because Greg wants the thing that worked.

## Cue behavior

Iven treats the cue as part of the live scene, not a sacred remembered sentence.

If the prior scene runs long, skips lines, or changes shape, Greg's expected cue can cease to exist. In Chapter 151 Iven tells him `Go.` Greg says that is not his cue. Iven answers `It is now.`

This does not make Iven the prompter. Teren still owns formal page/cue/reset authority. Iven is telling his current scene partner that the live entrance has arrived.

## Improvisation

Iven can alter lines to keep Greg responsive rather than rehearsed.

He is willing to:
- vary the debtor's explanation;
- change the uncle/aunt/coat/purse material;
- use Greg's unexpected line if it gives the scene something playable;
- recover a late or wrong constable without stopping the show.

He also lets dead lines die. Not every bad Greg answer requires rescue or commentary.

His humor is often partner pressure rather than standalone joke construction.

## Offstage rhythm

Iven sleeps whenever the work allows it, eats readily, and does not make preparedness look ceremonial.

This should not become a narcolepsy gimmick. The useful contrast is that he can look half asleep offstage and still know exactly when Greg is stepping on him once the scene starts.

His tiredness language can be practical. Before open rehearsal he asks whether Greg is tired, then warns him not to make the scene slow because he is tired and not to make it fast merely because Iven said that.

## Canon lines / fingerprints

- **CANON LINE:** `Waiting for ghosts.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Words.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Ghost.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Don't invent too much.`
- **CANON LINE:** `Very bad.` about learning a large replacement role during intermission.
- **CANON LINE:** `It is now.` when Greg says the live entrance is not his remembered cue.
- **CANON PATTERN:** changes a scene line so Greg must answer the current exchange instead of reproducing a successful prior bit.
- **CANON PATTERN:** gives terse timing/partner corrections without converting them into acting theory.
- **CANON PATTERN:** can be extremely sleepy offstage while remaining alert to partner timing once the scene begins.
- **CANON PATTERN:** treats a successful improvisation as usable in that run, not automatically repeatable next time.

## Voice tests — NON-CANON

- `You heard the old cue. I said the new line.`
- `If you want yesterday, find yesterday's audience.`
- `You can be tired after my line.`

Calibration only.

## Overlap allowed

Iven and Marek both understand live scene shape.

Difference:
- Marek is more socially expansive, performative, shameless, and likely to turn embarrassment into visible momentum;
- Iven is drier and more partner-local, often ending the correction after one usable sentence.

Iven and Serra can both correct Greg as scene partners.

Difference:
- Serra often speaks from what she physically or relationally has to answer as the partner in the scene;
- Iven often speaks from timing, line space, current cue, and whether Greg is forcing an old exchange.

Iven and Teren can both say `Go`, `Again`, or `No`, but Iven does not own the formal rehearsal reset or company page state.

## Watch list

Avoid:
- sleepy fool;
- secret acting master;
- Teren without pages;
- Marek with less energy;
- every note becoming `ghost` as a catchphrase;
- making his improvisation automatically better than written material;
- treating `scene shape` as a formal method he lectures about;
- making emergency substitution competence glamorous when his own example includes being very bad;
- turning peer correction into a mentor/student hierarchy with Greg.
