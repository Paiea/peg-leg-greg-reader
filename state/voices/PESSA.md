# PESSA — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Core lens

Pessa notices **what the body, road, route, or drill is actually doing** and what decision follows from that physical reality.

Her precision is embodied and field-practical. She is not Greg with fewer words and not Hessa transplanted outdoors.

When Greg wants a category, she often wants the consequence.

When Greg wants a model, she often wants the next usable decision.

When a trainee wants to count an outcome as success, she asks what the drill was supposed to train.

Later adaptive training sharpens this further: Pessa does not begin with a theory of disabled combat or by carving Greg's old fighting into a list of supposedly transferable parts. She first asks what he actually wants to be able to do, then what changes when the body meets one concrete object or demand.

Chapter 102 adds an important limit to Greg's analytical language. Simplifying an exercise does not mean the real movement has become isolated. Pessa may remove an object because it gives Greg too many things to blame, then still refuse his claim that she has isolated one variable.

Chapter 110 adds the next practical layer: once Greg can move through a simple plan, Pessa can deliberately make the plan stop working and watch whether he keeps choosing. The useful result is not a universal fallback rule. A stop, a wider route, finishing the current turn, or abandoning the shortest path may each be right once and wrong later because the physical situation changed.

## Spoken rhythm

Compact and declarative. She can explain fully when a correction needs a reason, but usually starts with the smallest physical truth that changes the attempt.

Common shapes:
- one-word correction;
- concrete observation;
- short explanation tied to body/ground/objective;
- reset and try again.

She does not usually turn a correction into a general theory unless the scene genuinely requires it.

## Adaptive training with Greg

Chapters 92-93 establish a useful method that future scenes should preserve without turning it into a formal named system.

Pessa refuses Greg's first instinct to decompose `combat` into a checklist and build an adaptation architecture from twenty minutes of observation. She narrows the goal until it becomes physically usable: what does Greg want to be able to do today?

From there she starts smaller than Greg wants:
- hold the shield;
- notice what changes;
- change crutch position;
- turn the head;
- track an object;
- push;
- stop when the body changes enough to matter.

The first phase is **inventory before architecture**. A useful observation does not automatically become a universal solution.

She is particularly alert to Greg solving the exercise instead of learning the body. If he moves the chair against a wall to prevent sliding, her answer is that he will then learn the wall. If he starts seated because seated is easier, he may prematurely decide seated is the answer.

Chapter 102 sharpens the method again. Pessa removes the shield after it makes Greg's turning problem louder, but she rejects Greg's description that she is `isolating turning`. Her answer is simpler: she wants to watch him turn. Head, shoulders, pelvis, right foot, missing-side support expectation, crutches, destination, another person, speed, direction, and fatigue continue interacting even in the simplified drill.

This distinction matters. Hessa may deliberately isolate or control a test variable because the question requires it. Pessa often cares about movement **as used**, where the parts stop being separable the moment the person actually turns, reacts, or occupies space with somebody else.

She can still simplify aggressively. Removing the shield, using circles, changing direction, taking away one crutch, giving unpredictable destinations, or changing the apparent urgency are ways to make a pattern easier to see without pretending the body has become a laboratory component.

Chapter 110 pushes this from observation into adaptive choice. Pessa uses irregular stakes, walls, her own body in the route, one versus two crutches, a fallen obstacle, and destination changes that arrive during a turn. She is not searching for the one optimal route. She is watching whether Greg can keep making choices after the first plan stops working.

That distinction produces several durable behaviors:
- stopping can be a legitimate chosen response rather than automatic failure;
- finishing the current turn before obeying a new direction may work once and fail the next time because the wall is closer;
- going around an obstacle may be safer until Pessa occupies that route;
- an open route can make Greg accelerate without deciding to;
- two crutches can provide support while also creating crowding;
- one crutch can create space while also making the missing side feel exposed.

Do not turn any of these into doctrine. Pessa's own correction is `Today.` The lesson remains attached to the body and conditions of that attempt.

When Greg hears a larger life principle in `whether you can keep making choices after the first plan stops working`, he immediately narrows it to `In this yard.` Pessa agrees. Preserve that jurisdiction. She is not secretly delivering a philosophy lecture through stakes.

Pessa can admit `I don't know` when Greg's changed body creates a training problem she has not yet solved. That uncertainty is local and practical, not a performance of methodological humility.

## With Greg

Pessa does not treat Greg's analysis as final merely because it is intelligent.

She is comfortable saying `No` when he is close but wrong, then giving the narrower physical correction.

She can use Greg as an observer or extra set of eyes while retaining final authority inside her lane.

She does not need to reassure him that his interpretation was almost right.

When Greg jumps from an event to a category, Pessa often pulls him one level back. `The problem is sequencing` may become `Maybe the problem is sequencing.` `Less torque` may become the more useful human answer `Feels safer.` `To isolate turning` may become `To watch you turn.`

She is also willing to let repetition establish ownership of a mistake. A repeated crutch collision can be useful because it shows the pattern belongs to Greg's movement rather than one random attempt.

Chapter 110 adds that Greg does not have to perform every available repetition. When fatigue is clear, Pessa can agree that he physically *can* do one more and still end the session. Ability to continue is not the same as a useful reason to continue.

## Jurisdiction with Hessa

Pessa does not compete with Hessa for total authority over Greg.

Hessa owns the magic lane. Pessa owns the movement/training lane. Pessa cares about magical work when it changes the body she is about to train, but she does not need the magical phenomenology itself if Hessa already owns that question.

Likewise, Pessa does not surrender her own stopping conditions merely because Hessa is involved. Her position is practical: she cares what she does, and tomorrow's training depends on swelling, hands, fatigue, and the actual physical response to today's work.

Their methods should not collapse into one scientific voice. Hessa may need controlled comparison. Pessa may explicitly reject Greg's attempt to describe integrated movement as isolated merely because one object was removed.

## With Alden

More coach than peer.

She likes repeatable attempts, shorter commitments, and letting fatigue expose what speed hides.

She does not confuse winning the exchange with succeeding at the drill.

Alden's eagerness is material to work with, not a personality defect to lecture away.

## Humor

Dry and situational. She can undercut Greg or Alden without changing register into a comedy voice.

Her jokes usually come from the immediate work or from somebody pretending a physical fact does not exist.

- making every correction a life lesson;
- turning `No` into a catchphrase;
- making her an all-purpose combat master outside manuscript evidence;
- letting Greg's abstract explanation overwrite her embodied one;
- confusing road priority with simple severity ranking;
- inventing a polished adaptive-combat methodology before the manuscript earns one;
- treating seated work, walls, crutches, or any one workaround as the answer merely because it solves one attempt;
- making every simplified drill into a laboratory-style isolated-variable exercise;
- turning `keep making choices` into a general resilience slogan detached from the physical yard;
- treating stopping as failure when stopping was the deliberate safe choice under the current geometry.
