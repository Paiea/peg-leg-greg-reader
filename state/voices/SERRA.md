# SERRA — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Identity

Serra is the recurring rehearsal/acting woman explicitly named in Chapter 143.

Keep her distinct from `SERA.md` and `SERA_DAIN.md`. Similar names are not identity evidence.

## Core lens

Serra notices **what a scene partner can actually play from**: path, distance, eye line, timing, what reaction she has been given, what information the scene still needs, and what the current audience/partner makes possible now.

Her theatre precision is actor-facing rather than page-facing or object-facing.

She is also a person before she is Greg's rehearsal condition. Her own mood, relationships, fatigue, and choices can change what she gives him onstage without owing him a technical explanation.

## Spoken rhythm

Direct, playable, and comfortable with short corrections.

Useful Serra shapes include:
- `Again.`
- `Choose.`
- `Once.`
- `Don't.`
- `Too grand.`
- `Listen.`
- naming what she physically had to answer from Greg rather than theorizing acting in general.

She can explain more when Greg asks a question that belongs to her experience as a scene partner, but she does not turn that answer into doctrine.

## Acting / staging behavior

Serra's strongest corrections come from relationship inside the scene.

Established patterns include:
- Greg moves twice when one committed move is useful: `Choose.` / `Once.`
- Greg nods during a silent role and she stops him because `then I have to answer the nod`;
- Greg's Sword voice can make her look at Greg instead of the prop;
- a voice can become `too grand` because the playable comedy changes;
- a line can work because the partner receives something usable even when Greg cannot explain why.

Chapters 147-151 deepen this substantially.

In rehearsal, Serra may **refuse a helpful recovery** if the recovery hides information she needs. When she skips a line and Marek covers it, she stops the scene: `Because you covered.` / `I need to know I skipped.` Scene survival is not always the rehearsal objective.

She also refuses to become a repeatable stimulus for Greg. When he expects the Princess movement/rhythm he learned earlier, Serra says she is not going to do the same thing. Asked why: `Because I don't want to.` The partner is allowed to change because the partner is a person.

In open rehearsal and live performance, she can restore missing information without making the repair visible. If Marek enters early, she can fold the missing Sword information into a new line and keep the scene moving.

A successful improvisation does not become fixed text. `A spoon` works during open rehearsal. In the paid show she gives `A chamber pot.` Asked about the change: `Spoon was yesterday.`

Do not turn these into universal acting rules. They are observations from somebody sharing the scene.

## Current-partner behavior

Greg's recurring failure is to answer yesterday's Serra rather than today's.

Serra may:
- change her path because the stage is different;
- sit where she previously crossed;
- hold the Sword differently;
- change pace because she wants to;
- change an improvised line because the prior version belonged to another run;
- give a missing partner a playable cue without making the rescue obvious.

Her Chapter 149 correction `Listen.` is not mystical advice. Greg cannot see her from the Sword position and keeps waiting for remembered movement. The words are the current usable cue.

## Humor

Social, quick, and comfortable inside rehearsal disorder.

Serra can tease Greg's accumulating theatre identities without dominating the scene. She can also let a ridiculous prop, bad costume choice, missing belt, or successful `spoon` joke remain funny without converting it into commentary.

Her humor can coexist with being tired or privately upset. Chapters 147-151 repeatedly show that personal strain does not erase professional competence or require emotional exposition before she can work.

## Disagreement / refusal

Serra can say `No` because something gives her the wrong partner behavior, forces an unnecessary response, hides a mistake she needs to notice, or breaks the scene she is currently playing.

Her refusal should not sound like Hessa's evidence boundary or Nessa's material continuity. It is often: **I cannot play or learn the next beat cleanly from what you just gave me.**

She also has a personal refusal lane. Greg does not automatically get context about the yellow-scarf woman or Serra's outside relationships. Marek can tell him `Not yours`; Serra does not need to turn private strain into Greg's subplot.

## Information behavior

Serra knows troupe history, roles, actor habits, and enough current work to move inside the company without explaining herself to Greg.

Chapter 143 establishes that she knows the names behind Greg's role labels: Iven, Marek, Orin, Davin, and her own.

She is not the company's contract, pay, or route authority.

She may have relationships and errands outside Greg's view. Chapters 145-151 preserve that independence. The yellow-scarf woman visibly matters to Serra, but exact prose does not yet settle the relationship and the voice page should not invent it.

## With Greg

The relationship is increasingly peer-like and low ceremony.

Serra can:
- correct his stage movement;
- laugh at him;
- give actor feedback;
- give him a blanket when the wagon floor is impractical;
- notice when his body answer comes too quickly after travel;
- adjust to his walking pace without announcing it;
- move on once the useful answer arrives;
- change her performance without asking Greg whether he is ready for the change.

Chapter 142 gives a clean travel pattern. Serra offers the wagon floor, understands why getting up is the problem, and pushes Greg a blanket for the crate. No pity speech.

Chapter 143 repeats the low-ceremony body awareness after a long ride. Greg's right leg almost folds; Serra is close without grabbing him; his first `yes` is too fast; she asks again and accepts `Stiff. Ordinary.`

Chapter 148 adds ordinary pacing. On the walk back from the municipal hall, she adjusts to Greg's pace after half a block without discussing it. Greg does not ask where she has been. She does not ask how a roof gave him an apple.

That mutual non-explanation matters. Peer intimacy does not require total access.

## Overlap allowed

Serra and Teren can both say `Again`, `No`, or give small performance corrections.

Difference:
- Teren protects page, cue, prompt, cut, reset, and company consequence;
- Serra protects partner relationship, staging behavior, playable reaction, and what the actor can do with the current beat.

Serra and Iven can both correct Greg as scene partners. Serra often speaks from what she must physically/relationally answer as Princess or another partner. Iven often pressures timing, cue, and scene shape from inside the exchange.

Serra and Nessa can both care where bodies and objects go. Serra's question is what the actor can play from. Nessa's is what the costume/prop/material path must physically do.

Serra can share Lyssa's low-ceremony practical awareness of Greg's mobility, but the relationship species is different. Serra is a theatre peer, not Greg's intimate partner.
