# TEREN — VOICE AUTHORITY

Exact manuscript prose outranks this file.

## Core lens

Teren's demonstrated theatre lane is **prompting and scene continuity at actor scale**.

He knows the page, but the page is not the whole job. He watches whether a particular actor is still doing the scene, actually searching for a line, waiting intentionally, breathing, recovering, or losing the ability to continue.

His precision is local and person-specific. One actor's hand tightening can be useful evidence for that actor without becoming a universal acting rule.

Teren can also give brief playable corrections when the immediate scene requires them, but do not inflate that into universal directorial authority.

Chapter 128 adds an important distinction: **a recovery can keep the scene alive and still be wrong for the intended entrance, prop handoff, or rehearsal question.** Teren can let an imperfect recovery continue, then reset the exact local problem without pretending survival made the original mistake correct.

## Spoken rhythm

Brief without being cryptic for its own sake.

He tends to give:
- the first few words an actor needs;
- one local cue to watch;
- one concrete reason when Greg asks the right-sized question;
- a prop or entrance noun when that is the missing thing;
- another run or a return to the page.

`Again` does a great deal of work in his scenes because rehearsal itself supplies the next explanation.

He does not need to turn theatre work into philosophy.

## Prompting behavior

Chapters 116 and 118 establish the practical shape of Teren's job.

A prompter is not a machine that knows the script. Teren must distinguish among silences that look similar from outside:
- an intentional pause;
- an actor still inside the scene;
- somebody breathing or thinking;
- somebody genuinely unable to continue;
- somebody with the wrong page or a changed rehearsal problem.

He can prompt too early or too late. The work is human, not sacred.

He uses the page as support while watching people and traffic. He may look at the stage, a particular actor, a doorway, or a work interruption rather than keeping his eyes buried in text.

His most useful explanations stay specific. When Greg asks how Teren knew one actor had forgotten, Teren tells him to `Watch his hand.` When Greg immediately asks whether that cue is universal, Teren says `No.` Asked why he mentioned it, he answers `For him.`

That is the durable principle: **person first, rule second, if a rule is useful at all**.

Teren also marks practical interruption reasons so the room can restart after six other things happen. `Door.` can be enough because the note is for future Teren, not for a theory of rehearsal.

## Rehearsal recovery

Chapter 128 expands Teren from prompting into a nearby but still local lane: keeping a rehearsal question intact while actors recover around mistakes.

Greg's temporary messenger work produces several kinds of failure:
- wrong blue door;
- late prop handoff;
- wrong exit;
- entrance on a plausible but early cue;
- forgotten letter;
- attention pulled toward a noisy floorboard or failed stage effect.

Teren's response is usually the smallest thing that lets the room test the next run:
- `Other way.`
- `Then go.`
- first words of a missed line;
- `Letter.`
- `Stop.`
- `Again.`

When Greg enters too early but the actors recover and the scene continues, Teren later answers Greg's `Was that wrong?` with `Yes.` The recurring rehearsal woman can simultaneously say `It worked.` Those claims do not conflict. The improvised recovery worked; the entrance was still wrong for the rehearsal question.

Likewise, when Greg forgets the letter and improvises, Teren does not need a lecture about failure recovery. He lets the scene run until there is a useful stopping point, identifies the missing object, restores it, and runs again.

Preserve this distinction:

**scene survival is not the same as executing the intended rehearsal task.**

Teren can value the first without silently grading the second as correct.

## Humor

Usually secondary to work. He may be dry, careless with his lunch, or willing to tease Greg, but he is not there to win banter.

Theatre work around him can be unserious without becoming unserious work.

## Disagreement / refusal

If Greg expands one useful observation into a general law, Teren should often pull it back to the exact actor or run.

He can refuse Greg's demand for a universal explanation simply because one is not needed to do the current job.

He also does not need Greg to return. Chapter 118's `Because I don't care.` is not cruelty. It preserves the low-pressure theatre relationship: Greg can come, not come, help, watch, or be replaced by the person whose chair/job it actually is.

Chapter 128 adds another refusal: Greg may want to optimize a floor sound, prop method, or improvised recovery that is not the current rehearsal problem. Teren can simply say `Stop`, `Later`, or run the intended entrance again.

## Information behavior

Teren explains enough to make the next observation usable.

He does not owe Greg a complete theory of acting, prompting, audience response, improvisation, or human behavior every rehearsal.

When the useful information is actor-specific, he says so rather than laundering it into a general principle.

When the useful information is simply which mark, prop, cut, or entrance the room is testing, he can name that and stop.

## With Greg

Greg wants to understand systems. Teren often benefits from stopping that process at the smallest useful scale.

The cleanest Chapter 116 sequence is:
- Greg sees a long silence;
- Teren does not prompt;
- the actor continues;
- later the actor genuinely forgets;
- Teren prompts;
- Greg asks how he knew;
- Teren points to one physical cue;
- Greg asks whether it is universal;
- Teren says no;
- `For him.`

Chapter 128 adds a second relationship pattern. Greg is no longer only watching prompt technique. Teren can hand him a temporary part because Greg is physically present and useful to the current rehearsal. The role can last longer than planned because the room keeps using him, then end without promotion or ceremony.

As Greg becomes familiar with the room, Teren's speech should compress further. Shared rehearsal context can replace explanation where earned.

Do not make every useful correction come from Teren. Chapter 128 already distributes authority: the recurring rehearsal woman owns staging and partner-recovery questions that are not Teren's page/prompt problem, while backstage workers own physical objects and routes.

## Voice tests — NON-CANON

- `He's still in it.`
- `Not for everyone. For her.`
- `Give him the first words, not the whole sentence.`

Calibration only.

## Overlap allowed

Teren can sound precise like Hessa or Pessa.

The distinction is source of precision:
- Hessa wants a body/magic claim that survives evidence and determines permission;
- Pessa wants the next usable physical decision under actual geometry;
- Teren wants a particular actor and scene to continue without unnecessary interference.

The recurring rehearsal woman's corrections may overlap Teren's use of `Again`, but her lane can be staging, partner relationship, and playable scene behavior while Teren remains strongest at page, cue, prompt, cut, and local continuity. Do not force a formal org chart from this overlap.

## Watch list

Avoid:
- aphorism machine;
- life coach;
- secret future-bluff instructor;
- universal theatre authority;
- generic acting guru;
- treating prompting as reading the script aloud;
- converting one actor's tell into a rule for all actors;
- every note becoming memorable wisdom;
- treating an improvisation that preserves the scene as automatically correct for the intended rehearsal task;
- making Teren explain every recovery when `Again` is enough;
- stealing staging or backstage authority from other theatre workers because Teren is the named recurring guide.

If a correction or prompt can be smaller and still keep the scene moving, prefer smaller.
